And in playful and serious matters. The distinguished Heumann reads: “and in serious matters.” But no alteration is needed. Those ancient authors of the second and third centuries certainly favor such verbal abbreviations, especially those who employ a brief and concise style and pursue abundance of substance rather than words. I would not hesitate to say that sufficiently numerous examples could be sought from Tacitus, Pliny, Suetonius, and other writers of that period. MENCKEN.—Thus Livy, 34.7.14: “And you ought to keep them in your power and guardianship.” Tacitus, *Histories* 4.36: “Abandoning themselves to luxury and banquets and nocturnal gatherings.” Pliny, *Panegyric* 21.3: “In our hearts and judgments you were father of the fatherland.” LINDNER.

Would will or reject the same things. Thus Heraldus printed it, on the authority, unless I am mistaken, of the Royal manuscript. Rigaltius and Sigismund Gelenius, however, have “to will or reject the same things,” which the distinguished Gruner explains as infinitives possessing the nature of nouns. Wopkenius, to whom these four words seem to savor of a gloss, should by no means be heard. Yet he himself, as though placing little confidence in this conjecture of his, immediately adds: [Col. 0234A] “Not infrequently the same or nearly the same thing is thus reiterated in different words, without necessity, and that by asyndeton [ἀσύνδετον]”; and on this point he appeals to chapter 3, “would dart up, would emerge”; 5, “a matter for indignation, for grief”; 11, “dead, extinct”; 37, “be burned, be cremated”; 24, “they know, they are aware.” The distinguished Heumann wishes the text to be read thus: “would harmonize, and would will the same things whether he wished or not.” But F. O. Mencken proves with sufficiently numerous examples that ancient authors very frequently employed this manner of expression; to these I add Pliny, book X, Letter 50: “It remains for you to send a surveyor or architect.” Plorusi [?], 9.10: “Lest power be corrupted by isolation or delay.” Our Minucius, 20: “Antiquity, delighted or captivated by fables.” The same, 25: “By whose food or disgust.” There can therefore be no doubt that Felix could have spoken as the text is commonly read. I would, however, more readily believe that he deliberately did so, the more clearly he himself understood that care had to be taken lest refined ears be offended by the repetition of those two little words of the same form and sound. LINDNER.

You would have believed that one mind . . . had been divided. There is no fault in these words. Lactantius, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*, chapter 8: “For they could not have remained united in such faithful friendship unless there had been one mind in both.” See Persius, *Satire* V.23 and the scholia there; and Jerome, Letters I and XV, [Col. 0234C] ed. BB. DAVIS.

Thus, he alone privy to my affections, himself my companion. I understand this as follows: “Thus he who alone shared my affections—as that most trustworthy companion himself can testify—also shared my errors.” Here is a similar passage in Cicero, who writes of Curio in book VII, Letter 32: “He is, as you know, among those dear to me.” Minucius might perhaps have expressed this as follows: “He himself shares my confidence, among those dear to me.” Scheffer deletes *conscius* (“privy”) and thinks the text should be read: “Thus my companion in affection himself was my companion in error.” Gronovius reads: “Thus he alone was privy to my affections, himself my companion in errors”; and he maintains that in these words Octavius confesses concerning himself that they scattered their sexual favors indiscriminately, when he acknowledges that his youth was spent according to their fashion. Heumann reads: “Thus he alone shared his affections, a companion in errors, when, after the mist had been dispelled, . . . I emerged.” The reasons why I cannot accept this emendation are, first, that several words must be thrown out, and I do not see how that can be justified; and, second, that an excellent part of the discourse [Col. 0234D] disappears. For Minucius affirms three things about his Octavius: first, that he was his friend; second, that through love for him he likewise adhered to his error; third, that together with him he returned from error into the light of truth. In Heumann’s emendation, however, the second member is absent. Let the reader observe besides the elegant play on words: *conscius* and *socius* (“privy” and “companion”), *amoribus* and *erroribus* (“affections” and “errors”). LINDNER.

Of wisdom and truth. That is, of true wisdom, namely Christian wisdom. Thus Tertullian called women who had attained wisdom “Christian women”; and he said that Socrates was wise “in relation to the truth” when he approached more closely to Christian doctrines. RIGALTIUS.

Went on ahead. So it stands in the manuscript; but the Roman edition, which is the earliest of all, has “went ahead.” Nothing, however, should be altered against the authority of the manuscript. Plautus has this form in *Menaechmi* 5.8.8. Compare, concerning *concurrere*, *decurrere*, and *procurrere* in the same form, Freinsheim’s index to Florus, s.v. *concurrere*. LINDNER.

Still clinging even now. Minucius speaks thus with reference to the time or event being described here. [Col. 0235B] LINDNER.—A sound reading, which various editors had corrupted, some printing *etiamnum* (“still”), others *etiam tunc* (“even then”). Apuleius, *The Golden Ass* 4: “And after dragging him a little way back from the road, they cast him down a very high precipice onto the nearest road, still breathing even now.” Here “even now” is Greek *eti* (ἔτι), “still.” HERALDUS.

**SUMMARY.—From the very opening of the dialogue, Minucius relates how pleasantly he was entertained by the recollection of the things that had happened to him with Octavius while the latter was staying with him at Rome, and especially by the memory of this debate.**

## I

### (I) 1

[Note: [Col. 0232C] To present a more exact collation and truer representation of the one surviving manuscript of the dialogue *Octavius*, numbers have been inserted into the text. Some of them, namely the Roman numerals, indicate the chapters or divisions of the manuscript; the others, that is, the ordinary numerals, indicate its folios. EDD.]

As I reflected and reviewed in my mind the memory of Octavius, my good and most faithful companion, such sweetness and affection for the man took possession of me that I myself seemed somehow [Col. 0233A] to return to the past, rather than to recall through memory things already completed and run their course. Thus, the more the sight of him was withdrawn from my eyes, the more deeply it became entwined with my heart and almost with my innermost senses.

### (II)

And not without reason did this excellent and holy man, when he departed, leave us with an immense longing for him, seeing that he himself had always burned with such great love for me that in both playful and serious matters his will harmonized equally with mine: he would will or reject the same things. You would have believed that one mind had been divided between two persons. 2 Thus, he alone was privy to my affections, himself my companion in errors; and when, after the mist had been dispelled, I emerged from the depth of darkness into the light of wisdom and truth, he did not reject me as a companion but—what is more glorious—went on ahead. And so, as my thought ranged over the whole period of our shared life and intimacy, my mind’s attention came to rest especially upon that discourse of his by which, through a most weighty debate, he restored Caecilius, who even then still clung to superstitious vanities, to true religion.

## CHAPTER II

[Col. 0235A] [Note: [Col. 0235B]

**CHAPTER II.—While their years were still innocent.** This refers to their tender years, which are generally ignorant of crimes; for Fulvius Ursinus dealt badly with the passage when he judged that the word “years” should be expunged. DAVIS.—Prudentius speaks of “innocent little age” in the contest of the martyr Romanus; Tacitus, book III, of “guiltless age.” HERALDUS.—Lactantius, book I, chapter 20: “When,” he says, “without any regard for natural affection, they destroyed tender and innocent souls—the age that is especially dear to parents,” etc. WOWER.—“While their years were still innocent.” Wopkenius thinks that the children must certainly be understood as those who are “still by reason of, or amid, their innocent years,” with the participle “existing” left understood by a conventional ellipsis. Minucius called the years “innocent,” just as Seneca called years “weary,” *Hippolytus* 267, and Quintilian “untrained years,” *Institutes of Oratory* I, chapter n. BURMAN.—Thus, I believe, the received reading [Col. 0235C] stands sufficiently confirmed, and there was no reason for Heumann to rewrite it: “and, what is more lovable, with their children still innocent.” I add that these words can be explained more conveniently as follows: “with the children still innocent through their years,” that is, during or because of their years. Cortius, on Cicero, *Letters* II, Letter 10, §5, certainly teaches that the ablative must sometimes be explained in this manner. Mencken interprets “children innocent in years” as “children whose years are innocent.” LINDNER.

**Speech . . . sweeter.** Meursius thinks that the text should be restored to: “speech, sweeter through the very brokenness of a stumbling tongue,” and Chr. Cellarius indeed printed it thus. But, to say nothing of the cacophony [κακοφωνία], the common reading, confirmed by the Royal manuscript, does not demand a physician’s hand; for it is the not uncommon construction known as apposition, concerning which G. J. Vossius, *On Construction*, chapter 2, may be consulted. See also Caspar Barthius, *Adversaria* XLIII.25. DAVIS.—The celebrated Heumann endeavors to evade the charge of cacophony brought against this emendation by citing for that purpose a passage [Col. 0235D] from Jerome, *Letters*, p. 49: “A chattering and stammering tongue becomes sweeter through its stumbling.” But who does not see that this passage is very different from one in which the ablative case is repeated five times? LINDNER.

**With uncontrollable joy.** I read “unrestrained.” Catullus, *Epigram* 36, and Tacitus, *Histories* IV.44, likewise spoke of “unrestrained” love—that is, love that is immoderate and renders a man no longer master of himself. In Lactantius, book V, chapter 1, scribes likewise wrote “uncontrollable” in place of “unrestrained.” HEUMANN.—I do not understand what reason Heumann has for demanding that “unrestrained” be read in place of “uncontrollable.” The word *impatient* is of the kind used especially of violent emotions, and so can correctly be used of joy. For if the ancients called grief “impatient,” I do not see why it should be thought less appropriate to attribute this word to joy, which possesses the same power to agitate the mind. Thus Justin, speaking of grief, 12.15: “He consoled some who were grieving rather uncontrollably”; and 32.33: “Offended by these events, Philip grieved day by day more uncontrollably over Demetrius’s death”; Aurelius Victor, *On the Caesars*, chapter 6: “Grieving rather uncontrollably because Piso had been preferred to him for adoption”; and in the *Epitome*, chapter 14: “Burning with grief and unable to endure it”; Pliny, book IX, Letter 22: “He grieves as one who does so most uncontrollably”; and Tacitus, *Annals* IV.17: “Then indeed he grieved uncontrollably that young men should be placed on a level with his old age.” MENCKEN.—Even if I were to concede as fully as possible that joy can be called “impatient” just as grief can—which I could scarcely believe—I nevertheless very much doubt whether unexpected, sudden, and present joy is so called. LINDNER.

**For my body.** Heumann writes “of my body.” But the construction of the substantive verb with the dative case is sufficiently worn smooth by usage. See, however, Bunemann on Lactantius V.2.5. LINDNER.

**For drying the humors**, in place of “in order to dry the humors.” For they used sea baths whenever they had contracted weakness because of unhealthy internal humors, with their sinews rendered too relaxed and lax. Suetonius, *Augustus* 82.5. Add Scriverius’s notes on Martial 10.13. GRONOVIUS.—Vitruvius also mentions this treatment, book V, [Col. 0236C] chapter 9. I also refer here to Symmachus, book VIII, Letter 46. Jacob Heinrich Christian Adami interpreted that passage in a special dissertation published at Leipzig in 1734 and reviewed there the following year in the *Acta Academica*, p. 120 ff. HEUMANN.

**At the vintage.** Heumann writes “because of the vintage.” This injury is inflicted upon an innocent little word. No one sufficiently acquainted with the many passages in authors—a large portion of which were collected by Johann Friedrich Gronovius on Seneca, *On Benefits* IV.6—in which *ad* denotes “at that time” or “about that time,” will judge it to have been less appropriately placed here. “Holidays at the vintage” are those held at vintage-time, namely vintage holidays; and, to tell the truth, the expression is more elegant with *ad* than it would be if *ob* were substituted for it. MENCKEN.—Compare also Cortius on Cicero, *Letters* III, Letter 5, note 9. There is another explanation, no less elegant and concise, according to which the holidays are said to relax judicial business “for the enjoyment of the vintage.” LINDNER.

[Col. 0236D] **For at that time.** Minucius sets forth two things that especially recommended this treatment, namely the vintage holidays and the mildness of autumn. The treatment was pleasant because of autumn’s mildness and suitable because of the vintage holidays. LINDNER.

**Autumn was moderating itself.** The most learned Meursius raised a difficulty here to no purpose, as though some words were missing about Caecilius being taken along as their companion on the walk. Accordingly, he found none of the learned men who subsequently edited the text agreeing with him. This was doubtless not because they thought certain circumstances could be omitted as though by a privilege that historians do not shun, but because they believed Caecilius’s companionship could sufficiently be gathered partly from the preceding words, where the author shows that he intends to discuss the discourse of Octavius by which the latter restored Caecilius to true religion through a most weighty debate; and partly from what follows, where Octavius describes Caecilius as clinging to Minucius’s side at home and abroad. The author arranged these two passages so aptly that from them the reader cannot suppose anything other than that Caecilius went out together with them. GRONOVIUS.

[Col. 0237A] **By walking along the shore.** Cellarius, with the distinguished Heumann as his supporter, printed “along the shore” in the dative. Yet the expression “to walk along” with a dative is unknown to ancient writers, nor do I find this verb joined with the dative case anywhere. Consequently, *inambulando* has the force of a gerund, not a participle; while “the shore” is placed in the ablative for “on the shore,” in the same way as in Livy XXIII.21: “Those who had fallen in the battle of Cannae”; Florus III.16: “Annius having been openly killed at the elections”; Curtius VII.7: “Two thousand infantrymen fell in that battle”; and Ovid, *Heroides* I.21:

> In short, whoever had been slaughtered in the Achaean camp;

and many other passages, some of which are cited by the distinguished Drakenborch on Livy IX.32. MENCKEN.

**So that both the breeze.** Heumann deletes “so that,” perhaps because he was offended that the sand’s sinking as it yielded to their footsteps would in this way be made a reason for walking, which he did not approve. LINDNER.

**To the gentle footprint.** “Footprint” here denotes the sole of the foot. Gellius, *Attic Nights* IX.4: “Having the soles of their feet extended backward, not pointing forward like those of other human beings.” Prudentius, *Against Symmachus*, book II, preface, verse 33:

> But he had begun to dip  
> The soles of his feet in the surface of the waves.

Caspar Barthius will supply further examples in *Adversaria* XLIII.25. There, however, he wrongly restores the text to: “would refresh their limbs with exceptional pleasure, and the sand, yielding to the gentle footstep, would sink down,” as though sand yielding beneath the feet afforded no pleasure at all to the walker. DAVIS.—“The sand yielding to the gentle footprint.” This passage has been variously assailed by interpreters. Ouzel, and with him Scheffer and Davis, understand “footprint” to mean the sole of the foot. Scheffer, however, could not stomach the question of how the soles, especially of people who do not wear shoes very often, could be called gentle. Moved by this difficulty, he threw out “gentle” altogether. Gruner, who with Wopken and Gronovius understands it metonymically of the impression made by the footprint or foot, resolves the matter thus: “The yielding sand was gentle to the footprint, because by yielding it made the place being trodden by the foot softer.” In this sense the ablative describes a quality, as in “a soft journey” in Quintilian, *Institutes of Oratory* 4.2, p. 350; “a soft path”; and Ovid, *Fasti* 3.15, “a soft and yielding vineyard.” Neither Davis nor Le Clerc, in *Bibliothèque choisie*, vol. XXIV, p. 124, ought to have agreed with Wower, who restored “soft,” which he claimed to have found in the Roman edition. I applaud Heumann, who says that Minucius meant that the sand yielded even when only lightly impressed by the walkers. One may say in the same way: “The pillow, yielding to a soft neck, sinks down”; German, *Es liegt sich weich*, “It is soft to lie upon.” LINDNER.

**The superstitious populace is accustomed.** I would prefer to read “is superstitiously accustomed.” HEUMANN.—Perhaps because he does not want the masculine gender joined to the word *vulgus* (“populace”). Yet Horace has this word in the same gender, *Satires* II.3.62:

> I shall show him that the whole populace  
> Is insane with an error like his.

So Mencken, who also prefers the comparative “more superstitious.” LINDNER.

**Bringing his hand to his mouth . . . pressed.** The Roman edition has “impressed.” HERALDUS.—Pliny, book XXVIII, chapter 11: “In worship we bring our right hand to our lips for a kiss.” Jerome, vol. VI, on Hosea 13, fol. 72: “Those who worship are accustomed to kiss their own hand, which Job denies that he did, saying: ‘If I kissed my hand while placing it against my mouth, this too would be accounted to me as the greatest iniquity.’” On the gesture, posture, movement, and bending of the body among worshippers, see Barnabé Brisson, *On Formulas* I, p. 35, and Pierre Pithou, *Adversaria* I.3. ELMENHORST.—The material Elmenhorst has just advised the reader to compare and consult may be seen most learnedly and concisely collected in the note J. G. H. Greppo appended to the Lyon edition and translation of the *Octavius*, pp. 193–199. EDD.]

**SUMMARY.—Octavius’s arrival at Rome during the judicial holidays was most welcome to Minucius. They both decided to go to the sea baths at Ostia, taking Minucius’s companion Caecilius with them. As they proceeded together toward the sea, Caecilius caught sight of an image of Serapis, brought his hand to his mouth, and worshipped it.**

For he had hastened to Rome on account of business and to see me, leaving behind his home, wife, and children—and what is especially lovable in children, while their years were still innocent and they were still attempting half-formed words, their speech made sweeter by the very brokenness of a stumbling tongue. On his arrival [Col. 0236A] I cannot express in words with what great and uncontrollable joy I exulted, especially since the unexpected presence of my dearest friend heightened my delight. And so, after one or two days, when frequent and constant association had already satisfied the eagerness born of longing, and we had learned by exchanging accounts all that our mutual absence had kept us from knowing about one another, we decided to make for Ostia, a most delightful city, because bathing in the sea offered a pleasant and suitable treatment for drying the humors of my body. Besides, at the vintage season the holidays had relaxed the demands of judicial business. For at that time, after the heat of summer, autumn was moderating itself toward mildness. And so, when [Col. 0237A] at dawn we proceeded toward the sea, walking along the shore, so that both the gently breathing breeze might refresh our limbs and, to our exceptional pleasure, the sand might sink as it yielded beneath a light footfall, Caecilius, upon noticing an image of Serapis, brought his hand to his mouth in the manner customary among the superstitious populace and pressed a kiss upon it with his lips.

## CHAPTER III

[Note: [Col. 0238B]

**CHAPTER III.—It is not the act of a good man, brother Marcus.** Octavius calls Marcus Minucius “brother,” as one Christian calls another Christian. But in chapter V Caecilius, while still a pagan, will likewise address the same Minucius thus: “although, brother Marcus, you . . .” This form of address was therefore one both of affection and of religion: certainly of both in Octavius, and of affection in Caecilius. RIGALTIUS.

**In the blindness of common ignorance.** That is, to permit him to remain in the religion [Col. 0238C] of the pagans. He calls the ignorance “common” because at that time those who were somewhat more discerning gradually perceived the deceptions of pagan religion and followed Christ, so that only the populace remained in pagan superstition. LINDNER.

**Fashioned into images.** Savaro says much about the verb *effigiare* (“to fashion into an image”) on Sidonius Apollinaris VI, Letter 12; to his material the following passages may be added. Papias: “*Effigiare*: to give form.” Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* II, p. 11: “Fashioned externally with marvelous images.” Martianus Capella, *The Marriage of Philology* VI, p. 130: “For it is recorded as being able to fashion all things into images.” Page 228: “Fashioned into marvelous forms.” Augustine, *On Heresies*, chapter 86: “Tertullian says that the soul is a body fashioned into a form . . . and that God is corporeal, though not fashioned into a form.” ELMENHORST. Differently in ed. BB, vol. VIII, p. 25.

**Anointed and crowned.** Both customs have been sufficiently illustrated by Jean Le Clerc on Genesis 28:18 and Carlo Pasquali, *On Crowns* IV.4. DAVIS.—It is entirely unknown how the Lyon edition came to assert that this reading, “anointed and crowned,” arose from a sound correction by very learned men and that the Royal manuscript reads nothing other than “arranged with ointments.” We testify that we have seen with our own eyes: “anointed and crowned.” EDD.

**The intervening distance from the city.** This is not the distance from Ostia to Rome, as Wopkenius supposed, since the interval between Rome and the shore—sixteen stadia—would be enough to weary even runners, much less allow bookish men out for a stroll either to be able or willing to complete such a distance. Nor is it an intervening distance through the city of Ostia, which on the side facing the sea was free of walls, a view that commended itself to Barthius, *Adversaria* XLIII.25, since upon completing this distance they would have reached not the open shore but fields and farmland. Rather, it is the distance lying between the city of Ostia and the place sought by the walkers, that is, the sea. This was rightly observed by a certain learned man in *Miscellaneous Critical Observations*, vol. X, part I, p. 13. Romans, after all, resorted to Ostia as our people resort to baths, mineral springs, or even vineyards. After staying there for some time, they took daily pleasure [Col. 0239B] in proceeding to the sea by walking along the shore. The sea is three stadia from the city of Ostia, a distance that could easily be completed within the space of an hour. Given these observations, we must agree neither with Davis, who deletes the word “city,” nor with Heumann, who restores “journey,” nor with Mencken, who rewrites it as “the distance of the road and journey.” LINDNER.

**Stretched.** So the manuscript; the Roman edition reads “beat,” as does Wower. Here “stretched” means “extended,” according to all the interpreters. For Heumann, however, it is not transitive but intransitive: overflowing water is said to “stretch” across the ground it has occupied, like an army on a field. The accusative “sands” is governed by the participle “washing over.” LINDNER.

**And, as the sea is always, etc.** I understand and supplement this as follows: “As the sea is always restless even when the winds have fallen (so it was then also, and), although it did not go out to land in white and foaming waves, nevertheless it went out in rippling and swelling waves”—the verb being supplied by zeugma. Zeugma is familiar from Sallust, *Jugurtha* 3, note 2; the other thing I supplied, namely the consequent clause in a comparison after its antecedent, is not equally familiar. Yet Cicero writes in book VII, Letter 28.6: “The republic was dearer to me than it is now”—with “it is” understood; Sidonius, II, Letter 8: “When brought to her perpetual resting place, she seemed more like one sleeping”—with “than one dead” understood. There was therefore no reason for the distinguished Heumann to emend the passage thus: “And, as the sea is always restless even when the winds have fallen, although it does not surge toward the land in white and foaming waves, it nevertheless licks the shore with rippling and twisting ones. We were exceedingly delighted when,” etc. LINDNER.

**Rippling and swelling.** So it is written in the Royal manuscript, not “twisting.” Note Jerome to Heliodorus: “Although the expanse of sea may smile, spread out like a pond, although scarcely the slightest breath may ripple the surface of the recumbent element.” And in the *Epitaph of Paula*: “Thus far we have sailed with favorable winds, and the gliding keel has furrowed the rippling surface of the sea.” HERALDUS.—The first man who reported from the Royal copy that it read “twisting” had inspected it with insufficiently attentive eyes. For in that book there had first been written *srispis tororisisque*, and the corrector intended it to be read *crispis torosisque*, “rippling and swelling.” Physicians have spoken of the “swellings” of veins, and poets of the “swellings” of manes. RIGALTIUS.

**Which in turn it would now draw back in.** Following the distinguished Gruner, I punctuate the passage in this manner. The sea is said to draw its waves back in by Ovid, *Heroides* XII.125:

> And vomits forth as many waves as it draws back in.

There Burman reads, “The Sabaean edition has ‘would dash against.’” Wower wishes “would dash against” to be read in the manuscript; in this, however, the most learned man is mistaken, for he did not inspect the manuscript himself but rashly trusted another man’s testimony, whereas Heraldus and Rigaltius defend the received reading. Gronovius prefers “would play against with its wave.” Davis reads “would dash the waves against,” in order to straighten out the syntax [σύνταξις]. [Col. 0240B] Barthius, *Adversaria* XLIII.25: “by its impact, and changes itself into itself.” LINDNER.

**These stories.** This is the full form of expression. Cortius supplies several examples on Cicero, book IX, Letter 25, section 13, note 2, and still more could be collected from Caesar. LINDNER.

**About the voyage**, namely his own. In familiar conversations, the time, place, persons, business, and finally anything set before the eyes supply occasions for discussion. LINDNER.

**Having traversed the path.** Heumann removes “having traversed,” on the ground that it was repeated from chapter 2; he does so without necessity. LINDNER.

**Raised above the earthy corruption.** By “corruption” he means the muddy dampness by which the boats would rot if left standing for a long time. RIGALTIUS.

**We see.** Berger, citing Longinus, proves that the present is used with force in narratives in place of the imperfect: *On the Natural Beauty of Oratory*, p. 203. LINDNER.

**That shard.** This is the full form of expression. See V, note. LINDNER.

**Or would swim out.** Unless I am mistaken, a corruption lies hidden in these words of Minucius, though perceptive scholars have not noticed it. I believe we should read “and would not swim out.” Sometimes the shard skimmed the surface of the sea without leaping, but at other times it was carried into the air by repeated bounds. Hence the author: “so that the missile would either skim the sea’s surface and not swim out, or, grazing the crests of the waves, would dart up and emerge.” Unless this emendation is accepted, Felix must be judged tautologous [ταυτολόγος]. Eustathius describes the former manner on *Iliad* Σ, p. 1219; Pollux the latter, *Onomasticon* IX §119. DAVIS.—“Would skim, or would swim out.” “Or” is altogether foreign to this passage and should therefore be deleted. For “would skim” and “would swim out” are synonymous expressions, just as are the following verbs, “would dart up” and “would emerge.” The former concern the shard’s course, the latter its leap. Wopken writes “would swim upon,” with Heumann’s approval. Davis emends to “would skim and not swim out.” LINDNER.

[Col. 0240D] **Would dart up, would emerge.** Minucius likes to join several words of the same meaning; hence errors [σφάλματα] have crept in, which we shall observe later. We therefore do not venture to condemn “would emerge,” as the man of most refined intellect, Johann Wower, did. In place of “would rebound,” which follows later, we have printed “would leap up,” following the Royal manuscript and the Roman edition. HERALDUS.—“Would dart up, would emerge.” The latter verb was thrown out by Wower, but is retained with better reason by Heraldus, who at the same time lays down this rule for editors of Minucius: “Minucius likes to join several words of the same meaning”—and that without conjunctions [ἀσυνδέτως]. LINDNER.

**Would leap up.** So it stands in the manuscript and the Roman edition; certain editors should not have printed “would rebound” in its place. For although that verb is not unsuitable here—Ovid too said in *Metamorphoses* XII.480, “Hail rebounds from the roof’s peak”—there is nevertheless no reason to reject “would leap up,” which is also more similar to the preceding “would dart up.” HEUMANN.]

**SUMMARY.—Indignant at the superstitious man’s act, Octavius sharply reproaches Minucius, declaring that the disgrace of this impious deed rebounds no less upon Minucius himself, as Caecilius’s host, than upon Caecilius.**

Then Octavius said: “It is not the act of a good man, brother Marcus, to abandon a man who clings to your side at home and abroad in such blindness of common ignorance that, in such broad daylight, you permit him to stumble against stones—fashioned into images, to be sure, and anointed and crowned—although you know that the disgrace of this error rebounds no less upon you than upon the man himself.”

While he was saying this, we traversed the intervening distance from the city and were already upon the open shore. There a gentle wave, washing over the outermost sands as though spreading them beneath a promenade, stretched across them. And, as the sea is always restless even when the winds have fallen, although it did not come out upon the land in white and foaming waves, it nevertheless did so in rippling and swelling ones. There we were exceedingly delighted by the wandering waves as we dipped the soles of our feet at the very edge of the sea: now, as the sea washed in, it played against our feet; now, flowing back and withdrawing our footprints, it reabsorbed its waves into itself. And so we proceeded gradually and quietly, tracing the gently curving shoreline and beguiling our journey with conversation. The conversation consisted of Octavius’s account of his voyage. But when we had spent a sufficiently substantial stretch of our walk in conversation, we turned our steps and once again retraced the same path. And when we reached the place where small boats, drawn ashore and resting upon timbers placed beneath them, lay raised above the earthy corruption, we saw boys exultantly competing with one another in a game of throwing shards into the sea. 4

This is the game: one picks up from the shore a rounded shard smoothed by the tossing of the waves; grasping that shard flat between the fingers, one bends forward as low as possible and spins it over the waves, so that the missile either skims the sea’s surface and swims out while gliding with a gentle impulse, or, grazing the crests of the waves, darts up and emerges as it is lifted by repeated leaps. The boy whose shard both sped farther and leapt up more frequently claimed the victory.

## CHAPTER IV

[Note: [Col. 0240D]

**CHAPTER IV.—To pay attention to nothing.** Whether you explain these infinitives as an interchange of moods or by the ellipsis [Col. 0241B] of the verb “began,” you will find that they nearly always possess a certain disjointed quality and are used in states of sadness, confusion, or alarm. Caesar, *Gallic War* 1.32: “The Sequani answered nothing but remained silent in the same sadness.” The same author, chapter 16: “The Aedui kept putting him off from day to day, saying that the grain was being collected, conveyed, and was close at hand.” The principle is the same as with asyndeton. LINDNER.

**Silent, anxious, withdrawn.** He describes a man tormented by grief and enormous cares, such as Homer’s Bellerophon, *Iliad* Ζ.201. RIGALTIUS.

**Withdrawn, his expression confessed that he was grieving over something or other.** The Royal manuscript and the editions present the wholly inappropriate reading: “withdrawn through grief, his expression confessed something or other.” For the face is an indicator of the emotions, and for that reason Cicero, *Against Piso*, called it “a kind of silent speech of the mind.” See also *On Duties* I.29. DAVIS.—**To be grieving.** The manuscript has “through grief,” from which Heumann produces “of grief.” It is elegant, certainly; but there is no less elegance in the received reading. “To grieve over something or other” means “to grieve because of something.” LINDNER.

**That characteristic liveliness of yours.** Lest anyone be offended by the conjunction of the words “that . . . of yours” and “that,” I shall show that this accords with the author’s usage. Thus our author, 21.5: “the rites themselves and the mysteries themselves”; 35.4: “the parent of all and lord of all”; 9.8: “people of every sex and of every age”; 10.2: “no temples, no known images”; 10.5: “to investigate the conduct of all, the actions of all”; 10.6: “he stands beside every deed, wanders through every place”; 7.4: “the established witness is, and a witness also”; 5.5: “sufficiently blessed and sufficiently wise”; 28.8: “heads of oxen and heads of rams”; 32.8: “he is present in darkness, present in our thoughts”; 23.8: “by what tortures and by what machines”; 24.4: “how many things are laughable, and many things too are pitiable.” Once this is settled, it remains to warn the reader that the interrogative “why?” must be repeated before the verb “I ask,” by zeugma. If anyone wishes to receive it into the text, he may do so with my leave. It is certainly clear that Heumann’s emendation is superfluous: [Col. 0241D] “Why, Caecilius, do I not recognize that characteristic liveliness of yours, and that cheerfulness of your eyes even in serious matters?” Nor should Cellarius be heard, who changes “that?” into “and why?” LINDNER.

**Rebuked.** Supply “you.” The pronoun is similarly omitted in Cicero VII, Letter 28.5: “For I understood from your conversation that you said you missed my spirit in my books.” LINDNER.

**A man of that very sect.** This is opposed to a fictitious and, as it were, shadowy adversary who assumes an opponent’s role merely for the sake of practice, as happens when companions debate among themselves. By what right, then, Heumann rewrites the passage, “So that I may debate with him today concerning his own sect,” others may judge. Wopken likewise misunderstood it when he thought of a sect shared with Octavius. Cellarius is plainly better, understanding the pagan sect to which Caecilius was still devoted. LINDNER.

**Than to join issue in the manner of wise men.** Thus we readily restore Minucius’s very own wording, corrupted in the copy by one transposed letter: “It is easier to debate among companions than to join issue in the manner of wise men. Among those structures for the protection of the baths,” etc. All editions until now had destroyed the sense through faulty punctuation. And those interpreters who assert that the manuscript reads otherwise than we have reported here are mistaken. Caecilius says that he will make Octavius understand that it is easier to debate and quarrel among companions with vulgar taunts and insults than to join issue in the manner customary among wise men. He then begins another sentence: “Among those structures for the protection of the baths,” etc. RIGALTIUS.—“To join issue in the manner of wise men,” that is, to debate precisely, as befits philosophers such as we profess ourselves to be. “To join,” with “arguments” understood, means “to debate”; “manner,” moreover, is used for “fashion.” Thus Lactantius 7.7.9: “Democritus therefore erred when he thought that human beings had been poured forth from the earth in the manner of little worms.” See also *On the Anger of God* 20.10. Some editions have: [Col. 0242C] “To creep upon the ground in the manner of cattle.” In the manuscript, according to the testimony of Heraldus and Rigaltius, the text reads: “than to join wisdom in the manner among those,” etc. From this Rigaltius was the first to produce: “to join issue in the manner of wise men. Among those,” etc. In this emendation I miss only one thing: the next clause begins too abruptly. Hence I suspect that the preceding clause ended with “manner” and the following one began with “just now,” examples of which construction I gave at note 3 of this chapter. The reason why the word was omitted in one clause or the other is readily apparent. No one, I believe, will agree with Heumann when he rewrites the passage: “If you please, let me debate with him; let me debate more intently among those structures for the protection of the baths. He will surely now understand that it is easier to discourse among companions”—I write “discourse” in place of “join”—“than to debate in the manner of wise men.” Heraldus reads: “than to join a wise hand.” LINDNER.
