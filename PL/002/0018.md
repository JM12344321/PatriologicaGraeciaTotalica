Nor should you suppose that only the works of the world were made through the Son, but also those things that God has done since then. For the Father, who loves the Son, has delivered all things into his hand [Note: [Col. 0174D] “Bosom,” Rhen.]; assuredly he has loved him from the beginning and delivered all things to him from the beginning. From that beginning the Word was with God, and the Word was God (John 1:1); to him all power in heaven and on earth was given by the Father (Matt. 28:8). The Father judges no one [Col. 0174C], but has delivered all judgment to the Son (John 5:22)—from the beginning, nevertheless. For when Scripture says “all power,” “all judgment,” “all things were made through him,” and “all things were delivered into his hand,” it permits no exception of time, because “all things” would not be all unless they belonged to all time.

Accordingly, it is the Son who [Note: [Col. 0174D] Seml. adds “also.”] judged from the beginning: striking down the most arrogant tower, scattering [Note: [Col. 0174D] “Destroying,” Seml.] the languages, punishing the whole world with the violence of the waters, and raining fire and sulfur [Note: [Col. 0174D] “Sulfur,” Jun.] upon Sodom and Gomorrah—the Lord [Note: [Col. 0174D] “God from God,” Seml.] from the Lord (Gen. 19:24). For it was he who also always descended to converse with human beings, from Adam down to the patriarchs and prophets, in vision, in dream [Note: [Col. 0174D] “Sleep,” Seml.], in a mirror [Col. 0175A], and in an enigma, always preparing from the beginning the course that he was to follow through to the end [Note: [Col. 0175C] “The infinite things that he was to pursue,” Seml.].

Thus he was always learning [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Thus he was always learning.” So too in the book *On the Flesh of Christ*: “And he was learning,” he says, “to deliver and to judge the human race in the guise of flesh not yet born.” Again, in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*: “They learn the flesh’s immunity.” And presently here: “But he was learning.” In the little book *To the Martyrs*: “Even in peace,” he says, “through toil and hardships they are already learning to endure war.” RHEN.], and no other God could have associated with human beings on earth than the Word who was to become flesh. He was learning, moreover, in order to lay down for us a path to faith, so that we might more readily believe that the Son of God had descended into the world if [Note: [Col. 0175D] “If” absent from Seml.] we knew that something of this kind had also happened formerly. For just as these things were written for our sake, so too were they done for our sake, upon whom the ends of the ages have come down.

Thus even then he was becoming acquainted with human affections, since he was also to assume the very substances of the human being, flesh and soul: asking Adam, as though he did not know, “Where are you, Adam?”; regretting that he had made the human being, as though he had not foreknown it; testing Abraham, as though ignorant of what is in a human being; taking offense at and being reconciled [Col. 0175B] to the same persons. The heretics seize upon any such things as though they were unworthy of God, for the destruction of the Creator, not knowing that these things were appropriate to the Son, who was also to undergo human sufferings—thirst, hunger, tears, birth itself, and death itself. For this reason he was made a little [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Something” absent from Seml.] lower by the Father than the angels (Ps. 8:6).

Yet the heretics, to be sure, will not assign to the Son of God as appropriate even those things that you attribute to the Father himself [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Yourself,” Seml.], as though he had diminished himself for our sake, although Scripture says that one was made lower by another, not that he was made lower by himself. What if, moreover, the one who was crowned with glory and honor [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Glory and honor,” Seml.] was one, and the one who crowned him another—assuredly the Father crowning the Son?

Besides, how could that invisible almighty God, whom no human being has seen or can see; he who dwells in inaccessible light; he who does not dwell [Col. 0175C] in things made by hands; before whose sight the earth trembles and the mountains melt like wax; who grasps the whole world in his hand as though it were a nest; whose throne is heaven and whose footstool is earth; in whom every place exists, while he himself is not in a place; who is the outermost boundary of the universe—how could that Most High have walked in paradise toward evening, seeking Adam; have shut the ark after Noah entered; have refreshed himself beneath the oak beside Abraham; have called Moses from the burning bush; and have appeared as the fourth figure in the Babylonian king’s furnace—although he was called the Son of Man—unless these things had taken place in an image [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Was called, and in an image,” Seml.], a mirror, and an enigma? Of course, even these things would not have been [Col. 0176A] believed [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Had been,” Jun.] of the Son of God if they had not been written; perhaps they ought not to be believed of the Father even though written—of him whom these people bring down into Mary’s womb, set before Pilate’s tribunal, and enclose in Joseph’s tomb [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Tombs,” Seml.].

Their error is therefore apparent from this. For, because they are ignorant that from the beginning the whole course of the divine dispensation has run through the Son, they believe that it was the Father himself who was seen, who met with human beings, who acted, who suffered thirst and hunger—contrary to the prophet’s declaration: “The eternal God will not thirst or hunger at all” (how much less will he die or be buried!)—and thus that the one God, that is, the Father, has always done those things which were done through the Son.

## CHAPTER XVII

They found it easier to suppose that the Father acted in the Son’s name than that the Son acted in the Father’s, although the Lord himself says [Col. 0176B], “I have come in my Father’s name” (John 5:43). Likewise, he says to the Father himself, “I have manifested your name to human beings” (John 17:6). [Note: [Col. 0175D] “Scripture also speaking in agreement.” *Condicere* is used in a novel sense for “to speak similarly” or “to assent.” RHEN.] Scripture also speaks in agreement: “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord” (Matt. 21:6)—assuredly the Son in the Father’s name.

But the Father’s names [Note: [Col. 0175D] “To the name; and the Father’s name,” Seml.] are also “Almighty God,” “Most High” [Note: [Col. 0176C] “Most High” absent from Seml.], “Lord of Hosts,” “King of Israel,” and “HE WHO IS.” Insofar as the Scriptures teach it, we say that these names were also applicable to the Son, that the Son came under these names, that he always acted under these names, and that under these names he manifested them in himself to human beings. “All things,” he says, “that belong to the Father are mine.” Why not the names also?

When, therefore, you read “Almighty God,” “Most High,” “God of Hosts,” “King of Israel,” and [Note: [Col. 0176D] “HE WHO IS.” The Lord God says, ἐγὼ εἰμὶ ὁ ὤν, “I am HE WHO IS”; and elsewhere: “HE WHO IS has sent me,” etc. Immediately afterward, as an interpretation of this expression, there is appended “he who is,” because many are called sons and are not. Whether that explanation is correct I would not venture to say. For who would believe that the tetragrammaton name Jehovah is signified there, in which, as the rabbis and Kabbalists relate, so many mysteries lie hidden? LE PR.] “HE WHO IS,” see whether the Son also is not indicated by them: “Almighty God” in his own right, because he is the Word of Almighty God and because he received [Col. 0176C] power over all things; “Most High,” because he was exalted at God’s right hand, as Peter proclaims in Acts (2:22); “Lord of Hosts,” because all things have been subjected to him by the Father; “King of Israel,” because the lot of that nation properly fell to him [Note: [Col. 0176D] “Happened,” Jun.; “properly went forth,” Latin.]; and likewise “HE WHO IS,” because many are called sons [Note: [Col. 0176D] Ciaccon. conjectures “gods.”] and are not.

But if they also wish the name “Christ” to belong to the Father, they will hear the answer [Note: [Col. 0176D] “They hear,” Seml.] in its proper place. Meanwhile, let the response already advanced here stand against what they also bring forward from John’s Apocalypse: “I [Note: [Col. 0176D] “Therefore,” Seml.] am the Lord who is [Note: [Col. 0176D] Seml. adds “and who was”; Jun.: “and who was and who is coming.”], and who was, and is coming, the Almighty”; and against any other place where they suppose that the designation “Almighty God” is not also applicable to the Son—as though he who is to come were not [Note: [Col. 0176D] “Is not” absent from Seml.] [Col. 0177A] almighty, when the Son of the Almighty is just as almighty [Note: [Col. 0177D] Seml. adds “the Son of God.”] as the Son of God is God.

## CHAPTER XVIII

Yet Scripture confounds them whenever it establishes that God is one, so that they do not readily perceive this sharing of the Father’s names in the Son. It is as though that same Scripture had not also set forth two as God and Lord, as we demonstrated above. “Therefore,” they say, “because we find two and yet one, both are one and the same, Son and Father.”

Scripture, however, is not in danger, so that you must come to its rescue with your argument lest it appear self-contradictory. It has a rational account both when it establishes one God and when it reveals two, the Father and the Son; and it is sufficient unto itself. It is agreed that the Son is named by it. For, with the Son preserved, it could rightly have defined as one the God whose Son he is. The one who has a Son does not thereby cease to be the one God himself [Col. 0177B], under his own name, whenever he is named without the Son. He is named without the Son when he is defined principally as the first person, who had to be set forth before the name of the Son, because the Father is first made known and after the Father the Son is named.

Thus there is one God, the Father, and apart from him [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Apart from him there is another,” Seml.] there is no other. When he himself makes this declaration [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Inserts,” Seml.], he does not deny the Son but another god; for the Son is not another God from the Father [Note: [Col. 0177D] Jun. adds “God.”]. Finally, inspect the context following declarations of this sort, and you will find that their definition almost always concerns the makers and worshipers of idols: the unity of divinity is to expel the multitude of false gods, while nevertheless possessing the Son, who, insofar as he is indivisible and inseparable from the Father, must be reckoned as being in the Father, even though he is not named.

Indeed, if he had named him, he would have separated him, saying: “There is no other besides me, except [Col. 0177C] my Son.” For he would have made even the Son “another,” by excepting him from the others. Suppose the sun were to say, “I am the sun, and there is no other besides me except my ray.” Would you not have censured [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Would he not have censured,” Seml.] the absurdity [Note: [Col. 0177D] Others read “unity.”], since the ray too is reckoned as being in the sun?

Accordingly, when he says that there is no other God besides [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Through,” Seml.] himself, he says this on account of the idolatry both of the nations and of Israel, and also on account of the heretics, who fashion idols with words just as the nations fashion them with their hands—that is, another God and another Christ. Thus, even when the Father declared himself to be one, he was acting on the Son’s behalf, lest Christ be believed to have come from another God rather than from him who had foretold: “I am God, and apart from me there is no other”; who shows himself to be one, but together with the Son, with whom he alone stretched out heaven.

## CHAPTER XIX

[Col. 0177D] Indeed [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Who,” Seml.], they will seize upon this declaration of his also as an argument for singularity [Col. 0178A]: “I alone,” he says, “stretched out heaven.” With respect to all other powers, he was alone, preparing beforehand against the conjectures of the heretics who maintain that the world was constructed by angels [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Who maintain that the world was constructed by angels,” etc. He is referring to the Valentinians and others like them, as well as the followers of Proclus and the Seleucians. RHEN.] and various powers. They make even the Creator himself either an angel, or else one who, though ignorant, was put forward for external tasks [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Adilla,” Jun.], such as the works of the world.

Or, if he stretched out heaven alone in that sense, how do these heretics perversely presume that he admits no companion, although that Wisdom says: “When he was preparing the heavens [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Heaven,” Seml.], I was there with him [Note: [Col. 0178D] “With him” absent from Seml.]”? And if the Apostle said, “Who has known the mind of the Lord, and who was present at his counsel?” (1 Cor. 2:15), [Note: [Col. 0177D] “Assuredly apart from Wisdom.” The meaning is: when Paul says, “Who was present at his counsel?” he means, “Who apart from Wisdom was present at his counsel—the Wisdom that was there with him?” RHEN.] he assuredly means, “apart from Wisdom” [Note: [Col. 0178D] “Was,” Seml.], who was there with him.

Nevertheless, in him and together with him God was joining the universe together, while the Son was not ignorant of what God was doing. But by “apart from Wisdom” he means “apart from the Son,” who is Christ, the Wisdom and Power of God according to the Apostle, the only one [Col. 0178B] who knows the Father’s mind: “For who knows the things that are in God except the spirit that is in him?”—not one who is outside him [Note: [Col. 0178D] “Him. Therefore he was,” Seml.]. There was therefore one with him who did not allow God to be alone, except in relation to all others.

But then the Gospel too must be rejected, because it says that all things were made by God through the Word, and that without him nothing was made. For, unless I am mistaken, it is also written elsewhere: “By his word the heavens were established, and by the spirit of his mouth [Note: [Col. 0178D] “Of his mouth” absent from Seml.] all their powers.” And the Word, Power, and Wisdom will himself be the Son of God. Thus, if all things were made through the Son, then, in stretching out heaven through the Son, he did not stretch it out alone, except in the sense in which he was alone in relation to all others.

Indeed, immediately afterward he speaks of the Son: “Who else casts down the signs of ventriloquists and divinations from the heart, turns the wise backward, and makes their counsel foolish? Establishing the words of his Son”—namely, by declaring, “This is [Col. 0178C] my beloved Son; listen to him.” Thus, by immediately introducing the Son, he is himself the interpreter of how he stretched out heaven alone: namely, alone together with the Son, just as he is one together with the Son.

Accordingly, the words “I alone stretched out heaven” will also belong to the Son, because the heavens were established by the Word. Because heaven was prepared with Wisdom—that is [Note: [Col. 0178D] “Wisdom in the Word,” Seml.] with the Word—assisting, and all things were made through the Word, it is also appropriate to say that the Son [Note: [Col. 0178D] “To the Son,” Wouw.] alone stretched out heaven, because he alone ministered to the Father’s work.

He will likewise be the one who says: “I am the first, and I am also among the things to come” [Note: [Col. 0178D] “Among the things about to come,” Seml.; “among” absent from Fran.]. The Word, namely, is the first of all things: “In the beginning was the Word”; in which beginning he was brought forth by the Father. The Father, however, having no beginning, since he was brought forth by no one and is unborn, cannot be regarded as first. He who alone always existed could have no position in an order.

Thus, if they thought that the same one must be believed to be both Father and Son in order to vindicate one God, his unity remains intact [Note: [Col. 0178D] “Intact,” masculine, Seml.] [Col. 0179A], because, although he is one, he has [Note: [Col. 0179D] “May have,” Seml.] a Son, who likewise is himself embraced by the same Scriptures. If they do not wish the Son to be reckoned second after the Father, lest being second cause two gods to be spoken of, we have shown that Scripture also mentions two as gods and two as lords. Yet, lest they be scandalized [Note: [Col. 0179D] Others read “might be scandalized.”] by this, we have explained the principle according to which they are not called two Gods or two Lords [Note: [Col. 0179D] “In which Lords,” manuscript of Wouw.], but according to which the Father and the Son are two. This is not by separation of substance but by dispensation, since we declare that the Son is indivisible and inseparable from the Father; another not in status but in degree. And although he is called God when he is named by himself, he does not thereby make two gods, but one, by the very fact that he is entitled to be called God from the unity of the Father.

## CHAPTER XX

[Col. 0179B] But work must still be devoted to refuting their arguments, should they extract anything from the Scriptures in support of their own opinion while refusing to consider the rest, which likewise preserves the rule—and indeed preserves the unity of the divinity and the import of the monarchy.

For just as in the Old Scriptures they retain nothing except, “I am God, and there is no other besides me” (Ps. 45:5), so in the Gospel they defend the Lord’s answer to Philip: “I and the Father are one” (John 14); and, “He who has seen me sees [Note: [Col. 0179D] “Has seen,” Fran., Paris, Rig.] the Father also”; and, “I am in the Father, and the Father is in me.” They wish the entire body of both Testaments to yield [Note: [Col. 0179D] “Believe,” Seml.] to these three passages, although the fewer ought to be understood in accordance with the greater number. But this is characteristic of all heretics. For because the passages that can be found in the thicket are few, they defend the few against the many [Col. 0179C] and adopt the later against the earlier. Yet the rule established for every matter always [Note: [Col. 0179D] “Always” absent from Seml.] from the beginning prescribes from the earlier and more numerous passages for the later, and assuredly also for the fewer [Note: [Col. 0179D] “In the earlier passages, and prescribes for the later, and assuredly also in the fewer,” Rhen.].

## CHAPTER XXI

Consider, therefore [Note: [Col. 0179D] “How many things prescribe for you.” *Quanta* is used for *quam multa* (“how many”), as more than once previously above.], how many things the Gospel itself prescribes for you [Note: [Col. 0179D] “Prescribe,” plural, Seml.] before Philip’s inquiry and before your entire argument.

First of all, the very opening of John the Evangelist immediately demonstrates that he who was to become flesh had existed previously: “In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1), “and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him nothing was made” [Col. 0180A]. For if these words may be received in no other way than as they were written, there is unquestionably shown to be one who existed from the beginning and another with whom he existed: the Word to be one, and God [Note: [Col. 0179D] “Of God, another Lord,” Seml.] another—although the Word also is God, but insofar as he is God’s Son, not insofar as he is the Father; one through whom all things were made, and another from whom all things came.

How we use the term “another,” however, we have already explained often. We necessarily say “another” so that we do not say “the same”; yet “another” not as though separated, but another by dispensation [Note: [Col. 0179D] “It is necessary that he not be the same, but not as though separated [Col. 0180D], another by dispensation,” Seml.; “It is necessary that he not be the same, but not as though,” Jun.], not by division.

It is this one, therefore, who became flesh, not the one whose Word he was. It was his glory that was seen [Note: [Col. 0179D] “As of an only-begotten from a father,” μονογενοῦς ἀπὸ πατρὸς. For by the expression “only-begotten” he rendered what appears in John 1. LE PR.] as the glory of an only-begotten from the Father, not as the Father’s own glory. This one alone [Note: [Col. 0180D] “Of one,” Seml.] expounded the Father’s bosom, not the Father his own bosom. For the statement precedes: “No one has ever seen God.”

This same one is also designated by John as the Lamb of God, not the one whose beloved he is. Certainly he is always the Son of God [Col. 0180B], but not the very one whose Son he is. Nathanael immediately perceived this concerning him, as Peter did elsewhere: “You are the Son of God” (John 1:50; Matt. 16:16). He himself confirms that they perceived this correctly: answering Nathanael [Note: [Col. 0180D] “Nathanael,” Rhen.], “Because I said [Note: [Col. 0180D] “He said,” Rhen.] to you, ‘I saw you beneath the fig tree,’ you therefore believe” (John 2:50); and affirming Peter to be blessed, since neither flesh nor blood had revealed it to him—that is, that he had perceived the Father also—but the Father who is in heaven (Matt. 16:17).

By this declaration he established the distinction between both persons: the Son on earth, whom Peter had recognized as God’s Son, and the Father in heaven, who had revealed to Peter that the Christ whom Peter recognized was God’s Son. When he entered the temple [Note: [Col. 0180D] “Into the temple,” Seml.], he called the sanctuary his Father’s house, as a Son. When he says to Nicodemus, “God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, so that everyone who believes in him should not perish but have everlasting life” (John 3:16). And again (ibid. 17): “For God did not send his Son into the world to judge the world, but so that the world might be saved through him. He who believes in him is not judged; he who does not believe in him has already been judged, because he has not believed in the name of the only Son of God.”

When John [Note: [Col. 0180D] “Of” omitted by Ciaccon.] was asked what was happening concerning [Note: [Col. 0180D] “But when John was asked.” He means John the Baptist, whose words are also those that follow in John 3, “The Father loves the Son,” etc.; for thus we have corrected the text in place of “loved.” PAM.] Jesus, he said: “The Father loves [Note: [Col. 0180D] “Loved,” Seml.] the Son, and has delivered all things into his hand. He who believes [Note: [Col. 0180D] “Believed,” Seml.] in the Son has eternal life; he who does not believe in the Son [Note: [Col. 0180D] “Believed in the Son,” Seml.] of God will not see God, but God’s wrath will remain upon him” (John 3:35–36).

Whom, then, did he reveal to the Samaritan woman? If it was the Messiah, who is called Christ, he surely revealed himself [Col. 0181A] as the Son, not the Father, since elsewhere too Christ is called the Son of God, not the Father. Thereafter he says to the disciples: “My purpose is to do the will of him who sent me, so that I may complete his work.” And to the Jews, concerning the healing of the paralytic: “My Father works until now, and I work” (John 5:17). “The Father and I,” says the Son.

Finally, for this reason the Jews desired all the more to kill him—not only [Note: [Col. 0181D] “Nevertheless,” Seml.] because he was breaking the Sabbath, but because he called God his own Father, making himself equal to God. He therefore said to them at that time: “The Son can do nothing of himself unless he sees [Note: [Col. 0181D] “May see,” Seml.] the Father doing it” (John 5:19). “For whatever he does, the Son likewise does. For the Father loves the Son and has shown him all the things that he has done, and he will show him works greater than these [Note: [Col. 0181D] “These things,” Seml.], so that you may marvel. For just as he raises the dead and gives them life [Col. 0181B], so too the Son gives life [Note: [Col. 0181D] “Will give life,” others.] to whomever he wishes.

“For the Father does not judge, but has given all judgment to the Son, so that all may honor the Son just as they honor the Father. He who does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent the Son. Amen, amen, I say to you that he who hears [Note: [Col. 0181D] “That even he who shall have heard,” Latin.] my words [Note: [Col. 0181D] “My” absent from Seml.] and believes him who sent me has eternal life; and he does not come [Note: [Col. 0181D] “Will not come,” Seml.] into judgment, but passes [Note: [Col. 0182D] Others: “has passed.” “Has passed”; others: “comes,” manuscript of Wouw.] from death into life. Amen, I say to you that the hour will come when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God; and when they hear, they will live. For just as the Father has eternal life [Note: [Col. 0182D] “Eternal” absent from Rhen.] from himself, so too he gave the Son to have eternal life in himself; and [Note: [Col. 0181D] “He gave him authority to execute judgment, in that he is the Son of Man.” Nearly all manuscripts and interpreters place *quia* (“because”) here, which is easier to understand. For St. Chrysostom did not employ that punctuation, because Paul of Samosata used it. LE PR.] he gave him authority to execute judgment, inasmuch as he is the Son of Man”—through the flesh, namely, just as he is the Son of God through his Spirit.

He further adds: “But I have testimony greater than John’s” (John 5:36–37). “For the works [Col. 0181C] that the Father gave me to complete, those very works bear witness concerning me that the Father sent me. And the Father who sent me has himself borne witness concerning me.” Then, by adding, “You have never heard his voice nor seen his form,” he confirms that it was not the Father but the Son who formerly was seen and heard.

Finally, he says: “I have come in my Father’s name, and you have not received me” (John 5:43). So thoroughly had the Son always existed under the name of God, King, Lord, [Note: [Col. 0182D] “And” absent from Seml.; “and Lord” absent from the manuscript of Wouw.] Almighty, and Most High. When they asked what they ought to do, he answered: “Believe in him whom God sent” (John 6:29). He also affirms that he is the bread which the Father would provide from heaven (John 6:32).

Therefore, everything that the Father gave him would come to him, and he would not reject it, because he had descended from heaven not to do his own will but the Father’s; and the Father’s will was that whoever saw [Col. 0181D] the Son and believed in him should obtain life and resurrection (John 6:35). Moreover, no one could come to him [Col. 0182A] unless the Father drew him [Note: [Col. 0182D] “Unless the Father draws him.” In these seven chapters beginning with Chapter Twenty, he attacks the Monarchians and proves from Scripture that the Son is another than the Father, and therefore that the Father did not make himself the Son. LE PR.]; everyone who had heard and learned from the Father would come to him (John 6:14).

He also adds here, “Not that anyone has seen the Father,” in order to show that the Word, through whom they are taught, belongs to the Father. But when many withdraw from him (John 6:66), and he offers his Apostles the choice whether they too wish to depart, what does Simon Peter answer? “To whom shall we go [Note: [Col. 0182D] “Shall we go,” Fran.]? You have the words of life, and we believe that you are the Christ.” Did Peter believe him to be the Father, or the Father’s Christ?

## CHAPTER XXII

Whose teaching does he say it is, at which they marveled—his own or the Father’s? Likewise, when they debated among themselves whether he himself might be the Christ—assuredly not the Father, but the Son—he says: “You do not even [Note: [Col. 0182D] “But,” Jun.] know me” (John 7:28), “or where I come from; and I did not come of myself, but he who sent me is true, and you [Col. 0182B] do not know him. I know him, because I am from him [Note: [Col. 0182D] “Because I am with him,” Seml.; “because I am from him,” Jun.], and he sent me.” He did not say, “Because I am he, and I sent myself,” but, “He sent me.”

Likewise, when the Pharisees sent men to seize him, he said: “For a little while longer I am with you, and I am going to him who sent me.” And when he denies that he is alone—“But there are I,” he says, “and the Father who sent me”—does he not reveal two, two who are nevertheless inseparable? Indeed, this was the entire substance of his teaching: that there are two who are inseparable.

For when he cites the law confirming the testimony of two persons, he adds: “I bear witness concerning myself, and the Father who sent me bears witness concerning me.” But if there were one, because the Son and the Father were the same, he would not have employed the law’s support, which requires belief on the testimony not of one but of two.

Likewise, when he is asked where the Father was, he answers that neither he nor the Father was known to them [Col. 0182C], thus declaring that two were unknown. If they knew him, they would know the Father—not because he himself was both Father and Son, but because, through their indivisibility, neither can be known or remain unknown without the other.

“He who sent me,” he says, “is true; and the things I heard from him are those I speak to the world.” Scripture interprets from outside the narrative that they did not understand that he had spoken about the Father, although they assuredly ought to have recognized that the Father’s words were in the Son by reading in Jeremiah (Jer. 1:9): “And the Lord said to me, ‘Behold, I have put my words into your mouth’”; and in Isaiah (Isa. 1:4): “The Lord gives me the tongue of instruction, that I may know when it is fitting to speak a word.”

So too he himself says again: “Then,” he says (John 8:28), “you will know that I am, and that I speak nothing of myself, but as he taught me, so I speak; because he who sent me is with me.” This too is testimony [Col. 0182D] to two indivisible persons.

Likewise, in the dispute with the Jews, reproaching them because they wished to kill him [Col. 0183A], he says: “I speak the things that I have seen in the presence of my Father, and you do what you have seen in the presence of your father”; and, “Now you wish to kill a man who has spoken to you the truth that he heard from God”; and, “If God were your Father, you would love me. For I came forth from God and have come”—and yet they are not separated [Note: [Col. 0183D] “We do not separate,” Seml.], although he said that he came forth, as some seize upon the occasion afforded by this saying. He came forth from the Father as a ray from the sun, a stream from its spring, or a shoot from its seed.

“I do not have a demon, but I honor my Father”; and, “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is the Father who glorifies me, whom you say is your God; yet you do not know him, but I know him. And if I say, ‘I do not know him,’ I shall be a liar like you; but I know him and keep his word.” And when he adds, “Abraham saw my day and rejoiced” [Col. 0183B], he surely demonstrates that it was the Son who formerly was seen by Abraham, not the Father.

Likewise, concerning that blind man, he says that he must do the Father’s works. After the restoration of the man’s sight, he says to him: “Do you believe in the Son of God?” (John 9:35). And when the man asked who he was, by revealing himself he assuredly revealed the Son, in whom he had said the man must believe.

Next, he professes that he is known by the Father and that the Father is known by him. He says that the Father loves him because he lays down his life, since he had received this commandment from the Father. And when asked by the Jews whether he himself was the Christ—God’s Christ, assuredly [Note: [Col. 0183D] “God,” Seml.], for even to this day the Jews await God’s Christ, not the Father himself, since it is nowhere written that the Father would come as Christ—he says: “I speak to you, and you do not believe. The works that I do in the Father’s name themselves bear witness concerning me.”

To what do they bear witness [Col. 0183C]? Assuredly that he was the one about whom they were asking, that is, God’s Christ. Concerning his sheep too, he says that no one would snatch them from his hand: “For what the Father gave me is greater than all things” [Note: [Col. 0183D] Others: “For the Father, who is greater than all, gave them to me”; Jun.: “For the Father who gave them to me is greater than all.”]; and [Note: [Col. 0183D] “I and the Father are one.” He cited these same words from John 10 earlier, in Chapter 20. The Monarchian heretics, however, distorted them, supposing that they established that the Son was not another than the Father, although the words state a unity of essence, not of person. LE PR.]: “I and the Father are one.”

Here, then, the foolish—indeed, the blind—wish to establish their position, failing to see, first, that “I and the Father” signifies two; next, that the final word “are” does not proceed from a single person, since it is expressed in the plural; and then that he says, “We are one,” not “We are one person.” For if he had said, “We are one person,” he might have assisted their position. “One person” appears to signify the singular number.

Yet because there are two, he uses “one” in the neuter gender, not the masculine. This does not pertain to singularity but to unity, likeness, conjunction, and the love [Col. 0183D] of the Father, who loves the Son, and to the obedience of the Son, who obeys the Father’s will. By saying, “We are one, I and the Father,” he shows that those whom he makes equal [Col. 0184A] and joins are two.

So thoroughly is this the case that he adds that he had shown them many works from the Father, none of which merited stoning. And lest they suppose that for this reason they ought to stone him—as though he had wished himself to be understood as God himself, that is, the Father, because he had said, “I and the Father are one”—he shows himself to be God insofar as he is the Son of God, not insofar as he is God himself:

“If,” he says, “it is written in the law, ‘I said, you are gods,’ and Scripture cannot be annulled, do you say that he whom the Father sanctified and sent into the world blasphemes because I said [Note: [Col. 0183D] “He had said,” Seml.], ‘I am the Son of God’? If I do not perform my Father’s works, do not believe; but if I do perform them, and you are unwilling to believe me, believe at least on account of the works. And know that I am in the Father and the Father in me.”

The Father, therefore, was in the Son through the works, and the Son was in the Father; and thus through the works we understand that the Father and the Son [Note: [Col. 0183D] “And the Son” absent from Seml.] are one. So thoroughly did he persist [Col. 0184B] in establishing this entire point: that two nevertheless should be believed to exist in one power, because the Son could not otherwise be believed unless two were believed to exist.
