§ V.—These are the arguments that the *Apologeticus* itself supplies in support of our position; I do not see what [Col. 0543D] can be answered to them. Let another passage of TERTULLIAN now follow, taken from another of his books—namely, the one he sent to the martyrs confined in prison. It is beyond doubt that the martyrs meant here must be those who had been arrested during the reign of SEVERUS. To strengthen them, he presents the following as his final argument (*To the Martyrs*, ch. IV, p. 468 of the *Works*):

> Everyone can endure, even for a human being’s sake, what he hesitates to endure for God’s sake. In this regard, let even the present times serve us as evidence: how many persons, and of what distinction, meet ends unexpected in view of their birth, rank, physical condition, and age, for a human being’s sake—either at his own hands, if they have acted against him, or at the hands of his adversaries, if they have stood by him.

I could immediately point out here that it is sufficiently apparent [Col. 0544A] that TERTULLIAN did not wish to make the emperor, whom he here calls “a human being,” the author of the evils inflicted upon those martyrs. Otherwise he would have written, “for the sake of that human being who is torturing you.” I set this reasoning aside, however, and consider the event itself to which these words refer. They commemorate the slaughter of many men distinguished by rank and merit. Let anyone who thinks that these words can be applied to anything other than the execution of those whom SEVERUS butchered on the occasion of the Gallic war stirred up by ALBINUS be counted ignorant of the history of SEVERUS. This was the time when innumerable and eminent persons, contrary to every expectation, were being hurried off to death because they were thought to have favored ALBINUS. Hear Spartianus (*Life of Severus*, ch. XII, pp. 177–178, Obrecht’s ed.):

> After innumerable men of ALBINUS’ party had been killed, among whom [Col. 0544B] were many leading men of the state and many illustrious women—

is this not precisely what TERTULLIAN says?—

> all their property was confiscated and augmented the treasury. Then many nobles of the Spaniards and the Sualli were put to death.

Immediately afterward, the same author adds a fairly long list of the most noble men deprived of life in those disturbances (ch. XIII, pp. 178–179). Capitolinus (*Life of Clodius Albinus*, ch. XII, p. 217) and Herodian (*Histories*, book III, ch. VIII, p. 147, Boecler’s ed.) may be joined to him. What, indeed, could explain TERTULLIAN more clearly? Nor, on the other hand, is there any doubt that ALBINUS, who is hinted at here, employed the same savagery, especially after he perceived that SEVERUS was laying the plots against him that Capitolinus describes (*Life of Albinus*, chs. VII–VIII, p. 211f.). Anyone who weighs this [Col. 0544C] must find it hard to accept that someone should nevertheless assert that Christians were not harassed under SEVERUS before A.D. 202. ALBINUS’ uprising, as I shall shortly show, fell in A.D. 196, and his defeat in A.D. 197. It makes no difference whether the passage is understood of the one year or the other. For it will in either case prove that illustrious Christian martyrs had existed under his rule long before the decrees of SEVERUS were promulgated.

TERTULLIAN’S *Scorpiace* could supply me with certain other evidence. But I now intend to summon another witness, Clement of Alexandria. Eusebius long ago reported that Clement composed his *Stromata*, which are still read today, during the reign of SEVERUS (*Ecclesiastical History*, book VI, ch. VI, p. 208). The earlier books in particular seem to have been written shortly after the beginning of his reign, when COMMODUS had not long [Col. 0544D] been dead. For in the first book, in which he establishes a chronological reckoning, he proceeds no further than the death of COMMODUS. [Non-Latin text omitted in the source.] “From the first contest, moreover, to the death of COMMODUS there are one hundred and eleven years” (*Stromata*, book I, p. 402, Potter’s ed.). Here he stops—a clear indication that COMMODUS had died not long before he wrote these things.

For my present purpose, it is enough that Clement unquestionably published these writings before A.D. 202. When the evils suffered by the Alexandrians had been increased by the emperor’s decrees, that excellent man was in exile and spent his time chiefly in Palestine and Syria. Learned men have correctly observed this, following Jerome (*Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. XXXVIII, p. 111, Fabricius’s recent ed.) [Col. 0545A] and drawing upon Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History*, book VI, ch. XI, pp. 212–213). No one would readily say that he could have devoted himself to so laborious a work during that turbulent and calamitous period. The very celebrated Dodwell himself, who often cuts down his own vines, acknowledged this and demonstrated by several arguments in two places that these *Stromata* were written long before A.D. 202 (*Dissertation III on Irenaeus*, § XXVII, p. 276f.; and *Dissertation on the Primitive Succession of the Roman Pontiffs*, ch. XV, § IV, p. 209).

We have therefore obtained precisely what we most needed. We have the defendant’s own confession. If Clement wrote when SEVERUS’ reign was not yet far advanced, it is established that those who deny that the harassment of Christians began before A.D. 202 are guilty of error. For the slaughter and punishment of the brethren were daily before his eyes as he wrote. Behold what he says: [Col. 0545B] [Non-Latin text omitted in the source.] (*Stromata*, book II, p. 494):

> Among us, however, springs of martyrs overflow daily—martyrs seen by our own eyes, who are burned, tortured, and beheaded.

Let those who deem it glorious to follow Dodwell’s precepts answer here, and above all let them reconcile the man with himself. COMMODUS met his fate, as schoolboys know, on the night separating the years 192 and 193. Suppose Clement wrote his first book three years after his death, in A.D. 195. Suppose again—though this is scarcely probable—that nearly two years intervened between the first and second volumes of the *Stromata*. [Col. 0545C] Nevertheless, already in A.D. 197 we shall find at Alexandria a fine company of those who, under Clement’s own observation, met a bloody death for the SAVIOR.

Here one may indeed marvel at Baronius’ acumen, for on account of these words of Clement he asserts that Clement’s books were composed after the tenth year of SEVERUS:

> Moreover, since it is established that in the books of the *Stromata* themselves mention is made of the persecution of Christians then in progress, it is necessary to affirm that he wrote after the tenth year of SEVERUS.

(*Annals*, vol. II, at A.D. 196, no. XXII, pp. 278–279.) A judgment unworthy of the author of the *Annals*! That most illustrious man forgets what he himself was shortly to maintain: that already in A.D. 198 injuries had been inflicted upon the Christians at Plautianus’ instigation. In this respect he commits a twofold error: [Col. 0545D] he makes both the sufferings of the Christians more recent than he himself thought and Clement’s books more recent than the circumstances permit.

§ VI.—I do not know whether it deserves to be added to these arguments that already in antiquity it was believed that Christians had been persecuted under SEVERUS long before A.D. 202. Those who do not strip the traditions of their forefathers of all credibility will not entirely despise this observation. I shall therefore produce Paulus Orosius, a writer of the fifth century, who relates that SEVERUS was grievous to the Christians before ALBINUS attempted a revolution—that is, in A.D. 196 at the latest (*Histories*, book VII, ch. XVII, p. 542, Mainz, 1615, octavo). These are his words, which Treculphus of Lisieux, a historian of the [Col. 0546A] ninth century, repeated without changing a single word (*Chronicles*, vol. II, book II, ch. XXIV, p. 488, Geneva ed., 1597, octavo):

> In the fifth persecution after Nero, he tortured the Christians, and very many of the saints throughout the different provinces were crowned with martyrdom. Heavenly vengeance immediately followed upon this profane presumption of Severus against the Christians and the Church of God. For Severus was at once swept—or rather dragged—back from Syria into Gaul for a third civil war. He had already waged one at Rome against Julianus and another in Syria against Pescennius; the third was being stirred up by Clodius Albinus, Julianus’ associate in the murder of Pertinax, who had made himself Caesar in Gaul.

He says, therefore, that the divine Power willed ALBINUS to disturb the peace of the empire because SEVERUS had acted unjustly toward the Christians. I certainly am not one who thinks Orosius a writer of the highest reliability. Nor do I make all [Col. 0546B] that he relates here my own. I wish him to serve only as a witness that, in his time, the opinion I defend was commonly accepted. If any credence is due to narratives handed down to us from our forefathers, no one will doubt that some must also be due to this one. I would immediately have cast aside a man whose superstition had been all too manifest if he had said that savagery toward the Christians was the cause of absolutely all the wars, including those waged against Julianus and Niger. As it is, I do not wholly disdain him, since he proceeds more scrupulously and does not always dream of the brethren’s punishments whenever he sees the whole world blazing with war.

§ VII.—I believe that what I have argued thus far is endowed with such clarity and evident force that anyone who should henceforth [Col. 0546C] attach himself to the opposing side would be unjust to himself. If the matter required it, I could have produced far more evidence; since it in no way demands this, I reserve it for another occasion. Thus all the arguments that the celebrated Dodwell, perhaps not without exertion, contrived in support of his position collapse.

He first attempts to wrest TERTULLIAN from us. For he understood that in the *Apologeticus* SEVERUS is excluded from the number of those who had laid violent hands upon Christians, although he does not even touch the altogether striking passages that we have examined. What, then, do you suppose he infers from this? Look and marvel: that the emperor’s decrees had indeed already been proposed at that time, but had not been conveyed to those regions in which TERTULLIAN lived (*Cyprianic Dissertation* XI, § 44, p. 69). Plainly, he strives with all his might to diminish the magnitude of the Christians’ sufferings. But what a lifeless [Col. 0546D] dream! It will perhaps not please even those who are ignorant of the condition of the Roman Empire.

Yet Ruinart is no less lifeless in refuting him; if ever there was a place where he seems to have been amid Gallic snows, it is here (*Preface to the Acts of the Martyrs*, sect. II, §§ 44–45, p. 46C). By hastily heaping together certain passages, he shows that the persecution extended far and wide. But what has this to do with Dodwell’s sophistry? Dodwell did not deny this; rather, he ventured to contend that the persecutions were not equally savage in all provinces, because the laws issued by the emperor had not reached them all. Unless I am mistaken, I have more securely rescued TERTULLIAN from this distortion.

I then adduced two further testimonies which, even if all the passages from the *Apologeticus* could be evaded—which is plainly impossible—[Col. 0547A] would nevertheless show that Christians had been tortured before the year he specifies. The suspicion for which the learned man contends is groundless. Yet let it stand, if anyone wishes. Other evidence still remains that is not exposed to this device. With these points unmoved, the arguments that the same author advanced to assign the beginning of all the afflictions to A.D. 202 will likewise fall (*loc. cit.*, § XLI, p. 68). They come down to two heads. He says that the pagans had lacked both leisure and an opportunity to attack Christians. The weakness of each contention is readily apparent when they are compared with what I have written. Let us nevertheless grant the learned man leave to speak:

> Previously there was no leisure, whether for the emperor himself, for the governors of the provinces, or for the common people and magistrates of the cities, whom peace from foreign enemies at least generally turns toward that wanton savagery.

I scarcely doubt that he was thinking of something else [Col. 0547B] when he wrote thus. What connection, I ask, is there between these propositions: “The Romans were entangled in war,” and “The Christians experienced no evils”? Either I understand nothing, or there is none. Amid the very clash of a most serious war, VALERIAN inflicted endless troubles upon the Christians. This will become clearer if we consider more distinctly the wars of that period. In those years the emperor was occupied with military service in the eastern regions and in Gaul. Let there have been peace there, as I shall concede for the moment, in the more divine assembly; but what impediment did this place in the way of the governors of other provinces, or of the common people, preventing them from summoning Christians before the courts? What is better known than that crimes are never more immune from punishment than when wars are laying everything waste? Finally, what is more commonplace than that [Col. 0547C] custom of the pagans by which they never created greater miseries for Christ’s disciples than when the Roman world was oppressed by military and other calamities? When asked what the cause was, the petty sacrificers confidently cried that all blame rested with the Christians. Arnobius reports their words (*Against the Nations*, book I, p. 16, Heraldus’s ed.):

> The gods are neglected, and there is now the greatest scarcity in the temples; the ancient rites lie open to mockery, and the most venerable ceremonies of former times have perished through the superstitions of new religions.

Hence those tears; hence their renewed frenzies whenever new disturbances arose. There was therefore no reason why the very distinguished Sébastien Tillemont—a man otherwise with few equals in this field—should have thought this argument of Dodwell’s so excellent that he ought to repeat it as his own (*History of the Emperors*, vol. III, part I, p. 146, [Col. 0547D] Brussels ed.). It is so far from proving anything that it instead affords some aid to the opposing side.

Still feebler is the second argument, that there had been no opportunity to mistreat Christians:

> At least no occasion presented itself that might stir the emperor’s anger against the Christians, since they had been neither Niger’s supporters nor Albinus’ supporters, as TERTULLIAN says.

I spare my words here, because I think nothing could have been said more thoughtlessly. Were emperors, then, never hostile to the Christian cause unless some Christians had attached themselves to men who were stirring up civil wars? Not even schoolboys would tolerate that. Nor is the principal question here whether SEVERUS himself raged against the Christians before A.D. 202. Although for him, and for others, the very name of Christian [Col. 0548A] was occasion enough. “For this reason we are tortured,” says our TERTULLIAN (*Apologeticus*, ch. II, p. 35), “when we confess; we are punished when we persist; and we are acquitted when we deny, because THE BATTLE IS OVER THE NAME.”

§ VIII.—More has been said than I had intended against those who contend that the *Apologeticus* was not produced before A.D. 202. Yet it has not been excessive, since in this way some light has been added to sacred history and the period of the Christians’ persecution under SEVERUS has been more carefully established.

I now proceed to those who prefer to go still further and maintain that the book appeared after SEVERUS’ death, after A.D. 210. These men are more easily excused, since their purpose is not so much to minimize the Christians’ sufferings as to extend them also into the beginning of CARACALLA’S reign. Nevertheless, they are far inferior to the others in the quality of their arguments, whose force here is almost nonexistent. The chief of this school [Col. 0548B] is the great Joseph Scaliger, and I am not at all surprised that his authority proved a snare to some. He says (*Notes on Eusebius’ Chronicle*, in the *Thesaurus of Time*, p. 229, last ed. of Alexander More):

> I doubt whether he wrote the *Apologeticus* under SEVERUS, since he says: “Only yesterday SEVERUS, most steadfast of emperors, repealed the Papian laws”; he seems to speak of a dead man.

For my part, I am not so acute as to see what compels these words of TERTULLIAN to be interpreted of a dead SEVERUS. He is praised. But who denies that living persons are commonly adorned with praise? The word “yesterday” is employed. But why must it therefore be understood of SEVERUS’ past reign, as though TERTULLIAN had compared it to a single day? You, most learned Havercamp, have correctly observed that it can be understood of a time not long past [Col. 0548C] (*Notes on the Apologeticus*, p. 287). I add that it must be so understood, unless we wish the most learned of the Fathers to have written nonsense. On Scaliger’s view, he would have meant: “SEVERUS, while he was still alive, repealed the Papian laws.” Silence, indeed! The Romans perhaps needed to be instructed that the emperor had not bestowed this benefit from the underworld. Accordingly, let “yesterday” here mean nothing other than “not long ago.”

Nevertheless, the very celebrated Peter Allix approved this argument of Scaliger’s and based upon it in particular his own opinion that the *Apologeticus* was not committed to writing before A.D. 211 at the earliest (*Dissertation on the Life and Writings of Tertullian*, with other works; Paris ed., 1680, quarto). How incautiously this was done is, I think, evident. The same author added [Col. 0548D] two other reasons for this computation. Although they have already been briefly refuted by the celebrated Dupin (*New Library of Ecclesiastical Authors*, vol. I, pp. 107–108, second ed.), my plan requires that I examine them again here.

He says first that it is evident that our work was composed after the book *On Prescriptions*, because in the *Apologeticus* TERTULLIAN pointed to that work, as it were, with his finger. This is not the place to discuss the date of the book *On Prescriptions*. For the meantime I shall adopt the opinion of Cave (*Literary History of Ecclesiastical Writers*, p. 43) and others, that it was published about A.D. 207. We need only see how the very distinguished Allix proves that this book is mentioned in the *Apologeticus*. The passage he produces is this (ch. XLVII, p. 519):

> But we readily PLEAD AGAINST our adulterators [Col. 0549A] that the rule of truth is that which comes from Christ and was transmitted through his companions, while these various commentators will be shown to be considerably later.

You see the learned man’s remarkable reasoning: TERTULLIAN used the word “plead by prescription”; therefore he referred to the book *On Prescriptions*. His argument will have some force only when he has shown that, before our author wrote about prescriptions, the word “prescribe” had been unheard of among the Romans.

He continues by saying that TERTULLIAN refers to the eclipse of the sun observed at Utica in A.D. 210. I am undone if he speaks the truth. But a man who thinks this can be elicited from the words he cites is excessively ingenious. Hear them (*Apologeticus*, ch. XX, p. 390):

> Even the functions of the seasons and the duties of the elements depart from their courses; even the order of nature is disturbed by monstrosities and portents.

Where [Col. 0549B] is the eclipse of the sun here? Where is Utica? I think he is speaking of the seasons of the year producing crops neither sufficiently early nor very well. But let us concede what is false, that something is said here of the darkening of the sun: by what means will it be demonstrated that the eclipse which occurred in A.D. 210 is indicated here?

§ IX.—At last we have thus arrived at those who acknowledge that the *Apologeticus* was written either before A.D. 200 or in that very year, and whom we recognized above as friendlier to the truth. They do not pursue the same course, but diverge in various directions. Some favor A.D. 196 (Cave, *Literary History of Ecclesiastical Writers*, p. 42); their reasons, for my part, are unknown to me. I showed above that PAGI once chose A.D. 198.

Caesar Baronius wishes the book to have appeared in A.D. 201 according to his customary reckoning—that is, in A.D. 199 according to the common era, which, as is well known, prevails [Col. 0549C] in investigations of this kind (*Ecclesiastical Annals*, vol. II, at A.D. 200, no. VII, p. 198; at A.D. 201, no. XXVII, p. 307, no. XXXVI, p. 310). He judges that when SEVERUS marched against the Parthians—which he supposes without reasons to have happened in this year—Plautianus, left at Rome as praetorian prefect, violated the Christians’ peace. For this reason he thinks that TERTULLIAN, then present in the city, as he supposes, wished by this book to repel those dangers. Anton Pagi has already removed the conjecture concerning Plautianus, proving that he went to Asia with SEVERUS (*Critique of Baronius*, vol. II, at A.D. 199, no. IV, pp. 92–93). The *Apologeticus* itself refutes Baronius’ contention that, when TERTULLIAN wrote, only the Christians living at Rome were being tortured; for it is certain that the work is addressed to the governors [Col. 0549D] of the provinces. The date of the Parthian war will be carefully discussed below.

The year 200 was chosen by very many, and those among the most learned men. Among them is JACQUES PAMÈLE (*Life of Tertullian*, prefixed to his edition of the *Works*), if we believe John Forbes of Corse (*Historical-Theological Instruction*, book VII, ch. VII, § 16, p. 320, vol. II of the *Theological Works*), who says he follows him; Baronius, however, draws Pamèle into his own camp. There is also the most diligent Tillemont (*History of the Emperors*, vol. III, part I, p. 94), although elsewhere (*Chronology*, at the end of vol. III of the *History*, p. 1221) he is uncertain whether it should better be placed in A.D. 201. There is L. E. Dupin (*Library of Ecclesiastical Authors*, vol. I, p. 91), and a good many others whose names I think it of little use [Col. 0550A] to enumerate here.

Some of these merely state their opinion and omit their reasons. For it is generally the fate of this work of ours to lie neglected and abandoned. The most diligent derive their evidence from the same passage that we shall call to our aid. They differ in assigning the events in question to their respective years—namely, the Parthian war and ALBINUS’ movements. Accordingly, nothing will contribute more to completing this inquiry than for me both to disclose and establish my own position. Once this is done, it can no longer be obscure what room could remain for the arguments of others.

I intend, however, to proceed methodically and by stages. The more general considerations will come first, and for the sake of those who date the book after SEVERUS, it will be demonstrated that it was written during his reign. A more specific inquiry will follow, and every impediment [Col. 0550B] will be removed by indubitable arguments. In the last place, the precise year will be considered.

§ X.—That TERTULLIAN composed this work during the reign of SEVERUS is shown both by certain passages I have already indicated above and by the one I shall now recite. “Moreover,” he says (ch. V, p. 294), “from all the emperors since then down to the present day, WHO ARE VERSED IN DIVINE AND HUMAN WISDOM, produce a single persecutor of the Christians.” He counts the man who was then administering the affairs of the empire among rulers versed in divine and human wisdom—that is, skilled in both kinds of law and in literature. Except SEVERUS, and there will be no emperor to whom these words apply. Either COMMODUS or CARACALLA must be named, the former of whom ruled the world before him, the latter after him. For PERTINAX [Col. 0550C] and JULIANUS, who held this eminence for only a few days after COMMODUS’ death, do not enter into consideration here. But what manner of men, I ask, were those whom I have named? Wise neither in divine nor in human matters, endowed with manners more truly bestial than human, thoroughly learned in crimes, not literature.

SEVERUS, by contrast, had devoted much time to learning the arts. Spartianus calls him “sufficiently devoted to the studies of philosophy and eloquence, and even excessively desirous of learning” (*Life of Severus*, ch. XVIII, p. 154). Dio praises his zeal and diligence, although he was not particularly capable of putting into practice what he had conceived in his mind. This, I think, is the meaning of the words I shall transcribe (book XXVI [sic], in the *Peirescian Excerpts*, edited by Henri Valois, p. 740, Paris, 1634, quarto): [Col. 0550D] [Non-Latin text omitted in the source.] For VALESIUS’ translation does not satisfy me in every respect: “More devoted to learning than proficient in it,” and so forth. Who could doubt that such a ruler could rightly be numbered among the learned? But there is no need of a longer proof. The fact itself places it beyond all doubt that this work was committed to writing under no emperor other than SEVERUS.

§ XI.—We have identified the emperor. From what has already been said, we next know that not all the years of his reign must be considered, but only those numbered from A.D. 193, when he assumed it, to A.D. 200. This cycle of eight years, therefore, will now occupy us. Yet no small portion of it will immediately be removed if we demonstrate that the book was written after the civil war stirred up by ALBINUS [Col. 0551A] in Gaul. This will be done with the aid of the following passage, which I present.

While that ingenious man contends that the members of the Christian sect had never plotted anything against the lives of rulers, he exclaims among other things (ch. XXXV, p. 300):

> Whence came the Cassiuses, the Nigers, and the ALBINUSES? Whence those who besiege Caesar between the two laurels? From Romans, unless I am mistaken—that is, from non-Christians.

In this passage he recalls to the governors’ memory the past calamities of the empire and enumerates the dangers from which the emperors had happily escaped. Among these he mentions in particular the uprising stirred up by ALBINUS. Accordingly, nothing more is required for certain knowledge than to inquire in what year those Albinian attempts were suppressed.

§ XII.—But here, indeed, is a new circuit. Those whose great authority in these matters very few fail to recognize disagree. Yet only a single year separates them. Some favor A.D. 197; others A.D. 198. Since we regard it as a matter of conscience not to omit anything pertaining to the examination of the whole question, we shall show by indubitable arguments which view ought to be held. This will be done all the more willingly because the whole dispute over the year of the *Apologeticus* depends upon this question.

The former opinion is the widespread one, defended by most compilers of the fasti (see Onofrio Panvinio, *Consular Fasti*, p. 241, Heidelberg, 1588), Caesar Baronius (*Annals*, vol. II, at A.D. 199, no. I, p. 293), Francesco Mezzabarba (*Imperial Coins*, p. 271, Milan, 1683), the celebrated Tillemont (*History of the Emperors*, vol. III, part I, pp. 72 and 404), and a good many [Col. 0551C] others. If these men had fortified their reasons with greater diligence, they would doubtless have preserved them inviolate and secure. They would have fortified them, I think, if they had imagined that anyone would be bold enough to overturn and reject them. I profess without any hesitation that I adhere to them. Before dismantling the apparatus of those who think otherwise, I shall set out the reasons for this decision. Once the foundations of our computation have been indicated, it will be easier to judge what value should be assigned to the other.

First of all, I wish it to be observed that the Albinian war did not last long, but began in one year and was concluded in the next. By great and hurried marches SEVERUS flew from the East into Gaul. The enemy, ALBINUS, was also at hand immediately. Without delay they engaged, and in several skirmishes—not, indeed, [Col. 0551D] very great ones—contended for control of the state. At the beginning the battle was fought with uncertain fortune, and ALBINUS had already been raised to the hope of victory. But shortly afterward, in the fatal and decisive battle near Lugdunum on the eleventh day before the Kalends of March, victory fell to SEVERUS and destruction to ALBINUS. All the writers we possess testify that all these events were accomplished within a brief period and were not prolonged beyond a second year. Nor do I see anything that can be objected in any way.

Now hear why I hold these two years to have been 196 and 197. I shall bring three arguments into the field. There is no one who calls, or could call, into doubt that PESCENNIUS NIGER, SEVERUS’ other rival, was defeated and deprived of life in A.D. 194. [Col. 0552A] Not long afterward SEVERUS sent assassins into Gaul to remove ALBINUS from the way, so that he might no longer have a partner in imperial rank. Besides Herodian (*Histories*, book III, ch. V, p. 133 in my edition), SEVERUS’ own letter to ALBINUS, which he had entrusted to the assassins for delivery to him, confirms this in Capitolinus (*Life of Clodius Albinus*, ch. VII, p. 212). Even a cursory inspection of it can show that it was written at least a year after NIGER’S death. When the outcome did not answer the emperor’s expectation and the prepared plot was uncovered, ALBINUS immediately took up arms against SEVERUS. On receiving this news, Severus thought no time should be given to repose; gathering at once what forces he could, he hastened toward Gaul with incredible speed and with all his characteristic vehemence, [Col. 0552B] and attacked ALBINUS. He was unwilling even to wait for the capture of Byzantium. [Non-Latin text omitted in the source], Herodian says of him (*Histories*, book III, ch. VI, p. 139), [non-Latin text omitted in the source]. He himself traveled without interruption, paying little regard either to feast days or to any hardships, equally enduring cold and heat.

Anyone who considers this haste cannot but determine that A.D. 196 marked the beginning and A.D. 197 the end of the Albinian war. I shall concede that the conspirators were dispatched by SEVERUS in A.D. 195. I shall add that they were detected in the crime when that year was nearly over. Finally, I shall say that SEVERUS was informed of the matter when A.D. 196 had already begun. Within not very many days the emperor, utterly intolerant of delay, who ran [Col. 0552C] rather than traveled, could have reached Gaul after receiving this news.

We shall prepare another supporting argument from the duration of the siege of Byzantium. Dio Cassius writes that this city was besieged by SEVERUS for a period of three years, [non-Latin text omitted in the source] (*History*, book LXXIV, p. 845, Frankfurt ed., 1606). All the circumstances of the event—as I see Tillemont had already observed (*Note XIV on Severus*, vol. III, *History of the Emperors*, part I, pp. 401–402)—require these words to be interpreted not of a completed cycle of three years, but only as indicating that the siege continued during three successive calendar years, although perhaps it began as the first was ending and was completed as the third began. This manner of speaking is very familiar to the Greeks. [Col. 0552D]

I shall not dispute to which year its beginning ought to be assigned, whether A.D. 193 or A.D. 194. It is enough that we owe to Dio the further information (*History*, book LXXIV, p. 844) that the city was surrounded by troops before NIGER had yet been killed. He fell in A.D. 194. Accordingly, whether the end of A.D. 193 is preferred, as some do, or the beginning of A.D. 194, we have prevailed in either case. Yet I do not deny that those who choose A.D. 194 are by far more correct, and for many reasons. The capture therefore falls in A.D. 196. But at the time when he marched to meet ALBINUS, the city had not yet been reduced to the emperor’s power, according to Herodian (*History*, book III, ch. VI, p. 139). Indeed, he appears already to have been on the road to Gaul when the news of the recovered city was brought to him. [Col. 0553A] If, therefore, the recovery of Byzantium and the expedition against ALBINUS occurred in one and the same year, and that year must necessarily be A.D. 196, it follows altogether that the battle of Lugdunum was fought in A.D. 197. If you advance to A.D. 198, you will simultaneously admit that the siege of Byzantium lasted four years, which is established by Dio to be utterly false.

The argument I shall present last is the strongest. A great mass of inscriptions in Gruter and others, and a great quantity of coins in Biragius, teaches that SEVERUS’ elder son, ANTONINUS, afterward called CARACALLA, was adorned with the title Augustus in A.D. 198. Moved by this evidence, learned men—though they dispute the day and month (see Anton Pagi, *Critique of Baronius*, vol. II, at A.D. 198, no. VII, p. 90; Heinrich Noris, *On the Decennial Vows of the Emperors*, p. 91; S. Tillemont, *History of the Emperors*, vol. III, part I, note XIX on Severus, p. 406)—do not doubt the correctness of the year, and advise that Spartianus, who says this occurred in A.D. 200, in CARACALLA’S thirteenth year (*Life of Severus*, ch. XVI, p. 182), must be set aside. I do not trouble myself with the grounds on which they criticize him; I conjecture that perhaps he should be understood to refer not to the title Augustus, but to that of *Imperator*, which the soldiers bestowed upon ANTONINUS. I both embrace the position itself and acknowledge that it is exposed to no doubts.

It also lends excellent aid to our computation at this point. It is clear from the historians that this honor was paid to CARACALLA in the year following the battle of Lugdunum. Herodian teaches that it happened at Rome shortly [Col. 0553C] before the Parthian expedition (*History*, book III, ch. IX, p. 148); Spartianus, as they say, that it happened in the East. I pass over other arguments. Both positions agree on the year 198, when the emperor marched against the Parthians. If, therefore, in A.D. 198, when the Parthian war was already imminent, ANTONINUS was saluted as *Imperator*, it cannot in any way be denied that ALBINUS died in A.D. 197.

§ XIII.—I indeed think these arguments are so constituted as to demonstrate that the common position concerning ALBINUS’ defeat is absolutely certain. If the matter demanded it, I would be concerned to add others. I now think it better to inquire what moved those who preferred to leave the well-trodden path. Their arguments are slight; nevertheless, they must not be omitted.

Let Denis Pétau hold the first place. [Col. 0553D] He assigns the beginning of the siege of Byzantium to A.D. 195. For this reason it also became necessary, since that siege continued for three years, for him to say that A.D. 197 was the year in which SEVERUS left the East and A.D. 198 the year in which he defeated ALBINUS (*Doctrine of Times*, book XV, p. 693, Paris ed., 1627). The error is clear, provided only that it be observed that its source lies in his wrongly assigning the beginning of the siege of Byzantium to A.D. 195. He was impelled to do this by the following consideration: he believed that while NIGER was still alive, SEVERUS could not have devoted himself to an undertaking of such magnitude as this siege. The learned man could have learned the contrary from Dio, as I have already indicated. Let the beginning of the siege of Byzantium therefore be recalled, with Dio, to its true year, A.D. 194, and [Col. 0554A] the facts themselves will compel Pétau to become reconciled with us (compare Tillemont, *History of the Emperors*, vol. III, note XVI on Severus, p. 404).
