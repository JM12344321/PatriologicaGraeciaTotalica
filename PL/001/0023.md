But more consonant with the Gospel is the opinion [Col. 0192B] of those—and specifically of the more accurate Cassiodorus—who assign the year of the Passion to the consulship of Gnaeus Domitius, son of Lucius, grandson of Lucius, Ahenobarbus, and Aulus Vitellius, son of Publius, grandson of Quintus. All those named above who write that Christ suffered in the eighteenth year of Tiberius agree with them. Anyone desiring more should consult Paulus of Middelburg, Bishop of Fossombrone, in his thirty-three books *On the Year and Day of the Lord’s Passion*.

I shall add only this passage from St. Augustine, book II of *On Christian Doctrine*: “Ignorance of the consulship in which the Lord was born, or in which he suffered, compelled some to err by thinking that the Lord suffered at the age of forty-six, because the temple, which bears the image of the Lord’s body, was built over that many years.” Here perhaps he tacitly refers to St. Irenaeus, book II, chapters 39 and 40, who endeavors to prove that Christ suffered at approximately [Col. 0192C] the age of fifty, and at least after the fortieth year of his life, from John VIII: “You are not yet fifty years old.” And indeed it is likely that this ignorance concerning the consulship arose from divergent copies of the Consular Fasti. Epiphanius, *Heresy* 51, citing certain Fasti entirely different from those now extant, writes that the Savior suffered when Vinnicius and Cassius were consuls; according to the more accurate Fasti, however, their consulship fell only in the thirteenth year of Tiberius. The same is supported by that manuscript book of consulships on which Cuspinianus noted, in Cassiodorus, that the two Gemini were placed in the eighteenth year of Tiberius.

### 15. On God Being Corporeal, Because Nothing Is Incorporeal

Vol. II, *On Repentance*, chapter 3, no. 12. Vol. III, *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 35, no. 124; *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter [Col. 0192D] 2, nos. 97 and 98; book II *Against Marcion*, chapter 16, no. 100; and, most explicitly, *Against Praxeas*, chapter 7, no. 54.

Long ago St. Augustine, in passages to be cited immediately, St. Fulgentius, book II of *On Faith, to Peter*, and Gennadius, in the book *On Ecclesiastical Dogmas*, attributed this error to Tertullian. But St. Augustine excuses him in these words, in book X of *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, final chapter:

“Fearing that a thing would be nothing if it were not a body, he was unable to think differently even about God. Yet although he is indeed acute—for so I read, instead of ‘since’—he is sometimes overcome by the truth when it appears, contrary to his own opinion. For what could he have said more truly than what he says in a certain passage”—namely, *On the Soul*, chapter 7, no. 98—“‘Everything corporeal is passible’? He ought therefore to have changed the opinion by which he had said elsewhere”—namely, [Col. 0193A] in *Against Praxeas*—“that even God is a body. For I do not think he was so foolish as to believe that the divine nature too was passible, with the result that Christ should now be believed passible and mutable not merely in the flesh, nor in flesh and soul, but in the very Word through whom all things were made—an idea far removed from the Christian heart.”

And again in the same place, in words also cited above: “But these things are to be trembled at, not laughed at. For he would never have been driven to them if he could have conceived of something that both exists and is not a body.”

Again, in *On Heresies, to Quodvultdeus*, heresy 86:

“He also says that God himself is corporeal, though not shaped”—where he appears to allude to book II *Against Marcion*. “Yet he is not therefore believed to have become a heretic. For it might somehow be thought that he called the very divine nature and substance a body—not the kind of body [Col. 0193B] whose parts must or can be conceived as some larger and some smaller, as are all things that we properly call bodies, although he entertains some such opinion concerning the soul; but, as I have said, it might be thought that he therefore called God a body because God is not nothing, is not emptiness, is not a quality of body or soul, but is everywhere the same, is not divided by any spatial extension, and yet remains immutably in his own nature and substance.”

Certainly the Author himself explains his meaning sufficiently. Thus he says in *Against Hermogenes*: “Granting that something is incorporeal, I speak only of substances, since substance itself is the body of each thing”; and in *On the Soul*, chapter 7, no. 96: “For it is nothing if it is not a body.” Likewise, in *On the Flesh of Christ*: “But since it exists, it must have something through which it exists. If it has something [Col. 0193C] through which it exists, this will be its body. Everything that exists is a body of its own kind; nothing is incorporeal except what does not exist.” Finally, in book II *Against Marcion*: “For,” he says, “we also read of God’s right hand, eyes, and feet; yet they will not therefore be compared with human ones merely because they share the same appellation. How great will be the difference between the divine body and the human body beneath the same names of limbs,” etc.

All the other Fathers, however, teach that God is incorporeal, and class the contrary assertion among the heresies, as is evident from Philastrius, St. Augustine, Epiphanius, Theodoret, Damascene, Isidore, and Rabanus in their catalogues of heretics, when they mention the Audians, or Anthropomorphites.

### 16. On the Wrath of God

Vol. I, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, chapter 2, no. 7. Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chapter 16, no. 211; [Col. 0193D] *Against Marcion*, book I, chapter 26, no. 181; book II, chapter 10, no. 94; book IV, chapter 15, no. 195, and chapter 31, no. 507.

At first sight Tertullian does indeed appear to attribute wrath to God in the passages cited, but almost everywhere he interprets it as God’s vengeance or retribution. And in truth he had to insist on this so often, not only against the Gentiles, as in the first passage, but especially against Marcion, who asserted that God was merely good, and not an avenger or judge. Hence Tertullian disputes against him at very great length and proves the contrary from the Scriptures, repeatedly employing passages that mention God’s wrath and thus his vengeance, especially in book I *Against Marcion*, chapters 25, 26, 27, and 28, and book II, chapters 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, and 16.

There is indeed one passage where [Col. 0194A] he appears to acknowledge emotions in God; but besides the fact that there he interprets “to be jealous” and “to be angry” as “to condemn” and “to afflict,” in the final passage of all he explains himself more fully, taking “God to be moved” and “motions in God” to mean wrath. Finally, in the said chapter 16 of book II he explains himself completely in these words: “Whether wrath, or jealousy, or severity, all these are due to sternness, just as sternness is due to justice.” Then, after citing Marcion’s objection—“If God is angry and jealous, and is elated and provoked, then he will also be corrupted, and therefore he will die”—he answers very aptly:

“Most foolish are those who predicate divine things from human ones, so that, because passions of this kind are found in man, who is of a corruptible condition, they therefore suppose them to have the same condition in God. Distinguish the substances and assign to them their respective affections, as diverse as the substances [Col. 0194B] require, even though they appear to share the same terms. For we also read of God’s right hand, eyes, and feet; yet they will not therefore be compared with human ones merely because they share the same appellation.”

### 17. That the Church Exists in Three Persons, Even Laypersons

Vol. V, *On the Exhortation to Chastity*, chapter 7, no. 40, and *On Modesty*, chapter 21, no. 191.

Tertullian had spoken properly in *On Repentance*, chapter 10, no. 60, since this was before every heresy: “In one and another the Church exists.” For there he was alluding to Matthew XVIII: “Where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I in the midst of them,” having shortly before premised: “Tell the Church.” Yet all the ancients interpret this of the rulers of the Church, [Col. 0194C] the bishops, not of laypeople; so too does Tertullian himself in the same passage, where he speaks of the priests before whom public confession was made. Entirely catholic too is the statement in *On Baptism*, chapter 6, no. 47: “Where the three are—that is, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit—there is the Church, because it is the body of the three.”

But the following statement in *On the Exhortation to Chastity* belongs to his heresy, as we also noted in his *Life*: “But even where three are, there is the Church, though they be laypeople. For each person lives by his own faith.” This is especially so because he repeats precisely the same thing in almost the same words in *On Modesty*. From that passage it is gathered that he had reached such a degree of madness as to think that the Church existed in three persons—namely Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla. To the same point belongs the fact that he also denies there that the Church is a number of bishops.

But we demonstrated the contrary at length in our Notes [Col. 0194D] on the former passage, even from Tertullian himself while he was still a catholic, and likewise against the Magdeburg Centuriators, who inferred from it that the Church exists among a few. Anyone desiring more should read all the books written by St. Augustine against the Donatists. He gathered together Scriptures from every quarter, from both the Old and New Testaments, establishing that the Church is not in one place alone in the world—Africa, for example—but is dispersed throughout the entire world, in accordance both with the prophecies of the Prophets and with the testimonies of the Apostles, which are clearer than the sun. To him we refer the Reader, lest we be more prolix here. We shall add only this: it pertains to the same heresy of Tertullian that he says in volume II, *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 4, no. 47: “Or do you not think that every believer is permitted to conceive and establish something, provided only that it accords with God?”

[Col. 0195A] ### 18. On the Ecstasy, or Madness, or Prophetic Spirit, of Montanus and of the Insane Prophetesses Priscilla and Maximilla, and Similar Persons

Vol. II, *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 1, no. 12. Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chapter 9, no. 111; chapter 45, no. 488; chapter 47, no. 543; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 11, no. 98, and the final chapter, no. 436; *Against Marcion*, book IV, chapter 22, no. 325, and book V, chapter 8, nos. 135 and 136; *Against Praxeas*, chapter 1, nos. 7, 8, and 10, and chapter 15, no. 93, and chapter 30, no. 183. Finally, in the fragments of the seven books *On Ecstasy*.

Long ago St. Pacian, St. Jerome, and Nicephorus charged Tertullian with this heresy in the passages cited in connection with the fragments of his books *On Ecstasy*, volume IV. There, from Jerome and Epiphanius themselves, we showed that the ecstasy in Adam [Col. 0195B] (Genesis II) was a deep sleep, not a displacement of the mind; and that the ecstasy in the Apostles (Matthew XVIII and Luke IX)—Peter, John, and James—was not madness, but an ecstasy of wonder, nor was it joined with any prophecy.

Next, from the Apostle (1 Corinthians XIV), however much the Author draws the contrary conclusion by wrongly adding the clause “in ecstasy,” it is established that the spirits of the Prophets are subject to the Prophets. For, according to the words of Jerome cited there, and Origen, Homily 6 on Ezekiel, prophecy is called a vision, which the Prophets therefore understood; for this reason they were called Seers. They did not, like madmen, speak involuntarily or produce sound without the mind. From this it may be gathered that what existed in Montanus and his false prophetesses was truly madness, not prophecy [Col. 0195C]; and thus, by God’s ordering, it came about that by this very designation the Author contradicted himself.

Before them, Apollinarius, Bishop of Hierapolis, beautifully established the same point against Montanus, as cited by Eusebius, book V, chapter 15. He writes that they were seized by a certain evil spirit and suddenly convulsed by frenzy and insanity of mind—which they called ecstasy—and began to utter strange sounds and to prophesy, contrary to what was required by the Church’s custom, handed down both by tradition and by succession from ancient times, from the age of the Apostles down to that day. He also cites—in Eusebius, the following chapter—the book of Miltiades against the same man, in which Miltiades shows that no one should believe any Prophet to have been accustomed to prophesy when struck by frenzy. For, as Eusebius quotes him, “they will not be able to allege any Prophet of the Old or New Testament [Col. 0195D] who was ever convulsed in this manner by an insane spirit—through ecstasy. Nor will they be able to boast concerning Agabus, Judas, Silas, Philip’s daughters (Acts XI, XV, XXI), Ammia of Philadelphia, Quadratus”—whom Jerome mentions in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*—“or any others besides, that they ever prophesied in that respect after the frenzied manner of these people.”

He afterward indicates that he had listed their names because they boasted that the women of Montanus had immediately succeeded all of them in the prophetic office. In the same manner, according to Jerome in the passage already cited, Tertullian, in the seven books *On Ecstasy*—the seventh of which he wrote against Apollonius—in confirmation of the same false prophecy of those women, wrote [Col. 0196A] that Melito was regarded as a prophet by his party, thus tacitly insinuating a kind of succession of prophecy as though it were genuine.

Finally, Apollonius, in his book against the Cataphrygians—as Eusebius records in the same book, chapter 17—testifies that when Zoticus, Bishop of Comana, came to Pepuza, where Maximilla was pretending to prophesy, he detected an insane spirit in her. Similarly Serapion, Bishop of Antioch—in the same work, chapter 18—says that blessed Sotas at Anchialus wished to cast the demon out of Priscilla, but those crafty hypocrites by no means permitted it.

Moreover, it does nothing for the Author’s case that in book V *Against Marcion* he contends that women too possess the right to prophesy, on the ground that in 1 Corinthians XI the Apostle imposes a veil even upon a woman who prophesies. For it has already been proved that [Col. 0196B] in those insane prophetesses Priscilla and Maximilla there was not true prophecy, but madness of mind. I pass over the fact that Tertullian himself, while still a catholic, attacks this practice in *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter XLI, in these words: “How brazen are the heretical women themselves, who dare to teach, to dispute, to perform exorcisms, to promise cures, perhaps even to baptize”—as we demonstrated there at greater length, nos. 260, 261, 262, and 263—and in *On Baptism Against Quintilla*, herself a woman of the Montanist faction, no. 2.

### 19. That Christ the Son of God Was Always Seen by Human Beings in Real, Though Unborn, Flesh

Vol. I, *Against the Jews*, chapter 9, nos. 114 and 115. Vol. III, *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 6, no. 58; *Against Marcion*, book II, chapter 27, no. 187; book III, chapter 6, no. 39, chapter 9, no. 65; book V, chapter 19, no. 296; *Against Praxeas*, chapter 14, nos. 86 and 87, chapter [Col. 0196C] 16, nos. 96, 98, 100, and 101; and volume IV, the metrical book V *Against Marcion*, chapter 5, no. 8.

What Tertullian writes—that the Son of God, not the Father, was seen or heard by human beings whenever Scripture records such a thing—does not seem paradoxical. He refers, namely, to when God appeared to Adam, Genesis III; Noah, Genesis IX; Abraham, Genesis XII, XVII, and XVIII; Lot, Genesis XIX; Hagar, Genesis XXII; Jacob, Genesis XXIX, XXXII, and XXXV; and Moses, Exodus III and following, XXXII, and XXXIII. He derived this almost word for word from St. Justin Martyr’s *Dialogue Against Trypho*, and in part from St. Irenaeus, book III, chapter 6, and book IV, chapter 23. They were followed by Novatian, *On the Trinity*, chapters 25, 26, and 27; Blessed Cyprian, book II of the *Testimonies Against the Jews*, chapters 5 and 6; and from these passages they proved [Col. 0196D] that Christ was called not only man or angel but also God. St. Ambrose in the preface to Luke, St. Epiphanius in the *Ancoratus*, Prudentius against the Anthropomorphites, and Arnobius on Psalm CIV do not teach otherwise.

They seem to have been led to this conclusion both by the words, “No one shall ever see God the Father”—as the Author reads—“and live,” Exodus XXXIII, and similar words in John VI, “Not that anyone has seen the Father, except him who is from God”; and, conversely, by Christ’s words in John VIII: “Abraham rejoiced that he might see my day; he saw it and was glad.”

Nevertheless, St. Augustine, in book III *Against Maximinus*, chapter 26, and book II *On the Trinity*, chapters 10–17, preferred to assert that God the Father and the entire Trinity were seen in the aforesaid passages through created things placed at their service, lest he agree with the Arian Maximinus. It was not enough for Maximinus to interpret those apparitions [Col. 0197A] as referring to Christ; he also endeavored to infer from them that Christ appeared to human sight through the substance of his divinity. The Author was certainly far removed from this heresy, however much some charge him with it.

But this is paradoxical and peculiar to Tertullian: in several passages he says that Christ was seen in true and solid flesh, although flesh not yet born. He did so through zeal not according to knowledge, in order to prove all the more strongly, against Marcion and his other followers, that the flesh of Christ which he afterward received from the Virgin was not phantasmal but true and solid, if he could be said to have been seen long before in real flesh.

Elsewhere, certainly, in *Against Praxeas*, following the opinion of St. Justin Martyr, who affirms that these apparitions were figurative, he explains himself:

“For we say,” he states in the former passage, “that the Son, under his own designation, [Col. 0197B] was invisible insofar as he is the Word”—which Maximinus nevertheless denied—“but was visible before the flesh in the manner in which he says to Aaron and Miriam (Numbers XII): ‘If there shall be a prophet among you, I shall be known to him in a vision, and in a dream I shall speak to him.’”

After citing the words of Exodus III, “Mouth to mouth I shall speak to him in visible form,” he adds:

“Therefore, when he reserves for Moses the sight of himself and conversation face to face for the future—for this was afterward fulfilled in the retirement upon the mountain, as we read in the Gospel, Luke IX, where Moses was seen conversing with him—it appears that previously God, that is, the Son of God, had always been seen in a mirror, and in an enigma, and in a vision and a dream, both by the Prophets and Patriarchs and by Moses himself up to that point.”

Nor does he speak very differently in the latter passage: “For he himself always descended to converse with human beings, from Adam down to the Patriarchs and Prophets, in [Col. 0197C] a vision, in a dream, in a mirror, and in an enigma.”

St. Augustine, however, in the passages already cited, prefers the view that Christ was indeed prefigured by these visions, but that God appeared through angels only figuratively. He proves this from Hosea XII, where the one who wrestled with Jacob is said to have been an angel, and Hebrews XIII, where certain persons are said unknowingly to have received angels as guests. To these may be added the fact that the God whom Moses said had appeared to him in the bush and on Sinai is testified by St. Stephen in Acts VII to have been an angel.

### 20. On Flight During Persecution Being Unlawful

Vol. II, book I *To His Wife*, chapter 3, no. 17; *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 1, no. 18; and volume V, the complete book on this subject.

We have refuted this error of Tertullian’s—which he expounds at length in the book [Col. 0197D] *On Flight in Persecution*, written, according to St. Jerome, Sophronius, and Nicephorus, against the Church—so extensively in our Notes on that work that there is no need for many words here. See there the antidote we appended to the end of the argument, drawn from the Author himself while still a catholic, volume II, *On Patience*, chapter 13, no. 88; from several passages of St. Cyprian; from St. Athanasius’s *Apology for His Flight*; and from St. Augustine, Letter 180 to Honoratus. From these we also answered almost every opposing argument, especially those in chapter 4, no. 35; chapter 5, no. 39; chapter 6, nos. 43, 47, and 49; chapter 7, no. 54; chapter 8, nos. 55, 57, and 58; chapter 9, no. 60; and chapter 18, no. 75.

The Author’s words cited are these: “The patience of the flesh fights in persecutions. If flight presses upon it, [Col. 0198A] the flesh serves as a soldier amid the hardships of flight; if prison overtakes it as well, amid that deprivation of light there is patience with the world.”

What of the fact that, from chapter 11 of the aforesaid book, he seems chiefly to condemn the flight of the superiors of the Church? For, according also to St. Athanasius’s letter to Dracontius and the cited passage of St. Augustine, they are not permitted to flee unless their flock flees, or does not seek its superior—that is, does not need his ministry—when the persecution is directed personally, so to speak, against bishops or pastors. Yet St. Athanasius demonstrates at length in his aforementioned *Apology*, both from many examples and from Scripture, that even in such a case they may flee. To it we refer the Reader, lest we be more prolix here.

Nor does it do anything for the Author’s case that Christ invites men to suffer, the contrary of flight: “Whoever is ashamed of me, of him shall I also be ashamed” (Mark VI), however much [Col. 0198B] in the *Scorpiace Against the Gnostics* he appears to infer from this that martyrdoms are commanded. For Tertullian acknowledges that this too is equally commanded: “When they begin to persecute you, flee from city to city.” Neither passage appears to be a command except according to persons, times, and causes: the latter, in a time of persecution on account of faith in Christ, for those whom the care of souls permits to be absent; the former, if we are apprehended, even in the very course of flight. Furthermore, since both are contained in the same chapter of Matthew, each must receive a sound interpretation, lest the Evangelist appear to contradict himself.

### 21. On Job Having Suffered the Voluntary Loss of His Children, Because They Were Not Restored to Him

Vol. II, *On Patience*, chapter [Col. 0198C] 14, no. 98.

It appears that the copies of the book of Job which Tertullian had seen lacked the statement, found in the Hebrew, Chaldean, Greek, and Latin copies, that seven sons and three daughters were restored to him. From this, I think, it came about that he followed this peculiar paradox, which I have been unable to find in any of the Ancients. It would certainly be superfluous to refute it at greater length, since Origen, who lived at approximately the same time, indicates in Homily 6 on Ezekiel that he had read this, as do all the Fathers after him, ancient, medieval, and more recent.

The Author would agree with them if he had said only that Job possessed twice over the other things he had lost, except his children, since he received only seven, and thus precisely as many as before. [Col. 0198D] Philip the Presbyter, St. Gregory the Pope, Bede, and Rupert, in their commentaries on Job, therefore interpret this to mean that the seven former children were considered still to be living with God.

### 22. On the Virgin Mary Having Given Birth to Christ by the Common Law of the Body’s Opening

Vol. III, *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 4, no. 31; chapter 20, no. 155; chapter 23, nos. 174 and 175; *Against Marcion*, book III, chapter 11, no. 78; book IV, chapter 21, no. 311; and book V, chapter 19, no. 304.

It is sufficiently clear that Tertullian wrote something of this kind, as did Origen, Homily 14 on Luke, and St. Ambrose on the same Gospel. Yet they may be understood, in accordance with St. Thomas Aquinas’s interpretation somewhere, to refer to such an opening [Col. 0199A] of the womb as did not violate the enclosure of virginity. We too noted this regarding Luke II, cited in his own support by the Author: “Every male opening the womb,” in the aforesaid *On the Flesh of Christ*, no. 175. In support of this we adduced the testimonies of a certain Greek interpreter and of St. Jerome, who acknowledge that Christ supernaturally opened the virginal womb, which had not yet been opened, and naturally preserved its closure, according to Ezekiel’s prophecy concerning the closed eastern gate through which the High Priest alone goes forth, yet which nevertheless remains closed forever.

To this also pertains St. Ambrose’s comparison in Letter 81, where he writes that Christ came forth from the Virgin’s womb just as waters flowed from the rock. Finally, there is the Author’s statement in the second passage cited from the aforesaid book: “What was novel in Christ’s birth [Col. 0199B] from a Virgin is clear: namely, solely that it was from a virgin.” He thereby signifies, both there and in the other passages cited here, that he pressed this point so far in order to prove Christ’s true birth from the Virgin against Marcion, who called it phantasmal. Later, however, when that heresy had been sufficiently refuted, the Fathers preferred to define that the Virgin Mother of God gave birth incorruptibly, with her womb closed.

### 23. On the Virgin Mary Marrying Once After Giving Birth

Vol. II, *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 6, no. 48. Vol. III, *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 23, no. 174; and volume V, *On Monogamy*, chapter 8, no. 55.

The Author does indeed appear to fall into this heresy rather obscurely, so that his words might in some manner admit a sound [Col. 0199C] sense. Yet since St. Jerome, when the heretic Helvidius cited Tertullian in his own support, does not deny that he wrote something of the kind, I too did not think Tertullian should be excused from it. It would be enough to reply with what Blessed Jerome says of Tertullian: “I say nothing except that he was not a man of the Church.”

But because he was likely misled by the same words of the Evangelists that misled Helvidius—Matthew XII, Luke VIII, and John VII, concerning the Lord’s brothers, whom in the aforementioned *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 7, no. 67, he asserts to have been true brothers, just as Mary was truly Christ’s mother, in order thereby to prove the truth of Christ’s flesh all the more—something must be added concerning the meaning of this expression.

Origen, Hilary, Euthymius, and Theophylact on Matthew; [Col. 0199D] Eusebius, book II of the *Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 1; St. Epiphanius, book III of the *Panarion Against the Antidicomarianites*; St. Ambrose and Oecumenius in their commentaries on Galatians II, think that those called the Lord’s brothers were Joseph’s sons by Mary of Cleophas, the widow of his brother, by whom he had six children: James, Joseph, Simon, Judas, and their brothers. This, according even to Origen’s testimony, followed the Gospel bearing the title of Peter or James.

But St. Jerome speaks more correctly in his commentary:

“Some suspect that the Lord’s brothers were Joseph’s sons by another wife, following the ravings of the apocryphal writings and inventing a certain petty woman named Escha. We, however,” he says, “as is contained in the book we wrote against Helvidius, understand the Lord’s brothers to be not Joseph’s sons but the Savior’s cousins, the children of Mary, the Lord’s maternal aunt, who is said to have been [Col. 0200A] the mother of James the Less, Joseph, and Judas, whom we read in the Gospel to have been called the Lord’s brothers; for all Scripture demonstrates that cousins are called brothers.”

Here he alludes both to the Scriptures concerning Lot, the brother of Abraham, Genesis XIII, and to Matthew XVII, where Mary, the mother of James and Joses, or Joseph, is said to have stood by the cross. Euthymius rightly noted that this cannot be understood of the Lord’s mother. Furthermore, see the ecclesiastical definition on this matter in St. Fulgentius, *On Faith, to Peter*, among the works of Blessed Augustine.

### 21. On the Thousand Years After the Resurrection of Bodies

*On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 25, no. 203; *Against Marcion*, book I, chapter 29, no. 203; book III, chapter 24, no. 191 and following; book IV, chapter 29, no. 471; and volume IV, [Col. 0200B] in the fragments of the book *On the Hope of the Faithful*.

What Tertullian thought on this subject I believe I have sufficiently set forth in volume IV, in connection with the fragments of the book *On the Hope of the Faithful*, from his own words: namely, that a kingdom of Christ is promised to us on earth before heaven, after the first resurrection, but in such a manner that in it the saints are refreshed with an abundance of all spiritual goods.

Although erroneous, Blessed Augustine nevertheless admits this opinion to be tolerable in some measure, in book XX of *The City of God*. I think this is because it may also be found in St. Irenaeus, book V *On Heresies*, chapters 32–35, in St. Justin Martyr’s *Dialogue Against Trypho*, and in Melito, who in this matter followed Papias, at least according to St. Jerome. They are also joined by Apollinarius, who vainly attempted to attack the books of Dionysius of Alexandria against Nepos; [Col. 0200C] Lactantius, book II, chapter 13, and book VII, chapter 24; Victorinus on the Apocalypse, before his work was corrected by St. Jerome; and, on Jerome’s testimony, Severus in the dialogue *Gallus, on the Life of St. Martin*.

Gennadius, in *On Ecclesiastical Dogmas*, nevertheless charges them—as he does Tertullian, but apparently incorrectly—with believing that the saints would enjoy the delights of drink and food. It is certainly evident that the heresy of Cerinthus, Marcus, and Nepos—whom Gennadius himself discusses in the same place, as does Eusebius in book III, chapter 22, and book VII, chapter 20, and, after Eusebius, Nicephorus somewhere—was very different, as was that of the other Chiliasts or Millenarians, against whom St. Epiphanius writes in *Heresy* 76. For they—like certain Anabaptists of the present day—expected even marital intercourse in that kingdom; indeed, Cerinthus and Marcus expected every kind of lustful indulgence. And, as [Col. 0200D] St. Jerome states, in accordance with the Jews’ δευτερώσεις (“secondary traditions”), they expected the disgrace of circumcision, the restoration of the temple, sacrifices of bulls and rams, Sabbath rest, the servitude of all nations, and consequently wars, armies, triumphs, the slaughter of the vanquished, and the death of a hundred-year-old sinner.

Moreover, because they based all these things upon the passage in the Apocalypse of St. John the Apostle—for all those writers attribute it to him—in chapter XX, “This is the first resurrection; blessed is the holy person who has a part in the first resurrection; in these shall be priests of the Lord and of Christ, and they shall reign with him for a thousand years,” something must be added here concerning the meaning of that passage.

Blessed Jerome, in the preface to book XVIII on Isaiah, says that the Apocalypse must be expounded not according to the letter, as the Judaizers do, but spiritually, as it was written. He does this himself in express words [Col. 0201A] in Victorinus’s manuscript commentary on the Apocalypse as corrected by him, following, I think, Dionysius of Alexandria in the aforementioned books against Nepos. St. Augustine, more explicitly, in *The City of God*, book XX, chapters 7, 8, and 9, interprets the first resurrection as the resurrection of souls, which is also recognized in Scripture, and the second as that of bodies. He interprets the thousand years either as the time of grace, which will remain until the end of the age, according to that mode of speech by which a part is signified by the whole, or as all the years of this age, so that the fullness of time itself is designated by a perfect number. He is followed by the commentary attributed to Ambrose, Primasius, Andrew, and Bede in their commentaries on the Apocalypse.

Nor does what is read in Apocalypse XXI concerning the city of Jerusalem descending to the new earth support them. For the Apostle John himself indicates that he is speaking not of an earthly Jerusalem when he adds: “And [Col. 0201B] I saw no temple in it”—although some of them, as stated above, believed that the temple too would be restored—“for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple.” Accordingly, the other Fathers interpret it more correctly of the Church, both as militant in this age, whose foundations are the twelve Apostles, and as afterward reigning in heaven.

The same applies to Galatians IV concerning the free Jerusalem, which is our mother, where there is a contradiction in the very addition “which is above”; and also to Philippians III, “our citizenship (πολίτευμα) is in heaven,” and consequently not on earth. It is therefore remarkable that these passages should be cited by our Author in favor of the aforesaid opinion.

The passages in Isaiah LXV and LXVI, Jeremiah XVI and XXXI, and finally Ezekiel XX, XXVII, and XXVIII, concerning the [Col. 0201C] restoration of Judea—to which the Author alludes only in these few words—seemed to support him more strongly. They are explicitly cited partly by St. Irenaeus and partly by St. Justin Martyr. But Jerome, in his commentaries, beautifully interprets all these things allegorically of the Church confessing Christ and of her mysteries, everywhere ridiculing those fables of the thousand years. For brevity’s sake we refer the Reader to him.

The response is easier regarding Genesis XIII and XV, which the Author, following Irenaeus, cites in his own support, concerning the first and second promises made to Abraham’s seed: first that it would be as numerous as the sand, and then also like the stars, as though these were the beginnings of an earthly and a heavenly dispensation. For it is established that the first promise was fulfilled by the multiplication of the children of Israel in the land of the Canaanites, or Palestine; while the Apostle [Col. 0201D] Paul interprets the second of the Church of the Gentiles in Romans IX and Galatians III. In the same manner our Rabanus also distinguished these two promises in his commentary on Genesis.

No less easy is the response regarding Genesis XXVII, where the first promise to Jacob—whose figure we know Christians from among the Gentiles to bear—is of heavenly dew, and the second of the richness of the earth. For Jacob himself interprets that blessing as earthly in the words he afterward addressed to Esau: “With grain, wine, and oil I have established him.” And if we wish to understand it allegorically, Procopius, in his commentary, interprets the heavenly dew as what is instilled into the souls of the devout, and the richness of the earth as that possessed by the earth which receives seed and renders it back a hundredfold.

Finally, the response is easiest regarding Luke XII: “Seek first the kingdom of God, and the remaining things shall be added to you.” [Col. 0202A] For the beginning of Christ’s discourse makes it clear that Christ is speaking of this life, not that future life after the resurrection: “Therefore I say to you: Do not be anxious for your soul about food, or for your body about clothing.”

Furthermore, we noted in connection with *On the Soul*, chapter 30, no. 376, that this error of the Millenarians was derived partly from Plato’s dialogue *On the Republic* and from the *Phaedrus*. Since the Author there regards such things as fables, it is remarkable that he afterward fell into the same error. But, as St. Jerome demonstrates sufficiently, he followed Papias, Melito, and the other aforementioned Authors, and especially St. Irenaeus.
