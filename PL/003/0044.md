And although. I change nothing in a sound passage. In chapter XI Caecilius could not stomach the Christians’ promising themselves perpetual life after death—and this as the just—while promising all others, as unjust, eternal punishment. Octavius replies to this: (1) the Gentiles deserve eternal punishment precisely as Gentiles, because ignorance of God is sufficient for punishment, even if in other respects one’s life is conducted honorably; Christians, however, are rendered worthy of pardon precisely as Christians, because knowledge of God conduces to pardon even if one’s life is not entirely without fault; (2) the Gentiles also deserve eternal punishment as criminals, whereas Christians deserve eternal life because they are far better than the Gentiles. Meursius emends: “And although ignorance of God may be sufficient for punishment, knowledge may conduce to pardon.” Heumann [Col. 0349D], however, reads: “And even if” (that is, “even supposing that”) “ignorance of God is sufficient for pardon, and knowledge conduces to punishment; nevertheless”; and he illustrates this from Luke 12:47. But I know of no one who has said “conduces to punishment.” LINDNER.

Although among some. That is, although some of our people observe our discipline less carefully. HERALDUS.—I read: “our discipline falls short of what is just.” Relevant here is that statement of Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 46: “But someone will say that even among our people some depart from the rule of discipline.” HEUMANN.

We are born husbands. Davisius and Jacob Gronovius rightly defend this verb against Meursius (I believe he meant to write Wowerius), who was eager to substitute “we are known.” Recently, however, Arntzenius, on chapter 6, page 34 of Pliny’s *Panegyric*, following Pricaeus as his guide, maintained that “we are known” should be restored. He evidently believes that, if Minucius had written “we are born,” it would follow that he denied original sin and believed that we were already Christians when we came forth from our mother’s womb. Whoever wishes may judge this judgment. For my part, I am not so sharp-sighted as to perceive the force of this argument. That Minucius certainly wrote “we are born” may be shown by Tertullian, whom he so frequently imitates, writing thus in *Apology*, chapter 46: “For his own wife alone, a Christian is born a male.” HEUMANN.—Pricaeus adduces these words in connection with Luke 1:34, where Mary uses the verb γινώσκω (*ginōskō*, “know sexually”); he therefore judged that Minucius, treating almost the same subject, ought to have written “we are known,” as he confidently alleges. This is by no means probable. Octavius is alluding to 1 Timothy 3:2: μιᾶς γυναικὸς ἄνδρα (*mias gynaikos andra*), “the husband of one wife.” GRONOVIUS.

Or a deserter. Here “deserter” does not mean one who crosses over from profane rites to Christianity; for in that sense all Christians could in some fashion be called deserters, since before professing allegiance to the true religion they were profane. Rather, it means ἀποστάται (*apostatai*, “apostates”), who, impatient of the yoke imposed by Christ, have relapsed into their former sins and by that very fact have ceased to be Christians. LINDNER.]

**SUMMARY.—Octavius then shows that just and devout persons are to be endowed with everlasting happiness, while the unjust are to be afflicted with eternal punishments. He next demonstrates clearly that the morals of Christians are far holier than those of the pagans.**

And yet men are warned by the writings of the most learned [printed text: “books”] and by the songs of the poets about that fiery river and the heat repeatedly encircling the Stygian marsh, [Col. 0348B] which they have handed down as prepared for eternal torments, having learned this both from the disclosures of demons and from the oracles of the Prophets. And for that reason even their king Jupiter himself swears solemnly by the burning banks and the black abyss; for, foreknowing it, he shudders at the punishment appointed for himself together with his worshipers. Nor is there any measure or end to those torments. There an intelligent fire burns the limbs and restores them; it consumes and nourishes them, just as the fires of lightning strike bodies [Col. 0349A] but do not consume them; just as the fires of Mount Henna and Mount Lesui [printed: Mount Etna and Mount Vesuvius] and of burning lands everywhere blaze yet are not exhausted: so that penal conflagration is not fed by the destruction of those who burn, but is sustained by the unconsumed laceration of their bodies.

No one but a profane person questions that those who do not know God are deservedly tormented as impious and unjust, since it is no lesser a crime to be ignorant of the Parent of all and Lord of all than to injure him. And although ignorance of God is sufficient for punishment, just as knowledge conduces [53] to pardon, nevertheless, if Christians are compared with you, although in some of us our discipline falls short, we shall still be found far better than you. For you forbid adulteries and commit them; we are born husbands solely for our own wives. You punish crimes [Col. 0349B] once committed; among us even to contemplate one is to sin. You fear accomplices; we fear even conscience [Col. 0350A] alone, without which we cannot exist. Finally, the prison seethes with people from your number; no Christian is there unless he is either accused because of his religion or has deserted it.

## CHAPTER XXXVI

[Note: [Col. 0350C]

**CHAP. XXXVI.—May it be the lot of fortune.** The manuscript reading makes no sense, and therefore I did not hesitate to replace the singular *sit* with the plural *sint*. Fortunes, that is, are the work of lot. Phil.: *On the Creation of a Prince*: “Lot concerns happiness, not virtue. Fortunes and rank are not judged, because they belong to lot; a person’s mind and actions are judged, because they are free.” Gronovius proposes: “Let lots belong to Fortune.” Meursius reads: “of Strong Fortune,” because Chance-Fortune was a goddess distinct from Fortune, had a temple across the Tiber, and was worshiped by those who sustained life without any trade. Heumann reads: “Let the lot belong to fortune,” that is: “I grant that lot is the work of fortune and that to fortune it is owed that one person is born in an illustrious station and amid great wealth, another lowly and poor.” LINDNER.—“Let the outcome belong to the lot of Fortune.” So reads the manuscript, and far better than those who read “of Strong Fortune.” For this is no place for that Roman goddess. Minucius is now speaking from the perspective of those who assigned to each person his own fortune on the basis of ἀποτελεσματική (*apotelesmatikē*, “astrological”), or judicial, astrology, by which the whole course of human life was supposedly governed. This was called the Lot of Fortune, or ὁ κλῆρος τῆς τύχης (*ho klēros tēs tychēs*), “the lot of fortune.” On the Lot of Fortune, see Scaliger on Book II of Manilius’ *Astronomica*; likewise Selden, *On the Syrian Gods*, treatise I, chapter 1, and Salmasius, *On Climacteric Years*. OUZELIUS.

[Col. 0350D] **Rank is not judged.** I do not understand what rank has to do with this. Minucius is arguing against astrologers, who maintained that the fates of persons at birth had been fixed by the various aspects and conjunctions of the planets. I restore what long ago pleased the distinguished Ferrerius: “Nativity is not judged.” Shortly afterward: “In us it is not birth that is punished, but the nature of our disposition.” See Petrus Faber, *Semestria*, Book III, chapter 33. DAVISIUS.

**Matter.** It is quite evident that “the future” ought to be written. HEUMANN.

[Col. 0351B] **Birth.** By which one person is noble or ignoble, rich or poor, and so forth. LINDNER.

**More truly and fully.** In the manuscript it is written *verius*, which represents *uverius* for *uberius* (“more abundantly”), just as in chapter 21 *veribus* stands for *uberibus*. [Col. 0351C] RIGALTIUS.—See the notes on chapter 19: “more nearly and more truly.” LINDNER.—Minucius, or Octavius, was permitted to proceed thus in this part, since Caecilius had dismissed these matters in so cursory a fashion. Chapter 11: “Many points are available for this, were the discourse not hastening onward.” GRONOVIUS.

**Who can.** In place of “how can.” The Roman edition wrongly reads: “who is able?” HEUMANN.—So too the manuscript. EDITORS.

**And pastures are fed from day to day.** I correct this to “pastures [feminine] from day to day are fed.” The use of *pascua* in the feminine gender was not unusual in that age. The glosses give: “Pasture, Νομή. Νομαί.” Arnobius on Psalm 22: “This, then, is the voice of the perfect which the Church received from the Lord’s Passion. Placed in a place of pasture, raised beside the water of refreshment.” Likewise on Psalm 106: “Appointing shepherds and pastures for them.” MEURSIUS.—“Livestock is fed.” The manuscripts read: “pastures are fed.” I think *pecua*, that is, “livestock,” was written. Prudentius in the *Passion of Romanus*:

> Draft animals, livestock, beasts beneath the yoke.

HERALDUS.—The manuscript reads “pastures are fed,” from which [Col. 0351D] Desiderius Heraldus extracted the received reading and, as it seems to me, quite correctly; for the ancients said *pecua* and *pecuda* just as they said *pecora* (“livestock”), as Nonius Marcellus shows by examples in chapter 2. Hence Cato in Gellius, *Attic Nights*, Book VII, chapter 3: “If anyone should wish to possess a larger number of livestock.” Livy, Book XXXV, chapter 20: “As the fields were flooded everywhere, livestock was swept away.” See also Prudentius, *Peristephanon*, Hymn X, 333. DAVISIUS.

**All of which.** Perizonius correctly restored “who possesses all things.” Minucius seems to have had before his eyes Paul’s statement in 2 Corinthians 6:10: “As having nothing and yet possessing all things.” HEUMANN.—The forcefulness in the words “all of which” ought not to be altered: it is as though someone were saying to us, “All these things that you see are yours,” a formula used by Apuleius, Book II of the *Milesian Tales*. GRONOVIUS.

**The defects of a human body.** This is equivalent to “the defects of the human body.” Lucretius, Book II, page 109:

> And the gleaming Meliboean  
> Purple dyed with the Thessalian blood of shellfish—

that is, with the blood of Thessalian shellfish. [Col. 0352B] Propertius is no different in Book IV, Elegy VIII, 23, nor Statius in the *Epithalamium of Stella and Violentilla*, verse 191. Prose writers use the construction similarly. Novatian, *On the Trinity*, chapter XXII (otherwise XVII): “God did not refuse to assume the human frailty of condition,” for “the frailty of the human condition.” [Col. 0352C] Cyprian, *On Mortality*, page 164, Oxford edition: “To the heavenly splendor of kingdoms,” for “to the splendor of heavenly kingdoms.” DAVISIUS.

**Nor can he fail to assist.** The matter itself declares that Minucius wrote: “Nor does he fail to provide.” HEUMANN.—For my part, I think it should read: “And can God fail to assist us, and does he despise us?” Jeremiah 14:8: “O hope of the Israelites and their savior in time of adversity, why should you be like a stranger in the land, and like a traveler turning aside to spend the night? Why should you be like a drowsy man, like a soldier unable to defend?” LINDNER.

**In adversities.** Delete “in,” which ought to be absent here just as it is absent from the following “dangers.” HEUMANN.

**The will.** David prays in a suppliant voice that God may do this in Psalm 139:23. Above he had said, “he weighs the disposition of each person”; now he adds, “he investigates the will.” Heumann restores “virtue,” which also depends upon the will and spontaneous choice. LINDNER.

[Col. 0352D] **Confident that nothing belonging to him can perish.** That is, knowing that those who are true Christians will be conquered by no calamity or torture so as to fall away from him—that is, from God. HEUMANN.]

**SUMMARY.—No less clearly does he teach that fate is nothing other than what God has decreed. The mind is free, and therefore a person’s action, not his birth, is judged. After this he makes it perfectly clear that the poverty reproached against Christians is not a disgrace but a glory, and that their suffering bodily evils is not punishment but military service.**

Let no one either derive consolation from fate or use it to excuse an outcome. Let the lot belong to fortune; nevertheless, the mind is free, and therefore a person’s action, not his rank, is judged. For what else is fate but what God has decreed concerning each one [Col. 0350B] of us? Since he can foresee the material circumstances, he also determines destinies in accordance with the merits and qualities of individuals. [Col. 0351A] Thus, in us it is not birth that is chastised, but the nature of our disposition that is punished.

Enough about fate—or, if only a little has been said because of present constraints, we shall discuss it elsewhere more richly and fully. As for the fact that most of us are called poor, it is not our disgrace but our glory; for just as the spirit is enervated by luxury, so it is strengthened by frugality. And yet how can anyone be poor who is not in need, who does not covet what belongs to another, who is rich toward God? The poorer man is rather he who, although he possesses much, desires still more.

Nevertheless, I shall say what I think: no one can be as poor as he was when born. Birds live without [54] an inheritance, and livestock is fed from day to day; yet these things have also been born for us, and if we do not covet them, we possess them all. Therefore, just as a traveler is happier the lighter he walks, so in this journey of life the person who lifts himself by poverty is more blessed than one who groans beneath the burden of riches. And yet, if we considered wealth useful, we would ask it from God; surely he whose is the whole could grant a portion. But we prefer to despise riches rather than possess them; we desire innocence more, we demand patience more earnestly; we prefer to be good rather than extravagant.

And the fact that we experience and suffer the defects of the human body is not punishment; it is military service. For courage is strengthened by infirmities, and calamity [Col. 0352A] is very often the training ground of virtue. Indeed, the powers both of mind and body grow sluggish without the exercise of labor. Thus all your brave men whom you proclaim as examples flourished in renown through their afflictions. Accordingly, God cannot fail to assist us and does not despise us, since he is both ruler of all and lover of his own; but amid adversities he tests and examines each person. He weighs the disposition of each amid dangers; right up to the final moment of death he investigates the human will, confident that nothing belonging to him can perish. Thus, just as gold is tested by fire, so are we proved by ordeals.

## CHAPTER XXXVII

[Note: [Col. 0352D]

**Mocking, he taunts?** The manuscript reads “snatching, he drives home.” Hence Gelenius emended it to “mocking, he drives home.” The verb occurred in this sense in chapter 24: “they know them, drive them home, and press them.” Rigaltius and other more recent editors, however, preferred “mocking, he taunts,” which is better known and more approved. Jacob Gronovius, for his part, carves out the reading “filling, he drives home,” that is, while occupied and engrossed in filling out and satiating the noise of the deadly instruments and the horror of the executioner, he also drives it home. Heumann deservedly called this opinion a waking dream; yet Heumann himself, who reads “exults,” does not have my assent. For in the exemplar, according to Rigaltius, it reads *stultat* (“plays the fool”; not here, but below. EDITORS). The Roman edition, however, plainly presents *insultat* (“taunts”). What it means to “taunt the noise of death and the horror of the executioner” is shown by Prudentius, among many other passages, especially *Peristephanon* II, 313 following and 397–409. LINDNER.

**Raises.** He imitates Florus, Book I, 21: “They who had raised their liberty against their masters themselves.” Firmicus, *The Error of Profane Religions*, [Col. 0353B] page 6: “And then, with freedom of speech raised aloft, he proclaims.” WOPKENS.

[Col. 0353C] **Mucius Scaevola.** Read: “as Mucius.” HEUMANN.—I, however, conjecture that some words have fallen out and should be supplied from the following paragraph: “You yourselves exalt afflicted men to heaven: Regulus, Aquilius, Mucius Scaevola, who,” etc. LINDNER.

**Against the king.** I write “in regard to the king.” HEUMANN.

**To be burned, to be consumed by fire.** “To be burned” has been displaced from its proper position. I restore it thus: “Not merely for their right hand to be burned, but for their whole body to be consumed by fire.” HEUMANN.

**Inspired.** So it stands in the manuscript. Although Heraldus defends it from Lactantius V.13.12, I have no doubt that “unexpected patience” is preferable. The passage in Lactantius is very different from ours. There “inspired patience” is required because it is greater than that of robbers; here “unexpected,” because no one would have expected such patience from children and little women. LINDNER.

**So that their disposition.** This passage has always seemed desperate to me. I restore it as follows, conveniently enough, though I do not know whether successfully as well. Two contraries are always opposed to one another: they are raised up and fall down; they are fattened and suffer [Col. 0353D] punishment; they are crowned and slaughtered; they are exalted and cast down. The manuscript has: “so that their disposition may freely be sold by the license of a ruined mind of power,” words that yield absolutely no sense. I reject “their disposition,” as Heumann already saw (or did?); I render “mind” as a nominative plural and “power” as an accusative. “Minds of ruined license” are the Praetorian soldiers, who freely sold powers—that is, commands and magistracies. They could not do this unless a predecessor had first been removed. An illustrious example of this occurred after Pertinax was removed by the soldiers through a violent death. Who can doubt that Minucius—who, according to Daniel Heinsius on Seneca’s *Hercules Oetaeus*, lived in those times—had this in view? I adduce Herodian’s passage on the subject, Book II, chapter 6:

> The soldiers brought the loudest-voiced among them up onto the wall and proclaimed that the imperial power was for sale. They promised to entrust the sovereignty to the man who would give the most money and to conduct him safely under arms into the imperial palace. … Then, for the first time, the character of the soldiers was corrupted, and they were taught an insatiable and [Col. 0354B] shameful greed for money and contempt for the reverence due to their rulers. … For the absence of anyone to prevent the sovereignty from being so indecently proclaimed and sold for money [Col. 0354C] became thereafter the origin and cause of a disorderly and insubordinate condition, as greed for money itself and contempt for rulers continually increased to the point of bloodshed.

Heumann emends the passage thus: “So that their ruined minds may be severely punished for the license of their power.” LINDNER.

**Since death is.** I punctuated this passage in this way long ago. I now see that Wopkens also approved the same punctuation. Nor am I surprised that Heumann did not subscribe to it, for he preferred to restore: “Which is not like a dream and slips away before it is grasped.” LINDNER.

**Are you a king?** The author now establishes in order that no one should be deceived by the three things he discussed above—riches, honors, and powers. (1) He speaks of powers: “Are you a king?” (2) Of riches: “Are you rich?” (3) Of honors: “Do you glory in the fasces and purple?” LINDNER.

**Nevertheless.** I do not assent to Heumann when he substitutes “for.” It has the force of “but,” which occurred in the same syntactical structure. LINDNER.

[Col. 0354D] **Against itself.** Read “among themselves.” HEUMANN.—Or rather interpret it in this way. LINDNER.

**No less the frenzy; and more protracted the disgrace.** The royal manuscript reads “with frenzy”; perhaps: “the frenzy is no less, the disgrace more protracted.” HERALDUS.—The exemplar reads “with frenzy.” This reading should by no means be scorned, although syllables of this kind frequently coalesce in the same manuscript: “disgrace with frenzy,” for “frenzy and disgrace.” RIGALTIUS.

**While the actor feigns love, he inflicts it.** Cyprian, *On Grace*, page 6 (Commelinus, Letter II, chapter 3): “Adultery is learned while it is watched; and because the evil of public authority acts as a procurer for vice, the matron who had perhaps gone to the spectacle chaste returns from the spectacle unchaste. Then too, how great is the corruption of morals, what incitements to disgrace, what nourishment of vice—to be defiled by actors’ gestures?” CELLARIUS.—Lactantius VI.20: “What else do the actors’ most shameless movements do but teach and inflict lusts?” ELMENHORST.—Remarkable indeed is the acumen of the anonymous Parisian editor, who restores “fixes within.” Minucius cleverly says that love is simultaneously feigned and fixed—indeed, implanted. HEUMANN.

[Col. 0355B] **Clothing them in sexual crimes.** Heumann restores “introducing,” which is a word proper to the theater, as may be learned even from Faber’s lexicon. But from that same book I also learn that the meanings of these verbs are used interchangeably. And just as one commonly says to “put on” flattery, different things, mortal form, chaste manners, another person’s character, and so forth, one may similarly clothe gods in sexual crimes, sighs, and hatreds. LINDNER.

**In reality … in fiction.** That is, at gladiatorial spectacles you demand that one person pierce another through, but when a simulated murder occurs on the stage, you shed tears. There is a similar passage in Lactantius V.20.12, most worthy of reading. HEUMANN.]

**SUMMARY.—Spectacles worthy of God are the torments most unjustly inflicted for the confession of Christ. Octavius demonstrates this clearly by comparing certain exceptionally brave [Col. 0352B] pagans with the holy martyrs. He further explains that Christians do not attend spectacles and processions because they know with utter certainty that these are no less impious than cruel.**

How beautiful a spectacle it is for God when a Christian grapples with pain; when he arrays himself against threats, punishments, and tortures; when, seizing upon the noise of death [55] and the horror of the executioner, he drives them home; when [Col. 0353A] he raises his liberty against kings and princes and yields to God alone, to whom it belongs; when, triumphant and victorious, he taunts the very man who pronounced sentence against him! For he has conquered who has obtained the object for which he contended.

What soldier would not challenge danger more boldly beneath the eyes of his commander? For no one receives the prize before the trial. And yet a commander does not give what he does not possess: he cannot prolong life, though he can honor military service. But God’s soldier is neither abandoned in pain nor ended by death. Thus a Christian can appear wretched, but cannot be found to be so.

You yourselves exalt afflicted men to heaven: Mucius Scaevola, who, when he had made a mistake concerning the king, would have perished among the enemy had he not lost his right hand. Yet how many of our people have endured, without any wailing, not merely for their right hand but for their whole body to be burned and consumed by fire, [Col. 0353B] although it was especially within their power to be released? Shall I compare men with Mucius, Aquilius, or Regulus? Our children and little women mock crosses and torments, wild beasts and all the terrors of punishment, with inspired endurance of pain. Do you not understand, you wretched people, that no one would wish to undergo punishment without a reason or could endure torture without God?

Unless, perhaps, you are deceived because people who do not know God abound in riches, flourish in honors, and possess great power. Wretched people! They are raised higher for this purpose: that they may fall farther. For these men are fattened like sacrificial animals [Col. 0354A] [56] for punishment; like victims destined for penalty, they are crowned. Indeed, some are exalted to commands and dominions precisely so that—[the transmitted text is corrupt]—their disposition may be freely sold by the license of a ruined mind and of power. For without knowledge of God what happiness can be secure, since death is like a dream? It slips away before it can be grasped.

Are you a king? You fear as much as you are feared; and however large the retinue surrounding you, nevertheless you face danger alone. Are you rich? It is unwise to trust fortune, and a great stock of provisions does not equip but burdens life’s brief journey. Do you glory in the fasces and purple? What an empty human error and hollow cult of rank—to glitter in purple while being filthy in mind. Are you nobly born? Do you praise your ancestors? Yet we are all born to an equal lot; we are distinguished by virtue alone.

We, therefore, who are judged by our morals and modesty, [Col. 0354B] deservedly abstain from your wicked pleasures, processions, and spectacles, whose origin in your sacred rites we know and whose harmful enticements we condemn. For at the chariot games, who would not shudder at the madness of a populace quarreling against itself? At the gladiatorial games, who would not shudder at the schooling in murder? At theatrical performances, too, the frenzy is no less and the disgrace more protracted. Now the mime either displays adulteries or represents them; now the effeminate actor, while feigning love, inflicts it. He dishonors your gods by clothing them [Col. 0355A] [57] in sexual crimes, sighs, and hatreds. By simulated sorrows he likewise provokes your tears with empty gestures and nods. Thus you demand murder in reality but weep over it in fiction.

## CHAPTER XXXVIII

[Note: [Col. 0355C]

**God’s inviolable gift.** That is, a gift given by God to humankind which human beings cannot violate—that is, cannot make it unlawful to touch or eat—when they turn it into a victim dedicated to demons. Our author has in view the principle stated by the Apostle in 1 Corinthians 10:25 and 8:7 and Romans 14:14, where he teaches that Christians may eat any food, even food sacrificed to idols, provided, however, that if a brother is present who doubts this freedom, they abstain from it for the time being. HEUMANN.

**Lest anyone suppose.** “Us” must be understood, but should not, as Heumann does, be inserted into the text.

**Yield.** I read “believe.” So in chapter 40, “believe God.” Meursius had already corrected this passage in that way. “To believe” is a Christian formula: Christians are said to believe God, while idolaters believe demons. HEUMANN.—I once thought “eat” should be read, as in Paul, 1 Corinthians 10:20, δαιμονίοις θύει (*daimoniois thyei*), “he sacrifices to demons.” But I now see that the common reading must be retained. For not yielding is an exercise of liberty; Christians “yield to God alone,” chapter 37. LINDNER.

[Col. 0355D] **We take.** Stewechius, on Apuleius’ *Metamorphoses*, Book X, page 382, restores “we pluck.” Davisius reports this but does not approve it; I agree with Stewechius. Virgil, *Georgics* IV, 134:

> To pluck the rose in spring and apples in autumn.

Indeed, this very passage seems to have been present to Minucius’ mind. HEUMANN.

**We use both scattered flowers and garlands.** Christians used flowers scattered, garlanded, and loose for simple enjoyment, but scrupulously abstained from that use and manner of arrangement which belonged to Gentile rites. CELLARIUS.—Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* IV: “flowers garlanded and loose”; I.10, page 406: “with flowers cast about, garlanded and loose.” Tertullian, *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 5: “flowers both inserted and interwoven, both free and loose.” ELMENHORST.

**The fragrance of a good flower.** Tertullian, *On the Crown*, chapter 5: “What enjoyment of a flower is there on the head? It is as contrary to nature to seek a flower with the head as food with the ear or sound with the nose.” And in chapter 42 of the *Apology*: “We know the garland with our nostrils. Let those who smell through their hair see to it.” [Col. 0356B] HEUMANN.

**Nor do we crown the dead.** The Laws of the Twelve Tables forbid elaborate garlands at funerals, according to Cicero, *On the Laws*, Book II, chapter 24; they therefore appear to have permitted others. Tertullian, *On the Crown*, chapter 10: “What is as worthy of an idol as that which is also given to a dead person? For it also belongs to the dead to be crowned in this manner, since they immediately become idols themselves through the garb and observance of consecration.” For this reason the early Christians abstained from the use of garlands at funerals. We noted above, on chapter 12, that they later cast flowers upon tombs. CELLARIUS.

**How you assign to a lifeless person either a torch when he does not feel [Col. 0356C], or a garland when he does not feel.** So reads the manuscript; but Gelenius published: “to a lifeless person either a torch when he feels.” Meursius conjectured: “to a lifeless person, or one who does not feel, a garland as though he felt.” Desiderius Heraldus emends: “to a lifeless person, as one who does not feel, a torch; as one who feels, a garland.” Finally, the distinguished Salmasius, *Plinian Exercises*, page 963, Paris edition, pronounces that it must be written: “you assign either a torch to one who feels or a garland to one who does not feel.” Yet all of them have missed the mark. With the negative particle deleted, it should be corrected to “a torch to a lifeless person or one who does not feel” (for a torch affords no use except to one who sees and therefore feels), “or a garland to one who feels.” Minucius himself adds the reason: since the blessed person does not need flowers and the wretched person does not rejoice in them. Of what benefit, then, are they placed upon the dead, even if the dead feel? DAVISIUS.—Gutherus, Book III, *On the Law of the Shades*, while preserving the authority of the ancient manuscripts, thinks that it was written: “either a torch to one who does not feel or a garland to one who does not seek it.” Gronovius adheres to the manuscript reading and explains it thus: “either a torch for one who does not feel, to dispel the darkness, or a garland for one who does not feel its sweet breath or fragrance.” Grunerus says this to be the author’s meaning: “I am [Col. 0356D] more astonished at you precisely because you crown the dead: how you assign this to a lifeless person—who must either feel the torch with which you customarily burn him or fail to feel the garland with which you think you honor him—since if the dead person is blessed, he does not need flowers, while if wretched, he does not rejoice in them.” For my part, I have always believed the sense of the passage to be this: it is unsurprising that we do not crown the dead, because we know that they do not feel. But why do you crown a lifeless person? Perhaps because he feels? Then why do you place a torch beneath one who feels? But the dead person does not feel. Therefore he should not be crowned. It is a dilemma of the kind Caecilius had presented above in chapter 11, and ought to be formulated thus: “A lifeless person either feels or does not feel: if he feels, why do you place a torch beneath him? If he does not feel, why do you give him a garland?” LINDNER.

**But we adorn funerals.** So Heraldus reads from the royal manuscript, as does Meursius. The common reading is “we perform funeral rites,” which is interpreted as “we conduct them,” because Festus says: “Among the ancients *adorare* meant ‘to perform.’” But Festus did not prove this by examples; and even had he done so, it would not follow with certainty that Minucius preferred to speak [Col. 0357B] ἀρχαϊκῶς (*archaikōs*, “in an archaic manner”) rather than with the Latins of his own and the immediately preceding age, with which he was thoroughly familiar. CELLARIUS.

**The hope of future happiness.** That is, “we are confidently encouraged toward the hope of future happiness because we believe in his present majesty.” Thus Claudian, *The Rape of Proserpina* III, 386: [Col. 0357C] “to encourage toward crimes.” I formerly emended it thus: “we await it calmly: modest, through our God’s generosity; confident, through the hope of future happiness; encouraged, through faith in the presence of his majesty.” Rigaltius published: “we await it, calm, modest, secure in our God’s generosity; we are encouraged in the hope of future happiness by the presence of his majesty.” Heumann writes that Minucius’ text was: “We who, secure in the power and generosity of our God, are encouraged toward the hope of future happiness by faith in his present majesty.” LINDNER.

**In the age of our time.** The expression is good Latin. Propertius, Book I, Elegy IV, 7:

> And every woman whom the age of a beautiful time has produced.

See Janus Broukhusius there. Sulpicius Severus, *Life of St. Martin*, chapter 20: “As is the age of our times.” Arnobius, Book II, page 58: “The longevity of past time has run its course.” Conversely, Aulus Gellius said “in the times of his age,” Book IX, chapter 4. DAVISIUS.—The learned Davisius rightly defends “in the age of our time”; but I doubt whether he changes “came to maturity” into “became known” without necessity. For the evangelical revelation, or the truth of the Divinity, is quite aptly said to have come to maturity around that time, insofar as it then, and not earlier or later, seemed to God fit to be brought into the light. Thus a thing is said to mature, or become mature, when it reaches the time appointed or appropriate for it. Quintilian, *Letter to Trypho*: “For I myself did not yet think that the books had sufficiently matured.” Compare Cicero, *First Oration against Catiline*, chapter 13: “The maturity of all your crimes and of your long-standing frenzy and audacity has erupted in the time of my consulship.” The metaphor seems to be derived from crops appearing in their proper season. WOPKENS.

**Let us regulate.** So that it degenerates neither into superstition, by which someone believes everything, nor into impiety, by which someone believes nothing. This middle way is called true religion. Heumann restores: “Let us obey those who think rightly.” LINDNER.

**Let it be preserved.** Heumann restores “let it be restored.” But “let it be preserved” is equivalent to “let it be kept,” and our author customarily employs compounds with the syllable *re-* in the sense of their simple forms. [Col. 0358B] LINDNER.]

**SUMMARY.—The Christians likewise abstain from food sacrificed to idols, lest anyone suppose that they either yield to demons or are ashamed of their own religion. Yet they do not reject every color and fragrance of flowers. They customarily use scattered, loose, and soft flowers and encircle their necks with garlands; but they consider it superfluous and useless to crown the head of a dead person. They bury their dead with the same tranquility with which they live, awaiting the crown of eternal happiness with the surest hope. Their true religion, therefore, once Gentile superstitions have been rejected, ought to be embraced by everyone.**

[Col. 0355B] As for our scorning the remnants of sacrifices and cups from which libations have been poured, [Col. 0356A] this is not an admission of fear but an assertion of true liberty. For although everything that comes into being, as an inviolable gift of God, is corrupted by no act, we nevertheless abstain, lest anyone suppose either that we yield to the demons to whom the libation was offered or that we are ashamed of our own religion.

Who doubts that we enjoy the flowers of spring, since we take both the spring rose and the lily, and whatever else among flowers possesses an enticing color and fragrance? We use them both scattered, soft and loose, and we encircle our necks with garlands. As for our not crowning the head, forgive us. We are accustomed to draw in the breath of a fragrant flower with our nostrils, not to inhale it with the back of the head or the hair.

Nor do we crown the dead. In this I am more astonished at you: how you assign to a lifeless person either a torch when he does not feel, or a garland when he does not feel, since the blessed person does not need flowers and the wretched person does not rejoice in them. But we [Col. 0356B] adorn funerals with the same tranquility with which [Col. 0357A] we live. We do not attach a withering garland, but await from God one living with eternal flowers; for, modest and secure in our God’s generosity, we are encouraged toward the hope of future happiness by faith in his present majesty. Thus we both rise again in blessedness and, through contemplation of the future, live out our present course.

Therefore [58] let Socrates, the Attic buffoon, look to it—glorious though he was through the testimony of a most deceitful demon after confessing that he knew nothing. Let Arcesilaus too, Carneades, Pyrrho, and the whole multitude of Academics remain in doubt. As for Simonides too: “forever … not to be comprehended” [the transmitted reading is incomplete; common text: “let him defer it forever”]. We despise the superciliousness of philosophers, whom we know to be corrupters, adulterers, and tyrants, and always eloquent against their own vices. We profess wisdom not in our clothing but in our mind; we do not utter grand things but live them. [Col. 0357B] We glory in having attained what they sought with the utmost exertion but could not find.

Why are we ungrateful? Why are we begrudged this, if the truth of the Divinity has come to maturity in the age of our time? Let us enjoy our good, and let us regulate right judgment: let superstition be restrained, impiety expiated, and true religion preserved.
