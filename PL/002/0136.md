DAEMON. II, 61 B, “To the daemon, as he wandered astray.” Simon calls his own harlot a daemon: her name was Helena, whom he took about with him and called Ennoea, Ἔννοια (“Thought”), his own thought and intelligence. Simon related that he had descended to seek this lost sheep, since she had been maliciously detained by the angels, as Irenaeus explains more fully in book I, chapter 20.

DAMNARE, “to condemn.” II, 48 A, “Therefore he also said that the heretic was condemned by himself,” i.e. by his own self, by his own judgment. Heretics pronounce sentence upon themselves when they withdraw from the Church by their own choice; this withdrawal appears to be the condemnation of their own conscience.

[Col. 1284A] DAMNATITIUS, “condemned.” II, 48 A, “Even though they shared nothing belonging to the condemned,” namely, heresies; i.e. those already condemned—just as *fictitium* means “what has been fabricated,” *donatitium*, and so forth.

DARE, “to give.” II, 963 B, “He gave his face to God,” i.e. he turned toward God.

DE, “because of,” “on account of.” I, 371 A, “Because of religion.” II, 318 A, “And necessarily severe because he is a judge, and, because severe, apparently savage.” I, 413 A, “It will confess itself to be a daemon in truth,” i.e. what it truly is. Compare Lactantius, book II, chapter 16. I, 416 A, “You worship them, as far as I know, even with the blood of Christians,” i.e. you not only slaughter Christians to gratify those gods but sometimes kill them at the gods’ own altars. I, 523 A, “But rather concerning our defense.” Junius explains: “This contributes more to our defense.” Others: “Let us remain rather in our own defense.” II, 204 B, “Of whose he makes use,” with “possessions” or something similar understood. He also employed this form of expression in the preceding books, *Against Marcion*, book V: “If it is understood as the Creator’s.” In the same book: “And it can be advanced from our party’s”; this is an imitation of the Greeks. II, 188 [Col. 1284B] A, “Just as he himself does from the Father’s,” i.e. from that which belongs to the Father. II, 792 B, “But so that the conclusion may make common use of the preface,” i.e. may furnish the function of a common preface, may do what a common preface usually does.

DEBELLATOR, “assailant.” I, 294 A, “Produce some assailant of the Christians,” i.e. not an exterminator but an attacker, a persecutor.

DEBELLARE, “to subdue.” II, 909 B, “Subdued for a time by their mothers.” I, 442 A, “Let the emperor finally subdue heaven.”

DEBERE, “to owe,” “ought.” I, 444 A, “I am the one for whom it is due to obtain,” i.e. I alone ought to obtain it.

DEBITUM, “debt,” “due.” I, 265 A, “If no debt of hatred is discovered,” i.e. no just reason why a good man ought to hate this profession. II, 303 A, “For all these things are due to severity, just as severity is due to justice.” II, 399 C, “Which men refer to themselves as a debt of grace.” II, 659 A, “All the more essential features, and those due in every respect to corporeality.” Thus he calls habit, boundary, length, breadth, and so forth.

[Col. 1284C] DEBUCCINARE, διασαλπίζειν, “to trumpet abroad.” II, 907 B, “Let us trumpet abroad none of those things which will earn a reward with him.”

DECEDERE, “to descend”; *de fastigio decedere*, “to be brought down from one’s eminence,” “to be subjected.” II, 1043 A, “He was brought low in captive *sarabara* [trousers].” II, 274 B, “By tortures you compel them to withdraw from their confession.”

DECIMARE, “to decimate.” I, 704 B, “What will Carthage itself suffer, when it is decimated by you?” Livy indicates what *decimare* means in these words: “As for the rest of the multitude, every tenth man was chosen by lot for punishment.”

DECLINARE, “to incline,” “to derive.” II, 556 B, “With the investigation of the Father turned aside.” Junius, with Rhenanus and others, reads “with the investigation inclined,” i.e. an investigation from which all hope has been taken away, as though the aids necessary for completing it were inclining and failing. Thus Cicero writes to Lentulus: “The matter is declining; strenuous efforts are being made.” II, 462 A, “Even then he was declining Christ in Judah,” i.e. he was prophesying that Christ would be “declined” or derived; for among Latin writers *declinare* means “to derive,” and hence in Varro, book IV of *The Latin Language*, words derived from primary words are called “declined.”

[Col. 1284D] DECOR, “beauty.” II, 236 B, “Just as one does who is merely beauty appearing.” Rigault, Junius, and others read, “what beauty.” Merely apparent beauty does something—that is, it wounds the soul.

DECRESCERE, “to diminish.” I, 1247 A, “Diminishing themselves whenever they encounter any person of higher rank,” i.e. lowering themselves to show that person reverence.

DECULCARE, “to trample down.” I, 511 A, “Diogenes tramples down Plato’s proud couches with another form of pride”: he treads upon them proudly, and as though from on high.

DECUMANUS, “of the tenth,” hence “enormous.” II, 1033 A, “Decuman waves,” i.e. great and extremely destructive waves. II, 739 B, “Shaken by no decuman waves.”

DECUTERE, “to cast off.” I, 579 B, “Daily he now casts it off with his whole body,” i.e. by now he has lost the skin over his whole body.

DEDAMNARE, “to annul a condemnation.” II, 1009 B, “But if he has also manifestly annulled the condemnation of the crime,” i.e. has rescinded the sentence of condemnation and absolved the condemned person.

[Col. 1285A] DEDERE, “to surrender.” II, 892 A, “Let them be surrendered to those women,” i.e. let them be ordered to pass over into their manner and custom. The metaphor comes from opposing armies. II, 544 B, “At the same time I surrendered myself to the order of wisdom.”

DEDICARE, “to begin,” “to inaugurate.” I, 340 A, “The body of your god is first inaugurated upon a gibbet.” II, 223 A, “Then, as though inaugurating it with embellishments.” In his *Various Readings*, Willem Canter reads: “Then, as though *delicating* it with embellishments,” and, following Nonius, explains this as “expounding, opening, making manifest.” But *delicare* means to explain and unfold in speech things that are obscure, not to do so by an actual work. II, 367 B, “As inaugurated together with the churches themselves.” II, 934 B, “But when the first crime, murder, was inaugurated in fratricide.” II, 802 B, “Consider that God was wholly occupied with and dedicated to him.” Festus says: “Properly, *dedicare* means to confer something by speaking.” God had first bestowed his labor upon that material and upon forming the human being, saying: “Let us make man,” and so forth. I, 1229 A, “From that point onward he inaugurated repentance in himself.” II, 725 C, “So too, in the beginning, sleep was inaugurated together with [Col. 1285B] ecstasy.”

DEDICATOR, “beginner,” “author,” “originator.” I, 293 A, “But we even glory in such an originator of our condemnation.” II, 781 C, “The originator of the new birth.”

DEDUCTOR. In the Eleusinian rites this was properly the name of the man who, holding a torch in his hand, went before the others and, illuminating the way, conducted them onward, as Eustathius teaches in his commentary on Homer, *Iliad* I, verse 279. I, 395 A, “The illuminator and guide of the human race”; Greek ἀρχηγός, “leader,” “originator.”

DEFAECARE, “to clarify,” “to refine.” I, 1246 B, “Refine the old age of the wine.”

DEFAECATIO, “refinement,” “purification.” II, 693 A, “Just as that bodily fluid too is seed, and arises through the purification of the flesh.”

DEFARINATUS, “ground into flour.” II, 586 A, “Carried to the millstones and ground into flour,” i.e. milled and reduced to flour.

DEFECTIO, “failure,” “departure.” II, 798 B, “We acknowledge the departure of the flesh alone,” i.e. death. II, 951 A, “And yet the weakness of the flesh does not excuse even a bloody departure.”

DEFENDERE, “to maintain,” “avenge,” “punish.” I, 289 A, “In other cases they maintain that proof must concern the deed committed, not the name.” That is, in the accusation [Col. 1285C] of those who are not Christians, they rightly judge that it is not enough for a person to be dragged into court; rather, the deed must above all be established, since even a completely innocent man can be accused of a crime. II, 277 C, “For why does he forbid an act to be committed when he does not defend its having been committed?” i.e. when he does not avenge or punish it, thereby defending the authority of his prohibition. II, 898 B, “And thus defended for the whole female sex,” i.e. vindicated. II, 903 A, “They too will have to defend certain insignia for themselves,” i.e. protect them, claim them for themselves, and assert them as though by their own right. I, 1280 B, “The lust of the flesh defends the functions of age,” i.e. it maintains that an age still suited to marriage ought to be occupied in marriage. II, 934 C–D, “Iniquities defended once for all,” i.e. avenged in the flood.

DEFENSA, “vengeance,” “defense.” II, 305 C, “Vengeance belongs to me, and I will avenge.”

DEFERRE, “to buy and carry away with oneself at an agreed price.” II, 1049 A.

DEFETISCENTIA, “weariness.” II, 722 A, “That sleep is weariness”—though it is rather the opposite of weariness, since weariness is removed by it.

[Col. 1285D] DEFICERE, “to fail.” II, 868 A, “They fail to attain the kingdom of God,” τοῦ λαβεῖν ἀπολείπονται, “they fall short of attaining.” A Hellenism. II, 84 A, “But through lack of knowledge they fail to understand this also,” i.e. they do not understand it; they lack the knowledge by which they might understand.

DEFIGERE, “to fix.” II, 368 C, “By which we fix,” i.e. we posit as fixed, establish, and determine. II, 880 B, “He fixed the readily growing parts,” namely, the nails; i.e. he forbade them to grow.

DEFINITIO, “definition,” “definitive sentence.” II, 222 A, “The crucial point of the definition is *is*; *was* makes it relational.” It is as though he said: “Is” is said of a thing simply in itself; “was” is said of a thing not simply and in itself, but with a relation, determination, and restriction of time. II, 294 B, “With the definition of death,” i.e. with a definitive sentence of death, as jurists speak.

DEFLORATIO, “selection of the choicest parts.” II, 562 B, “As something constructed from a selection out of all things.” Irenaeus: διὰ τὸ ἀπὸ πάντων εἶναι, “because it is derived from all.”

DEFLUXURA, “discharge.” II, 884 A, C, “Why should they be colored any more than the discharges?” By “discharges of drinks” he means what is excreted from drink.

[Col. 1286A] DEFORMARE, “to shape,” “disfigure.” II, 444 A, “Disfiguring by the outcome of both.” I, 340 A, “Because the clay does not shape the image before it has been built upon a cross and a stake.”

DEFRAUDATIO, “deprivation.” II, 723 A, “For every nature is annulled either by deprivation or by abnormality.”

DEFRUTUM, “boiled-down must.” II, 326 C, “Or *defrutum* from the rocks.” “They used to call must *defrutum*,” says Varro in book I of *The Life of the Roman People*, as quoted by Nonius, “if by boiling they had reduced it from two parts to one third.”

DEFUNCTUS, “suffering,” “death.” II, 580 A, “And it would unwillingly be subject to death.”

DEFUNDERE, “to pour out,” “cast.” I, 534 A, “And yet you cast statues for all of them.” I, 1259 A, “When he pours out his contributions of gold into an idol.”

DEFUNGI, “to discharge,” “complete,” “come to an end.” II, 281 C, “But so as to complete them when mature.” I, 1285 A, “Therefore, when one spouse has died by the will of God, the marriage also comes to an end by the will of God,” i.e. there is now no further exercise of marriage.

[Col. 1286B] DEGENERATUS, “not generated for.” II, 583 A, “The earthy element, not generated for salvation.” Just as we call a person *degenerate* who is not nobly born, so he calls that “not generated for salvation” which was not generated for salvation. Irenaeus: τὸ μὲν χοϊκὸν εἰς φθορὰν χωρεῖν, “the earthy element proceeds into corruption.”

DEGUSTARE, “to taste beforehand.” II, 818 A, “Being the first to taste judgment.” II, 125 A, C, “Meanwhile others, with clubs and claws, after tasting martyrdom in addition.”

DEHAURIRE, “to drain away,” “consume.” II, 809 C, “He will also be able to summon the flesh, however it may have been consumed, out of another,” i.e. used up. II, 1049 A, “For the pearl, precious even in its very name, drained it away.”

DEHONORARIA, “unseemly,” “dishonorable.” I, 470 A. See HONORARIA.

DEJERARE, “to swear solemnly.” II, 45 C, “Both demonstrated and solemnly sworn,” i.e. very strongly confirmed by an oath. Compare *On Repentance*, chapter 4.

DEJICERE, “to fall,” “to set,” used of stars. II, 1033 A, “The disordered confusion of the stars sometimes causes something to fall.”

DELATURA, “derivation,” “imputation.” II, 519 A, “I ask from what derivation [Col. 1286C] the very name ‘devil’ is applicable to the Creator.”

DELEGARE, “to enjoin,” “entrust.” I, 1251 B, “But the divine arrangement of a living and heavenly discipline enjoins it.” II, 660 A, “Or petitions are entrusted.” II, 290 A, “Nor would he have entrusted his bond under fear of death.”

DELIBARE, “to draw off.” I, 319 A, “While the blood is still being drawn off into a human being.” Blood is said to be drawn off into a human being while the embryo is nourished from the most excellent fluid of the human body. Others read *deliberatur*, “is deliberated upon.”

DELIBATIO, “a slight tasting,” “a superficial attack.” II, 549 B, “The attack will not be a piercing conquest,” i.e. we shall indeed bruise and touch lightly, but we shall not pierce through. A bruise pricks; conquest pierces through. II, 804 A, “And the very infusion of the male into the female.”

DELIBERARE, equivalent to *liberare*, “to free.” II, 583 B, “The animal substance, freed to an intermediate hope,” i.e. handed over to its own freedom and choice. He calls it an “intermediate hope” because the outcome mentioned above was uncertain. He seems to allude to the intermediate region where the Valentinians said that the souls of the righteous were refreshed. Junius prefers *delibratum*, i.e. left, as they say, in the [Col. 1286D] equilibrium of the central balance-beam and to its own impulse. See DELIBRARE.

DELIBERATIO, “release.” I, 1262 A, “We shall be harmed by the release of lesser things.” “Lesser things” here means money. *Deliberatio* seems to have been used in place of *liberatio*, “release.” It is a maxim of Christian philosophy that riches are counted among impediments.

DELIBRARE, “to balance as in a scale.” II, 412 A, “Not balanced through the torment of ten months,” i.e. suspended, as it were, in the scale of the womb. Others read *deliberatus*. He said that childbirth is “balanced” as long as the fetus is perfected in the womb according to the order of human birth, over a period of ten months. Havercamp, on the *Apology*, chapter 9, explains it thus: “While nature deliberates in the womb whether it wishes to form the conceived and commingled seed into a human being, or to allow it to flow away and undergo a natural miscarriage.” This does not please me.

DELINEATIO, “outline,” “type.” II, 582 A, “As an outline of the higher Christ.”

DEMANDARE, “to set forth,” “assert,” “premise.” II, [Col. 1287A] 326 A, “And, so to speak, to set forth nature.” II, 332 B, “The death of Christ is denied, although the Apostle asserts it so emphatically.”

DEMENTIRE, “to be mad,” as though “to depart from the mind.” I, 412 A, “So that he may appear to be mad in a different way.” Lucretius III, 464: “For he is mad and speaks deliriously.” II, 679 B, “For even when the human being is mad, it is the soul that is mad.”

DEMERERE, “to earn favor,” “propitiate.” I, 378 A, “Who has determined rules for propitiating himself,” namely, one who prescribes definite laws and rules for propitiating himself, i.e. rules through whose observance his favor can be elicited. I, 404 A, “By the multitude of so many divinities that must be propitiated.” II, 963 A, “Knowing what he should do to earn God’s grace.” II, 252 B, “I could earn favor in the case of one alone.” I, 1269 A, “Which labors in many ways to earn its lord’s favor.”

DEMORARI, “to delay.” II, 957 B, “Delayed meals,” i.e. meals postponed because of the station fasts; since these were prolonged, they customarily delayed meals. II, 977 A, “And they sigh for the authority of the delaying star,” i.e. the star that remains or tarries.

[Col. 1287B] DEMUTATIO, “change.” II, 876 A, “Change must, however, be distinguished from every indication of destruction; for change is one thing, destruction another.”

DENOTARE, “to censure.” II, 1030 C, “To censure modes of dress.” Others read: “These conditions mark out peace: both the food supply and leisure; all is well from the empire and from heaven.” For Junius, *denotare* means “to indicate by a public mark and proclaim by authority”—incorrectly. II, 1036 A, “Why do you censure the human being?” II, 551 A, “Nor do I censure anything else more in this sort of thing,” namely, the fable of the aeons. II, 177 C, “Would he not have censured the vanity?” II, 560 A, “I shall censure the perversity of the doctrine.”

DENOTATIO, “censure.” I, 1332 A, “And it exults in the very censure of itself.”

DENOTATUS, the same. II, 1044 A, “How many freedmen does passibility offer to the censured man?”

DEPALARE, “to establish and enclose by driving in stakes.” I, 330 A, “The city that Saturn had marked out with stakes remains to this day.” II, 222 C, “In a manner marking out the world with its elements at first unworked.” In Tertullian, *depalare* sometimes [Col. 1287C] seems to be the same as *propalare*: “to reveal,” “to show openly.” II, 484 C, “The Apostle is called the revealer of divine discipline,” i.e. its expositor and promulgator. Junius, however, more ingeniously, as usual, interprets this passage as follows: “This whole discourse,” he says, “is figurative, its allegory being taken from sculptors, who first mark out with stakes the unworked and rough wood, and afterward dedicate it when it has been skillfully fashioned and adorned.” The verb *depalo* is not derived from *palam*, “openly,” but from the noun *palus*, “stake.” To “mark out the world with stakes” means to set up the rough material as though it were to be formed and fashioned upon a stake. The sense would then be: presenting a coarse and rough work, as though it were a stake to be hewn into a trunk—as at I, 366 A: “How greatly is Attic Pallas distinguished from the trunk of the cross? And the grain-made Ceres, displayed without an image as a rough stake and shapeless wood?”

DEPERIRE, “to be lost,” “to diminish.” I, 260 A, “What is lost here to the laws that rule in their own kingdom?” i.e. what is diminished, and so forth.

DEPLUERE, “to rain down.” II, 584 A, “Let it rain down into good souls,” i.e. infuse and instill. Irenaeus: “But Achamoth sows the spiritual elements.”

[Col. 1287D] DEPOSTULATOR, “one who clamors for punishment.” I, 456 A, “Nor were any more insistent demanders of Christians,” those who demanded that Christians be put to punishment and shouted: “Christians to the lions!”

DEPRECARI, “to beg for mercy.” I, 259 A, “That sect, or the truth, asks nothing in mitigation concerning its case,” i.e. the Christian sect is not conscious that its case is insufficiently good, so that it should be compelled to implore the judges’ mercy.

DEPREHENDERE, “to find,” “overtake.” I, 1292 B, “Those who are found by the faith while in a marriage with a pagan,” i.e. are called to the Christian faith after they have been joined in marriage to an unbeliever, and are found in the marriage—that is, while they are married. II, 944 C, “Those found by the faith while in marriage.”

DEPRETIARI, ἐλαττοῦσθαι, “to be diminished,” “to lower the value of,” “despise”; transferred from bidding over merchandise. I, 500 A, “Epicurus depreciates every torment and pain.” II, 253 B, “If it can be depreciated in the Creator,” i.e. if it is possible for the Creator to be depreciated. II, 262 B, “You depreciate that in which [Col. 1288A] you both live and die.” II, 112 D, “You who flee the devil have depreciated Christ, who is in you.”

DEPUGNATIO, “disfigurement by assault.” I, 650 C, “And every assault upon the human face—that is, upon the divine image,” i.e. disfigurement produced by violence.

DEPUNGERE, “to discharge and take possession of a good received,” or “to put it to use.” II, 295 C, “If he had not duly discharged what he had duly received.”

DEPUTARI, “to be reckoned,” “be made like.” I, 284 A, “In which we are reckoned worthy of ridicule.” II, 588 B, “I am reckoned among angels—not as a male angel or a female angel,” i.e. I become like the angels and am no longer reckoned male or female. The dead will rise, each with his or her own sex; but there will be no functions of sex, because there will no longer be either birth or death. I, 370 A, “If perhaps we are reckoned among the Persians,” i.e. are said to practice the religion of the Persians. I, 1303 C, “The condemned are assigned to the penalty of death.”

DERIDERE, “to smile upon.” II, 758 C–759 A, “Which is mocked with caresses,” said of an infant.

DERIVATIO, “derivation,” “emanation.” II, 164 B, “But the Son is his derivation and portion,” i.e. ἀπόρροια, “an emanation,” who flowed forth from the Father. II, 171 A, [Col. 1288B] “In proportion to the derivation.”

DESCROBARE, “to sink more deeply, as though into a pit,” from *scrobis*, i.e. “ditch.” II, 805 A, “And in addition you sink it into gold of the most elaborate workmanship.”

DESAEVIRE, “to treat savagely and cruelly.” II, 347 A, “They savaged his sinews with nails.”

DESIGNARI, “to be indicated,” “to be exposed to everyone’s sight.” II, 1042 A, “He described Omphale, now exhibited as Hercules’ harlot.”

DESINERE, “to cease.” II, 21 A, “When even John had ceased to be certain concerning him,” i.e. even after John’s departure. Tertullian explains this passage in *On Baptism*, chapter 10, and *Against Marcion*, book IV, chapter 18. II, 904 B, “She ceased to be a virgin.” A Hellenism: παρθένον παύεται, “she is no longer a virgin.”

DESPOLIARE, “to strip.” II, 587 A, “To be stripped, however, is to put off the souls with which they seemed to be clothed.”

DESTINARI, ὁρίζεσθαι, “to be defined,” “appointed.” II, 248 A, “To designate with the finger,” i.e. to point someone out with an extended finger and mark him. II, 543 A; 1043 B, “Accordingly, those whom we now take aim at—the heretics,” i.e. whom we attack as though they were exposed to a blow.

[Col. 1288C] DESTINATIO, “purpose,” “destination.” I, 523 A, “Because the rationale of restoration is the purpose of judgment,” i.e. that for which judgment was appointed. The sense is: judgment acts so that each person may receive according to his merits.

DESTRUCTIO, “subsequent inference.” II, 211 A, “They charge God with more numerous and more unworthy destructions.” Here “destructions” are opposed to “preliminary constructions” (though these are not named) and mean the consequences established from premises, τὰ παρεπόμενα, “the consequences.”

DESULTRIX, “one who leaps away,” “deserter.” II, 592 A, “Except that she was a deserter.” Irenaeus: ἀποστᾶσαν, “one who had departed,” i.e. who had leapt away from the common rational order of the aeons and defected from it.

DETENTUS, “detention.” II, 587 B, “Subject neither to detention nor to sight.”

DETERGERE, when used of beasts, “to crush with the teeth and tear apart by impact.” I, 322 A, “The boar that bloodied him crushed him in the struggle.” II, 819 A, “Two words that are ready, decisive, and polished.”

DETINERE, “to accuse.” I, 1301 A, “What cause of this madness shall I accuse?” I, 283 A, “But now, although investigation and recognition of both have been neglected, the name is prosecuted.”

[Col. 1288D] DETRACTARE, διατυποῦν, “to formulate thoroughly.” I, 1292 A, C, “The judgment has been thoroughly formulated and plainly stated.” The prefix *de-* intensifies the verb’s meaning.

DETRACTATUS, “treatment,” “discussion.” I, 633 B, “Let us turn rather to our own discussions,” i.e. treatments of this subject.

DETRIMENTUM, “loss.” I, 1260 B, “Thus distaste for wealth provided beforehand the loss required by patience.” The sentence is obscure. It appears that it should be understood and punctuated thus: “Thus the Lord provided beforehand for patience a loss”—with “of property” understood—“a distaste for wealth, a contempt for riches,” the expressions standing in apposition. The sense is: the Lord first showed that loss of property must be endured with patience when he taught that riches should be despised.

[Col. 1289A] DEVEHERE, “to carry down or away.” I, 1164 A, “But carry us away from evils.” I, 1306 C, “But as one carried away from simplicity and sincerity.”

DEVERGERE, “to incline.” II, 30 B, “Whether it inclines toward the truth or rather toward heresy.” II, 235 A, “With matter inclining neither toward good nor toward evil.”

DEVESTIVUS, “unclothed,” one who can cover himself with no garment, i.e. no appearance and form. II, 363 C, note 5. Apuleius likewise used the verb *devestire*, “to unclothe,” in book III of the *Metamorphoses*.

DEVINCTIO, “binding spell.” I, 632 A, “You may wish to accomplish murder by the sword, poison, or magical bindings.” Magical bindings are the name given to superstitious and impious arts that customarily bind or knot figures by incantation to cause death or harm; conversely, practitioners are said “to unbind” when they reverse the curse and free someone from evil.

DEVORARE, “to devour.” II, 847 B, “And to be devoured by hearing.” This unusual metaphor derives from the fact that, since Christ is the Word of God and his flesh is bread, he is said to be devoured through hearing just as bread is devoured by the mouth. II, 875 B, “As though we were not said to devour bile and pain,” i.e. to conceal [Col. 1289B] and cover them and contain them within ourselves. II, 875 B, “How the mortal is devoured by life.” II, 1008 B, “While still under rebuke, he was in danger of being devoured.” II, 854 B, “Surely you suppose ‘devoured’ to mean nothing other than intercepted, abolished, and removed from every faculty of perception.” II, 809 A, “Who is able to rebuild the tabernacle of the flesh after it has fallen, been devoured, and been taken away by whatever means.” II, 804 A, “The clay, therefore, has been effaced and devoured in the flesh.”

DEVORATORIUS, “devouring,” “destructive.” I, 663 C, “After such crimes, so destructive of salvation.”

DEVOTAMENTUM, “a thing devoted,” “a curse.” II, 127 C, “And all his possessions would become things under a curse.”

DEVOVERE, “to devote.” II, 1046 A, “Devoted to their backs,” i.e. dedicated and reserved for the purpose of adorning even their backs. Others read *detorto*, “twisted onto.”

Διαμαστίγωσις, “scourging.” I, 626 A.

DICI, “to be called,” “be accused.” I, 270 A, “Whatever we are called when others are called,” i.e. when others are accused of the crime imputed to us.

[Col. 1289C] DICIBULUM, “a saying,” “piece of talk.” II, 575 A, “Ptolemy had a sufficient recollection of childish sayings,” i.e. familiar trifles, just as *dicibile* in the glossary means λόγος, “word” or “discourse.”

DICTATA, “instructions,” μαθητά, “things learned,” of any kind, as in the old glossary; instructions belonging to all masters, not only to teachers of letters. II, 620 A. Thus Suetonius, in the *Life of Julius Caesar*, chapter XXVI, says of gladiators: “That they should receive each man’s training and themselves give instructions to those practicing.”

DIFFERRE, “to disperse,” “spread.” I, 311 A, “Even what rumor did not spread.” Also, “to abstain.” I, 487 A, “Kept away from every fruit of life,” i.e. deriving no enjoyment from life, abstaining from every cheerful thing. II, 81 A, “I set the Apostle aside,” i.e. here I do not take account of the apostolic command, because it followed a long time afterward.

DIGERERE, “to arrange,” “set forth.” II, 660 A, “For they are most carefully arranged,” i.e. entered into a digest. II, 1038 A, “And Alexander relates it,” i.e. transmits it. I, 1256 A, “If you have likewise set forth what must be avoided.”

DIGESTUM, “digest,” “written account.” I, 516 A, “If they found anything objectionable [Col. 1289D] in the sacred records.” II, 365 A, “And from that circumstance come our written compilations,” i.e. διαστρώματα, “systematic expositions.” The sense is: on the occasion presented by the false apostles, the Holy Spirit was the author of the apostles and apostolic men’s committing sacred doctrine to written records, which are our compilations. II, 367 A, “For they are also accustomed to ascribe Luke’s compilation to Paul.”

DIGESTUS, “digestion.” II, 663 A, “And by digestion without channels.”

DIGITUS, “finger.” II, 221 C, “And with a corresponding motion of the finger,” and so forth. It is used of a person who assumes far too much authority and determines whatever he pleases according to his own will. Others think that the painter’s fingers are meant, forming various colors and anything whatsoever in manifold ways; he calls all these things in the next line “the embellishment of delivery.”

DIGLUBARE, “to skin.” II, 1038 B, “To have skinned the little sheep,” instead of *deglubisse*. Among the ancients it was customary not to shear sheep but to strip and pluck them, as Pliny attests, *Natural History*, [Col. 1290A] book VIII, chapter 48. Turnebus prefers *delibasse*, “to have tasted”; Latinus, *delibrasse*, “to have stripped.”

DIGNARI, as the Greek ἀξιοῦν, “to deem worthy,” hence “to deign.” II, 267 A, “In the fifteenth year of Tiberius, Christ Jesus deigned to flow down from heaven.”

DIGNE, “deservedly,” “with justification.” I, 1260 B, “Since permission was not granted even to do harm with justification,” i.e. by right and merit.

DIGNITAS, “dignity,” “excellence.” I, 1319 B, “Now the dignity of beauty should not be feared.” II, 880 C, “The justice of fitness and dignity.” II, 988 A, “Evil too has a degree of dignity, according as it is placed at the summit or in the middle of the worst things.”

DILIGENTIA, “care,” “zeal.” II, 58 B, “Alarmed carefulness,” which is always afraid that it may commit some fault. II, 585 B, “Carefulness for sins,” a desire prone to sinning, zeal for wrongdoing. I, 1303 A, “Daily diligence without hindrance.” By *diligentia* he means what in the *Apology* he calls “most diligent religion,” i.e. the assiduous and careful observance of discipline or religion. II, 104 A, “In mutual care and affection.” By these words he signifies all the duties of Christian devotion toward God and others, such as the affections of the faithful [Col. 1290B] in caring for and loving one another, which even the pagans praised and admired.

DILUERE, “to swallow down,” “wash away,” “flood.” II, 1035 A, “Finally, if the sea washes anything away.” I, 1201 B, “Is it not also marvelous that death is washed away by the bath?” I, 1203 B, “Whether one is washed in a pool or a basin,” concerning baptism. I, 615 A, “And by gaining the larger portion you dissolve fear,” i.e. release it. I, 1204 B, “And the spirit is bodily washed in the waters.” II, 803 B, “But the authority of the flesh may appear more diluted,” i.e. less. II, 687 B, “And consequently of a more diluted divinity.” I, 614 B, “Or you return more diluted from the funeral pyres.”

DILUVIO, “flooding.” II, 727 A, “That a virgin’s bladder flowed forth into the flooding of Asia.”

DIMINORARE, “to diminish.” II, 707 B, “There he is diminished to whom,” and so forth.

DINUMERARE, “to enumerate.” I, 268 A, “They enumerate within themselves,” i.e. they recollect and review inwardly within themselves—that is, they truly examine their own acts. For *numerare* means “to review.” Latinus prefers *deonerant semetipsos*, “they unburden themselves,” *deonerare* meaning to lighten the burden of an accusation. Heumann conjectures *et murmurant in semetipsos*, “and they murmur within themselves.”

[Col. 1290C] DIPLOMA, “official dispatch.” I, 426 A, “Sleepy dispatches” means public messengers and official dispatches, against which Tertullian is, as it were, angry because they had not proceeded more quickly to Cybele and her chief Gallus.

DIRECTO, “directly,” “openly,” “clearly.” I, 414 A, “Whom you directly know to be daemons.” I, 652 A, “Which it directly prohibits.”

DIRIGERE, “to proceed at law,” “bring an action.” II, 257 A, “From this point, therefore, I shall proceed with the utmost resolution,” i.e. I shall turn back against them the arguments that they have hurled against us. I, 1267 B, “Who, conducting a lawsuit with his adversary,” i.e. contending. II, 968 B, “For this reason we have proceeded through the individual kinds of fasting,” i.e. we have discussed them. II, 796 A, “To set disputed matters straight” means to make them clearer. II, 798 A, “The many who will need to be instructed, directed, and fortified.”

DIRIGERE, of the second conjugation, means “to be dissolved and subside.” The old glosses have ἀποπήσσομαι, “I melt away,” *derigeor*; ἀποπηγνύω, “I unfasten,” *diriguo*. One should read *derigeo* or *dirigeo*. A statue stands rigid, [Col. 1290D] a pediment stands rigid, and every elevated structure stands rigid; in their rigidity they possess, as it were, a kind of pride, and do not readily yield—indeed, they resist, for they have been compacted into hardness. But when they are dissolved, as metals by fire or ice by the sun, they are said to *derigere*, because they are no longer rigid. I, 689 A, “He most fully gave his followers a model by dissolving every elevation and raised structure of both dignity and power.”

DISCENTES, “learners,” i.e. disciples. I, 1212 C; 1254 A; II, 15 A; 43 A.

DISCENTIA, μάθησις, “learning.” II, 687 A; 690 A, “Those whose acts of learning become acts of recollection.”

DISCERNERE, “to separate,” “set apart.” II, 570 A, “Set apart for extermination.” What Tertullian calls “to set apart for extermination,” the Latin Irenaeus had called “to exterminate,” and the Greek ἀφανισθῆναι, “to be annihilated.”

DISCIPLINA, “strict preservation of order,” “corrective severity.” II, 538 B, “Discipline is not frightened.” Thus Cyprian in *On the Dress of Virgins*: “Discipline is the guardian of hope, the bond [Col. 1291A] of faith.” I, 307 A, “The origin of that discipline,” i.e. of the Christian sect. I, 444 A, “I who am killed because of his discipline.” I, 453 A, “Those who observe discipline out of regard for Caesar.” II, 980 A, “If discipline has persuaded him.” I, 499 A, “Your discipline is not so much to be feared where true innocence is concerned.” I, 700 B, “And assuredly that we act in accordance with the discipline of divine patience.” II, 679 B, “Contrary to the discipline of faith.” I, 681 B, “While we remain in observance of discipline, not in deviation from it.” II, 23 B, “They interpret without the discipline of reason.” I, 1152 A, “So that this discipline of prayer also.” II, 18 B, “And they faithfully transmitted to the nations the discipline received from Christ.” II, 58 B, “Discipline is the indicator of doctrine.” II, 810 B, “For even the earth receives its discipline from heaven.” II, 857 B, “Through the discipline of holiness, patience, justice, and wisdom.” II, 931 B, “And so they reproach the discipline of monogamy as heresy.” II, 989 A, “What are you doing, most lenient and humane discipline?” II, 1001 B, “For Christian discipline dates from the renewal of the Testament.” II, 1023 B, “Discipline governs the human being.” I, 378 A, “Who has determined rules [Col. 1291B] for propitiating himself.” II, 1024 A, “That the apostles acted not from discipline but from power.” I, 468 A, “We are a body through consciousness of religion and the divinity of discipline”; others read “the unity of discipline.” I, 477 A, “Judge the remaining order of discipline.” II, 684 B, “So greatly, in matters of this kind, even the discipline of our household is valued before the Lord.” Ibid., “But within the bounds of discipline.” II, 56 B, “The overthrow of discipline.” II, 585 A, “Nor do they observe any duties of discipline.” II, 977 B, “Thus you come from the field of your utterly lax discipline.” II, 983 C, “Through the irregularity of discipline.” II, 997 C, “The entire condition of salvation is established in the maintenance of discipline.” II, 1002 B, “Except in order always to impose upon them these abridgments of discipline.” I, 273 A, “And for establishing discipline by covenant.” II, 998 D, “And that they rendered interpretations better suited to the protection of disciplines.”

DISCIPLINATUS, “disciplined.” II, 104 A, “Then faith too was more vigilant in campaigning and more disciplined in fasts.”

DISCONVENIENTIA, “disagreement,” “incompatibility.” I, 618 A, “Amid so great an incompatibility of ways of life.”

[Col. 1291C] DISCRIMINARE, “to differentiate.” II, 985 B, “According to this difference among sins, the condition of repentance is also differentiated.”

DISCULPERE, “to carve.” II, 1043 A, “Carved.” Lipsius prefers *discalptum*, “engraved.”

DISPARTIBILIS, “divisible.” II, 233 A, “For its changes show it to be divisible.”

DISPECTOR, “one who attends circumspectly to each individual,” “overseer.” I, 499 A, “As something commanded by an overseer who cannot be despised.” I, 1329 A, “And not also the overseer of his own institutions.” I, 611 B, “God: clearly the overseer and arbiter.” I, 1300 A, “As though they were truly overseers of divine judgments.”

DISPENSATIO, “dispensation,” “arrangement.” II, 583 A, “The dispensation of this world.” He used “dispensation” above. Irenaeus: τὴν κατὰ τὸν κόσμον οἰκονομίαν, “the economy relating to the world.” II, 156 B, “Yet under this dispensation, which we call οἰκονομία, ‘economy.’” II, 722 A, “So that the processing of food would not readily proceed,” i.e. digestion.

DISPERGERE, “to frustrate,” “annihilate.” II, 296 A, [Col. 1291D] “Thus in another way he openly frustrated it.”

DISPERSUM, “scattered.” II, 246 B, “In scattered fashion,” σποράδην, “sporadically.” II, 583 B, “I shall now gather from scattered material,” ἐκ τῶν σποράδην, “from things scattered about.”

DISPICERE, “to consider,” “examine,” “weigh.” I, 259 A, “To examine openly.” II, 141 B, “And throughout the individual stories of the heavens they began to be examined concerning the command,” i.e. to be investigated and examined as to whether we had obeyed Christ’s command and remained steadfast in confessing Christ’s name before the powers. II, 302 A, “First, therefore, examine the judge’s justice.”

DISPONERE, “to establish.” II, 254 B, “Because the already-established rule of the supreme greatness forbids it,” i.e. once that rule has been established or that foundation laid, there cannot be two gods.

DISPOSITIO, “arrangement.” II, 19 A, “A rash interpreter of the divine nature and arrangement.” Lupus understands “arrangement” as the creation of the world. Rigault, better, understands it as the order by which God governs all things.

[Col. 1292A] DISPUNCTIO, “reckoning,” a term taken from monetary accounts and from ledgers of receipts and expenditures. I, 378 B, “For the reckoning of both kinds of merit.” II, 502 B, “And the reckoning of good and evil works.”

DISPUNCTOR, “one who audits accounts and renders to each according to his works in the scales of his justice and mercy.” II, 400 B, “As by a judge and marker of merits.”

DISPUNGERE, “to settle an account,” “discharge.” I, 462 A, “If it were lawful among us for evil to be settled by evil,” i.e. repaid. I, 500 A, “We who are judged under God, the observer of all things,” i.e. we who have God and his laws as our judge, since nothing can be hidden from his eyes. II, 705 B, “They themselves will not be entered into the reckoning,” i.e. will not be taken into account. II, 624 B, “And so let us settle in detail the order we have begun,” i.e. let us balance it, as it were, by totaling the accounts of receipts and payments from every side. For items approved in reciprocal accounts, votes, and reviews of soldiers or offices used to be marked with points, so that the remaining items might then be examined; these were called *dispuncta*, “checked off.” By contrast, *expuncta*, “expunged,” were items removed from the accounts as rejected or passed over. II, 311 C, “In both respects he will be recompensed by God,” i.e. in both respects satisfaction will be made to him; in both he will receive a reward. II, 415 A, “As though both office and honor had now been discharged,” i.e. completed and finished. II, 916 A, “And he pays out the reward of eternity.” II, 881 A, “Because even today Enoch and Elijah have not yet been brought to completion by resurrection,” i.e. have not yet completed it and attained its consummation.

DISSIPARE, “to shatter.” II, 108 C, “Shattered by torture,” I believe as punishment for his flight.

DISSOLUTRIX, “dissolver.” II, 721 A, “Death, the dissolver of the body.”

DISTANTIVUM, “extended in dimensions.” II, 659 A, “As that which is extended in three ways—I mean length, breadth, and height.”

DISTRAHERE, “to pull apart,” “sever.” II, 151 A, “Paul is severed when his head is separated and cut from his neck.” Septimius says in *Against Marcion*, book IV, that separation is the work of the sword.

[Col. 1292C] DIVIDERE, “to divide.” I, 459 A, “They divided the forum among themselves,” namely, seeking to secure the most convenient and best location.

DIVINATIO, “prophecy.” I, 391 A, “The truth of prophecy,” i.e. the divinity of Scripture can be concluded from the fact that its divination—that is, its prophecy—is fulfilled every day.
