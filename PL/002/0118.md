[Col. 1127B] Whether this book ought to be entitled *Against Apelles* or *Against the Apelleians* caused me to hesitate for some time, because Vincent of Lérins, among the other heretics against whom Tertullian wrote, enumerates Apelles; consequently, we also wondered whether the author had composed several treatises against Apelles himself and his disciples. But since, although Apelles is mentioned so many times and no one else is, Tertullian nevertheless makes no mention of any book against him except in this passage, it seems credible to me both that he composed only one and that it was entitled *Against the Apelleians*. This is especially so because blessed Cyprian also calls the disciples of Apelles “Apelleians,” Epistle 73, *To Jubaianus*.

Now the words of Tertullian already cited are indeed rather obscure, but their meaning is this: the principal subject of the book *Against the Apelleians* [Col. 1127C] was whether that fiery angel, the ruler of evil, who had both fashioned this world (according to them) and constructed flesh for the souls he had enticed—although for this purpose he had possessed the spirit, will, and power of Christ—had done anything worthy of repentance. For they used to say, as may be read in the book *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, ch. 51, Heresy 19: “He mingled repentance with the lower world, because he had not made it as perfect as the higher world had been fashioned.” Meanwhile, Tertullian turns this very point elegantly against them when he speaks of “refuting them from their own equipment.” What he means by calling this angel “the ruler of evil” is explained by Epiphanius, Heresy 44, according to the opinion of Apelles: “But he proved not to be good, and the things made by him were not well made, but were created by him in accordance with his own evil mind.”

On the souls enticed by that fiery god or angel according to Apelles, see above, vol. III, *On the Soul*, chs. 23 and 36, where the author adds, as a consequence, that Apelles held male and female souls to have existed before [Col. 1127D] bodies. On the world fashioned by him, see *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, chs. 34 and 51, Heresy 19. On human flesh consequently constructed by that same being, see ch. 8 of *On the Flesh of Christ*, from which these words are taken, and *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 5. Finally, concerning the flesh of Christ—not created by that angel but constructed from the heavenly stars—the author argues at length against Apelles in ch. 6 of the said book *On the Flesh of Christ*. Epiphanius agrees on all these points, except that he calls the being whom the author terms a fiery angel the “lower god”; this angel is, however, also acknowledged by blessed Augustine, [Col. 1128B] *Heresies* 23; blessed Isidore, *Against Marcion*; our Rabanus; blessed Honorius; and Gratian, in their catalogues of heretics.

Peculiar to our author is what has already been said about the repentance of the creator-angel, as is also the claim that they interpreted the figure of the wandering sheep (Luke 15) as referring to him—as though there had been joy in heaven among the angels of God over that one sinner converted to repentance. The author, by contrast, preferred to interpret it as referring to fallen humanity, sought out again through Christ, as did all the other ancients. Thus far concerning the points that contribute to understanding this fragment.

Since, however, we promised to present here a complete history of Apelles and his heresies, we shall do so in as few words as possible. Apelles, therefore, was a disciple of Marcion. To use the author’s words from the said book *On the Prescription* [Col. 1128C] *Against Heretics*, chs. 6, 30, and 51: having fallen into involvement with a woman, and having deserted Marcionite continence—which Marcion merely pretended to observe—he withdrew from his master’s sight to Alexandria. There he stumbled upon another woman, the virgin Philumene, who afterward became an enormous prostitute. Led astray by her signs and prodigies, he published a book which he entitled *The Manifestations of Philumene*, whom he followed as though she were a prophetess.

Indeed, the author calls him not merely a deserter but also a corrector of Marcion and a disciple above his master, *Against Marcion*, book IV, ch. 17. This was not only because, as we said there, he posited one higher God and called the other “Lord,” but made him an angel; it was also because of the other heresies listed a little earlier, which he introduced as novelties after being seduced by Philumene. He differed in no respect from Marcion in rejecting the God of Moses, the Law, and the prophets, as is stated in *On the Prescription*, ch. 51. He taught the salvation of souls alone and denied the resurrection of the flesh, which the author therefore attacks in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*; likewise, he used only the Apostle, and even that was Marcion’s version—that is, not the complete text. On most of these matters one may also consult Epiphanius, Theodoret, and John of Damascus.

But this detail is peculiar to our author: “He has, moreover, his own books, which he entitled *Syllogisms*, in which he attempts to prove that not everything Moses wrote about God is true, but that it is false.” Consequently, nothing else concerning these matters presents itself for annotation. I shall add only this: it is not surprising that the author wrote against the Apelleians, since in the frequently cited *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, ch. 30, he reports that distinct pupils and successors of Apelles were still surviving.

## X. SUBJECT OF THE BOOK *ON PARADISE*, FROM *ON THE SOUL* (CH. 55)

[Col. 1127]

[Col. 1127D] “But our dwelling is in the ether,” etc. “No,” you say, “it is in paradise, to which even then the patriarchs and prophets, [Col. 1128D] appendages of the Lord’s resurrection, migrated from the lower regions. And how is it that the region of paradise, [Col. 1129A] revealed to John in the spirit and lying beneath the altar, shows no other souls with it except those of the martyrs? How is it that Perpetua, that most valiant martyr, on the day before her passion, in a revelation of paradise, saw there only her fellow martyrs? Is it not because the sword that guards paradise yields to none except those who have died in Christ? [The text is apparently corrupt here; conjecturally:] A new death for God, extraordinary for Christ, is received in another, private lodging. Recognize, therefore, the difference [Col. 1130A] between a pagan and a believer in death. If you fall for God,” etc., “not amid mild fevers and upon little beds, but in martyrdoms; if you take up your cross and follow the Lord, as he himself commanded, your blood is the complete key to paradise. You also have our treatise *On Paradise*, in which we establish that every soul is kept apart in the lower regions until the day of the Lord.”

### [NOTE]

[Col. 1129A] Thus far the author; but he must be understood in a sounder sense, as we have noted more fully among the Paradoxes in the Prolegomena, where we shall answer all his arguments. It must be noted here that the author composed this book not only on the earthly paradise in which Adam was created, and to which Enoch and Elijah are said to have been translated, but also—and principally by design—on the spiritual paradise into which the apostle Paul is said to have been caught up in 2 [Col. 1129B] Cor. 12. Yet in this he disagrees with the other Fathers, who call that paradise heavenly—blessed Ambrose does so expressly in his commentary on that passage—whereas our author does not distinguish it from Abraham’s bosom, although he admits that the latter is higher than the lower regions. But on this also, see the Paradoxes in the Prolegomena.

He expressly acknowledges the earthly paradise when speaking of Adam in *Against the Jews*, ch. 2; *On Patience*, ch. 5; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 26; and *Against Marcion*, book II, chs. 2 and 10. Again, in *Against the Valentinians*, ch. 20, mocking the Valentinian Ptolemy because he dreamed that Paradise was a fourth archangel, he jokes as follows:

> “For they place this being above the third heaven as well, from whose power Adam supposedly drew while lodging there amid little clouds and little trees. Ptolemy remembered children’s sayings well enough: that apples grow in the sea and fish upon trees; in the same way he presumed there to be walnut groves in the heavens. The Demiurge works in ignorance, and therefore perhaps he does not know that trees ought to be established on the earth alone!”

Most openly of all, however, he acknowledges it in this very volume, in the poem *On the Judgment* [Col. 1129C] *of the Lord*, ch. 8, and in *Genesis*, chs. 2 and 3.

Blessed Irenaeus had already acknowledged the same paradise in *Against Heresies*, book III, ch. 55, and more openly in book V, ch. 5, where he says that the presbyters who were disciples of the Apostles taught that Enoch and Elijah had been translated into the paradise where the first man was placed, concerning which Scripture says (Gen. 2): “And God planted a Paradise in Eden toward the East, and placed the man there.”

Blessed Justin Martyr also agrees in *Apology* I, where he writes that Homer, in imitation of the paradise described by Moses, invented the garden of Alcinous. Likewise, Theophilus in book II *To Autolycus*, Novatian in *On the Trinity*, and Lactantius in the poem *On the Phoenix*, alluding to the quoted words of Scripture, report that paradise was situated in the East. Finally, Epiphanius, in the *Anchored One*, *Against the Origenists*, and the *Letter to John, Bishop of Jerusalem*, translated into Latin by blessed Jerome, argues at very great length from Scripture (Gen. 2 and 3) against Origen, who transferred paradise from the earthly realm to the heavens. He proves the same from those four rivers flowing out of paradise, whose water he writes that he himself had drunk; and he maintains that in the Apostle’s words at 2 Cor. 12 the third heaven is one thing and paradise another.

Blessed Augustine, in *On Heresies*, attributes the same heresy to the Seleucians and Herminians. Blessed Jerome also, somewhere in his *Commentary on Daniel*, commands all their ravings to fall silent. As for where paradise is located, I consider it superfluous to recount here the various opinions of different writers, since blessed Augustine writes in *Against Pelagius and Coelestius*, book II, ch. 23, that this belongs among questions that lie outside the faith.

## SUBJECT OF THE BOOK *ON THE HOPE OF THE FAITHFUL*, FROM *AGAINST MARCION*, BOOK III, FINAL CHAPTER; BLESSED JEROME ON EZEKIEL 36 AND ELSEWHERE; AND GENNADIUS

[Col. 1129]

[Col. 1129C] Tertullian says:

> Concerning the restoration of Judaea, which the Jews themselves also hope for just as it is described, being led by the names of places and regions: it would take too long to trace how the allegorical interpretation applies to Christ and the Church, and to its condition and fruit; and this has been set out in another work, which we entitle *On the Hope of the Faithful*. In the present work it would also be superfluous for the additional reason that the question concerns not an earthly but a heavenly promise. For we also confess that a kingdom upon earth has been promised to us, [Col. 1129D] but before heaven and in another condition—namely, after the resurrection, for a thousand years (Rev. 20 and 21), in Jerusalem, a city of divine workmanship brought down from heaven. The Apostle also designates this as our mother above (Gal. 4); and by declaring that our πολίτευμα (*politeuma*), that is, our citizenship, is in heaven (Phil. 3), he assigns it to some city—surely a heavenly one. Ezekiel also knew this city (Ezek. 48), and the Apostle John saw it (Rev. 21).

Blessed Jerome says:

> For we do not, according to Jewish fables—which they call δευτερώσεις (*deuterōseis*, “repetitions” or “secondary traditions”)—expect a jeweled and golden Jerusalem from heaven; nor shall we again suffer the injury of circumcision, offer sacrifices of bulls and rams, or sleep in Sabbath idleness. Yet many of our own writers promise this, especially Tertullian’s book entitled *On the Hope of the Faithful*, the seventh volume of Lactantius’ *Institutions*, the frequent expositions of Victorinus, bishop of Poetovio, and, recently, our Severus in the *Dialogue* to which he gave the name *Gallus*. And, to name the Greeks and join the first with the last: Irenaeus and Apollinaris.

The same Jerome says in the preface to book XVIII on Isaiah, alluding to the same book:

> Nor am I unaware how great a diversity of opinions there is among men, etc., concerning the promises of future things and the manner in which John’s Apocalypse ought to be understood. If we accept it according to the letter, we must Judaize; if we expound it spiritually, as it was written, we shall appear to contradict the opinions of many ancients: among the Latins, Tertullian, Victorinus, and Lactantius; among the Greeks—to pass over the others—I shall mention only Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons. Against him Dionysius, that most eloquent man and bishop of the church of Alexandria, wrote an elegant book, ridiculing the fable of the thousand years, a golden and jeweled Jerusalem upon earth, the restoration of the Temple, the blood of sacrificial victims, Sabbath idleness, the injury of circumcision, marriages, childbirth, the upbringing of children, the pleasures of banquets, and the enslavement of all nations; and, again, wars, armies, triumphs, the slaughter of the defeated, and the death of the sinner at a hundred years of age. [Col. 1131A] Apollinaris answered him in two volumes.

The same Jerome says in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*:

> Papias is said to have published a Jewish δευτέρωσις (*deuterōsis*, “repetition” or “secondary tradition”) concerning the thousand years. Irenaeus, Apollinaris, and the others followed him who say that after the resurrection the Lord will reign in the flesh among the saints. Tertullian, too, in the book *On the Hope of the Faithful*, Victorinus of Poetovio, and Lactantius are led by this opinion.

Gennadius says in *On Ecclesiastical Dogmas*, among the works of blessed Augustine, alluding to the same book:

> In the divine [Col. 1132A] promises we expect nothing earthly or transitory, as the Melitanians hope; not the union of marriage, as Cerinthus and Marcus rave; not anything pertaining to food or drink, as Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Lactantius assent to Papias, their authority; nor do we hope that after the resurrection Christ’s kingdom will exist upon earth for a thousand years and that the saints will reign with him amid pleasures, as Nepos taught, who invented a first resurrection of the just and a second resurrection of the wicked.

### [NOTE]

[Col. 1131A] Thus far the several testimonies concerning the subject of the book *On the Hope of the Faithful*. We added the last chiefly because it attributes to each author his own paradoxes, whereas blessed Jerome at first sight appears to fasten them upon everyone collectively. Yet, out of reverence, Jerome calls them “opinions,” not heresies or errors; in this Gennadius also imitated him.

But so that we may not be more prolix here concerning the opinions of others, or concerning the answer to all the arguments that Tertullian cites from Scripture in this passage, let the reader consult our Prolegomena among the Paradoxes. Here we shall say only that Tertullian’s opinion appears to us milder than the others, namely, the view about which blessed Augustine writes in *The City of God*, book XX, ch. 7:

> This opinion would indeed be tolerable if it were believed that certain spiritual delights would be granted to the saints in that Sabbath—that is, during the thousand years—through the Lord’s presence. “For we ourselves,” he says, “once held this opinion.”

Thus, what Gennadius attributes to the Melitanians—that they hoped for an earthly kingdom of Christ—applies to Tertullian, who described it in *On the Hope of the Faithful*, more properly than does what Gennadius imputes to him concerning food and drink. Perhaps this was why the author, above others, commends Melito in those books written for Montanus whose fragments follow, according to the passage that will be cited there from blessed Jerome. For, in the passage mentioned, he indicates in these words that this paradox also belonged to Montanus:

> This, etc., is also attested by the utterance of the New Prophecy, which is held among us to belong to the faith, inasmuch as it foretold that even an image of the city would appear to human sight as a sign before the city itself was manifested.

The author’s words that chiefly confirm what I have said are those cited above:

> Concerning the restoration of Judaea, how the allegorical interpretation applies to Christ and the Church, and to its condition and fruit—it would take too long to trace, etc.

For if the interpretation is allegorical, it is therefore not literal; and consequently one would wrongly impute to him—as applies more properly to Cerinthus and the Dimœrites, according to Epiphanius—the restoration of the Temple, the blood of sacrificial victims, Sabbath idleness, and the injury of circumcision.

The words he adds shortly afterward in the same passage tend to the same conclusion:

> We say that this city—namely, Jerusalem brought down from heaven—has been provided by God to receive the saints at their resurrection and to refresh them with the abundance of all goods, which are of course spiritual, in compensation for those things that in this age we either despised or lost.

For if it is an abundance of spiritual goods, it is therefore not an abundance of the temporal goods of which others dream. Meanwhile, he agrees with the others in this point, although erroneously: he confesses that a kingdom has indeed been promised to us upon earth, but before heaven.

## MENTION OF THE SIX BOOKS *ON ECSTASY*, FROM BLESSED JEROME’S *CATALOGUE OF ECCLESIASTICAL WRITERS* AND NICEPHORUS, *ECCLESIASTICAL HISTORY*, BOOK IV, CHS. 22 AND 34

[Col. 1131]

[Col. 1131C] Blessed Jerome says of Tertullian—and Nicephorus uses precisely the same words:

> Although he had remained a presbyter of the Church until middle age, he afterward fell into the doctrine of Montanus because of the envy and insults of the clergy of the Roman Church. In many books he mentions the New Prophecy; but he composed especially against the Church the volumes *On Modesty*, *On Flight in Persecution*, [Col. 1132C] *On Fasts*, *On Monogamy*, and six books *On Ecstasy*.

The same author says:

> Tertullian, having published six volumes against the Church, which he wrote περὶ Ἐκστάσεως (*peri Ekstaseōs*, “On Ecstasy”), etc.

The same Jerome says in his notice on Melito:

> Praising Melito’s elegant and declamatory genius, Tertullian, in the seven books *Against the Church, for Montanus*, says that many of our people considered him a prophet.

### [NOTE]

[Col. 1131C] Thus far Jerome. From the latter passage it appears [Col. 1131D] that these books were composed in Greek rather than Latin, as we shall explain more fully in the following fragment.

But, to keep our promises, we have thought it proper to add briefly what “ecstasy” means to the author and to other ancient ecclesiastical writers. A general definition is found in *On the Soul*, ch. 45:

> We call ecstasy a departure from the senses and something resembling madness. Thus at the beginning sleep was also inaugurated with ecstasy: “And God sent an ecstasy upon Adam” (Gen. 2:21), “and he fell asleep.” For sleep came upon the body for repose, while ecstasy came upon the soul in opposition to repose; and from that point onward the pattern combines sleep with ecstasy.

And a little later:

> This will be the distinctive quality of this madness: it does not arise from the corruption of good health, but from the order of nature; for it does not destroy the mind, but calls it away. To shake is one thing, to move another; to overturn is one thing, to agitate another. Therefore, the fact that memory remains available indicates soundness of mind; and when soundness of mind, with memory preserved, is nevertheless stupefied, it is a kind of madness. Hence we are said not to rave but to dream; hence also we remain rational, if we ever are so. For our understanding, although it is overshadowed, [Col. 1132C] is nevertheless not extinguished, except that it may itself appear to be inactive at the time. Ecstasy, moreover, also produces this effect from its own peculiar nature: it introduces images of wisdom into us just as it does images of error.

Briefer, however, and more consistent with the author’s purpose is the definition found in ch. 2 of the same book, where he speaks of Adam:

> An ecstasy fell upon him: the power of the Holy Spirit, the agent of prophecy.

And in ch. 21:

> God sent madness upon him, the spiritual power in which prophecy consists.

Yet the former kind of ecstasy could in some fashion be acknowledged in Adam, but not this latter kind, since it appears designed to confirm the enthusiasms of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla. Their own disciples admitted that these persons customarily spoke in ecstasy and as though mad; and after his middle age Tertullian himself was among those disciples.

To this belongs the following statement concerning Prisca or Maximilla in *On the Soul*, ch. 9:

> There is today among us a sister who has obtained gifts of revelations, which she experiences in the church, amid the Lord’s solemn rites, through ecstasy in the spirit.

This, indeed, is what he means in *Against Marcion*, book IV, ch. 22, [Col. 1133A] where he explains the words about Peter in Luke 9, “But not knowing what he was saying”:

> How was he not knowing? Was it through a simple error, or through irrationality—“irrationality” being the sense in which he also said in *Against Praxeas*, ch. 15, that Peter, John, and James did not experience the vision of God without irrationality and madness—which we defend in the cause of the New Prophecy, namely, that ecstasy, that is, madness, belongs to grace? For when a person is placed in the Spirit, especially when he beholds the glory of God or when God speaks through him, he must necessarily fall away from the senses, being overshadowed, of course, by divine power. Concerning this there is a dispute between us and the Psychics.

Here—as more fully in *On Fasts, Against the Psychics*—by “Psychics” he means Catholics, and he alludes to these six books *On Ecstasy*, which it is certain that he wrote against the Church.

But Epiphanius argues admirably against the Montanists in Heresy 48. After proving from the Scriptures that true prophets spoke under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit with constant reason and understanding, sound in mind and body, he comes to the prophecy of Montanus and proves from Montanus’ own words that he prophesied neither with a stable mind nor with mental understanding.

The same point appears to be served by blessed Jerome’s annotation in *Hebrew Questions on Genesis*: instead of “ecstasy,” that is, a departure of the mind, the Hebrew has [Hebrew term missing in the supplied text], which Aquila translated καταφοράν (*kataphoran*, “deep sleep”) and Symmachus κάρον (*karon*), that is, a heavy and profound sleep. Finally, the text continues: “And he slept.” The same word is also used of Jonah’s snoring sleep.

Yet Peter’s words, “not knowing what he was saying,” do nothing to support the insane prophecy of Montanus. Others prefer to understand them as referring to unconsidered speech, such as people customarily use when mentally overwhelmed, because Mark adds: “For they were terrified by fear.” And this is certain: Peter prophesied nothing there.

Moreover, the author also attempts to twist a passage of the Apostle, 1 Cor. 14, into confirmation of the same New Prophecy, with these words in *Against Marcion*, ch. 8:

> Let him produce some psalm, some vision, some prayer—provided it is spiritual—in ecstasy, that is, in madness, if an interpretation of the tongue has been added. Let him also prove to me that a woman among them has prophesied, one of those holier females of theirs, a speaker of great things.

But besides the fact that the phrase “in ecstasy” is not found there in the Apostle, the same passage says: “And the spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets.” From the Apostle’s immediately following words blessed Jerome refutes Montanus, and consequently Tertullian, in the prologue to his *Commentary on Habakkuk*:

> It is a prophet’s vision; and, against the perverse doctrine of Montanus, the prophet understands what he sees. He does not speak as a madman, nor, after the manner of raving women, utter sound without understanding. Hence the Apostle also commands that, if something is revealed to another while others are prophesying, [Col. 1134B] those who were speaking first should be silent.

From this it is understood that when someone voluntarily falls silent and gives another an opportunity to speak, he can both speak and remain silent when he wishes. But one who speaks in ecstasy—that is, involuntarily—has it within his power neither to remain silent nor to speak.

The same point is made by Jerome in the prologue to the prophet Nahum:

> For he does not speak in ἐκστάσει (*ekstasei*, “ecstasy”), as Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla rave; rather, what he prophesies is the book of a vision belonging to one who understands everything he says.

## NOTES ON THE BOOK *AGAINST APOLLONIUS*, FROM BLESSED JEROME’S *CATALOGUE* AND NICEPHORUS

[Col. 1133]

[Col. 1133B] In the passages cited above concerning Tertullian, they say:

> He composed especially against the Church the volumes *On Ecstasy*, six books, and a seventh, *Against Apollonius*.

Blessed Jerome says:

> Apollonius, a most eloquent man, [Col. 1133C] wrote against Montanus, Priscilla, and Maximilla a distinguished and lengthy volume, in which he asserts that Montanus and the insane prophetesses perished by hanging, and many other things. Among these, he relates the following concerning Priscilla and Maximilla: “If they deny that these women accepted gifts, let them confess that those who do accept them are not prophets; and I shall prove this by a thousand witnesses. But prophets are also proved by other fruits. Tell me: does a prophet dye his hair? Does he smear his eyes with antimony? Is a prophet [Col. 1134B] adorned with clothing and jewels? Does a prophet play at tables and dice? Does a prophet receive interest? Let them answer whether it is permissible to do this or not. It is my task to prove”—for so I read, instead of “to approve”—“what they did.”

> In the same book he says that it was the fortieth year, down to the time when he was writing the book, since the heresy of the Cataphrygians had its beginning. Tertullian, having published six volumes against the Church, which he wrote περὶ ἐκτάσεως (*peri ektaseōs*, “On Ecstasy”), composed a seventh specifically *Against Apollonius*, in which he attempts to defend everything that Apollonius censures. Apollonius flourished under the emperors Commodus and Severus.

### [NOTE]

[Col. 1133C] For two reasons I am persuaded that this book *Against Apollonius*, together with the six preceding books, was published not only in Latin but first in Greek. First, Apollonius was Greek and wrote in Greek, being a different man from the Roman senator whom blessed Jerome mentions subsequently; secondly, Jerome gives the title [Col. 1133D] in Greek, περὶ ἐκστάσεως (*peri ekstaseōs*, “On Ecstasy”). In the preceding fragments, however, he did not wish to add that they had been written against the Church.

Moreover, since blessed Jerome asserts that Tertullian attempted to defend everything Apollonius censured, we find it entirely true that blessed Augustine wrote of him in *On Heresies, to Quodvultdeus*: Tertullian passed over to the Cataphrygians whom he had formerly demolished. For above all, in *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, ch. 52, Heresy 21, he expressly calls heretics

> those who are called “according to the Phrygians,” etc., who said that the Holy Spirit had indeed been in the Apostles, but not the Paraclete; and that the Paraclete said more through Montanus than Christ set forth in the Gospel—and not merely more, [Col. 1134C] but also better and greater things.

Against the coloring of hair and staining of the eyes with antimony, which he nevertheless appears to have defended in these prophetesses, he argues expressly in *On Women’s Dress*, ch. 4, and more openly in *On the Adornment of Women*, ch. 5. There he attacks those

> who smear their eyes with soot,

reproaching God the craftsman whom [Col. 1134D] they correct; and in ch. 6 he attacks

> those who turn their hair saffron-colored, making an ill omen for themselves with their flaming head and bringing destruction upon the brain.

Against adornment with clothing and jewels, which he also tolerated in those women, he argues both there and in *On the Pallium*, ch. 4, and throughout the book *On Women’s Dress*, particularly on jewels in ch. 6.

The game of tables and dice, which he does not condemn here, as though treating another subject, he tacitly censures in prophets or teachers in *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 7, in these words:

> I ask you, Apelles—or you, Marcion—if perhaps, while playing at tables, etc., you were called away by such a messenger, etc.

Finally, in *Against Marcion*, book IV, ch. 17, he treats at great length of interest on loans, forbidden by Christ at Luke 6, although Montanus accepted it.

## MENTION OF SEVERAL LOST WORKS, FROM THE SAME PASSAGES OF BLESSED JEROME AND NICEPHORUS, AND FROM VINCENT

[Col. 1133]

[Col. 1133D] Blessed Jerome says of Tertullian:

> He is reported to have lived to an extremely advanced age and to have composed many minor works that are no longer extant.

Nicephorus says:

> But many others also contended for our faith in an altogether Christian spirit and fought against heresies. They committed to writing the received condition and customs [Col. 1135A] of the Church, and other things that they had received from their predecessors either in writing or without writing, and left a great many books to posterity. Gaius and Tertullian are deservedly numbered among them, for they certainly published very many [Col. 1136A] and very excellent writings, which it is not part of the present purpose to enumerate individually. He wrote, however, *On Modesty*, etc., and several others.

### [NOTE]

[Col. 1135A] Thus far those authors. And indeed, would that the *Index of Septimius Tertullian*, mentioned above in the fragment of the book *On the Garments of Aaron*, were extant! There is no doubt that it would mention many more of his writings.

Whether he wrote additional works against heretics once caused me to hesitate because of what Vincent of Lérins writes of him:

> The Marcions, Apelleses, Praxeases, Hermogeneses, Jews, pagans, Gnostics, and the others know how he overthrew their blasphemies by means of the many great masses of his volumes, as though with thunderbolts.

But when I noticed that the Valentinians and Quintilla were omitted there, together with twenty-five others whose catalogue he recounts in *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, [Col. 1135B] from ch. 45 to the end, as well as those against whom he wrote the latter part of *On the Soul*—Simon the Samaritan, Carpocrates, Menander, and others—I became persuaded that no work of this kind is now missing.

Therefore, I also consider the text of blessed Optatus of Milevis, *Against Parmenianus*, book I, corrupt where it reads:

> Marcion, Praxeas, Sabellius, and the others were in their own times defeated by Victorinus of Poetovio, Zephyrinus of Rome, Tertullian of Carthage down to the Cataphrygians, and by [Col. 1136A] other defenders of the Catholic Church.

For Sabellius began his heresy much later, in the interval between the Novatians and the Donatists, as is manifest even from the age of Dionysius of Alexandria, the first to write against Sabellius. He was a contemporary of blessed Cyprian, but younger. Accordingly, either some other heretic must be supplied in Sabellius’ place, or Optatus’ words must certainly be corrected to “Praxeas, a Sabellian,” because Sabellius partly revived that heresy when he contended that the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit were a single οὐσία (*ousia*, “essence”) and hypostasis of the divinity.

For the rest, to say something also concerning those books wrongly attributed to Tertullian by Erasmus in volume IX of the works of blessed Jerome: we explained above, among the fragments under *Question on Circumcision*, why the book *On True Circumcision* cannot be attributed to him. Still less does the style of the book *To a Sick Friend* resemble his; moreover, Scripture is cited there according to blessed Jerome’s edition. Finally, Tertullian is nowhere said to have written any dialogue.

## [Col. 1135] FRAGMENT: IN WHAT RESPECT TERTULLIAN THE MONTANIST DIFFERED FROM THE PSYCHICS

[Col. 1135B] The author of the book entitled *The Predestined*, book I, which concerns heresies, no. 26, reports the following verbatim:

> Holy Soter, pope of the City, and Apollonius, bishop of the Ephesians, wrote a book against them—the Montanists or Cataphrygians. Against these men Tertullian, [Col. 1135C] a presbyter of Carthage, wrote. Although he wrote everything well, excellently, and incomparably, he made himself blameworthy in this point alone: he defended Montanus, arguing against Soter, the aforesaid pope of the City, and asserting that the reports concerning the blood of an infant were false; that they confessed the Trinity in the unity of the Godhead, granted repentance to the fallen, shared the same mysteries, and observed one Passover with us. “We differ only in this,” he says: “we do not accept second marriages, and we do not reject Montanus’ prophecy concerning the future judgment.” Some object against Tertullian that he derived the soul by transmission—that is, that he said a soul is generated from a soul just as a body is generated from bodies—which the Catholic faith vehemently abhors.

The words of Tertullian that we have just transcribed are not found in his extant works. From this [Col. 1136C] it may be concluded, with Döllinger (*Origins of Christianity*, vol. I, ch. 27, trans. L. Boré), that they were excerpted from some long-lost Montanist work of our author.

Yet anyone, without being prompted, can readily see that Tertullian was not a contemporary opponent of Pope Soter, nor, as some have asserted, were the Tertullianists condemned by that holy pope, since it is clearly established that Tertullian embraced the heresy of Montanus nearly thirty years after Soter’s death.—ED.

## Fifth Appendix. [Col. 1135] NOTES ON TERTULLIAN’S BOOKS *ON BAPTISM* AND *ON REPENTANCE*, BY THE REVEREND FATHER DOM CORBINIAN THOMAS, A BENEDICTINE MONK OF THE CONGREGATION OF THE HOLY SPIRIT IN BAVARIA

## NOTICE

[Col. 1135D]

Dom Corbinian Thomas was born at Augsburg in 1694 and assumed the monastic habit in the imperial monastery of Elchingen in 1715.

For many years he taught the mathematical disciplines at the University of Salzburg, enjoying a great reputation for learning. [Col. 1136D] Afterward, having been appointed professor of sacred theology, he won for himself the admiration of all, as he had before. While laboring diligently in the discharge of these duties, he nevertheless published many most elegant and learned works of various kinds. Their catalogue is presented by Dom Ziegelbauer in his *History of the Literary Affairs of the Order of St. Benedict*, vol. II, p. 220; vol. IV, pp. 265, 304, 311. See also the *General Library of Writers of the Order of St. Benedict*, vol. III, p. 134.
