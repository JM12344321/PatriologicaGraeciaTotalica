Nor do I reject what Caecilius strove to maintain as one of his principal points: that a human being ought to know himself and to look about him to determine what he is, whence he comes, and why he exists—whether he is compounded from the elements, constructed from atoms, or rather made, fashioned, and endowed with life by God. Yet we cannot investigate and uncover this very matter without an inquiry into the universe, since things are so mutually coherent, connected, and concatenated [Col. 0284B] that, unless you have diligently examined the nature of divinity, you cannot understand that of humanity; nor can you conduct public affairs properly unless you have come to know this common polity of all, the world. This is especially so because we differ from wild beasts in this respect: they, bent forward and inclined toward the earth, were born to look out for nothing but food; whereas we, to whom an upright countenance and an upward gaze toward heaven [Col. 0285A] have been given, together with speech and reason, by which we recognize, perceive, and imitate God, are neither permitted nor entitled to remain ignorant of the heavenly brightness that thrusts itself upon our eyes and our senses.^21 For it is tantamount to the greatest sacrilege to seek upon the ground what you ought to find on high.

(XI) All the more, therefore, those who maintain that the entire ordered beauty of this world was not perfected by divine reason, but amassed from certain fragments randomly adhering together, seem to me to possess neither mind, perception, nor indeed eyes themselves. For when you raise your eyes to heaven and survey the things below and around you, what can be so evident, so universally acknowledged, and so transparent as the existence of some divine power possessed of the most excellent intelligence, by which all nature is inspired, moved, nourished, and governed?

Look at heaven itself: how widely it extends! [Col. 0285B] How swiftly it revolves! Whether it is marked out by the stars for the night or illuminated by the sun for the day, you will at once perceive what a marvelous and divine equilibrium of the supreme ruler is present within it. See also how the sun’s circuit produces the year; and see how the moon, by waxing, waning, and undergoing eclipse, completes the month. What need have I to say of the recurring alternations of darkness and light, whereby labor and rest are renewed for us by turns? A fuller discussion of the stars should indeed be left to astrologers—whether because they direct the course of navigation [Col. 0286A] or because they usher in the seasons for plowing and reaping. Each of these things required the supreme craftsman and perfect reason not only in order that it might be created, produced, and arranged, but cannot even be perceived, discerned, or understood without the highest intelligence and reason.

And when the succession of seasons and crops is distinguished by an unvarying variety, does not spring with its flowers, summer with its harvests, the welcome ripeness of autumn,^22 and the necessary olive crop of winter equally bear witness to their author and parent? This order would easily be disturbed unless it rested upon the greatest reason. Again, what great providence there is in inserting the temperate intervals of autumn and spring, lest winter alone should scorch with frost or summer alone sear with heat, so that the hidden and harmless transitions of the returning year might glide along their appointed tracks! [Col. 0286B]

Turn your attention to the sea: it is confined by the boundary of the shore. Look at every kind of tree and see how it is given life from the bowels of the earth. Behold the Ocean: it flows back and forth in alternating tides. Look at the springs: they stream from perennial channels. Observe the rivers: they proceed continually in their well-worn courses. Why should I speak of the suitably arranged straight ridges of mountains, the curves of hills, and the outstretched plains? Or why should I speak of the manifold defenses possessed by living creatures against one another—some armed with horns, some fenced with teeth, some firmly based upon hooves, and some bristling with spines; some secured by swiftness of foot, others by the soaring of wings? [Col. 0287A]

Above all, the very beauty of our form acknowledges God as its craftsman: our upright posture, our upraised countenance, our eyes placed at the summit as though in a watchtower, and all the other senses assembled as though in a citadel.

## CHAPTER XVIII

> **Note: [Col. 0287B]**
>
> **CHAPTER XVIII.—And among ourselves we are found.** *Inter se* is used for *inter nos*, after the manner of the Greeks, who commonly say ἐν ἑαυτοῖς, “among themselves,” in this way. So above, chapter 11: “to announce destruction to heaven and the stars [Col. 0287C] …; yet to promise eternity to themselves, though dead and extinguished, who, like us, are born and perish.” This is rarer among Latin authors. WOPKEN.—Latin writers call it “evocation” when the person of the verb differs from the noun or pronoun. Virgil, *Aeneid* VI, 347: “Each [singular] of us suffer [plural] his own shades.”
>
> **Was it not given by God?** Davis thought that the preceding words “and that the breasts,” etc., could not properly be connected with this unless the neuter plural were read, “were they not given by God?” But you may rightly understand the passage through a very common kind of ellipsis, as though we heard it more fully as “… was it not given?” and likewise “was it also given that the breasts,” etc. So we have already observed in the *Readings on Tully*. Tacitus, *Annals* I, 15: “But money was decreed from the treasury, and that he should wear the triumphal robe during the circus procession.” On this passage J. F. Gronovius should be consulted. Cicero, *Letters to Friends* I, letter 3: “I commend all his affairs to you … and especially that … you treat him in such a way,” etc.; supply “I ask.” Sallust, *Jugurtha*, chapter 29: “And after speaking briefly … about the odium caused by his deed, and that he should be received in surrender … he is received in surrender.” There “he urged,” or something of that kind, must be supplied. [Col. 0287D] Aurelius Victor, *On Illustrious Men*, chapter 1: “to whom he left the kingship to be held in yearly turns, and that they should rule alternately”; supply “he wished.” Novatian, *On Jewish Foods*, chapter 2: “He also introduced a distinction among birds, and which should be judged either unclean or clean”; supply “he distinguished” or “signified.” WOPKEN.
>
> **Provides for.** Since the present tense also occurs in the passage of Cicero, *On Divination* I, p. 375, from which our author’s passage was fashioned, there is no reason to read *consuluit*, “provided for,” with the distinguished Heumann. LINDNER.
>
> **Is deprived.** *Defici* here does not mean “to languish,” as Appel understood it, translating in his German version: “Britain, which is utterly enervated and exhausted by the sun’s heat.” That is plainly the opposite of what Minucius intended. It means “to be forsaken, to lack.” It would certainly have been better if he had followed his predecessor Ablancourt, who translated: “It warms England, which could not be warmed by the sun, with the mild vapors of the sea.” LINDNER.
>
> **The dryness of Egypt, etc.** The manuscript presents these words as follows: [Col. 0288B] “… The river Nile moderates; the Euphrates cultivates Mesopotamia; it compensates for the rains; the river Indus both sows,” etc. Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II: “The Nile irrigates Egypt. The Euphrates makes Mesopotamia fertile. The Indus not only gladdens and softens the fields with water, but even sows them, for it is said to carry a great abundance of seeds along with it.” Minucius reproduced this; I therefore readily allow myself to be persuaded that Minucius’s words have been corrupted by a gloss. For the words “it compensates for the rains” interpret the verb “cultivates.” I would prefer, however, with Heumann, to take them as an interpretation of “moderates the dryness.” L. Next, one should read not *Indus flumen*, “the river Indus,” but *Indum flumen*, as Horace has “the river Rhine” and “the Metaurus,” and Caesar “the river Rhône.” WOWER.—But if the punctuation is changed, unless I am mistaken, the correct reading will be: “The river Nile moderates the dryness of Egypt; the Euphrates cultivates Mesopotamia; the Indus compensates for the rains, and as a river is said both to sow and irrigate the East.” DAVIS.—“The river Nile tempers Egypt; the Euphrates compensates Mesopotamia for the rains; the Indus is said as a river both to sow and irrigate the East.” He added mention of rains to the verb “cultivate,” so that the mode of cultivation employed by the Euphrates might be evident, since otherwise *colere*, “cultivate,” is ambiguous, as it can also mean τῷ *arare*, “to plow.” If it were permissible to transpose the word, I would gladly prefer: “and the Indus compensates”; “the river is said to sow and irrigate the East.” For *pro imbribus*, “for rains,” Pliny prefers the fuller expression *vicem imbrium*, “in place of rains.” GRONOVIUS.
>
> **But if, upon entering.** On this argument see Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, 5, 6, 37.
>
> **Heaven and earth.** This is a gloss added in the margin by someone who wished to explain “this house of the world.” Had that annotator observed this, he certainly would not have thought that Minucius wrote: “Perceiving providence, order, law, you see.” LINDNER.
>
> **Unless perhaps.** Someone might readily believe that Minucius wrote *nisi forte*, since the alternative expression is not customary. HEUMANN.
>
> **Of which there are surely examples from heaven.** Supply “there are.” Heumann deletes “of which” and understands the passage to mean: “Earthly governments are examples from heaven,” that is, they were formed in the likeness of heavenly government. But Latin usage stands in the way. For an *exemplum* is not something formed in the likeness of something else; rather, an *exemplum* is the pattern of whatever subject is under discussion—a pattern that remains always and everywhere self-consistent and of which several copies may be produced. Hence he preferred to say “examples” in the plural, because several earthly governments were formed after the example of heavenly government. Lindner properly refutes the proposed substitution of the singular *exemplar*, “model.”
>
> **Or ended without bloodshed.** The opposition is far more suitable if you read *desiit*, “ended,” rather than *discessit*, “dissolved,” which is found in the Roman edition and in Heraldus. That this should be emended accordingly is confirmed by the fact that *On the Vanity of Idols*, chapter 5, also uses the verb “ended” when reproducing these words of Minucius. WOPKEN.
>
> **I pass over the Persians.** We are directed back to Book I, chapter 10, where it is merely said that the Persians agreed among themselves to bring all their horses before the palace at dawn on a fixed day, and that the man whose horse first uttered a neigh at sunrise should be king. As though that passage helped us understand Minucius, when instead Book X, chapter 1 should have been indicated, where Justin reports from Trogus that Artaxerxes, while still alive, made his son Darius king out of indulgence, thinking that nothing was taken from himself when conferred upon his son, and that he would derive a purer joy from fatherhood if, while still alive, he saw the insignia of majesty upon his son. But Darius formed a plan to kill his father. Behold a partnership in kingship that did not end without bloodshed! Yet it is irrelevant to cite it, because in the election it was not reason but the neighing of horses that decided. LINDNER.
>
> **And as for the Theban pair, I pass over the dead fable.** We have punctuated the passage thus, on the authority of the Royal manuscript and as the sense demands. Minucius undoubtedly wrote “the pair of Thebans,” nor did Cyprian read it otherwise when he spoke of “the brotherhood of the Theban brothers.” Statius speaks of “the unspeakable pair, the scepter fatal to twin tyrants, kindred crime, and the mighty war of a single womb.” But why does Minucius call it a “dead fable,” when shortly before his time it had been celebrated throughout Statius’s entire *Thebaid*? Perhaps he wrote *mutuam fabulam*, “the fable of mutual exchange.” For the brothers [Col. 0289C] Eteocles and Polynices had agreed to reign in alternating years, and each brought reciprocal destruction upon the other; hence it was a “fable of mutual exchange.” But the authority of another copy must be awaited. RIGALTIUS.—I should indeed have printed the text in this way with Rigaltius, since it comes closest to the reading of the ancient manuscript; I am surprised that the same most learned man, not adequately understanding it, immediately abandons it. Our author cites the example merely as an example, and by no means guarantees its truth. Hence he calls it both a fable and “dead,” that is, already obsolete and without force or efficacy, so that it cannot furnish valid assistance in demonstration—just as Plato in the *Phaedo* says ἐνδυμίωνα λῆρον, “an Endymion tale.” Nor is it otherwise in Virgil’s *Culex*:
>
> “Here too is the ancient tale, destined never to die throughout the ages:
>
> how Curtius, once from the midst of the city,
>
> devoted to war, was swallowed by an inundating chasm.”
>
> See Scaliger there, p. 35. GRONOVIUS.—I read and punctuate the whole passage: “I pass over the dead pair of Thebans and its fable; because of the kingdom of shepherds and a hut, the tale … is exceedingly well known.” MEURSIUS.—“The prematurely dead fable of the Thebans.” ELMENHORST.—“The fable of the Theban brothers,” according to an anonymous critic in *New Miscellaneous Critical Observations*, vol. I, p. 138: “the fabulous pair of brothers.” That is, Minucius does not believe the narrative to be true. He therefore immediately contrasts it with the history of Romulus and Remus. In the same way Horace, *Odes* I.22, called the Hydaspes “fabulous.” HEUMANN.—This discussion should be concluded in a few words—indeed, with the single word that is read most clearly in the Royal manuscript: *permortuam*, “utterly dead,” that is, an entirely empty and vanished fable. EDITORS.
>
> **History.** The Roman edition has *memoria*, “recollection,” as does the Royal manuscript presented by Heraldus.—“Because of the kingdom of shepherds and a hut, the story of the twins is exceedingly well known.” Jerome, treatise IX, part III, Letter 39, *To the Monk Rusticus, On Contempt for the World*: “When Rome was founded, it could not have two brothers as kings at the same time.” See also Lucan I, 92 ff. ELMENHORST.
>
> **Wars between son-in-law and father-in-law.** Pompey and Caesar; hence the familiar saying: “Father-in-law and son-in-law, you have destroyed everything.” RIGALTIUS.
>
> **Fortune did not contain two.** One is moved to wonder why Johannes Meursius substituted, “one lot did not contain two,” for Minucius was plainly looking to Lucan I, 109 ff.:
>
> “The fortune of a mighty people
>
> did not contain two.”
>
> DAVIS.—It is more likely, however, that he looked to Florus IV.2.14: “Thus they struggled for supremacy, as though the fortune of so great an empire could not contain two.” There Florus, as elsewhere, imitates Lucan; Minucius reproduces Florus, as we shall also see below at chapter 37. WOPKEN.—Heumann thinks Minucius wrote “fortune, two.” But transpositions of this kind are frequent in our author: chapter 31, “because of this fable the Gauls it,” for “the Gauls, because of this fable”; chapter 7, “at once you will discover them to have initiated,” for “to have initiated them”; chapter 9, “the head of an ass, a most disgraceful animal,” for “the ass’s head”; chapter 12, “worthy of pity, but of our gods,” for “but worthy of the pity of our gods.” LINDNER.
>
> **Among flocks.** Heumann deletes *in*, because “one king among bees” precedes. There is no need. Cyprian similarly says in *On the Vanity of Idols* V: “There is one king among bees and one leader among flocks.” Varro, *On Agriculture* I.2: “That which at the circus games marks the end of the final chariot race; that which in the procession of Ceres is customarily first.” LINDNER.
>
> **Would you believe the supreme power in heaven, the entire power…?** Minucius did not write it thus, for to repeat “power” in this fashion is the stammering of infancy. It therefore seems to me that the first occurrence of *potestatem* flowed from a corrupt hand. Deleting that word, I accordingly read: “Would you believe that the supreme authority in heaven is divided, and that the entire power of that true and divine empire is rent apart?” *Summa* denotes that which is first and foremost within the class of things under discussion. Velleius Paterculus II.76: “Born from the most obscure beginnings, he had little … to have received the supreme power.” Ovid, *Fasti* V, 71:
>
> “Romulus saw this, and called selected men ‘Fathers’:
>
> to them the supreme charge of the new city was referred.”
>
> Gronovius substitutes “majesty” or “authority”; Heumann, “unity.” Neither is necessary. Compare chapters VIII and IX, and Berger’s discussion of the pleasing repetition of words, *On the Natural Beauty of Oratory*, p. 239. [Col. 0290D] LINDNER.
>
> **By his word he commands, he completes.** Unless Minucius violated the laws of grammar, one must read: “who commands by his word all things whatsoever that exist, orders them by reason, and brings them to completion by power,” just as earlier: “who grants birth to all things …; who existed before the world.” DAVIS.
>
> **Can … is purer than touch.** I have restored the first word, omitted by Jacob Ouzel and Christopher Cellarius, from the early printed editions; the remaining words, absent from the manuscript, were restored from Cyprian by Johannes Meursius and Fulvius Ursinus—and rightly so. For the bishop of Carthage, in *On the Vanity of Idols* 5 §5, copies our author, just as our author modeled his own words on Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 17. DAVIS.—**Is purer than touch.** In place of these words the Royal manuscript has *potest*, “can.” Yet it is proved that these words are rightly inserted in its place: first, because otherwise the reason God cannot be comprehended would be missing; second, because Cyprian, Minucius’s epitomator, has these words in chapter 5; third, because Theodorus Canterus testifies in *Various Readings* I.7, published in 1574, that he produced these words from an ancient handwritten manuscript; fourth, because *potest* is rather disagreeable here and perhaps arose because a copyist, after omitting *tactu*, “by touch,” wrote *purior est*, “is purer.” When he did not understand this on rereading the passage, he changed it to *potest*, which had previously occurred after the verb “to be seen.” LINDNER.
>
> **He who does not wish to diminish.** It is astonishing, says Heumann, that this *non* held its place here for so long. Without it, the words would have this sense: God is unknown to the person who wishes to diminish God; for if he had discovered God’s majesty, he would never think of diminishing God. But according to the received reading, the sense is: “He who does not wish to diminish God’s greatness and think of it in an excessively humble and abject manner does not know it,” that is, he professes that he does not know this greatness. Which sense should be preferred is readily apparent. LINDNER.—On the contrary, the customary reading has an obvious and excellent sense: namely, God is diminished by the person who presumes that he comprehends and knows him; the person who does not wish to reduce God to the narrow comprehension of his own mind does not dare to do this. To know God is to diminish him; he who does not diminish him does not know him. EDITORS.
>
> **Seek for God.** I do not agree with Meursius and others, who, following Cyprian’s *On the Vanity of Idols*, chapter 5, substitute “the name of God”; for the Verona manuscript vindicates the dative case *Deo* even in Cyprian, and Justin Martyr, *First Apology*, chapter 80, defends the σύνταξις, “construction”: Ὄνομα γὰρ τῷ ἀρρήτῳ Θεῷ οὐδεὶς ἔχει εἰπεῖν, “For no one is able to utter a name for the ineffable God.” The ancient Christians commonly expressed the same thought. Justin likewise says in *Second Apology*, chapter 6, p. 12: Ὄνομα δὲ τῷ πάντων πατρὶ θετὸν, ἀγεννήτῳ ὄντι, οὐκ ἔστιν· ᾧ γὰρ ἂν καὶ ὀνόματι προσαγορεύηται, πρεσβύτερον ἔχει τὸν θέμενον τὸ ὄνομα, “There is no imposed name for the Father of all, since he is unbegotten; for whatever name he might be called by, he has as prior to himself the one who imposed that name.” [Col. 0291D] There one must certainly read ὃ γὰρ ἂν ὀνόματι, as will become evident to anyone who pays attention. See also *Exhortation to the Greeks*, chapter 20; Lactantius I.6 and *On the Anger of God*, chapter 2, from which it is clear that the Platonists preceded the Christians in this. Maximus of Tyre, *Dissertation* I, p. 12: “He does not state a name for him—that is, for God—because he does not know one”; shortly afterward he calls the divinity ἄρρητον φωνῇ, “ineffable in speech,” and in *Dissertation* XXXVII, ἀνώνυμον νομοθέτην, “the nameless lawgiver.” DAVIS.—The genitive *Dei*, “of God,” is governed by the substantive “name”; but the dative *Deo*, “for God,” is governed by the verb “seek,” with the same meaning. LINDNER.
>
> **If I should call him Father.** The epithets “earthly” and “fleshly” would undoubtedly be more suitably transposed thus: “If I should call him Father, you may suppose him fleshly; if King, you may suspect him earthly.” WOPKEN.
>
> **I hear the common people say.** Cortius, on Cicero III, letter 1.3, establishes the construction of verbs of perception with the infinitive in place of the indicative by sufficiently numerous examples. Hence the emendations of Heumann, who orders us to write “I hear it commonly said,” and those of Wopken—“The ignorant crowd say nothing else”; or “I hear those stretching out their hands say nothing”; or “Thus the common people, when they stretch them out, say nothing…”—collapse of themselves. LINDNER.
>
> [Col. 0292B] **But concerning.** Who with learning above that of the common crowd will not see that “but” must be cast out? In such a sequence of words Latin authors do indeed understand “but,” but do not express it. HEUMANN.

It would take too long to go through every detail. There is no member in the human body that does not serve both necessity and beauty; and, what is more marvelous, all possess the same general shape, yet certain individual features vary in each person. Thus we all appear alike, and yet as individuals are found dissimilar from one another.

What of the principle of birth? What of the desire for procreation? Was it not given by God? And was it not likewise given that the breasts should fill with milk as childbirth approaches, and that the tender offspring [Col. 0287B] should grow through the abundance of milky dew? God provides not only for the universe as a whole but also for its parts. Britain [Col. 0288A] is deprived of the sun, but is refreshed by the warmth of the surrounding sea. The river Nile moderates Egypt’s dryness; the Euphrates cultivates Mesopotamia; the Indus compensates for rains and is said both to sow and irrigate the East.

If, upon entering some house, you saw everything cultivated, arranged, and adorned, you would surely believe that a master presided over it and that he was far better than those good things. So too, in this house of the world, when you behold heaven and earth, providence, order, and law, believe that there is a lord and parent of the universe, more beautiful than the stars themselves and all the parts of the world.

Unless perhaps—since there is no doubt about providence—you think it necessary to inquire whether the heavenly kingdom is governed by the authority of one or the judgment of many. This very question is not difficult to resolve for anyone who reflects upon earthly governments, which surely have their models from heaven. When has a partnership in royal power ever begun in good faith or ended without bloodshed? [Col. 0288B–0289A] I pass over the Persians, who divined sovereignty from the neighing of horses; and I pass over the utterly dead fable of the Thebans. Because of a kingdom of shepherds and a hut, the story of the twins is exceedingly well known. The wars between son-in-law and father-in-law spread throughout the whole world, and fortune could not contain two rulers of so great an empire.

Look at everything else: among bees there is one king, among flocks one leader, and among herds one guide. Would you believe that the supreme power in heaven is divided [Col. 0290A] and the entire authority of that true and divine empire torn apart, when it is manifest that God, the parent of all, has neither beginning nor end? He grants birth to all things, but perpetuity to himself; he existed before the world and was himself a world unto himself; he commands by his word all things whatsoever that exist, orders them by reason, and brings them to completion by power.

He cannot be seen, for he is brighter than sight; he cannot be comprehended, for he is purer than touch; he cannot [Col. 0291A] be estimated, for he is greater than the senses. He is infinite and immeasurable, and is known in the fullness of his being to himself alone. Our understanding, however, is too narrow to comprehend him; and therefore we estimate him worthily only when we call him inestimable.

I shall express what I think: he who supposes that he knows God’s greatness diminishes it; he who does not wish to diminish it does not know it. Do not seek a name for God: “God” is his name. Names are needed where a multitude must be separated into individuals by the distinctive marks of proper designations; for God, who alone exists, the word “God” is the whole of his designation. If I should call him Father, you may suppose him earthly; if King, you may suspect him fleshly; if Lord, you will surely understand him as mortal. Remove the additions made to his names, and you will perceive his radiance.

What of the fact that I have universal agreement concerning him? I hear the common people, when they stretch their hands toward heaven, [Col. 0291B] say nothing other than “GOD,” and “GOD IS GREAT,” and “GOD IS TRUE,” and “IF GOD GRANTS IT.” Is this the natural speech of the common people, or the declaration of a confessing Christian? Those who maintain that Jupiter is supreme are mistaken in the name, but agree that there is a single power.

I also hear the poets proclaiming “one Father of gods and human beings,” and declaring that “the mind of mortals is such as the day that the parent of all has brought.”

## CHAPTER XIX

> **Note: [Col. 0292B]**
>
> **The poets proclaiming.** Heumann substitutes the singular, “the poet proclaiming.” But the discussion is by no means solely about Homer, as that most learned man maintains, although Homer is customarily called “the Poet” κατ’ ἐξοχήν, “preeminently”; it also concerns Ennius, whose words are: “one Father of gods and human beings.” Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II.2. And likewise Ennius:
>
> “… Father both of gods and human beings.”
>
> In the same work, chapter 25: “Jupiter himself is called by the poets the Father both of gods and human beings.” LINDNER.
>
> **Such as the day he has brought.** The Royal manuscript reads “directed the day”; and from this we think that the true reading can be elicited. We therefore believe that the text originally read: “such as the day into which the parent of all has brought him.” Homer, *Odyssey* XVIII, 136:
>
> Οἷον ἐπ’ ἦμαρ ἄγῃσι πατὴρ ἀνδρῶν τε θεῶν τε,
>
> “Such as the day that the Father of men and gods brings upon him.”
>
> HERALDUS.—The manuscript reads: “Such as the day that the parent of all has brought,” except that, through a slight scribal deviation, *duxerit* appears. It should therefore be read as we have printed it: “such as the day that the parent of all has brought”—οἷον ἐπάγῃσι ἦμαρ, “such as the day he brings upon him.” The mind is such as the day—the day on which the mind came forth. This is what Homer and Cicero’s translation, quoted by Augustine in *The City of God* V.8, mean. RIGALTIUS. Heumann prefers “has brought forth into the day.”—I would prefer “has brought upon him,” with Davis. GALLAND.
>
> **Nearer.** J. Wowerus needlessly substitutes “nearer to the truth”; *proximius* here means “more immediately credible,” as J. F. Gronovius perceived in *Observations* I.24, p. 191. DAVIS.—Heumann also substitutes “more distinctly.” But the question is not how distinctly all things were created, but who created them at all—whether the father of the gods, or, what approaches nearer to Christian truth, a spirit. LINDNER.
>
> **More truly.** I do not agree with Heumann’s substitution of “more richly.” So below, chapter 36: “we shall discuss it elsewhere both more truly and more fully.” Lactantius IV.30: “later we shall contend more fully and more truly”; there “more truly” is supported by the Wolfenbüttel, Gotha, Wittenberg, two Leipzig, English, and Reims manuscripts, and by every edition before Gallaeus. Far fewer acknowledge “more richly.” The same author, *On the Workmanship of God* XX.1: “Then I shall exhort you both more plainly and more truly.” Hyginus, *Poetical Astronomy*, preface: “Pursuing those matters that had been stated rather obscurely by Aratus, we have demonstrated them more plainly … I have ensured that no one could speak either more briefly or more truly.” Compare throughout Bunemann’s note on Lactantius VI.30. LINDNER.
>
> **In the beginning.** Virgil, *Aeneid* VI, 724 ff.
>
> **For these are the words.** That is, Virgil’s very words. [Col. 0293B] The Royal manuscript has “true.” For what Virgil adduces—that God is mind and spirit—is true. LINDNER.—**For these are his words.** The more recent editors have printed the text thus following Fulvius Ursinus. DAVIS.
>
> **For God.** The first verse together with the half-line occurs in *Georgics* IV, 221; the latter is taken from *Aeneid* I, 747, except that Virgil, for metrical reasons, reads: “Whence comes the race of human beings and cattle.” Here the poet portrays God according to the thought of the Pythagoreans. Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I.2: “Pythagoras held that a mind extended and circulated throughout the whole nature of things, from which our own minds were drawn”; from this statement concerning Pythagoras, Minucius and Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* I.5, derived their own account. >
> **Ho! Higher and more sublime.** Those who edited Minucius before this do not seem to have grasped his meaning, nor indeed did they perceive what the ancient copy suggested, being too trusting of the Roman edition. Following it they printed: “And the account of water and spirit is higher and more sublime than could have been discovered by a human being; it was handed down by God.” The manuscript reads: “Eo altior et a Deo traditum.” Once the aspiration and punctuation are restored, sense returns to a clause that otherwise coheres badly. After ascribing to Caecilius the opinion of Thales, which nearly agrees with that of Moses concerning God and the created world, he pauses with the interjection *Eho!*—“Ho!” Then, considering the sublimity of the doctrine, he says that it is too elevated to have been discovered or conceived by a human being; and immediately makes the absolute declaration, “It was handed down by God”—through Moses, namely, from whom Thales had received it. RIGALTIUS.—J. F. Gronovius, *Ecclesiastical Observations*, chapter 7, p. 80, thinks these words either crept into the text from the margin or were transposed and should be read in this order: “You see that the opinion of the foremost philosopher agrees profoundly with us. But it is higher and more sublime,” etc. Cellarius follows that great man too incautiously in every respect; for it is an ἐκφώνημα παρενθετικόν, “parenthetical exclamation,” and should retain its own place. Those most learned men were misled by the absurd opinion that Christian παλιγγενεσία, “regeneration,” or Baptism, was denoted by water and spirit, whereas Minucius was referring to Genesis 1:2, 9; see J. Le Clerc’s notes there. Jacob Gronovius further thinks that some corruption lies here because “higher” and “more sublime” are joined, although they do not differ from each other but mean precisely the same thing. Novatian, however, likewise says in *On the Trinity*, chapter 2: “God, who is more sublime than every sublimity and higher than every height.” Cyprian, *To Donatus*, p. 9: “Higher than the ground and more sublime than all this earthly power.” Let the customary reading therefore remain safe and intact. DAVIS.

> **But according to Anaxagoras.** Buhier, in Le Clerc’s *Ancient and Modern Library*, vol. XVI, p. 222, judges Minucius’s words to be corrupt: first, because God is not the motion of an infinite mind, but the infinite mind itself, which gives motion and arrangement to things; second, because Octavius confuses the craftsman with his work. He therefore restores the passage thus: “According to Anaxagoras, the arrangement and measure of things are said to be the work of an infinite mind.” For the word “God” may have been an explanation of “infinite mind” written in the margin and afterward admitted into the text. The passage of Cicero reproduced by our author occurs in *On the Nature of the Gods* I.11: “Anaxagoras maintained that the arrangement and motion—others read ‘measure’—of all things are determined and produced by the power and reason of an infinite mind.” In place of *motus*, “motion,” Buhier reads *modus*, “measure,” and understands it as the order and adornment that has given place to the variety perceived in this universe. In addition, Augustine, Letter 118.24, appears to have read *modus* in Cicero. Davis, who vigorously champions the contrary view, [Col. 0294C] appeals to the sequence of the argument and to the testimonies of Aristotle, Eusebius, and Hermias, all of whom join arrangement and motion when reporting Anaxagoras’s opinion. Heumann agrees with Buhier. LINDNER.

> **Is derived.** The manuscript corruptly reads *capitatur*. Hence Meursius is eager to substitute *captatur*, “is grasped.” Here are Lactantius’s words, I.5: “Pythagoras thus defined what God was: a mind that circulates and is diffused through every part of the world and all nature, from which all living creatures that are born derive life.” HEUMANN.
>
> **A natural, animate power.** Delete “natural,” which is not found in the passage of Cicero that Minucius had before his eyes. Evidently the copyist, his eye wandering, repeated the word “natural,” which had occurred shortly before, and then declined to delete it lest he introduce a blemish into his manuscript. Davis discussed this scribal custom admirably above at chapter 16; and Davis himself, in his note on Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I.13, thinks that “natural” should be eliminated from this passage of Minucius. HEUMANN.—Wopken conjectures that one should perhaps read, “a power or animate nature”; our author did not intend to reproduce Cicero exactly and word for word throughout. LINDNER.
>
> **To acknowledge as God.** Fulvius Ursinus and Heumann read *esse*, “to be,” in place of *nosse*, “to acknowledge.” Yet if you take “to acknowledge someone as God” in the same sense in which we say “to acknowledge an excuse,” the received reading is sound. LINDNER.

> **What of Democritus?** The learned Arntzenius, on Pliny’s *Panegyric* II.7, punctuates: “What? Democritus, although,” etc. But no change in punctuation is needed, unless one prefers: “What of Democritus, although … the inventor? Does he not…?” So above in this chapter: “What of Mantuan Maro?” and in chapter 7: “What of the principle of birth?” See also chapters 21 and 26, twice. WOPKEN.

> **First inventor.** In Lactantius III.17, the invention of the doctrine of atoms is ascribed to Leucippus. Something about the antiquity of that philosophy may be seen in Burnet’s *Archaeology* I.7. Burnet adopts the opinion of those who think this hypothesis owed its origin to Leucippus [Col. 0295B] or Democritus. Cudworth demonstrated, I believe, that it should be assigned a greater antiquity. In *The Intellectual System* I.1 he says that Pythagoras, Empedocles, Anaxagoras, Parmenides, Ecphantus, and Arcesilaus—or Archelaus, as Sidonius Apollinaris attests of him—favored this opinion, as did the majority of the ancient natural philosophers before Democritus; but Democritus and Leucippus were the first atomist atheists, while the supporters of atoms before Democritus were incorporealists and theists. *Miscellaneous Critical Observations*, vol. IV, tome III, p. 59. [Col. 0295C] LINDNER.
>
> **Which pours forth images.** These are *simulacra*, εἴδωλα, “images” or “eidola,” by whose impact Democritus thought vision was produced. See Diogenes Laërtius IX §44; Cicero, *On Divination* II, p. 4903, and *On the Nature of the Gods* I.12. DAVIS.
>
> **And nature itself.** That is, he says that nature is God. LINDNER.
>
> **He imagines gods who are either idle or nonexistent.** Minucius speaks thus because one who abolishes Providence must be judged to deny God’s existence. For, as Salvian elegantly says in *On the Governance of God* IV, p. 116: “Those who affirm that God observes nothing come close, when they deprive him of sight, to taking away his very substance as well.” Hence it came about that some thought Epicurus, lest he incur the displeasure of the Athenians, left the gods in existence in words but abolished them in fact, as Cicero testifies in *On the Nature of the Gods* I.30; they accordingly accused him of ἀθεότης, “atheism.” See Aelian, *Various History* II.31, and Theophilus, *To Autolycus* III, p. 121. DAVIS.

> **Aristotle of Pontus varies, assigning supremacy at one time to the world and at another to the divine mind.** These words are a pure and simple gloss. MEURSIUS.

> [Col. 0295D] **Mind, now soul.** Heumann reads, “now mind and soul,” but there is no need. It ought to be “now mind, now soul.” Yet our author quite often omits once a word that ought to be placed three times: chapter 8, “so ancient, useful, so beneficial.” Or, if anything must be changed, I would prefer, “mind now, now soul.” Davis should not be followed when, on Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I.14, he judges that the text should read, “for he calls the world, now soul.” LINDNER.
>
> **Discussed.** Read “discusses,” for present-tense verbs precede and follow. DAVIS.
>
> **A divine power.** That is, he believes a power—namely a divine one—to be God. LINDNER.
>
> **A discipline of exposition.** Heumann judges these words foreign to Latin ears and therefore, though doubtfully, substitutes: “For the Babylonian Diogenes, his disciple, approves of him when discussing it,” etc. But we have *disciplina dicendi*, “the discipline of speaking,” in Cicero, *For Archias* I.2; “the discipline of undertaking public affairs” in *For Sestius*, chapter 6; and “the discipline of understanding” in the same author’s *Orator* 71. “I possess a discipline” is equivalent to “I teach” or “I profess.” Just as “the discipline of speaking” is rhetoric, so I can say, “I possess the discipline of speaking,” that is, “I speak or profess rhetoric”; “I possess the discipline of undertaking public affairs,” that is, “I undertake public affairs or profess politics”; “I possess the discipline of understanding,” that is, “I understand, or sharpen my understanding by rules, or profess logic.” In the same way, “I possess the discipline of expounding and discussing” means “I expound and discuss,” or “I profess the doctrine that discusses how the account of the gods is to be brought within natural philosophy.” [Col. 0296C] LINDNER.
>
> **Of other things**, the manuscript reads; hence Meursius substitutes “of certain things.” Fulvius Ursinus and Christopher Cellarius are no less mistaken in preferring “that this class of things exists,” etc., for without any necessity they omitted a suitable word. From the manuscript’s *caetera tum*, Davis makes *caetera tantum*, “the other things only.” GRONOVIUS.
