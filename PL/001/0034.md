[Note: [Col. 0303D] **On account of gold no limb is light.** She appears like some Zenobia led in triumphal procession at Rome, who was so burdened by the weight of her ornaments that she struggled beneath them, as Trebellius Pollio writes of her; indeed, a woman often carries the entire patrimony of her ancestors upon the smallest parts of her body. Tertullian describes this splendidly in *On the Apparel of Women*, chap. IX:

“A tender neck bears forests and islands. The delicate skin of the ears expends a whole account-book, and the left hand plays upon each separate finger with the contents of separate moneybags. Since women’s minds are therefore enslaved to such things, Cato inveighs against them in Livy, book XXXIV:

“What honorable object, even in words, is put forward as a pretext for this women’s agitation? ‘That we may glitter in gold and purple,’ they say”; and shortly afterward: “That there may be no limit to expense or luxury.”

In the same passage Lucius Valerius judges that indulgence should be granted them in these matters:

“Neatness, ornament, and adornment—these are women’s distinctions. In these they delight and glory; this our ancestors called a woman’s world.”

[Col. 0304A] Gregory of Nazianzus, *Against Women*, mocks such excesses, affirming that no beauty is added because “they bind hands and feet with golden chains and enjoy a servile glory.” Paul teaches in 1 Timothy II [Col. 0304B] 9 that women’s adornment is conspicuous: ἐν καταστολῇ κοσμίῳ, μετὰ αἰδοῦς καὶ σωφροσύνης κοσμεῖν ἑαυτᾶς, μὴ ἐν πλέγμασιν, ἢ χρυσῳ, ἢ μαργαρίταις (“to adorn themselves in respectable attire, with modesty and self-restraint, not with braided hair, gold, or pearls”).

Hence Zeno the Martyr, *Sermon II on Avarice*, condemns those ornaments as particularly unbecoming in Christians:

“Handmaid of Christ, do you reject a false idol? Believe me, you worship upon yourself the one whose ornament and whose image you do not put off. You proceed to the Church of God with your whole body gilded by elaborate workmanship, burdened with accursed metal—delicate everywhere, yet strong beneath the loads”—

(I correct this to *molibus*, “loads,” in place of *mollibus*, “soft things,” which stands in my copy; the following words sufficiently prove it)—

“and because of the very rigidity of your adornment you do not bend in prayer; you do not stretch out your hands; you disdain to prostrate a breast swollen with necklaces. Indeed, you bend your neck not from religion but from weight; when will you perform ἐξομολόγησιν (“confession”), you who are more concerned for adornment than for salvation?”

And yet this madness has also invaded men, as Micyllus says of himself in Lucian’s *The Cock*, p. 245: ἐγὼ δὲ, τὴν ἐσθῆτα τὴν ἐκείνου ἔχων, καὶ δακτυλίους βαρεῖς, ὃσον, ἑκκαίδεκα ἐξημμένους, τῶν δακτύλων (“Meanwhile I wore his garment and had heavy rings—about sixteen—hanging from my fingers”); that is: “Meanwhile I was clothed in his garment and wore heavy rings inserted upon about sixteen fingers.” Indeed, rings were forged in lighter and heavier forms, for summer and winter.]

[Note: [Col. 0304C] **On account of wine no kiss is freely given.** Chrysostom treats most eloquently of how disgraceful this is in a woman, in Homily XXVII on Acts XIII:

“Nothing is fouler than a woman who lives in luxury, nothing more shameful than one who is drunk. The bloom of her appearance is dimmed; the serenity and purity of her eyes are disturbed, as though a cloud had passed over the rays of the sun. It is an illiberal and servile occupation, full of every baseness. How unpleasant is a woman breathing wine, reeking of what is stale and corrupted, belching up the moisture of corrupted flesh, weighed down and unable to rise, redder than she ought to be, full of yawning and thick mist.”

You may find this in precisely the same words in Homily LIV *To the People of Antioch*, excerpted from that passage. Valerius Maximus VI.III.9 certainly speaks truly:

“And indeed every woman who immoderately craves the use of wine closes the door to all virtues and opens it to pleasures.”

For, according also to Lucian, *On the Syrian Goddess*, p. 892, [Col. 0304D] Ἃμα δὲ οἴνῳ (“And together with the wine …”), etc.; that is: “Together with the entrance of wine comes confidence in speaking; and it is not especially shameful to have failed to obtain one’s object, since every detail of what is done is consigned to oblivion.”]

[Note: [Col. 0305B] Junius reads “was concerned for.”]

[Note: [Col. 0306C] **To Serapis, now become Roman.** Thus the native brilliance of our author’s antiquity must be restored, as I have also shown above. Shortly afterward read, as printed, “now become Italian.” Lucan elegantly says in I.63, addressing Nero:

> But to me you are a god already;

and in IX.158 the fierce Pompeian, intending to avenge his ancestral ashes, threatens in these words:

> I shall cast forth from her shrine Isis, now a goddess to the nations.

These passages—and many others like them throughout all the ancient writers—must be corrected in this fashion. Seneca the tragedian likewise says in *Hercules Furens*, line 2, “the ever-foreign [Col. 0306D] Jupiter.”

## CHAPTER VII

**ARGUMENT.—He therefore challenges them to prove that those whom they consider guilty of the gravest crimes are indeed infant-killers, devourers of children, and finally incestuous persons, and urges them no longer heedlessly to believe rumor, whose origin is often false and whose report uncertain.**

[Col. 0306B] We are called the most criminal of men because of a sacrament involving infanticide, the meal that follows from it, and incest after the banquet—an incest procured by dogs that overturn the lights, acting, of course, as pimps for the darkness and for the shamelessness of impious lusts.

[Note: [Col. 0306B] Junius omits “because of.”]

[Note: [Col. 0306B] Rhenanus reads “a meal of raw flesh.”]

[Note: [Col. 0306D] **And the meal that follows from it.** Some books read, “And a meal of raw flesh,” as though Christians had been charged with feeding upon the raw flesh of a slaughtered infant. Tertullian himself seems to indicate this in *To the Nations* I, when he says: “Yet who ever came upon his own child half eaten?” Here, however, he speaks only of feeding on blood and of bread by which the blood’s gore is gathered up; nor, among what he afterward presents as the instructions of the father of the rites, does he say anything about devouring raw flesh. Nor does he do so in the corresponding passage of *To the Nations*, but speaks of “bread broken in blood, and blood in which the bread is saturated.” Indeed, he says that to convict the Christians they ought to have produced not only child-killers but also cooks: “Let the child-killers and cooks be produced, and the very dogs that procure the unions; the case would be settled.” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0307B] **Dogs that overturn the lights.** People believed that candlesticks were tied to the dogs and were overturned when the animals leapt toward bones thrown before them. See the following chapter. Among Christians of a later age we read of candles being cast down and extinguished during excommunications, a practice illustrated by Lord Du Cange with many examples in his *Glossary of Medieval and Late Latin*. But I do not think this custom was in use in Tertullian’s age.]

[Note: [Col. 0307A] Rigaltius emended from a manuscript: “procure darkness, and then the shamelessness of impious lusts.” Junius deletes “impious.”]

[Note: [Col. 0307C] **Procure shamelessness.** Lucian in *The Lapiths*: ἀλλὰ πολλὰ ἐπράχθη, καὶ δεινὰ ἐν τῷ σκότῳ (“but many dreadful deeds were committed in the darkness”), etc.; that is: “And many serious offenses were committed in the darkness. When at last someone brought a lamp, Alcidamas—for he was a Cynic philosopher—was caught lifting the flute-girl’s garment and preparing to assault her by force.”]

[Note: [Col. 0307A] Wouwer and Havercamp read: “darkness, then the shamelessness of impious lusts they procure.”]

Yet we are always merely *said* to do these things, and you take no care to discover the truth of what has so long been said of us. Therefore either discover it, if you believe it, or cease believing it, since you have not discovered it. From your own deliberate neglect we enter the preliminary objection against you that what even you yourselves do not dare to investigate does not exist.

You prescribe a very different task for the executioner in dealing with Christians: not that they should say what they do, but that they should deny what they are.

[Note: [Col. 0307C] **That they should deny what they are.** “But,” says Tertullian, “do not claim that Christians are condemned after confessing their own shameful conduct. That is so false that by torture you compel them to deny this name, whereas, if they were participants in a crime, its confession ought by every means to be extorted.” Cyprian, following in the footsteps of his master Septimius, says to Demetrianus, p. 256:

“Accused persons who deny that they are implicated in the crime charged against them are tortured, so that the truth of the offense, which is not brought forth by the informer’s voice, may be expressed through bodily pain. But now, when I voluntarily profess and cry aloud, and repeatedly attest again and again that I am a Christian, why do you apply torture to one who confesses? I ought to have been tortured if I denied it.” HAV.]

The origin of this teaching, as we have already stated, is from the reign of Tiberius. Truth began together with hatred of itself; as soon as it appeared, it became an enemy.

[Note: [Col. 0307D] **The origin of this teaching.** “Origin,” “assessment,” “profession”; our author often uses the word thus. See chap. V.]

[Note: [Col. 0307D] **Truth began together with hatred of itself**, etc. The sense is: as soon as truth appeared, it began together with hatred of itself and became an enemy. They therefore not only hold it in hatred but also persecute it as hostile. Junius punctuates thus: “It is from Tiberius, together with hatred of itself.” The Montpellier collation: “Truth began together with hatred of itself, and at the same time is an enemy.” Barraeus: “Truth took hold; as soon as it appeared to exist.” The Leiden parchment manuscript reads: “From Tiberius”; it deletes τό (the Greek article introducing the quoted word “is”) “is.” “Truth began together with hatred of itself; at the same time it appeared and is an enemy.” I read: “Truth began with hatred of itself: as soon as it appeared, it is an enemy,” that is, the moment it appeared. It may also be punctuated thus: “The teaching began together with hatred of itself; truth, as soon as it appeared, is an enemy.”]

It has as many enemies as it has outsiders: the Jews are enemies in consequence of rivalry, the soldiers through extortion, and even the members of our own households through nature.

[Note: [Col. 0307B] The Paris manuscript reads “strictly speaking.”]

[Note: [Col. 0308B] **The soldiers through extortion.** Thinking that they are currying favor with their emperors, they betray, expose, and denounce their fellow soldiers. Tertullian again uses the term “extortioner” in *On Flight in Persecution*, chap. XII: “For what does that extortioner say? ‘Give me money’—surely so that he may not betray him.” And in chap. XIII: “Since you know it is unlikely that our extortioners, once brought into friendship by Mammon, will then receive us into the eternal dwellings.” Likewise, as here he uses “extortion,” so in *To Scapula*, chap. IV: “Pudens also, when a Christian was sent to him, understood from the written charge that the man had been subjected to extortion; he dismissed him after tearing up that same charge.”

Florus too, book III, in the epitome of the Jugurthine War: “Yet Numidia did not lightly suffer itself to be shaken.” And Flavius Vopiscus, in his *Tacitus the Emperor*: “Tacitus himself was deeply moved by this speech, and the whole senatorial order was shaken.” Indeed, Sallust himself, *Catiline*, chap. XXII: “This deed had at first shaken his fellow conspirators.” He has similarly used “rivalry” for “hatred,” as explained above. In place of “as many outsiders as,” the Montpellier collation has “so many outsiders,” an evident corruption. HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0308C] **Through nature.** Through the natural hostility which they bear toward us, as Zephyrus says. Certainly, a slave angered against his master because his freedom has been taken away is everywhere conceived as harboring a hostile disposition toward him. “For it is natural both to hate the man whom you fear, and, if you can, to attack the man whom you dread,” says Minucius Felix, p. 31 of Ouzel’s edition. Long before him Seneca had said the same in *On Benefits* IV.19: “No one loves those whom he fears.”

He may be thought to have had regard to that saying of our Savior by which he reveals the lot of the Gospel, Matthew X.36: Καὶ ἐχθροί τοῦ ἀνθρώπου οὶ οἰκιακοί αὐτου (“And a man’s enemies are the members of his own household”). Relevant here also are the words of Chrysostom in his homily on Saints Bernice and Prosdoce, whom their own father betrayed together with his wife Domnina, their mother. His words are:

“For this war was twofold”—he is speaking of the condition of the Church—“one part arising within, the other without; the former from members of the household, the latter from enemies; one brought upon them by strangers, the other by relatives,” etc.]

Every day we are besieged; every day we are betrayed; most often we are seized in the very midst of our assemblies and congregations. Who ever came upon an infant wailing in such a manner? Who ever disclosed to a judge bloodstained mouths of Cyclopes and Sirens just as he had found them?

[Note: [Col. 0308A] The Paris manuscript reads “Sirens” in a variant form.]

[Note: [Col. 0308D] **Reserved.** Thus the Puteanus manuscript and the Montpellier collation. The preceding words, “bloodstained, just as he had found them,” show this to be the true reading. Nevertheless, I do not wholly condemn *reseravit*, “unbarred,” which Barraeus, Pamelius, and La Cerda have; for it can mean that the savage maw of a beast-like human being, closed as though by the bolt of its row of teeth, was forcibly broken open, so that pieces of human flesh not yet sent down into the stomach might be discovered in the mouth. He joined Sirens to Cyclopes because it was thought that both men and women were allied in this crime among the Christians. Rhenanus wrongly has “evidence” in place of “judge.”]

[Note: [Col. 0308A] The Agobardinus and Lyons codices read “reserved the evidence”; Heraldus, Rigaltius, and Havercamp, “reserved it for the judge”; Rhenanus, “reserved the evidence”; Gangnaeus and Semler, “disclosed it to the judge.”]

Who ever detected any unclean traces of such things even upon their wives?

[Note: [Col. 0308B] Havercamp conjectures: “ever any.”]

Who, on discovering such crimes, concealed them or sold his silence, while nevertheless dragging the people themselves to trial?

[Note: [Col. 0308D] **Who, on discovering such crimes, concealed them or sold his silence.** Investigators of crimes are said to “sell” offenses when, after discovering wicked deeds through investigation, they are bribed not to report them before the tribunals. Yet who could believe that criminals were able to buy off their crimes when they themselves were being dragged before the proconsul every day? PAM.

**Or sold his silence, while dragging the people themselves.** The interpreters are silent, or—with the exception of Rigaltius—are variously confused and agitated, as is Heraldus, whose note La Cerda, as often, made his own. Yet the sense is very simple. Tertullian says, “Who, on discovering such crimes, concealed them or sold them?”—not the people, as La Cerda absurdly says in the exposition he placed before his notes, but the crimes themselves, concealing them for an accepted payment—“while dragging the people themselves,” that is, while nevertheless hauling the accused themselves into court for those crimes? Others have “had discovered” in place of “had found.”

Minucius, looking back to this passage, says:

“Nor did we understand that these tales were always being bandied about by the same men and were never investigated or proved, and that throughout so long a period no one came forward to betray the deed, though he would have obtained not only pardon for the act but even favor in the judgment.”

Rhenanus reads “while selling the people themselves.” HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0308B] The Paris manuscript reads “selling.”]

If we are always in hiding, when was what we commit ever betrayed? Indeed, by whom could it have been betrayed? Certainly not by the guilty themselves, since by the rule of all mysteries an obligation of silence is owed.

[Note: [Col. 0309C] **Since by the rule.** The Leiden manuscript gives *cum vetus ex forma*, so that it may be referred to *fides*, “obligation,” which follows. Julius Firmicus, *Astronomica* VII, Preface:

“When Orpheus revealed the ceremonies of the rites to unknown men, at the first threshold he demanded of those whom he initiated nothing other than the necessity of an oath, and demanded it with a certain terrible religious authority, lest the secrets of the religion he had devised and composed be betrayed to profane ears.”

“And Plato constantly kept Timaeus frequently with him, lest he should ever disclose the venerable records of secret discussions to the unskilled; for all these things suffer loss when they are thrust upon ruined and desperate minds.”

Among the Pythagoreans, our Porphyry consecrated religious silence for our feasting mind. HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0309B] Semler reads “of every mystery.”]

The Samothracian and Eleusinian mysteries are kept secret. How much more should such things be concealed, when their betrayal will meanwhile provoke even human punishment while divine punishment is being reserved?

[Note: [Col. 0309D] **Samothracian.** “On the island of Samothrace,” says Zephyrus, “certain initiations are said to have existed as remedies against particular dangers. There too the mysteries of the Corybantes and the rites of Hecate were celebrated, and dogs were sacrificed to her at the cave of Zerinthus.” PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0309D] **The Eleusinian mysteries are kept secret.** Nothing is better known, especially since the Eleusinian rites were made more familiar through Meursius, who treats this silence at greater length in chap. XX, from which source everyone drinks. Hence Horace’s familiar lines, *Odes* III.2:

> I shall forbid the man who has divulged  
> The sacred rite of Ceres to remain beneath  
> The same roof with me, or to launch  
> A fragile boat with me.

Our author, however, attributes their silence to shame when he exposes their nakedness in *Against the Valentinians*, chap. I: “For those Eleusinian mysteries too, themselves the heiress of Attic superstition, are silent out of shame.” [Col. 0310C] HAV.

Nearly all the Fathers mention these rites. They were rites dedicated to Ceres and Proserpina, instituted by Musaeus and customarily celebrated in a certain district of Attica called Eleusis. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0309B] The Paris manuscript reads “will destroy them now.”]

[Note: [Col. 0309B] Franciscus reads “are reserved.”]

If, therefore, the guilty themselves are not their own betrayers, it follows that outsiders are. And how do outsiders possess the knowledge, when even pious initiations always exclude the profane and guard against witnesses—unless impious people are less afraid?

[Note: [Col. 0309B] Franciscus reads “impious.”]

[Note: [Col. 0310C] **Pious initiations.** Thus Rigaltius from the Fulda manuscript, and before him Heraldus and La Cerda; so too Tertullian himself in *To the Nations* I.7. One must have an iron mouth and a leaden heart not to approve it. The text used to read “impious initiations.”

“For,” he says, “since pious initiations”—that is, those which are considered pious and sacrosanct among you—“exclude outsiders, is it not much more fitting that impious ones should do so, since they are also terrified by fear of the law?”

At the end the Montpellier collation reads “unless the impious,” with *si* deleted, incorrectly; for above, in chap. III, he likewise spoke elegantly: “unless perhaps some word in the name sounds barbarous or ill-omened,” where *si* is likewise redundant, as it is in the passage La Cerda cited there from Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* V.20:

> Unless perhaps, cruel man, this very thing  
> You demand—that he perish.

Likewise in another passage from Pliny, which may be seen there. Shortly afterward the Fulda manuscript reads “unless perhaps the inconsiderate man.” HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0309B] Franciscus reads “and witnesses.”]

The nature of rumor is known to everyone. It is one of your own sayings:

> Rumor is an evil swifter than any other.

Why is rumor an evil? Because it is swift? Because it reports? Or because it is very often false? Even when it conveys some truth, it is not without the vice of falsehood, for it subtracts from, adds to, and alters the truth.

[Note: [Col. 0309B] The Paris manuscript reads: “The nature of rumor is known to everyone. It is rumor …”]

[Note: [Col. 0309B] Semler reads “the evil of rumor.”]

What of the fact that its condition is such that it persists only while it lies, and lives only so long as it does not prove its report? For once it has proved it, it ceases to exist and, having as it were completed its function of reporting, hands over the fact. From that point it is the fact that is held and the fact that is named. No one says, for example, “They say this happened at Rome,” or, “Rumor has it that he was allotted a province,” but rather, “He was allotted the province,” and, “This happened at Rome.”

[Note: [Col. 0310B] The Paris manuscript reads: “Rumor has it that this happened at Rome.”]

Rumor, the name of uncertainty, has no place where there is certainty. Would anyone believe rumor except an inconsiderate person? A wise man does not believe what is uncertain.

[Note: [Col. 0310B] Havercamp emended the passage thus; others read: “because a wise man does not believe what is uncertain.”]

Everyone must judge that, however widely it has been spread through self-display and however strongly constructed through assertion, it must once have arisen from a single originator and thereafter crept through the offshoots of tongues and ears.

[Note: [Col. 0310B] Rigaltius reads “will be.”]

[Note: [Col. 0310B] The Paris manuscript has a variant form of “judge.”]

[Note: [Col. 0310D] **Everyone must judge**, etc. He wishes it to be understood that, however great the display with which rumor is spread and however openly it is affirmed, it nevertheless has only one originator, and all those others are no more to be regarded than if only one person were speaking. Indeed, through enlargement, alteration, subtraction, and addition, the truth is lost. HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0310B] The Paris manuscript deletes “it.”]

[Note: [Col. 0310D] **The offshoots of tongues.** Thus he elegantly calls what the Hebrews termed “another tongue.” Hence “the offshoot of incest” in chap. I; and in *Prescription Against Heretics*, chap. XXXII, the disciples and successors of the apostles are called offshoots of those whom the apostles appointed to the episcopate—“offshoots of the apostolic seed.”]

Thus the defect of a tiny seed obscures all the rest of the report, so that no one reflects whether that first mouth may have sown a lie, as often happens either from a disposition toward rivalry, from the caprice of suspicion, or from a pleasure in lying which is not newly acquired but innate in certain people.

[Note: [Col. 0310B] The Paris manuscript reads “obscure.”]

But it is well that time reveals all things—as even your own proverbs and maxims testify—through the arrangement of nature, which has so ordered matters that nothing remains hidden for long, even what rumor has not spread.

[Note: [Col. 0310D] **Time reveals all things, as even your own proverbs testify.** Aulus Gellius, book XII, chap. 2: “Another of the ancient poets, whose name I cannot now recall, said that Truth was the daughter of Time.” There are also verses of Sophocles to this effect:

Πρὸς πάντα κρύπτε μηδὲν, ὡς ἁπανθ` ὁρῶν

Καὶ πάντ` ἀκούων, πάντ` ἀναπτύσσει χρόνος (“Hide nothing in any matter, since Time, seeing everything and hearing everything, reveals everything”). RIG.]

Rumor, therefore, has deservedly remained for so long the sole party privy to the Christians’ crimes. This is the informer you bring forward against us—the informer who has not yet been able to prove what it once proclaimed and, over so long a span of time, strengthened into accepted opinion.

[Note: [Col. 0311B] **Christians’ crimes.** The following words occur here: “What is said always exists, because what exists ceases to be said.” Only the Fulda manuscript supplies them. Scioppius wrote beside them, “A witty saying, worthy of Tertullian’s claw.” Rigaltius passed them over; La Cerda rejects them and admits that he cannot grasp the sense, which here might be that what is said always “exists”—that is, Christians are always regarded as impious because this is said of them—whereas what truly exists is not said but simply exists. But this is a remarkable sophism, like that in Aristophanes’ *Frogs*, Act V, scene 1, where Aeschylus catches Euripides out:

ΕΥ. Εἶτ` ἐγένετ` αὖθις ἀθλιώτατος βροτῶν (“EURIPIDES: Then again he became the most wretched of mortals”).  
ΛΙ. Μὰ τὸν Δί` οὐ δήπον παύσατο (“ΛΙ [uncertain speaker label; the context identifies Aeschylus]: No, by Zeus! Surely he did not cease [to be wretched]”).

EURIPIDES: “Afterward he suddenly became the most wretched of men.”

AESCHYLUS: “No, by Jupiter! For he never ceased to be wretched.”

They are speaking of Oedipus. Apart from this, the sense here would be excessively strained. Accordingly, after a pause for reflection, I confidently pronounce that these words belong with those found earlier in this chapter: “And it lives only so long as it does not prove its report.” HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0313D] It is astonishing how greatly commentators sometimes labor over the easiest matters, since “harsh grimaces” mean nothing other than a certain disagreeable manner of laughing or an ungenerous contraction of the mouth, while “clothed in hides” means the same as “clothed in skins.” EDD.]

**ARGUMENT.—He shows that the charges are not merely false but, as contrary to the testimony of nature, incredible, and does so in vehement language. He then removes the objections that new Christians are supposedly led astray through ignorance and imposture and afterward do not dare to denounce them for fear of punishment, since, on the contrary, they are people who prefer death to shameful acts.**

[Col. 0311B] To invoke the testimony of nature itself against those who presume that such things ought to be believed, behold, we set before you the reward of these crimes: they promise eternal life. Believe it for the moment. For what I ask concerning this is whether even you who believed it would value eternal life so highly that you would attain it with such a thing upon your conscience.

[Note: [Col. 0311C] **To invoke the testimony of nature itself.** After showing that empty and very often false rumor ought not to be believed, he attempts to demonstrate from human nature itself that the crime charged against Christians cannot apply to any human being. HAV.]

Come, plunge the blade into an infant who is no one’s enemy, guilty before no one, the child of all.

[Note: [Col. 0311D] **Come, plunge the blade.** He presents this as though it were the law of Christianity by which a person about to be initiated into its rites is prepared. He says “plunge the blade” with reference to the wounds which people believed were made in the infant with needles thrust through the surface of a layer of meal. HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0311D] **The child of all.** Born from a promiscuous and uncertain union. Learn from Epiphanius concerning the Gnostics, Heresy XXVI:

ἐὰν δὲ καὶ προληφθῇ τις αὐτῶν (“But if any one of them should also be overtaken …”), etc.

“But if someone has been overtaken so that the emission of the natural discharge enters and the woman becomes pregnant, hear what still more grievous and horrible deed such people commit. At whatever time they finally undertake the act, they remove the fetus, receive it, and pound it in a kind of mortar with a pestle. After mixing in honey, pepper, and certain other spices and ointments to avert nausea, they all assemble—the companions of these swine and dogs—and partake of the crushed child. When they have thus completed the eating of human flesh, they afterward pour out prayers to God. ‘We were not mocked by the prince of desire,’ they say, ‘but have gathered up our brother’s transgression.’” HAV.]

Or, if this is another person’s office, simply stand beside a human being dying before he has lived.

[Note: [Col. 0312B] **A human being dying before he has lived.** Frequent darts—sharp ones: he presses on and drives the point home. Lucan likewise, II.109:

> By what crime could little children deserve slaughter?  
> Yet it is enough that they are now capable of dying.

On an ancient marble, where the funeral of an old woman and a little boy is lamented, there is this conclusion:

> Barely to have seen the light was enough for one who might have lived.]
