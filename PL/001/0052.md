## CHAPTER XXXVIII

[Note: [Col. 0466D] “There is nothing for us to speak, see, or hear.” The Fulda manuscript has: “For nothing.” The others have: “There is nothing.” But Rigaltius deleted τὸ (“the”) “there is,” perhaps rightly. Yet I cannot—indeed, I refuse—to follow him and Rhenanus in ordering the words “to speak, see, or hear” also to be banished. All the manuscripts acknowledge them, as do all the other editions, and finally they do not conflict with the sense. “To speak” refers to the disorderly shouts of the partisans, who, while contending and rioting among themselves over the charioteers whom they favored, each encouraged his own faction with acclamations. Hence that famous charioteer Scorpus is called by Martial:

> The glory of the clamorous circus.

[Col. 0467C] “To see” refers to the indecent gestures and nudity of the theater and the exercise-ground; “to hear” pertains to the arena, since it is certainly not humane to drink in with pleasurable ears the groans of the falling and the howling of those torn apart by beasts. **HAV.**]

Since these things are so, a sect that does no evil ought to be tolerated; for there is no reason to fear that people of this kind will assemble in unlawful factions, seeing that they avoid the places of faction and long ago learned to renounce the spectacles, the circus, the theater, the arena, and the exercise-ground—from which people commonly return cruel, mad, wanton, frivolous, turbulent, and for that reason fit for conspiracy. If, therefore, Christians choose other pleasures, and choose them harmlessly, they ought not to be disturbed, since they in turn leave the nations undisturbed in theirs.

Accordingly, and with no less leniency, this sect ought to have been counted among lawful associations, since it commits nothing of the kind ordinarily feared from unlawful factions. For, unless I am mistaken, it is agreed that factions are prohibited out of concern for public order, [Note: [Col. 0464C] Pamelius and others: “and public order.”] lest the state be split into parties—a circumstance that would readily disturb elections, assemblies, councils, public meetings, and even spectacles through the rival collisions of partisan enthusiasm, [Note: [Col. 0464D] “Through the rival collisions of partisan enthusiasm.” Such were those well-known factions of the net-fighters and shield-fighters, and of the Blues, Greens, and similar groups, known especially from Suetonius and other writers; concerning them, see Lipsius, *Saturnalia*, Book II, chapter 24. Chrysostom also alludes to them in the homily on Isaiah 6:1, where he forbids collisions in church: “Nor do we forbid a voice of praise, but a voice of absurdity and disorder, and your mutual contentions, hands rashly and idly raised into the air, stamping feet, and indecorous and effeminate behavior—the practices of those who frequent theaters and circus games, entertainments, and diversions. From there destructive examples are brought among us; from there irreligious and vulgar cries; from there foolish gesticulations of the hands, contentions, contests, unstable behavior, and tasteless, disorderly shouting.” Thus the whole city would be divided into parties, like the crowd at the spectacles. In the same passage: “From countless contentions, from rashly uttered oaths by those who speak evil,” etc. **HAV.**] since by then men had also begun to hold their venal and mercenary service of violence as a source of profit. [Note: [Col. 0465A] “Men—the venal and mercenary service of their violence.” In place of “venal,” the Leiden manuscript presents “they might wish,” while the Fulda manuscript deletes the word “men.” But I think the text should read: “since everyone had already begun to regard the base and mercenary service of his violence as a source of profit”; the meaning would be that people of every rank and condition, driven by factions, exposed themselves and their property to danger and destruction. For the condition of the commonwealth is most wretched when avarice has overthrown good faith, integrity, and the other good arts, and in their place has taught arrogance, cruelty, contempt of the gods, and the treatment of everything as venal, as Sallust rightly observes in *Catiline*, chapter 10; when friendships and enmities are no longer judged according to the thing itself but according to advantage, as he teaches in the same place. **HAV.**]

[Note: [Col. 0465A] Havercamp corrects the text to: “everyone—the base and mercenary service of his violence.”]

But for us, who are cold toward every burning desire for glory and office, [Note: [Col. 0465B] “Toward every burning desire for glory and office.” He elegantly called the desire for glory a “burning desire,” since it virtually sets people on fire; hence poets use the verb “inflame” and similar words. Thus Iphiclus, added to the Argonauts in Valerius Flaccus, Book I.75, was added not to labor:

> But, having been admonished, to impart keen counsels,  
> And to inflame the heroes with their great ancestors’ praises.

Thus, when he says in the proper terminology that Christians are “cold” toward that burning desire, he shows from the inner sanctuary of philosophy that they are wise men, holding that they ought not to administer a commonwealth whose morals are corrupt. These words, moreover, seem to have been sketched from Plato, whose most beautiful relevant passage you may see below at chapter 46, on the words “and necessarily desire.” Sallust also says of himself in *Catiline*, chapter 3: “And although I dissented from the evil conduct of the rest, nevertheless the same desire for office, with the attendant ill repute and envy, tormented me as it did the others.” The Fulda manuscript reads “glory,” while Aldus deletes τὸ (“the”) “from.” **HAV.**] there is no necessity for an assembly, and no concern is more foreign to us than public affairs. We acknowledge one commonwealth shared by all: [Note: [Col. 0465C] “One commonwealth shared by all.” This too comes from the definition of the wise, for whom the whole world is a home and every place a native land. The words of John Chrysostom concerning Saint Lucian are not far removed from this. When questioned under torture, he was asked: ποίας εἰ πατρίδους; Χριστιανος εἰμι φησι (“What is your native land? ‘I am a Christian,’ he says.” The recovered Greek is grammatically corrupt, but its sense is clear.). Τί ἔχεις ἐπιτήδευμα; Χριστιανὸς εἰμι (“What occupation do you have? ‘I am a Christian.’”). Τίνας προγόνους; ὀ δὲ πρὸς ἅπαντα ἕλεγεν, ὄτι Χριστιανὸς εἰμι (“Who were your ancestors? But to every question he said, ‘I am a Christian.’”). That is: “‘What is your native land?’ ‘I am a Christian,’ he replied. ‘What trade do you profess?’ ‘I am a Christian.’ ‘Who were your ancestors?’ But to everything he said, ‘Do you hear that I am a Christian?’” [Col. 0465D] Thus Aristippus said εἶναί τε πατρίδα τὸν κόσμον (“and that the world is his native land”), and Crates of Thebes, loftier than he, says in the tragedy:

Οὐχ εἷς πάτρις μοι πύργος, οὐ μὶα στέγη,

Ἔτοιμοις ἡμῖν ἐνδιαιτᾶσθαι πάρα (“No single tower or dwelling is my native land; rather, every land’s city and home are available for us to inhabit.”).

That is:

> No single tower or house is my native land,  
> But all the earth’s strongholds and homes  
> Offer us a ready dwelling and supper-room.

It is, of course, as Aristophanes says in *Plutus*, verse 1:

Πατρὶς γὰς ἐστι πῶσ` ἒν` ἅν πράττῃ τὶς ἒυ (“For one’s native land is wherever one fares well.” The recovered Greek is textually corrupt, but the supplied context makes the sense clear.).

That is: “Each man’s native land is wherever he fares well.” I have written the passage with Rigaltius, with the Leiden manuscript agreeing; for Aldus and all the others—the source text is incomplete here. Barraeus, Rhenanus, and others have “we know.” The Leiden manuscript presents the entire passage thus: “For us, etc., who are cold, no concern is more foreign than public affairs, since we acknowledge one commonwealth shared by all: the world.” Just as here he calls the world the one commonwealth of all, so in the following chapter he calls nature the one mother of all. **HAV.**] [Col. 0466A] the world.

Equally, we renounce your spectacles [Note: [Col. 0466A] “Equally, the spectacles.” Junius wished the word “equally” to be attached to the preceding words and a full stop placed after it. Unfairly. For if anything had to be changed, it would be preferable to read: “We are fair toward your spectacles,” which can be reconciled with what follows. But the old reading is entirely sound. For just as Christians renounced the forum and senate-house because they did not desire praise and glory, so [Col. 0466B] they also renounced spectacles, since these both arose from idolatry and violated human and divine laws. The Fulda parchment reads: “And indeed.” Christians rightly renounced spectacles, since they themselves, having been made a spectacle to the world with Paul in 1 Corinthians 4:9, possess spectacles in abundance in the word of God. Let us hear Prosper of Aquitaine, *The Promises and Predictions of God*, “On the Glory of the Saints,” in the peroration: “If there is pleasure in watching,” he says, “you have here Christ as a spiritual charioteer, and Elijah, who was conveyed in a fiery chariot as far as the turning-posts of heaven, and Pharaoh’s chariots drowned in the deep. In the contest you have holy Daniel, conquering the lion not with steel but with prayer; and the bears attacking those who assailed the prophet Elisha with insults. In the holy spectacle you also have, not the indecencies of mimes, but virgins consecrated to Christ. In place of trick-riders you have Rebecca’s twins mystically at play. You have David dancing before the ark, and Christ stripped naked in his passion, presenting that very history of his before his servants and handmaids. [Col. 0466C] Finally, you have Jeremiah’s weeping and lamentations sung, not with tragic but prophetic art.” Add our author’s *On Spectacles*, chapter 29. **HAV.**] to the same extent that we renounce their origins, which we know were conceived from superstition; and we stand apart from the very things with which they are concerned. [Note: [Col. 0466A] “We presume,” Latinus; “we prefer,” the Paris edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0466C] “The things with which they are concerned—we stand apart.” This is the consistent reading of all the manuscripts, which the old editions of Barraeus, Aldus, and Rhenanus had corrupted into “we pass over the things exacted—or transacted.” Pamelius and others restored the true reading. But where they had placed “and from the very things,” Aldus printed “from the very things.” Rigaltius has “which very things,” contrary to the manuscripts. Tertullian elegantly says that Christians “stand apart from” those very things with which spectacles are concerned—that is, the things forming their subject matter, namely madness, lust, and cruelty—because Christians renounced these when they became Christians and consequently no longer live to them, but are dead, as Peter speaks in 1 Peter 2:24, and Paul likewise in Romans 6:2 and Colossians 3:7. Indeed, they are nothing to worldly things, just as in chapter 47 he aptly calls the god whom the Epicureans fashion for themselves a nobody in relation to human affairs. **HAV.**]

We have nothing to do, in speech, sight, or hearing, [Note: [Col. 0466D] “Since we stand apart from the very things with which they are concerned.” He says “stand apart from” as one says “survive.” We therefore stand apart from the things that we pass over and omit, and from which we are wholly estranged: namely, from the madness of the circus, the immodesty of the theater, etc. Marcus Cato likewise used “pass by” for what is commonly called “spurn” or “despise”: “I passed by their arguments,” he says. For so the manuscripts of Pliny’s preface addressed to Vespasian read, where those words of Cato are cited near the end. **RIG.**] with the madness of the circus, [Col. 0467A] [Note: [Col. 0467C] “The madness of the circus.” Chrysostom, among others, taught in the passage quoted above that disorderly shouts were uttered there. Tertullian shows in *To the Martyrs*, chapter 1, that the same practice also prevailed in the arena: “Nevertheless, not only their trainers and overseers, but even ignorant and entirely superfluous people encourage the most accomplished gladiators from afar, so that commands dictated by the crowd itself have often proved useful.” **HAV.**] the immodesty of the theater, [Note: [Col. 0467C] “The immodesty of the theater.” Elsewhere he calls it by its proper name, “the council-chamber of lust and immodesty.” There the plots of the plays, the characters, the words, and everything else are obscene, shameful, and unworthy of a good man’s hearing and sight. Chrysostom therefore inveighs sharply against them everywhere, along with others. Thus in his thirteenth homily on 1 Corinthians 4: “They assemble theaters and lead there choruses of prostitutes, boys who prostitute themselves, and those who outrage nature itself; they make the whole populace sit in an elevated place and so provide recreation for the city.” And on Matthew 11, Homily 37: “For absolutely everything done there is most shameful: words, dress, hairstyle, gait, voices, songs, melodies, rolling and movement of the eyes, flutes, pipes, and the very plots of the plays—all, I say, are full of shameful wantonness,” etc. For these reasons those sitting there ought not to dissolve in laughter, but to groan with tears and grief. He therefore severely rebukes Christians who dared to watch these abominations, in Homily 7 on Matthew 2: “For even when you have seen him”—Christ—“you leave him so that you may run from him to the theaters and desire instead both to see and to hear a mime. You leave Christ lying in the spiritual manger, but hurry to watch a prostitute lying on the stage.” And soon afterward: “Yet from this table flows the fountain of spiritual blessings, and immediately abandoning it you run to the theater to gaze at swimming women and at that sex exposed to the public eye.” And in the same place: “You abandon the fountain of this blood and the venerable cup and hasten to that diabolical fountain, so that you may watch a prostitute swimming and suffer the shipwreck of your soul; for she is a kind of immense sea of lust, in which bodies are not submerged but souls suffer shipwreck. She plays naked, swimming amid the waters, while you, looking down from the lofty tier, sink into the depths of lust.” See also the much more extensive discussion to the same effect that follows there. Again, the same author in the homily *On David and Saul*: “Do you not fear, do you not tremble, when with the same eyes with which you look there upon the bed set in the orchestra, where detestable tales of adultery are performed, you behold this sacred table where the awe-inspiring mysteries are celebrated? When with the same ears you hear both a prostitute speaking obscenely and the Prophet and Apostle introducing you to the mysteries of Scripture?” Finally, in the homily *That All Vices Originate from Idleness*: “At the very time when we were discussing these things here, others idly watched the devil’s pageant in the theaters. They then heard the songs of prostitutes; but you feasted upon the sweetest spiritual teaching. They fed upon the devil’s most impure dishes; but you were anointed with spiritual ointment.” Hence Cyril of Jerusalem rightly says in *Mystagogical Catechesis*, or *Catechesis* I.4, when explaining the devil’s pageantry that we renounced in baptism, that it is θεατρομανίας καὶ ἰπποδρομία, κυνηγορίαν καί πᾶσαν τοιάυτην ματαιότητα (“a passion for the theater and horse-racing, hunting, and every such vanity”).] the savagery of the arena, [Note: [Col. 0468D] “The savagery of the arena.” There nothing else is done but that men, laying aside humanity, may become rabid beasts. And so let Chrysostom also come forward here, in Homily 13 on 1 Corinthians 4: “What need is there to mention the frauds and deceptions that occur in horse races, and those that occur in contests of beasts? [Note: [Col. 0468D] “The emptiness of the exercise-ground.” The same Chrysostom, in a homily on Psalm 118: “You, children of the Church, ought not to be corrupted by the vanities of spectacles.” Bulengerus, Panvinius, Vellerus, Faber, and innumerable others have written about these games. The Athenians were deliberating about establishing a gladiatorial spectacle in imitation of the Corinthians. There the philosopher uttered this sound judgment: Μή πρότερον ταῦτα, ὧ Ἀθηναιοι ψηφίζεσθε, ἀν μή τοῦ Ἐλέου τὸν βωμον κατέλητε (“Athenians, do not enact these measures before you have overthrown the altar of Mercy.”), that is: “Athenians, do not enact these measures before you have overthrown the altar of Mercy.”]

[Note: [Col. 0470D] “And now household elders.” This is the ancient reading of the best manuscripts and editions; in its place others, such as the Aldine editions and Rhenanus, preferred to put “and those subdued by age,” which I do not approve equally. I write the words with a hyphen, like those concerning a noble horse in Virgil, *Georgics* III.92, when it has grown old:

> This one too, when burdened by sickness or now more sluggish with years,  
> Fails: remove him from the stable, nor pardon his shameful old age.

The most elegant Andreas Schottus establishes this reading admirably against Canterus and others in *Observationes Humanae*, Book II, chapter 51. But if, with Junius, you prefer to understand the words simply of those who are “members of the household of faith,” from Galatians 6:10, a passage of Justin lies ready to hand. Thus in Book II, chapter 10, he calls Artaphernes, the paternal uncle of Artamenes and Xerxes when they were contending for the Persian kingdom, a “household judge,” just as our author says in chapter 1, “household testimony.” But I do not accept this interpretation. **HAV.**]

Thus, in order that the nations may withdraw from their prejudgment, he freely communicates to them the discipline of the Christian association, as they called it, and all but sets before our eyes the condition of the ancient Church: namely, how Christians were accustomed to assemble in one place; to whom, for whom, and for what they prayed; how they delivered the admonitions and declarations of Scripture; exercised moral censure; ordered the assembly; collected alms; and distributed them for the burial of the dead, the support of the needy, orphans, and retired elders, or the relief of shipwrecked persons and of those banished to the mines, islands, or prisons for the faith. [Col. 0467B–0468A]

He teaches that so great an affection is free from every stain and deserves the true title of love—a love which among the nations is only impure and feigned. Among them banquets are sumptuous, costly, lustful, and infamous; among Christians they are sober, moderate, chaste, and devout. Such a fellowship, therefore, should be called not a faction but a council.
