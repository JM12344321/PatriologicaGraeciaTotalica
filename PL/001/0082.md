Then Tertullian himself indicates quite clearly that he was absent from Rome at that time. For after the words just cited from him he said: “Behold, in that most religious city of the earlier Aeneadae”—that is, Rome—“there is indeed a Jupiter” (*ibid.*, chap. 9, p. 317). You see that he calls it a *city* and signifies by the words “in that city” that he was distant from it. Moreover, where he enumerates the ancestral and indigenous gods of certain nations, he says: “I have, I think, set forth the Roman provinces, but not the Roman gods belonging to them, because they are no more worshipped at Rome than those who, throughout Italy itself as well, are enrolled by municipal consecration” (*ibid.*, chap. 24). Yet surely, if he had dedicated this book to the Roman senate, he would not have used the words “Roman,” [Col. 0741D] “Romans,” and “at Rome,” but rather “your,” “yours,” and “by you.” Nevertheless, he spoke in the same manner elsewhere also (*ibid.*, chap. 21, p. 392; chap. 35, p. 451).

They further insist that in this book no mention is made of the Roman senate, but that, just as at the beginning the “chief authorities” and “governors” are addressed, so at the end too the “governors” are addressed. You have already heard his earlier words; now I would have you hear the later ones: “But go on, worthy governors—you will be much better in the people’s eyes if you sacrifice Christians to them” (*ibid.*, chap. 50, p. 530). By that title, however, he does not address Roman senators or magistrates, but other governors and chief authorities in the city and province where he was living—namely, Africa and Carthage.

[Col. 0742A] What, moreover, of the fact that, after showing that human victims were sacrificed to the gods among various peoples and among the Romans themselves, and that human blood was tasted in Catiline’s conspiracy, he then added: “I range too far afield: today, here”—that is, in the very city where he was writing these things—“blood caught in the palm from the slashed thigh of those consecrated to Bellona, and given as food, marks them” (*ibid.*, chap. 9, p. 321)? Who, they will say, does not therefore see that he openly distinguishes his own city from Rome?

Finally, he contrasts Christians, “who fear God, not the proconsul,” with the pagans (*ibid.*, chap. 45). The pagans therefore feared the latter, but certainly not the former. A proconsul, however, held this highest magistracy not at Rome but in the provinces. Yet the other supports of the preceding view, by which we showed that Roman rulers [Col. 0742B] were addressed, are neither destroyed nor weakened by these arguments.

Accordingly, a third opinion, intermediate between the two thus far explained by us, is held by those who think that this *Apologeticum* was directed by Tertullian not merely to the governors of one city or province, but to those of the entire Roman Empire, as widely as it extended. If this can be proved, the advocates of the other opinions will easily be able to reconcile their positions. Whether it is true, however, must be investigated by us more carefully, because its defenders have not demonstrated it with sufficient precision. First, then, it is certain that this book was sent and presented not to governors individually named, but to governors in general. For if some had been addressed by their own names, and no others besides them, there could have been no dispute or controversy. For once they had been named in this manner, the case would surely have been settled and complete.

[Col. 0742C] Next, everyone admits that Roman governors are addressed in this book, and not merely once. For why should that be denied which has already been proved and is again confirmed by these words: “That I may look to those rulers of the nations: Numa Pompilius was a man” (*ibid.*, chap. 21)? Yet he addresses not only these governors, but the Quirites and even the Roman common people themselves in this way (*ibid.*, chap. 35): “I call upon the Quirites themselves, the native populace of the seven hills”—that is, of the city of Rome—“to say whether the Roman tongue spares any emperor of its own.” Yet, you will say, it is one thing to address certain people in one’s book and another to send and dedicate one’s book to them. Quite right. But why, [Col. 0742D] I ask you, did he address all the Roman governors, the Quirites, and the common people by their own names, unless he knew for certain that his book was to be sent to them?

Yet we have shown clearly enough that not only Roman governors but others also are addressed in this book of Tertullian. For the arguments set out in defense of the second opinion prove no less plainly that this book was written for others as well as for the Roman senate or Roman governors.

To this may be added that our author, in this *Apologeticum*, just as in the two books *To the Nations*, pleads the common cause of all the Christians who existed throughout the world. Therefore, just as he sent those books to all nations, to whom will it not seem most probable that he sent the *Apologeticum* to all [Col. 0743A] the governors of the Roman Empire?

Nor should you say that he also wrote, in defense of the Christians, another “little book,” as he himself calls it, which he directed *To Scapula*, placing that man’s name at its head. For why, I ask you, did he inscribe upon this little book which you raise as an objection the proconsul’s proper name? Why does he everywhere signify in it that he is writing specifically to him? Why, on the contrary, did he nowhere insert the name of any single individual or of certain governors into the *Apologeticum*, a much weightier and longer book? Is not the reason precisely that he judged that the *Apologeticum* should be written generally to all pagan governors? And this certainly appears to be the sense of the opening words of that book: “If it is not permitted to you, chief authorities of the Roman Empire” [Col. 0743B]—that is, you who throughout the Roman Empire are the chief men, leaders, and governors—“in an open and elevated place, on” (this last particle is lacking in all our manuscripts and in the older printed editions) “almost the very summit of the city”—that is, to those presiding over each city. For there he spoke indefinitely: “Chief authorities of the Roman Empire, who preside for the judgment of others in an open and elevated place, upon the very summit of any city whatsoever.” This interpretation, and the third opinion, has at least this advantage: it easily removes the difficulties proposed by the advocates of the other positions and reconciles those differing positions themselves. For from this it is easy to understand why he sometimes addressed certain governors and at other times different ones in this book, which he was writing at Carthage. We have, moreover, set out all the arguments for those three opinions, whether proposed [Col. 0743C] by others or added by us, in our candid and straightforward manner, so that everyone may readily recognize which carry greater and weightier force and embrace the opinion they judge the truer.

## ARTICLE III. Whether Tertullian Inscribed His Own Name upon This *Apologeticum*; Its Title, Subject, Division into Chapters, and Chapter Summaries

Learned writers of our age debate whether Tertullian placed his own name at the head of this book. To some it seems more probable that he suppressed it, since at the very beginning he openly complains that the path to a public defense [Col. 0743D] was barred to him and to other Christians. For these are his exact words: “If, finally, as has very recently occurred, hostility against this sect, overactive through private informations” (our manuscripts and the better printed codices read “judgments”) “has barred its defense” (in certain printed editions the words “has barred the way” are added), “let truth be permitted to reach your ears at least by the hidden path of silent letters” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 1, p. 259). If, therefore, Christians were not permitted to defend their cause publicly and before judges, how much less was Tertullian permitted to do so, when he attacked the errors of the pagans so vehemently, lest by disclosing his name he rashly expose his life to certain and unavoidable danger? For at that time he had not yet assumed the proud and more audacious spirit of the Montanists, but was obedient [Col. 0744A] to the precepts of the Catholic Church, by which every Christian was forbidden voluntarily to expose himself to dreadful torments and the cruellest death.

“But what,” others say, “if, inflamed with a desire for martyrdom, he feared torture and death for the confession of Christ all the less because he desired them the more ardently? What also if he was fully persuaded that his name could not long remain hidden from others, but that his distinctive style of speaking would immediately make it plain to everyone?” Although, therefore, he did not dare present himself openly before the judges, he could nevertheless prefix his name to the hidden “path of silent letters,” as he himself calls it, since he could not conceal it for long. Nor does there appear to be any reason why he should have [Col. 0744B] withheld his name from the title of this book rather than from those of his other works *To the Nations* and *To Scapula*.

But whatever judgment may be made concerning whether Tertullian’s name was concealed or expressed, no one should have any doubt that it at least did not long remain unknown to Christians. For it was not unknown to Eusebius or Lactantius, who cited this book, as we have seen, without concealing his name. They therefore had at hand certain copies of this book, undoubtedly transcribed in Tertullian’s own time, in which his name had been placed at the head. In our two most ancient manuscripts its title is: “Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*”; and at the end these words are read: “Here ends the *Apologeticum* of Quintus Tertullian.” In all the other codices as well, both manuscript and printed, this book is entitled either *Apologeticum* or *Apologeticus*. [Col. 0744C] The authenticity of this title is proved not only by their testimony, but also by that of Lactantius, by whom the book is called *Apologeticus*, and of Eusebius, by whom it is cited in these Greek words (Lactantius, *Institutes*, book V, chap. 4, p. 470; Eusebius, passage cited above): [non-Latin text omitted], that is, “an apology and defense of the Christians.” To cite more authorities in support of a matter so certain and well established would be as tedious for us as it would be burdensome to others.

Short as this title is, however, it embraces the subject of the entire book. For in it Tertullian undertook the defense of the Christians and demonstrates plainly that, contrary to every prescription of every natural, divine, and human law, they were condemned and killed by the pagans merely on account of their name, without their case [Col. 0744D] ever being heard, although the pagans themselves were guilty of all the crimes of which they falsely—indeed, most falsely—accused those Christians. From what has been explained more fully in the analysis of this book presented by us, it therefore follows most clearly that its whole subject is expressed by that title.

Although this very book is divided into various chapters in all printed editions and even in the most ancient manuscripts, that division did not originate with Tertullian. For who, however slightly learned, does not perceive at first glance how inaccurate and imperfect it is? [Col. 0745A] So many of those who prepared new editions of the book were convinced of this that they did not hesitate to alter that division. We think no one disapproves of their attempt. Yet no one will deny that they have not yet attained their goal and that this book may still be divided into chapters more satisfactorily.

Moreover, if it is certain that this book was not divided into chapters by Tertullian, it is assuredly far more certain that the summaries or arguments of those chapters were not composed by him. In books written by any ancient hand, they are so inept and so disorderly that nothing worse could have been produced. Accordingly, those responsible for the later editions of this *Apologeticum* endeavored to amend them. Their correction was indeed not altogether useless, but it was not so complete that they do not still require more careful revision.

## ARTICLE IV. With What Applause This Book Was Received; by Whom It Was Translated into Greek; in What Style It Was Written; and Whether Tertullian Properly Discharged the Office He Undertook as Defender of the Christian Religion

[Col. 0745B]

This book was certainly received and read with the highest applause and common approval of all Christians, Greek as well as Latin. To prove this we need no witness other than Eusebius, who recorded that it was held in such esteem among the Greeks that they translated it into their native language. For it is cited by him in precisely these words: “[non-Latin text omitted] … [non-Latin text omitted]” (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, chap. 2, p. 41): “Tertullian … in the *Apology*, which was indeed composed by him [Col. 0745C] in Latin, but was afterwards translated into Greek.”

Certain writers of our age, however, distinguished for their learning, disagree among themselves concerning the author of this Greek translation. Some think it was Eusebius himself. Others deny this. For when Eusebius transcribed from this book several things which Pliny had reported to Tiberius concerning Christ’s miracles, he omitted many things that would nevertheless have greatly served his purpose. But this argument is not of great weight. That argument seems weightier which observes that several other testimonies taken from this book by Eusebius were not translated into Greek with sufficient accuracy. Yet it matters little to the Church to know who translated this book into Greek. [Col. 0745D] We, however, are more readily persuaded that it had been rendered into Greek before the time of Eusebius, when tyrants were raging against the Christians with a fiercer heat of persecution. For he seems to signify to us, not altogether obscurely, that he himself had not read this book before it was circulated in the Greek language. However the matter may stand, it is certainly clear from this how highly this *Apology* was esteemed by Greek readers.

“But what,” someone will say, “did not Lactantius pronounce a very different judgment upon it in these words? ‘Septimius Tertullian was skilled in every kind of literature, but in expression he was lacking in ease and polish and was very obscure. Hence he did not attain sufficient celebrity either’”—that is, fame and praise (Lactantius, book V of the *Divine Institutes*, chap. 1, p. 459). [Col. 0746A] But whoever raises this objection should, I ask, attend to what we said above: first, that Lactantius praises the universal learning of our Tertullian (above, chap. 1, art. 5). Second, when he said that Tertullian did not attain sufficient celebrity, this should not be understood as though he had pleaded the cause of the Christians with arguments of insufficient force, but as referring to his difficult, unpolished, and highly obscure diction and expression. And surely we think that no one who has so much as touched this book with the tips of his lips will fail immediately to admit that it is strewn with frequent thorns of words alien to good Latin, with ruggedness and rough places, and is often wrapped in great obscurity. Yet we also believe there is no one who will not admit that, even if Tertullian in this [Col. 0746B] book does not charm and delight his readers with verbal graces, elegance of language, and the sweetness of polished and refined discourse, he nevertheless strikes them with the marvelous sharpness of his style and the gravity of his thoughts; and that almost all his arguments possess such force and weight that the more equitable are convinced by them, while the more obstinate are crushed and overwhelmed. Yet we cannot conceal that, sometimes carried away by the impulse of his harsher temperament, he employed certain arguments that many consider weak or even false. Below, however, we shall demonstrate that they have not attended carefully enough to how much he aimed at brevity in this book, and that many things they regard as false are in fact found to be true if they are attentively compared with other matters that he himself explains more fully elsewhere.

[Col. 0746C] Yet one may, not altogether without justification, miss in this book a better arrangement and order of the arguments, which would have cast greater light upon the entire work and enabled Tertullian more easily to win the reader’s goodwill and approval.

But someone will still object to us with Lactantius, who pronounced this judgment upon the same *Apologeticum* of Tertullian: “Although Tertullian fully pleaded the same cause of the Christians in the book entitled *Apologeticum*, nevertheless it is one thing to reply to accusers—which consists solely in defense or denial—and another to give systematic instruction, as we do, in which the entire sum of the doctrine must be contained” (Lactantius, *Institutes*, book V, chap. 4, p. 470). For he declares that two things should have been done by those who undertook the defense [Col. 0746D] of the Christian religion against the pagans. The first was to reply to those who charged the Christians with many crimes. The second was so to expound and demonstrate the doctrine of the Christian religion that anyone might be fully and perfectly instructed in it. He admits that the first part of this case was fully pleaded by Tertullian, but certainly not the second. Tertullian, however, considered it sufficient for his purpose to overthrow the foolish accusations of the pagans, who charged Christians with every most grievous crime, and to vindicate their innocence and integrity of morals. How soundly he demonstrated this will become evident to everyone from the analysis of this book already given and from what will be said below.

Moreover, he did not leave the instruction and explanation of Christian [Col. 0747A] doctrine entirely untouched. For he explains its principal heads briefly indeed, but with such sincerity and candor that everyone may readily understand that nothing is more consonant with both divine institutions and right reason, as we shall show later. Lactantius doubtless desired a longer exposition and proof of these teachings. But in our earlier dissertations we showed that this complaint is not entirely justified (*Dissertation on Minucius*, chap. 2, art. 2; and *on Lactantius*, chap. 3, art. 2).

# CHAPTER III. The Manuscript Codices and Editions of This *Apologeticum*, and the Paraphrases, Scholia, and Observations of Various Writers upon It

## ARTICLE I. The Various Manuscript Codices of This Book

[Col. 0747B]

Many manuscript codices of the *Apologeticum* published by our Tertullian have indeed survived down to our age. Beatus Rhenanus mentions two, with whose aid he prepared an edition of this and of Tertullian’s other books. The first was preserved at the Transjuran monastery of Payerne, and the second in the library of the monastery of Hirsau. Both abound in so many—and almost entirely the same—errors that one appears to have been copied from the other. Sigismund Gelenius obtained another, a very ancient codex of Tertullian’s works and, as he himself says, one of excellent quality, [Col. 0747C] from the monastery of Masmünster; but he has not explained clearly enough whether this *Apologeticum* is one of the books of Tertullian missing from that codex. Three additional codices of the same works exist in the Vatican Library. Alanus Copus, supplied with these by the most illustrious Cardinal Sirleto, collected their readings differing from the printed editions and transmitted them to Pamelius, who used them in preparing a new edition of Tertullian’s works. But three further copies were supplied to Pamelius himself: two from the monasteries of Saint-Amand and Saint Bavo, and a third, an English codex, in the possession of the Englishman John Clement. Yet neither Pamelius nor anyone else has informed us of their quality or antiquity.

[Col. 0747D] In our own France other manuscript codices of these books from every period are found, several of which seem inferior to none in venerable antiquity or elegance of script. One of these, formerly belonging to the brothers Dupuy, is preserved in the library of our Most Christian King. Another was owned by Jacques Bongars. Desiderius Heraldus corrected his separate edition of the *Apologeticum* against both.

Nicolas Rigault, however, complains with no slight indignation that the first and genuine, highly accurate copies of Tertullian’s works had long ago been so corrupted by those unable to understand the language of this African writer that scarcely any hope remained of restoring the true text. [Col. 0748A] Yet he admits that his edition received a great accession of light and distinction from variant readings collected and published by Rhenanus, and from other, better readings collected and published by Fulvius and Junius. He further informs us that he had at hand the aforementioned codex of the brothers Dupuy, another belonging to Pierre Pithou, and the variant readings of the Fulda codex, copied, as he says, from the original in a volume belonging to the most illustrious Monchal, Archbishop of Toulouse. With the aid of these codices, and especially of these readings sent to him, he boasts that he corrected the text of the *Apologeticum* in 250 places. But he remained entirely silent as to the quality and antiquity of those two codices and of the original from which the Fulda readings had been excerpted.

We, however, obtained not only those first two [Col. 0748B] codices, but also several others, whose character and quality must be explained. The first, the Dupuy codex, appears to more expert judges to have been copied in a most beautiful hand around the year of Christ 900. It contains only Tertullian’s *Apologeticum* and, as we have said, is preserved in the library of our Most Christian King under the number 3971. The most illustrious Abbé Le Tellier, with his customary great kindness, ordered Nicolas Clément to make it available to us.

The second, as we have said, once belonged to Pierre Pithou and was given to the college of the Reverend Fathers of the Oratory of Jesus at Troyes. It was preserved in their library and, through their altogether exceptional kindness, was sent to us. Men more practiced in examining ancient codices judged from the form of its characters that it was written around the eleventh [Col. 0748C] century. But it is marred throughout by various errors due to the ignorance of the first scribe; another, more learned hand either corrected them or indicated that they required correction by adding obeli and marginal notes.

Several treatises of Tertullian were transcribed in that codex, and an index of them appears on its first page. This *Apologeticum*, however, was omitted from the index. There therefore appears to be room for suspicion that it was written out later by some scribe after the other books. For that index was written by the same hand as the other books. But the man who subsequently copied the *Apologeticum* was more ignorant than the others and unacquainted with the Latin language, and he left the frequent errors with which that codex abounds throughout. It also displays a different system of word abbreviation from that visible in the other books. [Col. 0748D] Nevertheless, certain sufficiently competent judges of ancient handwriting think that this book, like the others, was written by the same scribe. The codex does not, however, contain the entire *Apologeticum*, but only as far as the beginning of the thirtieth chapter; the remainder, constituting a quarter of the whole book, is wholly lacking.

The third manuscript codex was supplied to us by the most illustrious Achilles de Harlay, a man beyond all praise. After presiding for many years over the Parlement of Paris with truly admirable knowledge of law, equity, integrity, and other eminent virtues, he resigned that dignity with as much fortitude of spirit as universal regret, and died while always maintaining the same constancy of life. That codex, in elegance [Col. 0749A] of script, is not inferior to the Dupuy codex, nor does it seem to us to have been copied a hundred years later. Yet we find in it most of the same readings, both good and bad, as in the latter.

The fourth and fifth codices belong to the Colbert Library and are marked with the numbers 989 and 5,271. The older seems to have been committed to writing about 700 years ago, and the more recent no earlier than 400 years ago. Charles Duchesne, the distinguished custodian of that library, made both codices available to us at the command of the most illustrious Abbé Colbert.

We also received variant readings from two other manuscripts. The first belongs to the monastery of Murbach, at the end of which these words are read: “Pray for the lord abbot of Murbach.” [Col. 0749B] This was Bartholomew of Andlau, who was placed in charge of that monastery in 1455 and thereafter, and wished the very numerous manuscripts collected from everywhere to be marked with the same subscription. These words appended to the *Apologeticum* were written in a much more recent hand than the *Apologeticum* itself, which appears to have been written six or seven hundred years ago. One of the monks of that monastery excerpted its various readings with the greatest diligence, down to the smallest detail, and sent them to us; we owe no small thanks to his kindness.

The other manuscript whose readings differing from the printed editions were sent to us is one of those with which the most illustrious Abbé Giusto Fontanini enriched his library at Rome. He is sufficiently known for his learning [Col. 0749C] and should never be named by us without due praise. He supplied this codex to our Claude Devic, who, although devoted to more important studies, arranged for the variant readings he had extracted from it to be conveyed to us.

We carefully collated the five aforementioned codices—the Dupuy, Pithou, Harlay, and the two Colbert codices—with the edition prepared by Rigault. From this collation we collected a great many variant readings which he had, often without giving any reason but, as it seems, repeatedly on his own authority, either wholly cut away or altered. For he admits that he used only two codices, as we have said, those of Dupuy and Pithou, together with the variant readings from Fulda. But those readings [Col. 0749D] were found in a volume quite foreign to their original setting, and Rigault could not know by whom or with what diligence they had been collected. Nor did he ascertain—or at least he informed no one—of what quality and age that Fulda codex was, on whose authority he altered Tertullian’s text for the most part. For after newly and carefully collating the Dupuy and Pithou codices and the others mentioned, together with the variant readings of still others, we clearly observed that they are altogether opposed to Rigault’s corrections. Who would say that the doubtful reliability of a single Fulda codex should be preferred to the authority of so many others, both better and older? But lest anyone think that less credence should be given to us than to him, some examples of amputations and alterations of this kind [Col. 0750A] must be presented.

Thus, in the first chapter of the *Apologeticum*, only the following is read in Rigault’s edition: “All who formerly hated because they were ignorant cease at once to be ignorant,” etc. (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 1, p. 262). But in our manuscripts and the earlier editions: “All who formerly hated because they did not know what it was that they hated cease at once to be ignorant,” etc. Do you see that the words “what it was that they hated” were rejected by Rigault? Nor should you say that they were omitted by the printers. For then you would charge Rigault with the highest negligence, one that cannot be pardoned in anyone placed in charge of an edition of a work of this kind. Later, no more than this appears in the same edition of Rigault: “Venus wounded by a human arrow, Mars,” etc. (*ibid.*, chap. 14). But in the same printed books and manuscripts: “Venus [Col. 0750B] wounded by a human arrow because she wished to snatch away her son Aeneas, who had nearly been killed by the same Diomedes; Mars,” etc. Is this too, then, to be attributed to the printers’ carelessness or haste? But if Rigault thought that these words had intruded into the text from the margin or elsewhere, why did he not inform us of this addition? But come, and you will recognize other passages similarly cut away from our author’s text. Elsewhere, according to our codices, Tertullian had left it written: “I shall indeed call the emperor ‘lord,’ but in the ordinary sense” (*ibid.*, chap. 34). Rigault, however, saw fit to remove the last three words. Not much later Tertullian has: “So far is it from being proper for the emperor to be called God, not only,” etc. Yet in the same older copies it is written: “So far is it from being proper for the emperor to be called that which cannot be believed, [Col. 0750C] not only,” etc. Who therefore removed the words “which cannot be believed” from the text, if not the man who thought them useless and superfluous?

What of the fact that the following words of Tertullian were not merely excised but transposed by him: “The Epicureans were permitted to establish some truth concerning pleasure. How do we offend you if we presuppose other pleasures? If we are unwilling to know how to take delight, the injury is ours, if perhaps not yours. But we reject what pleases you, and our pleasures do not delight you” (*ibid.*, chap. 38). In the other, older printed and manuscript codices, however: “How do we offend you if we presuppose other pleasures? If we wish to know how to take delight, the injury is ours—if perhaps ours, not yours. But we reject what pleases you, and our pleasures do not delight you. Yet the Epicureans were permitted [Col. 0750D] to establish some truth concerning pleasure, that is, equanimity of mind, and the ample affairs of the Christian woman” [the final phrase is textually uncertain]. The last word, “Christian woman,” however, is not found in the printed editions. What then? Merely because of this single word, perversely added by a careless scribe, was anyone entitled to alter, distort, and mutilate Tertullian’s text? In the same manner, however, Rigault elsewhere deleted these words of Tertullian: “We touch God” (*ibid.*, chap. 40).

If you desire yet another example of alterations of this kind, it need not be sought far away. For not much later, in the same text of Tertullian, he altered the words of the editions and manuscripts, “I do not bathe at dawn,” and ordered that it be written: “I do not bathe toward night.” [Col. 0751A] Many other passages of this book, similarly interpolated and badly mutilated by Rigault, could easily be produced. But we have judged that their enumeration should now be omitted, lest the same points be repeated. For those of greater importance will indeed have to be treated below when occasion is given. We had resolved to present at the end of our dissertation absolutely all the variant readings of this book excerpted either by us or by others. But because one of our associates, who is diligently laboring to prepare an edition of all Tertullian’s books, asked that this province be entrusted to him, we judged that his very reasonable wishes should be satisfied. We therefore consider it sufficient for our purpose to indicate in this dissertation the principal and noteworthy words of Tertullian that have been corrupted, interpolated, excised, and altered, so that his meaning and opinion may [Col. 0751B] more readily be understood by everyone.

## ARTICLE II. The Various Editions of This *Apologeticum* and of Tertullian’s Other Books

One could find very few works of the ancient Fathers of the Church that have been printed more often than this *Apologeticum* and the other writings of Tertullian. It was published not only together with them, but also separately. For it alone emerged from the printers’ workshop into public light through the zeal and care of Giovanni Battista Egnazio.

Another edition of this *Apologeticum* was produced at Paris in 1613 through the labor and effort of Desiderius Heraldus, who appended to it, as we noted in its proper place, the *Octavius* of Marcus Minucius [Col. 0751C] Felix (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, chap. 2, art. 3).

Before and after that time, however, the *Apologeticum* was published with either most or all of Tertullian’s other works. To review briefly the principal editions of these works: Beatus Rhenanus first arranged for them to be printed at Basel in 1521, and then in 1528. Other editions based upon these were published in precisely the same manner in 1525, 1536, and 1538; another, prepared through the efforts of Sigismund Gelenius, appeared before the public in 1562. Then, in 1580, René Laurent de la Barre provided us with another edition, in which Tertullian’s treatises were corrected by comparing, as he says, old and recent copies. [Col. 0751D] No edition of these books, however, was reproduced more often than the one which, under the care of Jacobus Pamelius, was brought into public light in 1579. For it was reprinted in various cities of Europe in 1579, 1584, 1596, 1597, 1599, 1608, 1616, 1627, 1658, 1662, and 1664.

Furthermore, Luis de la Cerda, chief professor of theology among the Reverend Fathers of the Society of Jesus, published most of Tertullian’s works with new labor and care; but he omitted in particular those written by Tertullian against Marcion and against the Catholic Church. He nevertheless comprised the works he published in two volumes, which issued from the press in 1624, 1630, and 1641.

In 1634, Nicolas Rigault likewise arranged for all Tertullian’s works, comprised in one volume, to be published through new [Col. 0752A] study and in new type. But as regards the *Apologeticum* now under discussion, we have already observed that he altered, transposed, and even wholly excised many passages from the text of our African writer at his own pleasure and on his own authority.

In the following year, 1635, however, another edition subsequent to that of Rigault emerged from the printers’ workshop at Paris. Its editor, making no use of any further manuscript, wished Tertullian’s books *On the Pallium* and the *Apologeticum* to be reprinted according to Pamelius, but all the rest according to Rigault’s text. Yet the author of the preface goes completely astray in thinking that the books *On the Pallium* and the *Apologeticum* as edited by Rigault were so perfect that they might seem to have been presented by him to the public [Col. 0752B] for the first time. For, from what we noted a little earlier and shall note later, who does not see that this book was in many places corrupted rather than corrected by Rigault? Nevertheless, in 1641 that entire edition was again printed by the printers exactly as it had been prepared by Rigault.
