Thus, after professing that with desire he had desired to eat the Passover as his own—for it would be unworthy of God to desire something belonging to another—[Col. 0460C] he took bread, distributed it to his disciples, and made it his body by saying, “This is my body” [Note: [Col. 0460C] “His own body,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.], [Note: [Col. 0459D] “That is, the figure of my body.” These words are not to be understood as the Calvinists wish—whom Rigaltius favored excessively both here and elsewhere—but as their context requires them to be understood and as the polemical theologians explained them, among whom the most learned cardinals Perron and Bellarmine hold first place. It will suffice to cite the latter:

“A third passage follows from Book IV *Against Marcion*, where we read as follows: ‘He took bread, distributed it to his disciples, and made it his body by saying, “This is my body”; that is, “the figure of my body.” But it would not have been a figure unless there were a true body.’

“I answer: the earlier words of this [Col. 0460C] testimony, which all the sacramentarians place in their front line as a kind of Achilles, support us most strongly; the later words do no harm to our case. For the statement, ‘He took bread and made it his body by saying, “This is my body,”’ plainly indicates a real [Col. 0460D] change of the bread into Christ’s body. The statement, ‘That is, the figure of my body,’ does not mean—as the sacramentarians believe—that the Eucharistic bread is a figure of the Lord’s body, but that what was formerly a figure in the Old Testament has now been changed into the reality of the body. For ‘the figure of my body’ is connected not with the immediately preceding expression ‘my body,’ but with the pronoun ‘this,’ so that the sense is: ‘He took bread and made it his body, saying: “This”—that is, the bread which was formerly a figure of my body—“is now my body.”’ Here the pronoun ‘this’ points to bread, not bread that remains unchanged, but bread that is changed, as we said above.

“That this is so is proved, first, from the words that follow: ‘But it would not have been a figure unless there were a true body.’ For if by ‘figure’ he meant the Eucharist, he would not say ‘it would not have been,’ but ‘it would not be.’ He is therefore speaking of a figure in the Old Testament, which undoubtedly would not have been a true figure unless a true body corresponded to it.

“Second, the same is evident from the words that follow a little below: [Col. 0461C] ‘Why,’ he says, ‘does he call the bread his body, rather than the melon which Marcion had in place of a heart? Marcion did not understand that this was an ancient figure of the body of Christ, who says through Jeremiah: “Come, let us cast wood upon his bread”—that is, the cross upon his body. Thus the illuminator of antiquities sufficiently disclosed what he had then intended the bread to signify, by calling the bread his body.’ Here Tertullian says that Christ called the bread his body rather than a melon or some other food in order to fulfill Jeremiah’s figure, in which the name ‘bread’ signified Christ’s body. Christ therefore did not institute a figure of his body in the Eucharist, as our opponents wish, but fulfilled the [Col. 0461D] ancient figure by making his body out of bread.

“Third, this is evident from other words that follow, where, in explaining Jacob’s prophecy (Gen. XLIX), ‘He shall wash his robe in wine, and his cloak in the blood of the grape,’ he says: ‘By the robe he indicated the flesh, and by the wine the blood. Thus even now he consecrated his blood in wine, he who then prefigured wine in blood.’ Here IT MUST BE NOTED that, just as in Jeremiah bread signified the body because the Lord’s body is the food of the faithful, as we said above, so in the patriarch Jacob wine signifies the Lord’s blood because the Lord’s blood is the drink of the faithful. But Christ, in order to explain these figures or prophecies, delivered to us his body under the species of bread and his blood under the species of wine. And certainly, if he had not in reality given his true body and blood, he would not properly have explained or fulfilled the prophecies. For, as Tertullian says, Jeremiah foretold the true body by bread, and Jacob the true blood by wine. Tertullian’s words, ‘He who then prefigured wine in blood’ [Col. 0462C], refer to Jacob’s words, ‘his cloak in the blood of the grape.’ For he means that by the blood of the grape wine is understood, while by wine the Lord’s blood is understood, as he himself explains when he says that the robe indicates the flesh, and the wine the blood.

“Fourth and finally, the same is evident from the course of the entire book. For his purpose was to show that Christ did not destroy the Old Testament in the Gospel, but fulfilled it. Tertullian therefore reviews many of Christ’s works and shows that in all of them Christ took care to fulfill the figures of the Law. Thus in this passage also he does not introduce a figure instituted in the New Testament, but only the fulfillment [Col. 0462D] of ancient figures.”

Thus far Bellarmine (*On the Sacrament of the Eucharist*, book II, ch. 7). EDD.]

## CHAPTER XLI

“Woe,” he says, “to him through whom the Son of Man is betrayed.” It is therefore now established that “Woe” must be understood as an exclamation of imprecation and threat, and attributed to one who is angry and offended—[Col. 0462B] unless Judas was going to incur so great a crime with impunity. But if with impunity, “Woe” is meaningless. If not with impunity, then he was certainly to be punished by the one against whom he committed the crime of betrayal.

Moreover, if Christ knowingly allowed a man whom he himself had chosen for his company to fall into so great a crime, do not now raise again against the Creator your objections concerning Adam, since they can also be turned back against your own god: either he was ignorant, since in his providence he did not prevent the man from sinning; or, if he was ignorant, he was unable to prevent him; or, if he both knew and was able, he was unwilling. He must therefore be judged malicious for allowing his own man to perish through sin. I advise you, then, to recognize the Creator in this instance as well, rather than make your “best god” equal to him contrary to your own doctrine.

For by consigning Peter, who had spoken somewhat presumptuously, [Col. 0463A] to denial, he also reveals to you a jealous God. [Note: [Col. 0462D] “For Peter too had spoken somewhat presumptuously.” In these very few words the author expressed Christ’s conversation concerning the foretold denial of St. Peter, which he treats at greater length in the book *On Flight in Persecution*, omitting what intervenes in Luke. PAM.]

He also had to be betrayed with a kiss [Note: [Col. 0463D] “He also had to be betrayed with a kiss.” Epiphanius, *Refutation* 66, also proves the reality of Christ’s body from these words of Luke, to which our author alludes: “And Judas drew near to kiss him.” In *Refutation* 67 he adds that Marcion also excised from what follows what Peter did when he struck and cut off the high priest’s servant’s ear, and, so far as appears, the entire account of Peter’s denial, since our author also omitted it here. PAM.], namely as the prophetic Christ, and therefore as the son of the one who was loved by the people with their lips (Isa. XXIX).

When brought before the council, he is asked whether he himself is the Christ. Concerning which Christ would the Jews have asked, unless their own? Why, then, did he not reveal to them even then that he was another? “So that he could suffer,” you say. That is, so that your supremely good god might plunge men who were still ignorant into crime. Yet even if he had told them, he would still have suffered. “For if I tell you,” he says, “you will not believe” [Note: [Col. 0463D] “You do believe,” Rhen., Seml., Ober.]. Since they would not believe, they would have persisted in killing him. And why would he not all the more certainly have suffered if he had revealed himself as belonging to another god and, for that very reason, as the Creator’s adversary?

Would he therefore not have suffered? He refrained from showing himself as another even then; but since [uncertain reading; the Latin is textually corrupt here] they wished to extort a confession from his own mouth, although they would not believe him even if he confessed—men who ought to have recognized him from his works fulfilling the Scriptures—[Col. 0463B] it was fitting for him to conceal himself, since recognition was already owed to him of their own accord.

Yet even then he stretched out his hand to them: “From now on,” he says, “the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God.” For he was suggesting that he was the Son of Man from Daniel’s prophecy (Dan. VII), and the one seated at God’s right hand from David’s Psalm (Ps. CIX). Thus, enlightened by this saying and by comparison with Scripture as to whom he wished himself to be understood to be, they say: “Then are you the Son of God?” Of which God, except the only one they knew? Of which God, except the one whom they remembered saying to his Son in the Psalm, “Sit at my right hand”?

But he replied: “You say that I am” [Note: [Col. 0463D] “As though not I,” Jun.]. Yet he confirmed that he was what they had said when they questioned him again. But how will you prove that they spoke interrogatively and not affirmatively: “Then you are the Son of God”? Since he had indirectly shown through the Scriptures that he was to be understood as the Son of God, they may have meant: “Then you are the Son of God, which you are unwilling to say openly.” In the same way, [Note: [Col. 0463D] “And so,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] his reply, “You say it,” was also affirmative. Indeed, such was the force of his declaration that they persisted in the conclusion conveyed by it.

## CHAPTER XLII

For when they brought [Note: [Col. 0463D] “Produced,” elsewhere.] him before Pilate (Luke XXIII), they began to accuse him of calling himself a king—Christ, without doubt, the Son of God, who was to sit at God’s right hand. Otherwise they would have charged him under another title, uncertain whether he had called himself the Son of God, unless [Note: [Col. 0464D] “If,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] he had pronounced “You say it” in such a way as to affirm that he was what they said.

Likewise, when Pilate asked, “Are you the Christ?” he answered correspondingly, “You say it,” lest they seem to have given a fuller response because they feared the governing authority.

The Lord, then, was placed on trial. “And he set his people in judgment.” “The Lord himself came [Note: [Col. 0464D] “Will come,” Fran.] into judgment with the elders and rulers of the people,” according to Isaiah (Isa. III, 13). From that point onward he fulfilled everything written concerning his passion.

“There the nations raged, and the peoples devised vain things” (Ps. II, 1). “The kings of the earth stood forth, and the rulers were gathered together as one against the Lord and against his Christ”: the nations were the Romans who were with Pilate; the peoples, the tribes of Israel; the kings, represented in Herod; the rulers, in the chief priests.

For when he was sent by Pilate to Herod as though a gift, he also vindicated the reliability of Hosea’s words; for Hosea had prophesied concerning Christ (Hos. X, [Col. 0464B] 6): “And they shall lead him bound as a gift to the king.” Herod, indeed, was delighted to see Jesus, but heard not a single word from him. “For like a lamb before its shearer, so he did not open his mouth” (Isa. LIII, 7), because the Lord had given him the tongue of instruction, so that he might know how he ought to utter a word (Isa. L, 4). This, namely, was the tongue which he then proved to be fastened to his throat in the Psalm (Ps. XXI), by not speaking.

And Barabbas, though utterly guilty, is granted life as though he were good; but Christ, though utterly righteous, is demanded for death as though he were a murderer. Two criminals are also crucified around him, so that he might, of course, “be reckoned among the lawless.” [Note: [Col. 0464D] “But two criminals are also crucified around him.” In Luke the Greek is κακοῦργοι (*kakourgoi*, “criminals”), which he correctly translates as *scelesti*, “criminals.” That rendering corresponds more closely to Isaiah LIII, which he cites here, as though repeating what is found in Luke XXII: “And he was reckoned among the lawless”; in the Evangelist’s Greek, μετὰ τῶν ἀνόμων (*meta tōn anomōn*, “among the lawless”). PAM.]

Marcion entirely removed the account that his clothing [Note: [Col. 0464D] “His” inserted by Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] was divided among the soldiers [Note: [Col. 0464D] “Partly” added in Latin editions.], partly being allotted by lot, because he had regard to the prophecy of the Psalm (Ps. XXI, 19): “They divided my garments among themselves, and cast lots for my clothing.” Remove the cross itself, then! Yet the same Psalm does not remain silent about it: “They pierced my hands and my feet” [Note: [Col. 0464D] “My” omitted in Rhen., Seml., Ober.].

His entire end is read there: “Dogs surrounded me; the assembly of evildoers encircled me. All who watched me mocked me with their noses. They spoke with their lips and shook their heads. He trusted in God; let God deliver him.” Since you already possess the testimony of the garments as plunder belonging to your falsehood [Note: [Col. 0464D] “You possess,” Rhen., Latin editions.], the whole Psalm constitutes Christ’s garments.

And behold, even the elements are shaken. For it was their Lord who was suffering. Otherwise, if it were the adversary who had been injured, heaven would have blossomed with lights; the sun would rather have exulted with its rays; the day would rather have stood still, gladly watching Marcion’s Christ hanging upon the gibbet. These arguments too would have belonged to my case even if they had not been foretold.

“I shall clothe the heavens in darkness,” says Isaiah (Isa. L, 3). This will be the day of which Amos also speaks (Amos VIII, 9): “And on that day, says the Lord, the sun shall set at noon”—you have here also the indication of the sixth hour—“and he shall darken the earth.”

The temple veil too was torn, when the angel broke forth and abandoned the daughter of Zion (Isa. I, 8), “like a watchman’s hut in a vineyard and like a shelter in a cucumber field.”

O how thoroughly he persevered in presenting Christ himself even through the thirtieth Psalm! He cries aloud to the Father so that, even as he dies, he might fulfill the prophets with his final utterance. Having said this, he expired.

Who expired? Was it the spirit itself, or the flesh breathing out the spirit? But the spirit could not breathe out itself. The one who breathes out is one thing; that which is breathed out is another. If the spirit is breathed out, it must necessarily be breathed out by another. But if there had been only spirit, it would have been said to depart rather than to breathe out its spirit. Who, then, breathes out the spirit except the flesh, which breathes while it possesses the spirit and, when it loses it, thus breathes it out?

Finally, if there was no flesh [Note: [Col. 0465D] “Had been,” elsewhere; one of the two readings is omitted in Rhen., Seml., Oberth.], but only a phantom of flesh, and the phantom was spirit, then the spirit breathed out itself and departed in breathing itself out. Without doubt, the phantom departed when the spirit that constituted the phantom departed; and the phantom appeared nowhere together with the spirit. Did nothing, then, remain upon the wood? Did nothing hang there even after the spirit was breathed out? Was nothing requested from Pilate? Was nothing taken down from the gibbet? Was nothing wrapped in linen? Was nothing laid in a new tomb?

Yet it was not nothing. What, then, was it? If it was the phantom, then Christ was still within it. If Christ had departed, then he had taken away the phantom. It remains for heretical shamelessness to say that a phantom of the phantom remained there.

But Joseph too knew that it was a body [Note: [Col. 0465D] “If,” elsewhere.], which he handled with complete devotion—that Joseph who had not agreed with the Jews in their crime: “Blessed is the man who has not gone away in the counsel of the ungodly, nor stood in the way of sinners, nor sat in the seat of plagues [Note: [Col. 0465D] ‘Pestilence,’ elsewhere.].” For the one who would bury the Lord also had to be prophesied and, already then, deservedly blessed.

## CHAPTER XLIII

Thus [Note: [Col. 0465D] “If,” elsewhere; Jun. deletes one of the two readings.] prophecy does not overlook the service of those women either (Luke XXIV), who came to the tomb before daylight with spices prepared. For Hosea says concerning this (Hos. VI, 1): “So that they may seek my face, they shall keep watch for me before daylight, saying: ‘Let us go and return to the Lord, because he himself has seized us and will heal us; he has struck us and will have mercy upon us; he will heal us after two days; on the third day we shall rise.’”

For who would not believe that these words revolved in the thoughts of those women, between their grief at the present abandonment, by which they imagined themselves struck by the Lord, and their hope in his resurrection, by which they rightly supposed that they themselves would be restored?

Since the body was not found, “his burial had been taken away from their midst,” according to Isaiah (Isa. LVII, 2). Two angels also appeared there. He was accustomed to employ approximately that number of attendants at his side, since the Word of God is established upon two witnesses. [Note: [Col. 0465D] “So many *laterenses*.” *Laterenses* are attendants or bodyguards, because they enclose one’s side. RHEN.]

Isaiah also foresaw the women returning from the tomb and from that vision of angels (Isa. XXVII, 11): “Women,” he says, “coming from the vision, come”—that is, to announce the Lord’s resurrection. [Note: [Col. 0465D] “The women returning from the tomb.” Here too he shows that the Evangelist agrees with Isaiah XVII: “Women coming from the vision have come.” Here we omit what was formerly read in the first position, “Come,” [Col. 0466D] because it is not read more than once either in the Septuagint, now or formerly, or in St. Jerome, Cyril, Procopius in his *Commentary*, Origen on Matthew, or Rufinus on the Creed among the works of St. Cyprian. It is well, moreover, that the disciples’ unbelief persisted, so that our defense might remain intact to the very end: Jesus Christ presented himself to his disciples as no other than the one who had been prophesied.

For when two of them were traveling and the Lord joined them, without appearing to be who he was, and even feigning ignorance of what had happened, they say: “But we were hoping that he was the redeemer of Israel”—certainly their own Christ, that is, the Creator’s. To such an extent had he not presented himself to them as another. Otherwise, they would not have thought him the Creator’s Christ; and when he was thought to be the Creator’s, he would not have tolerated this opinion concerning himself if he were not the one he was thought to be [Note: [Col. 0465D] “Is thought,” Rig., Venet., Seml.]. Or else he himself will be the author of error and a violator of truth, contrary to the title of the “best god.”

Nor did he show himself to them after the resurrection as anyone other than the one whom [Note: [Col. 0466D] “Which,” Paris, Fran.] they said they had thought him to be. He did, indeed, rebuke them: “O foolish and slow of heart, in failing to believe all the things he spoke to you!” The fact that he had spoken them proves that he belonged not to another god, but to the same God [Note: [Col. 0466D] “And less concerning God,” Seml.; “and a hand concerning God,” Rhen.].

The angels likewise said to the women: “Remember what he spoke to you in Galilee, saying that the Son of Man must [Note: [Col. 0466D] ‘Would have to,’ Wouwer manuscript.] be betrayed and crucified, and rise on the third day.” And why did it have to happen, unless because it had been so written by God the Creator?

He therefore rebuked [Note: [Col. 0466D] “Reproved,” elsewhere, following Seml.] them as men scandalized only by the passion and doubtful about the resurrection announced to them by the women—circumstances through which they had failed to believe that he was the one they had thought him to be. Thus, because he wished them to believe that he was precisely what they had thought, he confirmed that he was the one they had thought him to be: namely, the Creator’s Christ, the redeemer of Israel.

What, moreover, could be clearer concerning the reality of his body? When they hesitated lest he be a phantom—or rather, when they believed him to be a phantom—he said: “Why are you troubled, and why do thoughts arise in [Col. 0466D] your hearts? See my hands and feet, because it is I myself; for a spirit does not have bones, as you see that I have” [Col. 0467A] [Note: [Col. 0467A] “As you see me having,” Rhen., Seml., Obert.].

Marcion, I believe, deliberately refused to erase from his Gospel certain things that contradict him, so that, on the basis of those things which he could have erased but did not, it might either be denied that he erased those which he did erase [Note: [Col. 0468A] “Not,” Seml.], or said that he erased them with good reason. He spares only those passages which he can subvert by interpretation just as effectively as by deletion.

Thus he wishes the statement, “A spirit does not have bones, as you see that I have,” to be referred to the spirit in this way: “as you see that I have”—that is, as you see that I, like the spirit, do not have bones. But what rationale is there for this contortion? He could have said plainly: “Because a spirit does not have bones, as you see that I do not have.”

Why, moreover, does he offer for their inspection his hands and feet—limbs composed of bones—if he had no bones? Why does he add: [Col. 0468A] “And know that it is I,” namely the one whom they had previously known as bodily? Or, if he was entirely a phantom, why did he rebuke them for believing him to be a phantom? Indeed, while they still did not believe, he asked for food precisely in order to show that he even had teeth.

We have fulfilled our undertaking, I think. We have presented Jesus Christ—in the prophets’ teachings, judgments, dispositions, meanings, mighty works, sufferings, and even resurrection—as no other than the Creator’s Christ. Indeed, when he sent the apostles to preach to all nations, he commanded that their sound should go out into all the earth and their words to the ends of the earth, thereby fulfilling the Psalm.

I pity you, Marcion: you have labored in vain. For Christ Jesus in your Gospel is mine. [Note: [Col. 0467B] “For Christ Jesus in your Gospel is mine.” That Marcion corrupted and falsified the Gospel is proved both by this passage and by the final chapter of the preceding book—“And your Gospel,” etc.—and also by chapter 29 of this book. St. Epiphanius testifies that Tertullian possessed that corrupted manuscript of Marcion. [Col. 0468B] No one who reads this book can doubt that it was Luke’s Gospel that had been corrupted, for this becomes manifest everywhere. Verse 25 of St. Luke XXIV is also quoted in corrupt form: “O foolish Galatians,” etc. For that wording is quoted not from the received Gospel, but from Marcion’s Gospel. LE PR.]

## SYNOPSIS

[Col. 0467B]

—Just as in the preceding book Tertullian used the Gospel of St. Luke, which Marcion had appropriated for himself, so now, from Paul’s Epistles—which Marcion had mutilated and arranged in a different order as a volume published under his own title, *The Apostolic Book*, with the exception of those to Timothy and Titus and the Epistle to the Hebrews—Tertullian proves that the Apostle proclaims no God other than the Creator.
