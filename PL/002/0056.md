We know, I say, very well their origin too, and we know [Note: [Col. 0546C] “And” omitted in the Venetian edition] why we call them Valentinians, although they may appear not to be so. For they departed from their founder, but their origin is by no means erased; and if they happen to change, [Col. 0546B] the very change is evidence of it.

Ptolemy afterward paved that path by distinguishing the names and numbers of the Aeons into personal substances, though substances defined as existing outside God; Valentinus had enclosed them within the very fullness of divinity as thoughts, affections, and movements. From there Heracleon also drew certain bypaths, as did Secundus and Marcus the magician [Note: [Col. 0546D] **Marcus the magician.** On him see the most learned preliminary dissertations of Dom Massuet prefixed to the works of Irenaeus, Dissertation I, article 11, § VI, p. 50. He sets out in concise form almost the same things that Rhenanus gives here: Irenaeus relates that Marcus, the corrector of his master Valentinus, was highly skilled in magical imposture, through which he seduced many men and no small number of women. Those converted to him regarded him as supremely learned and perfect, and as possessing the greatest power from the invisible *Logoi*. Yet he accomplished all this through the tricks of Anaxilaus and magical deception, being a true forerunner of Antichrist.

From the same author we shall present one or two examples of this utterly depraved man’s tricks. Having mixed wine for a chalice, he pretended to give thanks—that is, to *eucharistein* (εὐχαριστεῖν), what we now call consecrating—and after a lengthy invocation he caused it to appear purple and red, [Col. 0547B] so that the simpler people might think that blood had dripped by divine power into his chalice, and therefore all might desire to taste from that cup and thus become participants in heavenly grace.

Another kind of imposture: he presented a woman with a mixed chalice and ordered her to give thanks. Then, bringing out a larger vessel, he poured the smaller chalice—which had been made a Eucharist—into it, to the great amazement of the spectators, since the larger vessel was not only filled but even overflowed.

Furthermore, he had an attendant demon, what ordinary people today call a familiar spirit. He ensnared women, especially those of respectable birth and [Col. 0547C] wealth, making them prophetesses with these words: “Receive from me a bridegroom, take him, and be taken in him. Behold, grace has descended upon you; open your mouth and prophesy.”

He also mocked the chastity of many women. At any rate, it is established that in Asia he secretly carried off the wife of a certain deacon, a woman of uncommon beauty. When she returned after a long time, she declared that Marcus had corrupted her not only in mind but also in body, and she spent the remainder of her life in perpetual public confession.

Hence a certain holy man cited by Irenaeus rebuked this deceiver in words of this kind: “Marcus, maker of idols and observer of portents, expert in astrology and the magical art, through which you confirm doctrines of error by displaying signs to those whom you seduce—operations of apostate power which your father Satan furnishes to you through the angelic power Azazel, having you as the forerunner of wickedness opposed to God.”

This Marcus boasted that he had adapted the mysteries of numbers to Valentinus’ dreams. As for Theotimus, however, I have found no mention in [Col. 0547D] any author I have seen thus far. Perhaps one should read “Tatian”; for concerning him Tertullian wrote above, in *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 52 (*Heresy* XX), that he was wholly in agreement with Valentinus. Axionicus alone, at Antioch to this very day [Note: [Col. 0547B] “Arionicus” in the Vatican manuscript, according to Junius], consoles the memory of Valentinus by preserving his rules intact [Note: [Col. 0547B] “Regularly,” Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür].

Otherwise [Note: [Col. 0547D] “Only so much persuasion is permitted to this heresy.” Irenaeus: “By persuasive conduct they entice simpler persons, under the guise of good men, into a manner of inquiry.” And shortly afterward: “Subtly adorned with a persuasive covering.” RIG.], this heresy is permitted to commend itself only as much as it is customary for a prostitute (*lupa*, literally “she-wolf”) to make up her appearance every day [Note: [Col. 0548B] “To surpass,” Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür]. If they construct anything new, they immediately call their presumption a “revelation,” and their cleverness a “charism”; they acknowledge not unity but diversity. We therefore foresee that, once their customary dissimulation has been set aside, many will divide over particular points and will even say in good faith [Note: [Col. 0548B] Unless I am mistaken, “being about to say” is the reading of the first edition. To refute these arguments thoroughly, nothing seemed better to us than to arrange for Irenaeus’ own genuine words to be placed beside the text of Septimius, proceeding on equal footing and in parallel columns for comparison. From this it will become more certain than certainty itself, and it will be impossible for anyone to be deceived, that when Tertullian wrote his little work he had before him not merely the authentic works of the most holy martyr, not merely works composed in the original Greek, but also works already rendered in a Latin translation.

To make this shine forth more clearly, we shall bring to light in distinctive type every word of Irenaeus’ old translation which Tertullian either used or alluded to. ## CHAPTER VI

Accordingly, in this little book [Note: [Col. 0549B] “Because,” Venetian edition], in which we promise only an exposition of that mystery [Note: [Col. 0549B] Franciscus: “we promise an exposition of that mystery alone”; Rhenanus: “first setting forth only”], lest anyone be overwhelmed by darkness because of names so foreign, forced, compounded, and ambiguous [Note: [Col. 0549B] The Venetian edition corruptly reads “heat.” Oberthür, Semler], I shall first explain how we intend to use them. We shall therefore use Greek forms for the most part. Nor, indeed, will Greek forms be absent where Latin ones are used, but they will be marked above the lines, so that this may serve as the sign of personal names, because of the ambiguity of words that also bear another meaning [Note: [Col. 0549B] Others read the singular “ambiguity”].

[Col. 0550A] [Note: [Col. 0549D] **The assault will not consist of a transfixing taste of battle.** That is: we shall indeed brand and take a taste, but we shall not pierce through. I shall display wounds but not inflict them. If laughter too occurs somewhere, justice will be done to the subject matter itself: many things are so unworthy of serious refutation that they must not be honored with gravity [Note: [Col. 0550B] Junius reads “adorned”]. One must certainly take care that its laughter is not itself laughed at, should it prove unworthy. But wherever laughter is deserved, it is a duty. Finally, I shall begin in this way.

## CHAPTER VII

Ennius, the first of all Roman poets, simply declared that the heavens were “the highest upper rooms,” naming them from the breadth of their extent [Note: [Col. 0550B] Rhenanus and Semler read “from a broad extent”], or perhaps because he had read in Homer of Jupiter feasting there.

But it is astonishing how many upper realms above upper realms, [Col. 0550B] and how many heights above heights [Note: [Col. 0550C] **Heights above heights.** Irenaeus: “In invisible and ineffable heights.” RIG.], the heretics have hung, raised, and spread out as a dwelling for each one’s god. They define him as unbegotten, immeasurable, infinite, invisible, and eternal—as though they immediately proved that he exists if they define him as being such as we know he ought to be, so that [Note: [Col. 0551B] “So that” is absent from Rhenanus and others] he may thus also be said to have existed before all things. ## CHAPTER VIII

Behold, then, the second Tetrad: Word and Life, Humanity and Church. Because it had sprouted for the Father’s glory, these too, exulting in this number [Note: [Col. 0552B] Rhenanus, Junius, and Semler read “for this number”], wish to offer the Father something of their own of the same kind. They bubble forth other offspring, likewise marital, [Col. 0553A] through the coupling of the two sexes. On the one side, Word and Life pour forth a Decad of Aeons together; on the other, Humanity and Church produce two more than that, equaling their parents, because they themselves, two together with those ten, make as many as they themselves begot.

I now give the names of those whom I called the Decad: Bythos (“Depth”) and Mixis (“Mixture”); Ageratos (“Unaging”) and Henosis (“Union”); Autophyes (“Self-produced”) [Note: [Col. 0553B] Rhenanus and Semler omit “Ageratos and Henosis; Autophyes”] and Hedone (“Pleasure”); Acinetos (“Immovable”) and Syncrasis (“Commingling”); Monogenes (“Only-begotten”) and Macaria (“Blessedness”).

By contrast, the following will constitute the number twelve: Paracletus (“Advocate”) and Pistis (“Faith”); Patricos (“Paternal”) and Elpis (“Hope”); Metricos (“Maternal”) and Agape (“Love”); Ainus (reading uncertain; the parallel Greek has Aeinous, “Eternal Mind”) and Synesis (“Understanding”); Ecclesiasticus (“Ecclesiastical”) and Macariotes (“Blessedness”); Theletus (“Willed”) and Sophia (“Wisdom”).

I am compelled here to present a comparable example of what these names deserve. In the schools [Note: [Col. 0553B] The old editions and manuscripts read the uncertain *isclis*. This is perhaps an African word. [Note: [Col. 0553C] **Φεῦ.** They acclaim *Pheu!* It is here the cry of students offering congratulations and praise and standing amazed at such eloquence in their teacher, as the same commentator notes. Among the Greeks φεῦ is the cry not only of one grieving but also of one expressing wonder. Here, moreover, Tertullian alludes to the words of Irenaeus, book I, chapter 5: “Iou, iou! Pheu, pheu! For one must speak tragically concerning this fabrication of names.” Epiphanius illustrates this by adding the following paraphrase: “Holy Irenaeus too, mocking them for the things stated above, introduced that tragic cry, ‘Iou, iou! Pheu, pheu!’ For one may truly utter this tragic cry over such a calamity—the calamity of those who described these ridiculous fabrications of names, and of the man who, with such audacity and entirely without shame, imposed a name upon his lie.” Feuardent establishes from Aristophanes’ *Birds* and Sophocles’ *Ajax* that Irenaeus should be read in this way—and, in my judgment, Epiphanius as well, in both Greek and Latin. You have heard Fortunata, Hedone, Acinetus, and Theletus; now acclaim: “The household of Ptolemy—Φεῦ (*Pheu!*—‘Bravo!’)” [Note: [Col. 0554B] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read the corrupt *Ptolomaeifae*].

This will be that mysterious Pleroma, the fullness of a thirtyfold divinity. Let us see what privileges belong to these numbers—four, eight [Note: [Col. 0554B] Junius suspects that “and ten” should be added], and twelve. Meanwhile, at thirty the whole fertility comes to an end: the force, capacity, and generative lust of the Aeons have been castrated. As though so many clusters of shoulders did not still remain, along with no other [Note: [Col. 0554B] Junius suggests “and a thousand other”] names from the nursery! Why, indeed, are not fifty or a hundred begotten? Why are not Stercolae (“excrement-cleaners”) and Syntrophi (“foster-companions”) also named [Col. 0554B]?

[Note: [Col. 0553D] **Why are Stercolae and Syntrophi not also named?** What precedes sufficiently indicates that these words should be understood with reference to the nursery—that is, to attendants upon cradles and to the fellow-nurslings and playmates of children. But all these conjectures depart too far from the traces of the ancient text, which must be followed as closely as possible, even at the risk of contamination.

[Col. 0554B] The old books read: *Stercejae et Syntrophi*. Others have *Sterceae*; two Vatican manuscripts, *Stercojae*. This last comes nearest to the truth. Copyists’ hands frequently make such an error as writing *j* for *l*: *Stercojae* for *Stercolae*. Septimius is plainly joking and asks the inventors of the Aeons—who imagined various chambers throughout those many stories and couplings of the sexes—how it comes about that all fertility is contained within the number thirty, as though numerous other names from the nursery did not remain. “Why,” he asks, “are Stercolae and Syntrophi not also named?” By *Stercolae* he means nurses who clean or move cradles, formed in the same way as *Stercolus*, the deity presiding over the manuring of fields.
