COMMUNICATION. II, 58 C, “And communication after deliberation,” namely, in prayer, assembly, and every sacred matter. [Col. 1275A] No one was admitted to it unless, after prior deliberation, he had been found thoroughly approved. II, 789 A, “Not from communication with the womb,” i.e. from that defilement which occurs in intercourse, which he had previously called “the genital passion.”

COMMUNICATOR. II, 1027 A, “And from there they return as communicants,” that is, with the right of peace and communion.

COMMONLY, in a common manner. I, 376 A, “What can be seen commonly,” i.e. with the eyes, hands, and senses.

COMPACT, closely packed, frequent, dense. II, 549 A.

COMPARISON. II, 579 A, B, “For comparison with the animate being,” i.e. so that this one may be formed in conjunction with the animate being and compared with it.

COMPEER. I, 284 A, “But now, as they wish, your compeers,” i.e. those like you, who bring against Christians—though falsely—the charges that they themselves actually commit.

CONTRIBUTORY [COMPARCINUS]. II, 586 A, “That contributory Soter.” Semler has *compactitius* (“put together”); Junius reads *comparcivus*, i.e. one toward whose constitution and construction everyone [Col. 1275B] had contributed—or, as was formerly said, “contributed jointly”—his share. Naevius, *The Fourfold Twins*: “No one spared himself in his work.”

FELLOW-FEELING. II, 799 B, “A fellow-feeling of judgments,” among you, namely, between whom a common concern arising from a common judgment intervenes. Conversely, in the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, he prefaces his argument by saying that he has now incurred a concern peculiar to his own opinions. Later he calls it “a favorable communion of sentiments.”

ABRIDGE. II, 362 A, “For the New Testament has been abridged and freed from the ragged burdens of the Law.” II, 575 B, “Just as the word has been abridged, so also the washing.”

SHORT METHOD; SUMMARY. II, 248 A, “Lest the short method of prescription, invoked everywhere, be attributed to lack of confidence.” By “the short method of prescription” he means this thesis: “What is introduced afterward will be deemed heresy to the same extent that what was handed down formerly and from the beginning is held to be truth.” II, 519 B, “In my *Prescription against All Heresies*, I am accustomed to establish a short method based upon the testimony of chronology.” [Col. 1275C] I, 1214 B, “And thereafter the prerogative of inseparable intimacy can confer the short method of baptism.” II, 894 B, “By nature, brevity of speech is both agreeable and necessary.”

COMPENSATE, exchange. II, 1041 B, “Who exchanged the whole lot of his epithet for feminine adornment.”

ASCERTAINED, in deponent form. II, 222 B, “Whence, then, did Hermogenes ascertain it?” used for the active *comperit* (“he ascertained”), just as *consultari* is used.

ASCERTAINED. II, 312 B, “Certainly on the basis of what has been ascertained,” i.e. not rashly, but from a fact duly ascertained.

RESTRAIN. I, 262 A, “Who restrains the old law,” i.e. abolishes it, so that he may introduce the new one in its place.

CROSSROADS. II, 882 B, “And the crossroads of the stomach.” He thus calls the meeting-place and receptacle into which, namely, everything edible that is put in through the mouth converges.

EMBRACE, in a passive sense. II, 225 C, “And they were embraced,” i.e. they were bound together and contained within the embrace of heaven. Cicero likewise [Col. 1275D] used the verb “to embrace” in *Tusculan Disputations* V, 14: “In that class within which the happy life is embraced”; see Davies there; and *For Roscius of Ameria* XIII: “Within which single crime all crimes appear to be embraced.” Other Vatican manuscripts have *contemplabantur*, in a passive sense, i.e. “they were joined and fitted together.” See CONTEMPLATE.

ARRANGE; EFFECT. II, 432 A, “So as to effect a settlement between him and his brother concerning the inheritance to be divided.” On the same page: “For effecting peace between the brothers.” I, 363 A, “Adulteries are arranged in temples.”

MASTER OF; POSSESSED OF. II, 1035 A, “We are possessed of the true history.” II, 217 C, “Accordingly, he would have made us masters both of heaven and of earth,” i.e. the Holy Spirit, who taught us the secrets of heaven, would have instructed us concerning heaven, from which created things had been made. The same Spirit would also have taught us concerning the earth from which things were created, and thus would have made you masters both of heaven and of earth. II, 965 A, “And they make them masters of hidden things.” II, 396 A, “He both revealed this will of the Law [Col. 1276A] and gave mastery of it.” II, 726 A, “If we dreamed while masters”—of ourselves, namely—and the soul did not in some fashion fall away from the senses of its body.

COMPOSITE. II, 659 A, “Everything composite and constructed is dissoluble.”

JOINT POSSESSOR. I, 682 B, “Joint possessors of the world, not of error.”

COLLISION, contention. I, 400 A, “Nor is there any other greater collision between us and them.” I, 464 B, “It would also disturb the spectacles with the rival collisions of factions.”

RECKON; REGARD. II, 995 B, “But those who have not reckoned God.” II, 768 A, “Not reckoning,” namely, i.e. despising.

ATTEMPT. I, 483 A, “And if any fruits of trees attempt to grow there,” etc., i.e. if by chance any trees still remain there, they promise fruit to the eyes of onlookers, but those fruits dissolve into ashes when touched by the fingers of those who pluck them.

BECOME ONE FLESH. II, 786 B, “Thus it becomes one flesh and one inward substance [Col. 1276B] with that to which it is joined.”

BECOMING ONE FLESH. II, 941 C, “An occasion for a third becoming-one-flesh may break in.”

YIELD; DESIST. II, 156 A, “And to desist from the resolve to receive spiritual gifts.” II, 110 B, “Which yielded among our teachers.”

ASSEMBLY. Festus: “A place where people come together in council, where several persons under their own jurisdiction sit.” Frontinus teaches that municipalities were also formerly called *conciliabula*. I, 463 A, “Municipalities, assemblies, and the camps themselves.” I, 639 B, “For not only these assemblies of the spectacles.”

HARMONIZE. II, 994 C, “When you think you have harmonized the blending of colors correctly.”

JOINING. II, 1030 C, “By the joining of the light.” The opening through which light entered and the mouth of the tunic were closely packed with material heaped more densely, or were secured with a stronger strap, generally made of felted work, which the Latins called *concilium*. Mercerius prefers *consilio*, i.e. “by the principle of color.” II, 1041 A, “The design of the name,” i.e. the name that he adopted [Col. 1276C] and attached to himself.

COMBINED. II, 1003 C, “Combined and ashen, composed with disgrace and horror”: in the squalor of penitents, in sackcloth and ashes.

MAKE OF ONE COLOR. II, 995 A, “With figures making them of one color.”

INCORPORATE. II, 988 B, “Divine Scripture has incorporated us.” II, 1009 C, “For when he has been incorporated again into the Church.”

INCORPORATION. II, 366 A, “As interpolated by the defenders of Judaism, for incorporation with the Law and the Prophets.” I, 1207 B, “Certainly human ingenuity will be able to summon spirit into water, and effect their incorporation,” etc. He means hydraulic organs, such as those described by Vitruvius in Book X of *Architecture*.

INCORPORIFIED. II, 577 A. See *Against the Valentinians*, chapter 23.

CONCUBINE. I, 1277 A, “The patriarchs also had concubines.” II, 921 A, “Indeed, they also mingled marriages with concubines.”

BEDFELLOW. II, 815 B, “The insistent and disturbing [Col. 1276D] bedfellow of his own blood,” as though a man who has placed beneath his pillow a sword stained with the blood of the slain were lying upon that blood.

WELL-CULTIVATED. II, 1036 A, “Better cultivated and more pleasant than Alcinous’s orchard.”

EXTORTION; SHOCK. I, 703 A, “Soldiers by extortion”: soldiers who, thinking that they are ingratiating themselves with the emperors, hand over, betray, and denounce their fellow soldiers. II, 952 A, “Amid the shock of the whole world.” I, 308 A, “When his extortion was understood from the indictment,” i.e. when he had heard how those who had reported his name in their indictment had used violence against him to extort money. For *concussio* is terror inflicted under the guise of authority or by order of the governor, or else the extortion of money from another person through false accusation or violence.

EXTORTIONER is one who extorts money through false accusation or by inspiring terror. II, 115 B, “What does that extortioner also say?” concerning Simon in the *Acts of the Apostles*. II, [Col. 1277A] 118 B, “Our extortioners, brought into friendship through Mammon.”

EXTORTION, the same as *concussio*: a form of civil robbery in which someone is compelled by terror to give something against his will. II, 117 B, “In the cause of almsgiving, not of extortion.”

GIVE MUTUALLY. II, 738 A, “And once peace had again been given mutually, he restored it to its proper position,” that is, after the kiss of peace, which was the seal and conclusion of prayer, as he himself says near the end of the book *On Prayer*. By “peace given mutually” he means the kisses exchanged between brothers or believers who had taken part in the duty of laying out the dead. Here *condere* means “to give mutually.”

SAY IN AGREEMENT; ASSENT. II, 176 B, “Scripture also speaking in agreement.” II, 150 B, “But Peter alone had spoken in agreement with the man of God,” i.e. had spoken together with Paul; Peter had said things consonant with Paul. II, 268 C, “They had reached agreement concerning fellowship in the Gospel,” i.e. they had come to terms. II, 905 B, “By which they agreed upon the whole confusion.” See CONFUSION. I, 294 A, “If a station-fast is to be observed, [Col. 1277B] the husband appoints a day for the baths,” i.e. he attaches to himself friends who will bathe at his house, at a time when a great deal of service was required and the woman could not observe the station-fast. II, 657 B, “The philosophers agree that all things consist of contraries.” II, 985 A, “We pronounce sins to be the causes of repentance.” II, 207 B, “They would appoint livestock to meet wild beasts,” so that one common shade or common shelter might enclose sheep and wolves, as though they had met amicably at the same appointed place.

SEASONER. II, 686 C, “Plato became the seasoner of all heretics.”

CREATION is the Greek κτίσις, “creation.” II, 207 B, “When the revelation of the sons of God has redeemed creation from evil,” from Romans VIII. I, 281 A, “Nor any other creation.” II, 148 C, “Just as also the totality of creation”; Greek τὸ ὅλον τῆς κτίσεως, “the whole of creation.” II, 895 C, “If, therefore, there is any new creation in Christ.” II, 502 B, “Whom every created thing would serve.” I, 1313 A, “Because incense is God’s creation.” I, 632 A, “For adulterating uses of the divine creation.” II, 84 A, “Thus, therefore, creation has also been brought into ill repute in connection with the pleasures of the spectacles.” II, 995 [Col. 1277C] B, “For whom also every more agreeable created thing has been snatched from the throat,” i.e. every edible thing created and fashioned by God. II, 656 B, “The whole creation,” i.e. whatever has been fashioned and created by God. II, 702 B, “Lest any preparation for burial be exposed.” Junius reads: “lest any preparation for burial putrefy.” Here *conditio* means seasoning or a preparation applied to fish. Thus he makes sport of Empedocles’ words, because Empedocles had called himself a fish.

CONDITIONAL. I, 678 A, “But I was a conditional slave.” A slave is called *conditionalis* when assigned and bound to a particular condition. II, 638 A, “Like the conditional threat of the sword.” II, 354 B, the same words. II, 137 C, “When, through strength of mind, they shake off the king’s conditional threats.”

CONFESSION. II, 649 B, “Which among us are condemned by the confession that they are apocryphal,” i.e. because it is acknowledged that the books are apocryphal. I, 471 A, “They become the foster-children of their own confession.” II, 1028 B, “Whom cruelty cast down after he had wrestled with tortures in the battle of confession.” [Col. 1277D]

GATHER; PREPARE. II, 52 B, “For his material he gathered a slaughter of the Scriptures.” Junius proposes: “For his material he made a joint-hewing of the Scriptures.” *Concaedes* is a heap of branches cut from a forest.

CONFISCATED. II, 116 A, “Who possess their soul alone among confiscated goods.” Suetonius used the word *confiscatum* where, in speaking of money as confiscated, he means money kept in the treasury chests.

FAN TOGETHER. I, 656 B, “From intercourse they fan together the sparks of lust.”

CONFLICT. II, 458 C, “The conflicts of the world.” I, 1223 A, “At the same time we also make satisfaction for former things through the conflict of flesh and spirit.” II, 806 B, “By conflicts of the soul I mean fasts,” etc.

CONFEDERATE. II, 570 A, “So that in him, according to the Apostle, all things may be confederated.” Perhaps [Col. 1278A] *condendum* (“created”) should be read, for Irenaeus has: Ὅπως ἐν αὐτῷ τὰ πάντα κτισθῇ, “so that in him all things may be created,” etc., Colossians I.

CONFORMABLE. II, 863 A, “Conformable to the body of his glory.” II, 877 A.

CONFUSION. II, 1033 A, “The differentiated confusion of the stars.” He is his own best interpreter in the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*: “The clusters of stars are rekindled, and the absences of constellations, which temporal differentiation had removed, are restored.” II, 905 B, “Through the common pledge of conscience, by which they agreed upon the whole confusion.” He almost said, “They agreed upon the whole shamelessness of marital dishonor”; but Junius interprets: “By a common agreement concerning all things, they pledged to one another an indiscriminate association in both law and fact.” Incorrectly.

CONFUSED. I, 273 A, “O judgment confused by necessity!” i.e. one which necessarily sins against justice in some respect; or rather one by which Trajan, wishing both to meet the hatred directed against Christians and to spare their just cause, satisfies neither properly.

ENCOUNTER. II, 549 B, “Although I have postponed [Col. 1278B] the encounter,” i.e. the refutation. See PLAY. I, 664 B, “And to be judged in the very encounter of the flesh.”

GREGARIOUS. I, 1247 A, “Not notorious for banquets, not gregarious at revels.”

CONCEPTION. II, 560 A, “And a conception of the unbegotten.” Irenaeus: ἀγέννητον κατάληψιν, “a conception of the unbegotten.”

FUSE [CONISCARE]. II, 1049 A, “He fused the dish.” Salmasius and others read *conflavit*, “he melted down.”

THROWN BACK TOGETHER. II, 732 B, “Nor with the internal organs thrown back together, as though their compartments had been emptied out.”

KINSHIP OF DOCTRINE. II, 45 B, i.e. doctrines handed down by one and the same author, as though by one parent.

SOWN TOGETHER. II, 721 B, “Strato affirms that sleep is the separation of the conjoined spirit.”

CONSCIENCE. I, 261 A, “Which must be judged not from the outcome but from conscience.” The meaning is: Even if by some chance you hated the Christians with perfect justice, nevertheless you would act unjustly by persecuting with hatred people whom you have not ascertained to be worthy of hatred [Col. 1278C], since you burn with blind hatred of them. I, 375 A, “As though we had passed them over from consciousness,” i.e. had kept silent out of fear. I, 438 A, “Because it is within your knowledge,” i.e. because you know perfectly well that it is your crime. I, 452 B, “Conscience, rather,” i.e. the innermost worship of the mind. II, 44 B, “To whom no consciousness of continuance grants admission,” i.e. to whom no one will grant a place and admit them, because he is conscious that they have either endured for some time or remain established still. II, 1045 A, “A priestly platform” for “the platform of a priest.” II, 928 A, “If you fill a new wife from your own conscience.” I, 289 A, “No law owes to itself alone an awareness of its own justice.” I, 328 A, “We appeal and make our challenge from you yourselves to your conscience.” I, 617 B, “Whether the soul’s conscience was formed by God.” I, 468 A, “We are a body through shared consciousness and the divinity of our discipline.” I, 1316 B, “Those from whom the consciousness of true chastity is absent.” II, 943 A, “We shall be re-formed in substance, not in consciousness.”

FELLOW INITIATE [CONSECRANEUS], one initiated into the same rites. I, 366 A. [Col. 1278D]

FELLOWSHIP IN SERVITUDE. II, 1316 A, “Who by my right of fellowship in servitude and brotherhood.”

SEAL. II, 540 A, “Which they seal,” i.e. admit to the sacred rites. Rigault reads, “They seal the tongue,” i.e. impose silence. Those initiated into the mysteries were called μεμυημένοι (“initiates”) and σφραγισθέντες (“those who had been sealed”). Philo, *On the Monarchy*; Irenaeus, Book I, chapter 24; Prudentius, *On the Martyrdom of St. Romanus*; Arnobius, Book III.

TAKE ONE’S STAND. I, 284 A, “Now I shall take my stand upon the case of innocence,” i.e. I shall take up arms. This manner of speaking is taken from athletes and gladiators, who plant their foot in the arena so that they cannot easily be tripped or thrown down. I, 500 B, “We have taken our stand against the accusation of every crime,” i.e. standing firm, we defend the ground we had taken. II, 931, “So that a stand must now first be taken upon this point.”

CONSISTORY. I, 1298 A, “As though from a new consistory of public lusts.” [Col. 1279A] II, 832 A, “So that he may be helped or harmed through the outcomes of his own consistory,” i.e. the outcomes predetermined and fixed by the consistory in which judgment concerning man is made—namely, the counsel of God. The metaphor is drawn from forensic practice, for in law the supreme council is called a *consistory*. I, 649 B, “We separate ourselves from the theater, which is the private theater of shamelessness.”

CONSOLE; HONOR. I, 645 A, “Thus they consoled death with murders.” II, 624 B, “The citizens of Clazomenae honor Hermotimus with a temple.” II, 647 A, “For whom the injury is not to be consoled, but rather insulted.” II, 1044 B, “And he consoles his neck, made more unclean by its position, with necklaces,” i.e. the shamelessness of utterly absurd luxury consoles the necks condemned to the wretched service of washing the latrine. II, 547 A, “An unimpaired preservation of Valentinus’s rules consoles his memory.”

DOUGH. II, 418 C, “Besides being stuffed with burdens of dough.” By “burdens of dough” he means a mass of flour compacted by sprinkling with water. Exodus XXII, 34: Ἀνέλαβε δὲ ὁ λαὸς τὸ σταῖς αὐτῶν πρὸ τοῦ ζυμιωθῆναι τὰ [Col. 1279B] φυράματα αὐτῶν, “And the people took up their dough before their mixtures had been leavened,” etc. Hesychius: Σταῖς, φύραμα ἀλεύρου πυροῦ, “*stais*: a mixture of wheat flour.” An old glossary: Σταῖς, *adeps*, “fat.” Likewise: Φύραμα, *massa, conspersio*, “mass, dough.”

BEHOLDER. I, 1331 A, “God is the beholder of the heart.”

DEFILE. I, 644 A, “If the equipment of the contests is defiled by idolatry because of profane crowns.”

CONFIDENTLY; STEADFASTLY. II, 256 A, “So that I may employ the defense of the argument presented more confidently,” i.e. more freely and boldly. II, 384 C, “But because he did not steadfastly defend the disciples,” i.e. not courageously. II, 469 A, “So that you may expound him confidently,” i.e. fearlessly, freely, and without concealment. I, 1220 A, “Who did not even steadfastly permit a woman to learn,” i.e. did not permit her to inquire absolutely or question obstinately.

CONFIDENCE; CERTAINTY. I, 1218 B, “For then the confidence of the rescuer is accepted.” It means the confidence of one who rescues a person in danger: conscious of his own right, he comes to the rescue without doubt or hesitation, and thus is admitted, accepted, and praised. II, 659 B, “But also from the certainty [Col. 1279C] of grace through revelation.” He means heavenly grace—a grace whose existence is most certainly established.

ESTABLISH. II, 260 C, “So that most of the natural philosophers feared to establish a beginning and an end for the world,” i.e. to attribute them to it.

ESTABLISH. II, 869 B, “Which he established in his members,” i.e. declared established and fixed, so that it adheres by the roots.

CONSTITUTION. II, 29 A, “Concerning the endeavor to adopt a different constitution,” i.e. στάσις, the “stance” or status of the case.

BIND TIGHT, to hold most severely constrained. II, 303 A, “Because they cut, burn, amputate, and bind tight.”

CONSUBSTANTIAL. II, 563 A, ὁμοούσιος, commonly “coessential”: “equal and consubstantial things.”

CUSTOM. II, 970 B, “Let them not seek what they are accustomed to, nor customary things, nor desire their own possessions, so that they may live more luxuriously in prison than at home.”

ASK ADVICE, in deponent form. II, 212 A, B, “Or whom he asked for advice.”

SUM UP. “Where the soul itself sums up,” i.e. where the soul, [Col. 1279D] as it departs from the body, reckons up each of its actions within it and draws the total, like money gathered from many accounts.

DESPISE [COMTEMNERE]. I, 1257 A, “I disdain to inquire what that angel of perdition was first—evil or impatient.”

COMBINE. II, 858 B, “They compound the fetus from body and soul.”

BE JOINED AS RAFTERS, in a passive sense. II, 225 C, “Thus heaven also lay over the spirit and the waters, and they were embraced.” According to Junius, other readings from the Vatican manuscripts have *contemplabantur*, used in the terminology of building. “For a *templum*,” says Festus, “is a horizontal beam which, in the upper part of a building, is placed crosswise above the rafters.” Vitruvius understands it in this way in Book IV, chapter 2. Hence rafters are said *contemplari*, i.e. to be joined to one another by means of a crossbeam, to be linked together, and to be bound fast where they terminate in their crossbeam. Thus, therefore, the spirit and the waters are said *contemplari* with the heaven, i.e. to be joined and [Col. 1280A] fitted together as within their crossbeam (ibid., chapter 10). I, “For contemplating and distinguishing the stones” [reference incomplete in the source].

REGARD. I, 477 A, “But because with God there is greater regard for the humble,” i.e. care for the poor.

CONTEND, hold a disputation. II, 56 B, concerning heretical women.

DARKEN, to carry out in darkness. I, 272 A, “Because he had shrouded incest in darkness.”

FELLOWSHIP BY TOKEN. II, 32 B, “And fellowship by the token of hospitality”: a more intimate bond established through a *tessera*, a token of hospitality, by the presentation of which a person was immediately recognized if it happened that someone had died. So too *contesserare*, “to enter into fellowship by a token.”

ATTESTATION. I, 1259 B, “I would call it not a testing of faith, but a typological attestation,” i.e. an example, figure, sacrament, or mystery.

IMMEDIATELY. I, 414 B, *In continenti*, i.e. at once, in a single glance, immediately. II, 17 A, “And immediately he adds the heresies.”

CONTINENCE. II, 42 B; 43 A, [Col. 1280B] “A deserter from Marcion’s continence.” Marcion is called continent because he forbids marriage.

CONTAIN; MAINTAIN. I, 534 A, “Because no provision was made for man by containing and sustaining anything,” etc., i.e. because nothing by which provision might be made for man to persist in life could or ought to have been created after man already existed. II, 915 C, D, “If we maintain it,” i.e. if we wish to hold it tenaciously and to put forward the will of God as a pretext for all our actions.

OPPOSITION. II, 703 B, “Which I might set against them under the name of opposition.”

GRIEVE. II, 385 A, “Because he preserves the more important honor of the Sabbath—that of not causing grief.” II, 1003 A, “But if anyone has caused grief, he has not caused grief to me.”

DISHONOR. II, 903 B, “Those who by agreement have now rejected their common dishonor.” Junius thinks those unfair to Tertullian who understand these words solely of the marital use of the bed; he thinks that the afflictions and persecutions common to married persons, which at that time befell them through the injury and dishonor inflicted by others, are instead called “common dishonor.” But I fear that the good Junius—who here, as in many other passages, acted as defender of the author’s harsher statements—may appear an unfair interpreter. [Col. 1280C]

AGREEMENT. I, 1267 B, “Agreement will settle the matter,” i.e. convention, consent, or compact.

SUMMON; ENCOUNTER; FIND. I, 328 A, “We are summoned as defendants on charges of sacrilege and treason.” I, 446 A, “We are summoned on a criminal charge.” II, 24 B, “And so I shall be nowhere so long as I am encountered everywhere,” i.e. so long as I encounter everywhere those who chant at me, “Seek and you shall find,” two grievous evils present themselves to me. One is that there will nowhere be an end to seeking, and I must always wander in uncertainty. The other is that I might wish myself thus to be nowhere, i.e. I would have had to wish that I had never known the doctrine of Christ, because my condition would have been more tolerable if I had never known it. II, 305 B, “When you have found both in the Creator,” i.e. detected them. II, 368 B, “We shall encounter these matters,” i.e. we shall make trial concerning them. II, 773 C, “But we shall encounter another argument of theirs.” [Col. 1280D] II, 719 C, “But that through which the sin is committed is summoned.” II, 91 A, “In whose antiquities, solemnities, and offices it is found,” i.e. it appears, is found, is detected.

REPROACH. II, 543 A, “And it excuses the reproach of falsehood by means of images.” The officiants of the Eleusinian rites approach that image of the male organ as though it were a divinity, in the posture of worshippers. They are therefore liable to the charge of falsehood, since they worship one thing with their eyes and another with their minds. For they say that what they worship is not the image but the nature of things expressed through that image. This is to excuse the accusation of falsehood by means of images—to wash away one lie with another.

BECOME ONE INWARD SUBSTANCE. II, 786 B, “Thus it becomes one flesh and one inward substance with that to which it is joined.”

FELLOW OBJECT OF HATRED [COODIBILIS] is the Greek συμμισούμενος, “hated together with another.” II, 374 C; ibid., 449 D.

ABUNDANCE. II, 262 B, “You reject the sea, but only as far as [Col. 1281A] its abundance,” i.e. the fish that breed and multiply abundantly in the sea. Marcion denied that these, as ἔμψυχοι (“ensouled” or animate beings), should be used as food, and judged them too sacred to be put to human use. II, 871 B, “But abundance arises as interest upon the loan.”

HEART. I, 1175 A, “God, however, is a hearer not of the voice but of the heart.” II, 161 C, “Properly, from the womb of his own heart.”

SKIN. II, 1038 A, “To play from one’s own skin”: a proverb drawn from the chameleon, which throughout its body reproduces whatever color it comes close to, except red and white. Accordingly, the saying clearly applies to a fickle and inconstant man who changes himself with the occasion into every guise.

HORNY. II, 970 B, “And horny against the nails.” The meaning is: as devoid of feeling as horn. See NAIL. II, 741 A, “Or as through a horny mirror.” He imitates Plato, who in the same way, in the *Charmides*, calls the flesh a “horny mirror.” Both allude to the gates of horn and ivory through which dreams pass in Homer. [Col. 1281B]

HORNED. I, 396 A, “Either a horned or a feathered lover.”

CORPOREALITY. II, 842 A, “Lest the corporeality of the soul, by furnishing an occasion for corporeal forms, exclude the corporeality of the flesh.”

BECOME EMBODIED. II, 1032 B, “Embodied,” i.e. compacted and composed from different substances. II, 757 A, “And be embodied in flesh.” Lactantius uses “God embodied” in this sense in *Institutes*, Book IV, chapter 26: “God, therefore, was embodied and clothed in the garment of flesh.”

EMBODIMENT. II, 758 B, “Nor do you reject embodiment as dangerous to God.”

CORPOREAL SUBSTANCE. II, 570 B, “Into rival equivalences of corporeal substances.” II, 335 A, “All these illusions of an imaginary corporeal substance in Christ.” Rigault appropriately cites at this point the words of Boethius in the book *On Faith*: “Even today there are those who deny that he bore our corporeal substance.” II, 653 A, “Perhaps more [arguments] will be constructed [Col. 1281C] to remove the corporeal substance of the soul.”

CORPOREAL. II, 214 A, “As though a corporeal substantive,” i.e. something that possesses a body.

BODY. II, 230 A, “Since the substance itself is the body of each thing”: σῶμα (“body”), by misuse, for what is commonly called “essence.” II, 162 C, “For who has denied that God is a body?” II, 842 B, “For it was also for this reason that we established beforehand that the soul is a body,” etc. II, 130 B, “And concerning the remaining body of each heretic’s doctrine.” I, 432 A, “And the age made a body of times.” II, 521 C, “Christ plainly belongs to the Law, if he is the body belonging to the shadow.”

CORRUPTIVE. II, 303 B, “Because passions of this sort are found in man’s corruptive condition.”

CORYBANTIC. I, 426 A, “And Corybantic bronze instruments.” The Corybantes, or Curetes, were employed with Rhea the nurse to guard Jupiter. La Cerda, following Strabo, thinks that they were called Curetes as though κουροτροφήσαντες, “those who reared the boy”; unless instead they were named from guardianship, for βαίνειν means φυλάττειν, “to guard,” whence Homer says, ὃς Χρύσην ἀμφιβέβηκας, “you who stand guard over Chryse.” [Col. 1281D]

TRAGIC BUSKIN-PLAY. II, 563 B, “But beyond the curtain there is another tragic buskin-play,” i.e. another tragic scene, another tragic act.

FRAMEWORK OF BONES. II, 854 B, “Whose frameworks still survive,” i.e. σκελετοί, “skeletons,” the remains of bones that adhere to the spine in the manner of a lattice. Thus Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, Book VIII: “You would think the breast hung down and was held only by the lattice of the spine.”

ENTRUST. I, 1220 A, “Moreover, baptism ought not rashly to be entrusted,” i.e. committed and administered to one who requests it.

CREMATE; BURN INCENSE. II, 795 B, “When he cremates for the cremated,” i.e. when he sacrifices to the dead. He uses words that make the custom appear more ridiculous. II, 92 B, “And the Christian who was not permitted to burn incense will be cremated in accordance with military discipline.” He will be cremated in the manner of a military pyre, filled with the rites of the most superstitious idolatry. *Cremare* means, namely, to burn incense to an idol.

[Col. 1282A] INCREASE. II, 281 A, “For the increase of the human race.” II, 691 B, “Increases and decreases.”

RATTLE, one of children’s toys; Greek κρόταλον, “rattle” or “clapper.” II, 618 A, “And about to give the signal for war not with a trumpet but with a rattle.”

HEAD-ORNAMENT, an ornament of the head which makes a rattling sound when the head moves. Festus. II, 1044 B. Turnebus reads *crepidulam*, “little slipper.”

JUDGES. II, 129 B, “The *Critae*, whom we understand to be censors.” Ibid. C, “The annals of the *Critae*.”

TORMENTING. In Apuleius, *cruciarius* is one who bears a cross, a gallows-bird; subsequently, one who loads another with torments. II, 13 A, “And tormenting ends.”

RAW. II, 246 C, “A raw life,” Greek τὸ ζῇν ὠμόν, “raw living.” II, 401 C, “Nor could raw faith therefore attain it”: fresh, beginning, and unprepared faith, opposed to a matured and cultivated nature. I, 1321 D, “Not wholly into a raw and bestial condition.” I, 1247 A, “Who do not occupy entrance halls with nocturnal and raw salutations.”

[Col. 1282B] HAVE UNDIGESTED FOOD, said of food not yet digested. I, 322 A, “The very bellies of bears are sought while they still hold undigested human entrails,” i.e. the human flesh deposited in those animal tombs has not yet been digested when the beasts themselves are sought for a banquet.

CROSS means the clasp by which the boss of the toga was secured. II, 1047 A, “It is henceforth entrusted to no clasp.”

ELBOW. II, 743 A, “And to push them away with the elbow,” i.e. to spurn and reject people imbued with so perverse an opinion.

ARSE; FUNDAMENT. II, 1020 A, “Resin from the fundament.” Salmasius and others read, “resin from the armpit.” Hence *alipilarius*, one who removed hair from the armpits with resin and depilatory ointment. For *alipila* was a resin with which the armpits were depilated.

ADORNMENT. I, 1309 A, “By adornment we mean what they call a woman’s finery,” namely gold, gems, and garments.

[Col. 1282C] WEDGE. II, 270 B, “For by this wedge of truth every heresy is driven out,” i.e. by this argument, formed like a wedge-shaped battle line in defense of the truth. The metaphor is taken from military discipline. II, 797 C, “With what wedge we must charge,” that is, with what wedge-shaped battle line, with how firm a wedge for breaking through the enemy ranks. II, 988 B, “This is our wedge, our structure.” II, 546 A, “We have armed this first wedge of the encounter.”

TEND; WORSHIP. II, 976 B, “Things tending God,” i.e. worshipping him. I, 655 B, “Who is groomed even in womanly ways,” i.e. who is feminized with the utmost care in voice, gesture, and gait, so that in dancing the stories of women he may be taken for a woman. II, 706 B, “A delay carefully arranged for the end, until the punishment is complete.”

ATTENDANCE; WORSHIP. II, 976 B, They are said “to render attendance,” θεραπείαν, “service” or “worship.” Lipsius, Letter 95, says that Tertullian alludes to the custom of Roman women who held a mirror for Juno, arranged her hair, and so forth, in the manner of attendants. Seneca, *On Superstition*.

SECRET-SERVICE OFFICERS. II, 118 C, “In the registers of the *beneficiarii* [Col. 1282D] and the *curiosi*.” *Beneficiarii* were soldiers selected through the favor of the emperor for particular duties or services. Thus they attended upon the emperor in order to execute his commands. From their corps or body came the *Curiosi*, who were placed in charge of the imperial postal service and were also called *stationarii*, being assigned to stations. Among other things, the first law of the Code *On the Curiosi* imposes upon them the duty of remembering to report crimes to the judges. They therefore sought out and reported the names of accused persons, and brought the accused themselves before tribunals and courts. The old account of the martyrdom of Bishop Fructuosus says: “The *Beneficiarii* were sent to his house”; and shortly afterward: “The soldiers said to him, ‘Come; the governor summons you,’” etc. The anonymous author of the *Life of Chrysostom* in the Suda says, ἑλκόμενος ὑπὸ τοῦ κουριόσου τῆς πόλεως, “dragged away by the *curiosus* of the city.” And Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book IX, chapter 9: Βενεφικιαρίων ὕβρεις καὶ σεισμοί, “outrages and extortions by the *beneficiarii*.” Offices of this kind ultimately degenerated into misconduct, robberies, and extortions, since the *Beneficiarii* and *Curiosi* generally connived at the wickedness of the worst sort of men. For, themselves corrupted by annual or monthly payments, they concealed the crimes of tavern-keepers, gluttons, thieves, and pimps; so much so that they kept at home registers of such disgraceful agreements. Since they also cruelly executed the commands of emperors, proconsuls, or governors against Christians as though against criminals, Christians too finally began to pay them sums of money under agreements for periodic payments. Septimius says that it is disgraceful and shameful to the Christian name for Christians also to be counted as tribute-payers in the registers of the *Beneficiarii* and *Curiosi*, among gluttons, thieves, tavern-keepers, and pimps. [Col. 1283A]

CURIOSITY. I, 265 A, “Here alone human curiosity grows sluggish.” The meaning is: In this respect too you depart from human nature, because it is always inquisitive and investigates more deeply the things it encounters. Heumann prefers “urban curiosity.”

RUN; CONVERGE. II, 908 C, “By whatever instructor they were established, whether a spiritual man instructed by individual revelations of the Paraclete [Col. 1283B], or merely a believer possessing only the common faith, they run toward the same God,” i.e. converge upon the same God and must be understood as referring to the same God. II, 300 C, “Likewise the other good things through which the good work of good severity runs.”

RUN ABOUT. I, 452 B, “To run about in crowds for the purpose of injuries.”

GUARDIANSHIP; CUSTODY. II, 539 A, “The duty of guardianship,” by which they conceal their doctrine, like those accustomed to guard something carefully. II, 771 A, “Whom also by the means afforded by unrestricted custody.” Lucian: διαφθείροντες τοὺς δεσμοφύλακας, “corrupting the prison guards.” I, 325 A, “And surely human blood would not be lacking for you in the hearing of imprisonments and the condemnation of prisoners.”

GUARDHOUSE. I, 622 A, “So that you, blessed ones, may perhaps consider yourselves transferred from prison into a guardhouse,” i.e. so that you may judge this concerning yourselves: that you are being kept for freedom, not for captivity and death. For the prison of those awaiting condemnation, or already condemned, is the guardhouse of the *stationarii* and scouts, who perform the duty of their station and keep watch as from a lookout, as it is called in the book *Against the Jews*, chapter 3. [Col. 1283C]

SKIN. II, 1042 A, “Struck within the skin and beyond it.” Cleomachus was struck “within the skin” when he had already been made a passive homosexual, and “beyond the skin” when he was a boxer, because of the wounds inflicted by boxing gloves, which penetrated beneath the skin.

WAVE-MOLDINGS are all projecting architectural works that flow lengthwise like water—upright or inverted ogees and similar forms. Vitruvius, *Architecture*, Book III, chapter 3, and Book IV, chapter 6. Hesychius: Κυμάτια τὰ χείλη διὰ τὸ κυμαίνειν, ἢ αἱ ὑπεροχαὶ παρὰ τέκτοσι καὶ λιθοποιοῖς, “*Cymatia*: rims, because they undulate, or the projections used by carpenters and stonemasons.” I, 674 A, “And to extend the wave-moldings.”

CYMBAL. II, 1043 B, “He walked with a cymbal.”

DOG-HEADED [CYNOCEPHALUS], κυνοκέφαλος, “dog-headed”; δεινός, “terrible”; ἀναίσχυντος, “shameless.” II, 126 B, “By this Dog-Head, namely,” i.e. the devil, who harasses the human race with canine envy. Junius understands it of Plautianus, at whose instigation the tide of disasters against Christians flared up in the time of Severus.

CYNOPAE, or Cynopes, are dog-headed men, concerning whom Pliny, *Natural History*, Book VII, chapter 2, writes from the Indian accounts of Ctesias. I, 312 B. But at I, 370 A, the reading is *Cynopennae*. Pamelius and La Cerda read *Cynophanae*; Wouwer, *Cyclopes*; but Heraldus has *Cynopenae*. [Col. 1283D]
