But what is this compared with that wine which is preserved in the ancient castle called Schrofenstein, near Landeck in the County of Tyrol, and is said to have attained an age of more than four hundred years? Certainly, they report that the cask has now been overcome and eaten away by decay, while the wine itself is enclosed on every side by its own tartareous crust, so that it cannot leak out anywhere. And this wine—for I was permitted to taste some offered by a distinguished friend in the neighborhood—has a tartareous smell and taste, of which one might rightly say what the same [Col. 1232D] Cicero says in the passage cited: “Excessive age possesses neither the sweetness we seek, nor indeed is it any longer tolerable.” I except its spirits, which are thoroughly heroic and fit to rekindle the final sparks of old age. I have been pleased to mention these matters by way of a digression arising from the occasion.

**You should bestow upon your soul.** That is, you should be equally liberal and generous toward it. It is otherwise evident, however, that Tertullian held far too crude a conception of the soul. For throughout his *On the Soul*, and especially in chapter 38, he asserts that it grows to maturity at puberty no less than the body does; that there is also in the soul a desire for food; and, in chapter 9, that it is visible in a certain color, and so forth.

**Injured by sinning.** These words are undoubtedly ironic, directed against those who suppose that, even while indulging the body in everything, they can make satisfaction for their sins.

**They go about in a circuit**, that is, they walk about or make the rounds.

**Magistracies.** A magistracy is an office in the [Col. 1233A] commonwealth carrying the authority to pronounce judgment; among the Romans the term also sometimes denoted the very persons invested with such authority. There were many kinds of magistracies. There were urban magistrates, who administered justice in the City itself, and then provincial magistrates, who administered justice in the provinces subject to the Roman commonwealth, such as proconsuls, propraetors, legates of proconsuls and propraetors, provincial quaestors, and so forth. Among the urban magistrates there were also higher magistrates, who were elected in the Centuriate Assembly, such as consuls, censors, praetors, and so forth; and lower magistrates, who were customarily elected in the Tribal Assembly, such as urban quaestors, tribunes of the plebs, plebeian aediles, and so forth. In each of these classes there were ordinary and extraordinary magistrates. Thomas Dempster treats these matters fully and accurately throughout Book V, chapter 5, of his *Roman Antiquities*.

**Neither are they ashamed nor does it irk them.** We have already touched upon certain matters concerning candidates in the note “Candidate for remission” on chapter 10 of *On Baptism*. Now, however, it seems appropriate to say a few things briefly about them, recalling them, as it were, from their origins. Since it was customary for magistrates to be elected in the assemblies by the votes of the people, while [Col. 1233B] many among the people did not recognize by face or know the merits of the persons aspiring to those honors, the latter had to solicit the people’s votes by recounting their merits and presenting themselves in person. First, therefore, so that they might be recognized, they dressed in a gleaming white garment—the distinction between this and an ordinary white garment was that the former had been polished with a kind of chalk to a particular brilliance and greater splendor—and for a *trinundinum*, a period of twenty-seven days, since a market was customarily held in the forum every ninth day, they appeared on the Campus Martius, sitting upon the elevation called the Hill of the Gardens so that they might be more conspicuous. Then, as the people arrived, they went about among them, greeting everyone they met and winning them over by services of every kind.

This method of conciliating the people consisted chiefly of four parts: nomenclature, flattery, assiduity, and generosity.

Nomenclature consisted in knowing how to address every citizen by his own name; otherwise citizens might complain that they had thus far been neglected or had not been held in [Col. 1233C] the same regard as others. Since this was exceedingly difficult amid so great a multitude, every candidate enlisted assistants who would whisper to the office-seeker the names of individual passersby. They were called *nomenclatores*; Cicero, however, in his speech *For Murena* calls them *monitores* (“prompters”), and Festus calls them *fartores* (“stuffers”), because they secretly “stuffed,” as it were, the citizens’ names into the candidates’ ears. It was called flattery when they greeted people not merely by their proper names but also with more flattering forms of address, calling them fathers, brothers, patrons, and so forth, as though they were most closely bound to them. Assiduity consisted chiefly in canvassing, grasping hands, and soliciting votes. They canvassed, or made the rounds, in squares and streets, and not only at Rome but also in other Italian cities where they knew that many citizens were staying for business. They made these rounds either in person or through other friends enlisted to perform a share of this service and sent to those places, or already established there. Hand-grasping was performed by taking each person’s right hand, kissing it, kissing it fondly, [Col. 1233D] and so forth. It was called solicitation when, amid entreaties that were often downright offensively humble, they recalled benefits previously received from the people; they even bared scars that they might have received on the front of their chests in battle for the fatherland, and so forth; then they repeatedly begged that, in consideration of such things, the people would confer their votes upon them. Since they could not accomplish these things by themselves, they also employed other distinguished men, called *suffragatores* (“supporters”), who rehearsed the candidates’ virtues before the people, and so forth. To these services was added generosity, by which they won over the people through distributions, gifts thrown to the crowd, banquets, gladiatorial games, distributions of money previously deposited with intermediaries as a pledge of good faith, and so forth. It is known that some men at times exhausted their entire patrimonies through gifts of this kind.

Lest anyone suppose that we have exaggerated these matters unduly, it certainly seems pertinent to reproduce here several passages from Cicero, where Quintus Tullius instructs his brother [Col. 1234A] in the *Handbook of Electioneering for the Consulship* as follows:

> Many city-dwellers and industrious men, many freedmen influential and active in the forum, are engaged there; recruit those whom you can through your own efforts and those whom you can through mutual friends. Take care that they are eager for your success: exert yourself, seek them out, send people to them, and show that you regard yourself as having received the greatest favor from them. Then take account of the whole city, of every association of districts and neighborhoods. If you attach their leading men to your friendship, you will easily hold the rest of the multitude through them. Afterward, see that you have all Italy mapped out and comprehended in your mind and memory by tribes, and do not allow there to be any municipality, colony, prefecture, or indeed any place in Italy in which you do not have as strong a support as may suffice. Search out and investigate men from every region; get to know them, seek them out, secure them, and see that they campaign for you in their own neighborhoods and are, as it were, candidates on your behalf.

Do you imagine, however, that this business was finally completed merely by doing these things? Far from it: many more duties remained, which you will learn more exactly from Cicero in the same work, and it will be a pleasure to read him.

[Col. 1234B] But to what end have all these things been recounted, and at such length? Plainly so that the force of the argument which Tertullian presses so insistently here may be perceived all the more deeply. These burdens—so serious, so troublesome, so protracted, and almost insurmountable—had to be undertaken by the most eminent men in the Roman commonwealth, men who had already long rendered it the finest service. They bore them with such industry, such labor, and even such willingness, every sense of shame being easily overcome by the hope of honors; yet they were invited by no reward except one that was earthly, unstable, and afterward all too swiftly passing away, surrounded and, so to speak, purchased at the price of so many and such serious troubles and adversities. We, by contrast, though CHRISTIANS, blush to follow Christ, who “had to suffer these things and so enter into his glory” (Luke 24:26). Yet those candidates had committed no offense against the commonwealth but had instead rendered it excellent service; whereas our concern is the pardon of sins, the restoration of grace within us, and the HEAVENLY GLORY thereby attained and possessed for eternity.

[Col. 1234C] **Insults.** Who could believe that these candidates were not sometimes treated rather insultingly or disdainfully by certain men, perhaps enemies or less-than-friendly persons, given the license then enjoyed by the people; or even treated rather negligently by men who were otherwise their friends, amid interests so numerous and so divided? But let us hear Saint Cyprian, who in his *Letter to Donatus* describes these things most elegantly, as is his custom:

> The man who, conspicuous in brighter attire, appears to himself to shine in purple—at the price of what filth did he purchase his brilliance! What disdain of arrogant men did he first endure! What haughty doors did he besiege as a morning caller! How many insulting footsteps of puffed-up men did he precede, attended beforehand by wedges of clients, so that afterward a ceremonial procession of men greeting him might itself precede him—a procession subservient not to the man, but to his power?

And he continues in the same vein.

**Of their vows**, that is, of their desires.

**The ignobility of their garments.** For my part, I find nothing ignoble in this very kind of dress. Indeed, at other times those who were reckoned among the Romans as belonging to a more honorable station [Col. 1234D] and not to the common crowd customarily wore white garments. These were employed not merely when magistracies were being sought, but also in sacred rites, at more solemn banquets, at weddings, in military service, and in triumphs, as a symbol of moral purity, innocence, joy, integrity, and victory, as may be read at length in Johann Wilhelm Stucki, *Antiquities of Banquets*, Book II, chapter 26. I would therefore be more inclined to believe either that the remaining form of the garment worn by candidates differed from that of other people, or that it was made of the cheaper cloth customarily worn by the ignoble populace; or—and this I consider much more probable—that Tertullian is speaking of a period when the candidates’ condition had already fallen in esteem even among the Romans themselves and was regarded as ignoble, because ambition had by then corrupted everything excessively and was tending toward the public corruption of the commonwealth, so that it had become necessary to punish it even by public laws enacted against electoral canvassing.

[Col. 1235A] **They affect.** It is one thing to be disposed toward something and to seek it in moderation; it is quite another to affect it, that is, to be carried toward it by an immoderate desire and to press one’s inclination toward it with excessive solicitude beyond the bounds of what is right. Such is the custom of those who do not bring merits equal to the things desired, or suitable qualifications, yet strive to obtain their desires by every means. They are not unlike young birds which, though not yet sufficiently feathered, flap their wings prematurely and, with confidence far too immature, are seen attempting flight.

**They forestall them with salutations.** Thus they pursued their patrons even by nocturnal and excessively persistent exertions, pouring themselves into any atria they found open—or, more accurately, thrusting themselves in and all but breaking through. He calls these salutations “undigested,” because they were poured forth—or rather belched out—before the food from dinner had yet been properly digested.

**Diminishing themselves.** They made themselves smaller not merely by lowering the head, but by bending the whole body deeply, gaping like chameleons after the popular breeze they hoped to catch.

[Col. 1235B] **Distinguished by no banquets.** Indeed, in the note above on “Neither are they ashamed nor does it irk them,” we indicated that candidates—while their condition was still flourishing—sometimes provided the people with lavish and costly banquets, and so forth. But later, when plebeians too began to be mingled among the candidates, the kitchens themselves grew colder amid their slender and excessively hungry fortunes.

**Of the same flock.** Just as one who is not in the flock but lives alone is called *segrex*, “separate from the flock,” so here he calls *congreges* those who gather together with others, or belong to the flock. Thus Apuleius has in Book VIII of *The Golden Ass*: “The excellent shepherd at last allowed me to join the flock (*congregem*).”

**The fleeting joy of a single year.** It is well established that magistracies among the Romans, following the example of the Athenians, lasted only a year and were rarely extended for several years, almost never except under the emperor Tiberius; see Tacitus, *Annals*, Book I, chapter 80. For, as Dionysius of Halicarnassus says in *Roman Antiquities*, Book IV, chapter 79:

> The alternation of ruling and obeying, and the laying down of power before it corrupts the mind, restrains insolent dispositions [Col. 1235C] and does not permit the characters of rulers to become intoxicated by excessive license.

**The quest for axes and rods**, that is, the quest for the consulship or other magistracies; for here the sign is put metonymically for the thing signified.

The rods were several rounded staffs bound with a strap into a bundle, in which an axe was fixed with its blade projecting. These fasces, therefore, were granted as insignia to the higher magistrates. By them were signified those magistrates’ power of life and death through the axe, and the punishment through the rods of lighter crimes and offenses deserving flogging. When the consuls proceeded in public, fasces with axes were carried before them by an equal number of lictors; but when the consuls pronounced judgment in the forum, the lictors stood apart behind them. Vetulonia, a city of Etruria, is believed to have been the first to employ this custom. Silius Italicus says of it in Book VIII:

> And Vetulonia, once the glory of the Maeonian nation:  
> She first granted that twice six fasces should go before,  
> And joined to them as many axes, with silent terror.

**Of eternity.** Namely, of the eternal punishment to which we are to be condemned unless we return to our senses through repentance [Col. 1235D] while there is still time.

**The chastisement of diet.** This is accomplished through more frequent fasts.

**Attire**, that is, the cultivation of clothing, which is restrained by sackcloth and haircloth.

**They impose upon themselves.** Thus we Christians sometimes tend to be far more liberal toward human beings—and even prodigal in pouring out our possessions upon them—although we are often bound to them by no tie of gratitude and no debt requiring that an injury be repaired. They may consequently appear not so much enriched as burdened by our favors. Indeed, we ourselves are an excessive burden to ourselves, while in the meantime we bear Christ’s “easy yoke and light burden” (Matt. 11:30) with excessive impatience, or refuse it altogether. Yet if anyone owes God a debt because of an injury, he must not therefore despair as though he lacked the means to pay. For, as Saint Ambrose says in *On Repentance*, Book II, chapter 9:

> He who owes God possesses more resources for payment than one who owes a human being. [Col. 1236A] A human being demands money in return for money, and this is not always at the debtor’s disposal; God requires affection, which lies within your power. No one who owes GOD is poor unless he has made himself poor. And if he has nothing to sell, he has something with which to pay. Prayer, tears, and fasts are the property of a good debtor, and far richer than if someone were to tender money from the proceeds of estates without faith. Accordingly, no condition of debtors is better than that of those who are under obligation to God. In order to make satisfaction to God your creditor, you do not therefore have to sell yourself and all your possessions.

But God must be entreated with the servant in the Gospel, Matthew 18:26: “That servant therefore fell down and entreated him, saying: Have patience with me, and I will repay you everything.” What, then, happened? “The master, having pity on that servant, released him and forgave him the debt.” How, then, was a man who had no means of repayment going to repay? He was to repay not from his own resources but from another’s—from the merits and satisfaction of our Christ; and yet these are not another’s, since Christ willed them to be ours.

Therefore, since those ambitious Roman candidates [Col. 1236B] so willingly undertook such great labors to obtain a fleeting dignity, how much better and more generously are we treated, since by the same effort—and one far lighter—we can both make satisfaction and at the same time invest profitably for ourselves in grace, the kingdom of heaven, and EVERLASTING GLORY. “Let magistrates now go their way”—thus I conclude with Saint Cyprian in the cited *Letter to Donatus*—“and consuls or proconsuls: let them glory in the insignia of a year-long dignity and in twelve fasces.”

**They bind with, as it were, a long rope.** Perhaps he has in view Proverbs 5:22 [Note: [Col. 1236D] Rather, Isaiah 5:18. See La Cerda on this passage, volume I of this work, column 1247. ED.]: “His own iniquities shall ensnare the wicked man, and he is bound by the cords of his own sins.” These ropes are indeed long and scarcely capable any longer of being broken. “From a perverse will,” says Saint Augustine in *Confessions*, Book VIII, chapter 5, confirming these things all too fully from his own experience, “lust was made; and when lust was served, habit was made; and when habit was not resisted, necessity was made”—a necessity, certainly, to be overcome by no human strength, but solely through the GRACE OF OUR LORD JESUS CHRIST.

## Notes on Chapter XII

[Col. 1236C]

**You reconsider**, used for “you consider,” as very often elsewhere. A person considers something in his mind when he thinks about confessing his sins and is now preparing to apply his mind seriously and effectively to that confession.

**Gehenna.** Gehenna is by no means, as some have thought, a Greek name, but a Hebrew one, signifying the same thing as “the Valley of Ennom.” Jeremiah 7:30–31 says of it:

> They built the high places of Tophet, which is in the VALLEY of the son of Ennom, that they might burn their sons and their daughters with fire, and so forth. Therefore, behold, the days are coming, and it shall no longer be called Tophet and the VALLEY of the son of ENNOM, but the Valley of Slaughter, and so forth.

“The Hebrews relate,” says Saint Jerome on this very text of Jeremiah, “that GEHENNA was named after this place (*gê hinnom*), because the entire people of the Jews perished there after offending God.” He explains the matter more fully in his commentary on Matthew, chapter 10:

> This valley, a small and level stretch of ground, was well watered, wooded, and full of delights, and within it was a grove consecrated to an idol—

MOLOCH, so often execrated by [Col. 1236D] Holy Scripture.

> The people had fallen into such madness that, forsaking the vicinity of the TEMPLE—

for this valley was close to Jerusalem—

> they offered victims there, and the rigor—

that is, their altogether monstrous and unspeakable cruelty—

> of religion—

namely, idolatrous religion—

> overcame the delights—

of that place, TOPHETH, named from DELIGHTS—

> and they burned their own sons for demons, or initiated them. And that place was called GEHENNOM, that is, the VALLEY of the children of HENNOM.

Thus Saint Jerome there. “Others report,” says the Latin translator of our Calmet in the *Biblical Dictionary*, under “Gehennom,” “that the city’s sewer was situated in that valley, where a perpetual fire was maintained for consuming carcasses and filth.”

**Do not hesitate.** The meaning is: do not deliberate with yourself for a long time over whether to seize upon this remedy or to postpone it any longer.

[Col. 1237A] **A treasury of eternal fire.** Thus he calls Gehenna, or hell; likewise in the *Apology Against the Nations*, chapter 47, he calls it “a subterranean TREASURY of hidden fire reserved for punishment.” Nor is this surprising, since all things preserved in certain repositories are sometimes called treasures. Thus Cicero, in *On the Orator*, calls memory “the treasury of all things.” Indeed, in Plautus I find even the expression “a treasury of evil.”

This word otherwise properly belongs to the Greeks, among whom θησαυρός (*thēsauros*, “treasure”) is thought by some to be derived from τιθέναι εἰς αὔριον (*tithenai eis aurion*, “to lay up for tomorrow,” or for future times), and by others from ἀπὸ τῆς θέσεως (*apo tēs theseōs*, “from the placing”)—namely, of gold—because gold in particular, and other precious things of that kind, are customarily hidden from the eyes and knowledge of other people and kept under stricter guard. I also find that ants and other animals which prepare and bury food for themselves for the winter were called by the Greeks θησαυριστικὰ τῆς τροφῆς ζῶα (*thēsauristika tēs trophēs zōa*), “animals that store up their food.”

That near the center of the earth there is an immense cavern filled with what resembles a fiery ocean raging in perpetual whirlwinds is the common conviction of natural philosophers. Moreover, [Col. 1237B] that this very place is hell is suggested by all those holy Fathers who believed that the souls of the damned are tormented by true and material fire—namely, as Saint Cyprian says in *On the Praise of Martyrdom*, “by torturing flames throughout the dreadful night of dense darkness, and by the perpetual raging fires of a smoking furnace,” and so forth. Read, if nothing else, Saint Augustine, *The City of God*, Book XXI, chapter 10.

Certainly, that treasury of infernal fire must be said to be hidden in some subterranean place, in the very bowels of the earth, until some other place more fitting than this presents itself: one most remote from that supreme and most radiant abode of the blessed, where nothing but “the shadow of death and everlasting horror dwells” (Job 10:22). Since the limbo of the Fathers itself, into which Christ descended to the underworld, was a subterranean place, why should we not assign to hell the lowest and deepest place of all within the earth’s bowels? There, by material fire, not only are those spirits and the souls of the damned even now tormented in marvelous but real ways, [Col. 1237C] but one day, after receiving back their bodies, they too will burn throughout all eternity, ever to be burned and never consumed.

**Little smoke-holes.** Thus, ironically, he calls the fire-spewing mountains scattered throughout the earth and already terrible in Tertullian’s time, especially Vesuvius and Etna, which not a few natural philosophers regard as the furnaces of hell. For they say that the central fire of our terrestrial globe is driven upward through fire-conducting channels into various other receptacles or fire-reservoirs, and from there is further forced through other conduits of this kind, variously intertwined, as far as the surface of our earth, until at last it finds an outlet through the cavernous vents of those mountains and sometimes discharges itself into the open air with incredible violence.

There are also some who say that the wailings of damned souls are sometimes heard in these mountains, though others regard this as entirely fabulous. I judge, however, that it could easily have happened that the subterranean roarings of these mountains, often heard even at a distance, impressed this terrifying apparition upon credulous and terror-stricken minds.

[Col. 1237D] In order that we may examine these little smoke-holes more deeply, it will be useful to draw certain details from an eyewitness, the immortal Athanasius Kircher, and from the preface to his *Subterranean World*, in his own words, lest I appear to have added anything. Driven by a desire to see the phenomenon, he overcame immense difficulties and struggled up to the summit of VESUVIUS, a mountain of Campania:

> I saw the entire crater illuminated by fire—horrible to relate!—amid an intolerable stench of burning sulfur and bitumen. Utterly stunned by this extraordinary spectacle, I believed that I was gazing upon the dwelling place of the infernal regions, in which nothing else seemed to be missing except the dreadful apparitions of demons. The mountain’s horrible roarings and rumblings could be perceived, together with an indescribable stench and smoke mingled with dusky globes of fire, which both the floor and sides of the mountain were continuously belching forth from eleven different places.

When he had prepared his *pantometrum* to measure that terrible crater, [Col. 1238A] he found that its circumference was nearly thirty thousand paces and its depth eight hundred paces.

He continues:

> The mountain, precipitous on every side in the form of a hollowed cylinder, descended around its circumference πρὸς τὰς ὀρθὰς (*pros tas orthas*), “at right angles.” In the center of the floor nature seemed to have established something like its own hearth—a true workshop of Vulcan’s kitchen, seething with an everlasting outpouring of smoke and flames, and occupied, by some hidden mechanism, in refining, separating, and burning sulfur, bitumen, and the other kinds of minerals, in preparation for the deadly devastation soon afterward to be produced. For the vapors enclosed within, as incapable of being contained as they were, shook off the burden placed upon them with such violence and force, accompanied by dreadful crashes, that the mountain seemed to be agitated by an earthquake. Whenever this occurred, the softer upper portions of the mountain, shaken and loosened by the tremor, fell like hills into the bottom of the abyss; and through the varied reflection of the sound they aroused such a crash as even a man of undaunted courage could scarcely endure. The matter continuously belched from [Col. 1238B] the center of the mountain formed what resembled a new mountain, furnished with a marvelous variety of streaks. The varied boiling of liquefied minerals shaped it, by their flow and color over every part of its circumference: now green from copper, then tawny from sulfur, arsenic, and sandarach; now red from cinnabar and red lead; now black from vitriol mixed with water, or ash-gray from the very ashes themselves. Thus it was fashioned by nature’s ingenious brush, and so forth.

Such is Kircher’s account of that Vesuvian smoke-hole. It ought not to seem surprising that the size and internal structure of its crater were described differently by different writers, since after every eruption, as was necessarily the case, it was found to have assumed a different appearance.

But now behold another smoke-hole of this kind: ETNA in Sicily, described by the same Athanasius, again as an eyewitness (*autoptēs*, αὐτόπτης). In Book IV of the *Subterranean World*, section I, chapters 8 and 9, he speaks as follows:

> At its highest summit the mountain opens into an enormous crater twelve Italian miles in circumference—

others assign it thirty, others again forty miles in circumference, and some fewer, [Col. 1238C] since this infernal crater gapes and expands to a greater or lesser degree at different times and according to the circumstances and effects of its horrible fury—

> and by a steep inward slope it narrows down toward Tartarus itself: a precipice horrible to behold, with flames and smoke bursting forth both from the depths and from the mountain’s side, accompanied by a dreadful roaring not unlike thunder. It is so fearsome that the mere imagination of imminent fire and ruin immediately dismays anyone, however daring and intrepid, and turns him away from what resembles an infernal abyss.

After another description of this immense chasm he continues:

> The gulf is so deep that it escapes every gaze, and is made fearsome by rocks rising like pyramids from its sides. And although those sides incline downward in a straight and perpendicular course, the rock faces descending in parallel, nevertheless, according to the laws of optics, because of the immense distance, they seem to converge at the center, and so forth. At the bottom—marvelous to relate!—I remember always observing, amid the continual belching forth of mineral matter, [Col. 1238D] what resembled a pool glittering with molten metal. The sides everywhere vomit forth perpetual smoke through corresponding passages in very many places, which at night we discovered to be kindled flame. The gulf is never without rumbling and roaring, and sometimes emits sounds so dreadful that they make even the mountain itself tremble. In a word, whoever desires to behold the marvelous power of GOD, the Best and Greatest, should approach mountains of this kind; and, astonished and stupefied by the ineffable effects of nature’s wonders, he will repeatedly be compelled to proclaim from the deepest affection of his heart: O DEPTH OF THE RICHES OF THE WISDOM AND KNOWLEDGE OF GOD! How incomprehensible are your judgments, and how unsearchable your ways! and so forth.

(Rom. 11:33.)

**They provoke blows.** The histories of all past ages attest that terrible destruction has been inflicted by mountains of this kind, which sometimes belch out entire conflagrations and seem to vomit forth hell itself—although a little later our author more correctly calls them only sparks from hell. [Col. 1239A] Thus Diodorus Siculus, in Book V of his *History*, relates that when Sicily was anciently inhabited by the SICANI, an eruption of Mount Etna burned all their crops and filled the entire neighboring region so deeply with ashes that, despairing of any further cultivation of their soil, they were compelled to withdraw into the western parts of the island, far from that mountain’s fury. Many centuries later, a new Italian people, the SICELS, as though ignorant of the disasters of the past, led a new colony across the strait into the deserted neighborhood of Etna.

See next what Cicero recounts in Book II of *On the Nature of the Gods* concerning the immense mass of ashes cast out from the burned-out bowels of the same mountain, which brought such dense darkness upon the entire surrounding country that for two days no man could recognize another. Saint Augustine also testifies in the final chapter of Book III of *The City of God*:

> We read that among them the sea grew so hot from Etna’s fires, which flowed from the mountain’s summit as far as the nearest shore, that [Col. 1239B] the rocks were burned and the pitch of ships was dissolved, and so forth.

Again, through the same surge of fire, Sicily was so completely filled with ash that it buried, crushed, and destroyed the roofs of the city of Catana. Moved to compassion by this disaster, the Romans remitted that city’s tribute for the year. What the horrible furies of Mount Etna were in the time of the two Plinys—that is, in the first century, in the year 76—we learn from two letters of the younger Pliny to Tacitus, the sixteenth and twentieth letters of Book VI. They cannot be read without immense agitation of mind, unless the horror of that spectacle is softened by the elegance of their style.

I do not mention here the eruption which in the year 812 drove the undaunted emperor CHARLEMAGNE from Sicily into Italy; nor the next one, which in 1537 vomited forth such an enormous quantity of sparks and ashes that they reached even across the sea into Italy and, if we believe Varenius, *Geography*, Book I, chapter 10, proposition 5, “ships at sea, though two hundred leagues distant from Sicily and sailing toward Venice, suffered damage.” [Col. 1239C] Nor do I mention anything about many other eruptions of this kind, certainly no less severe. Yet I cannot pass over two examples that occurred in the preceding century.

The first I excerpt from the account of Don Pietro Squillaci, a priest of Catana who was himself present during this disaster, as it appears in Kircher, Book IV, section I, chapter 9:

> When the entire surrounding country already seemed on the point of being overturned by dreadful convulsions of the earth, at last, on the eleventh of March—the year was 1669 from the birth of Christ—the mountain suddenly split open into three terrible chasms. The first of these vomited forth a vast quantity of fire and many stones, the very smallest of which equaled a weight of three *cantara*, or three hundredweights. The fiery matter, projected aloft, was dispersed at a distance of two miles—I always mean Italian miles—into the smallest particles and raged upon the ground below in the form of a fiery rain. Whenever the said furious mountain gaped open, it produced such crashing and such an earthquake that I cannot express its horror [Col. 1239D] in any words.

The crash was increased by the shouting of the lamenting people of Catana, to whom the world appeared to be ending. Those chasms vomited forth fire and sand in such quantity that the fields glowed red with living flame and the town of Catana, four miles from Etna, was covered, as it were, with blackened sand.

> All that fire, however, flowed away in two streams—

he would more accurately have called them rivers—

> which embraced and licked around Mountpelier—

although he had earlier said that its circumference was half a mile—

> so that it projected like an island or rock amid the flames. One of the principal streams buried the village of Mountpelier, so that every trace of it was erased. The entire fiery mass extended six miles in width, and near the beginnings of the two streams to a height of approximately four *canne*, more or less according to the variation of the terrain.

It is better, however, to read this entire account in Kircher, [Col. 1240A] where many other pertinent matters are explained at greater length. I promise that you will not do so without trembling of mind, if there is any piety in you—especially if you join to it the letters concerning the same eruption by Paolo Boccone of Palermo in Sicily and by Pierre Michon, otherwise known as the Abbé Bourdelot. Then indeed you will confess that the Mantuan poet was not stirred by excessive poetic frenzy when he poured forth his terrible song about this mountain in Book III of the *Aeneid*:

> . . . Etna thunders with horrific ruin,  
> And sometimes bursts into the air a cloud  
> Smoking with a pitch-black whirlwind and white-hot ash;  
> It lifts up globes of flame and licks the stars.  
> Sometimes, belching, it raises rocks  
> And the mountain’s torn-out entrails; with a groan  
> It heaps molten stones beneath the skies  
> And boils up from its deepest floor.

And yet those were only streams from this subterranean furnace, filled with an immense mass of liquefied minerals. [Col. 1240B] But what if you add other mountains, sometimes no less terrible than Etna? I do not mean Vesuvius in Campania alone, but Stromboli and Vulcano in the Lipari Islands, besides other fiery mountains of this kind in the Azores and the Canaries, islands of the Atlantic Ocean, and countless others. Finally, suppose you summon that terrible Hekla of farthest Iceland, a land seemingly congealed from ice itself. How immense a mass of fire do you think is required to inflame so many most capacious furnaces? If you combine them all into one, what else would you call Etna and Vesuvius but the little smoke-holes of that fiery Ocean raging in the more hidden recesses and innermost bowels of the earth?

How great, then, I ask, must be the force, the fury, and the heat of this Ocean, with so immense a mass of metals, minerals—each of which by itself customarily generates the greatest heat—and other combustible materials heaped together, as it were, into a single mass! Yet how very small—and this is truly astonishing—is the fear of it among many sinners!

Observe, however, what the fear [Col. 1240C] of merely temporal death could accomplish among those wretched people of Catana. “Lamentation,” Squillaci continues in his account, “disciplinary scourging, prayers, processions, fasts, abstinence, and expositions of the Forty Hours ceased in none of the churches, and produced such sorrow that the rocks themselves might have wept.” Yet the hearts of certain hardened sinners are now more stony than rocks, for they are not even moved by fear of eternal death.
