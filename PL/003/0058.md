Yet he presses the matter more vigorously, and argues thus against Caecilius and the pagans: “Your records and tragedies [Col. 0504C] glory in acts of incest, which you gladly both read and hear. Thus you also worship incestuous gods.” He had used the term “records” above: “Look at the records in the books.” This word was frequently employed by Aulus Gellius. For in one place he says, “It is written in books of records” (A. Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book V, chap. 5); and elsewhere, more simply, “We read it written in ancient records” (book IV, chap. 6). “Records,” therefore, means the same as writings, commentaries, and things committed to writing in order to preserve the memory of events. Tertullian likewise used the word in this sense: “Among the other records” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 18); and shortly afterward, “the hoary records” (chap. 19, p. 19). Elsewhere he says: “According to the records, the Jewish people—that is, the more ancient people—forsook God [Col. 0504D] and served idols” (*Against the Jews*, book I, chap. 1, p. 205). Octavius therefore rightly reproaches Caecilius because pagans gladly read and listened to “Records”—that is, writings and books, whether histories or works of poets, in which the incestuous acts of their gods were represented in writing—and tragedies, in which they were represented through voice and gesture. For no one is unaware that in the comedies and tragedies of both Greek and Latin poets, and in the histories of other pagans, the gods’ acts of fornication, adultery, and incest are celebrated no less frequently than shamelessly. If you have the leisure and inclination, see what Lactantius (*Divine Institutes*, book VI, chap. 20, p. 19; and *Epitome*, chap. 63), Cyprian (Cyprian, *Epistle to Donatus*, [Col. 0505A] p. 5), Augustine (Augustine, *City of God*, book II, chap. 8 ff.), and others said about these comedies and tragedies. But we shall have more to say about them in this and the following dissertations.

Finally, Octavius charges the pagans with incest in another way: because newborn children, as he had already said and as we observed above (preceding chapter, art. 3), were very frequently exposed by their parents. For it happened not infrequently that those utterly depraved and lustful parents polluted themselves through abominable incest with their now-grown sons or daughters, who had been reduced to slavery or were living as prostitutes, either without recognizing them or while pretending not to recognize them. Hence Clement of Alexandria says: Παιδὶ πορνεύσαντι, καὶ μαχλώσαις θυγαθράσιν ἀγνοήσαντες πολλάκις μίγνυνται πατέρες, οὐ μεμνημένοι τῶν ἐκτιθέντων παιδίων, καὶ ἄνδρας δείκνυσι τοὺς [Col. 0505B] γεγενηκότας ἀκρασίας ἐξουσία· ταῦτα οἱ σοφοὶ τῶν νόμων ἐπιτρέπουσι: “Fathers often have intercourse with a son who has become a prostitute and with daughters practicing prostitution, without recognizing them. Unmindful of the children they exposed, fathers are made into their daughters’ husbands by unrestrained license in lust. These things the wise lawgivers permit” (Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogue*, book III, chap. 3, p. 226). Thus he assails the pagans, and their monstrous savagery and cruelty in exposing their children, with biting irony. Nor is it without cause that he complains that incest frequently resulted from this practice. Nevertheless, Tertullian (*Apology*, chap. 9) and Lactantius (*Divine Institutes*, book VI, chap. 20, p. 618 ff.) develop at greater length and more explicitly our Minucius’ argument against them. To avoid undue length, we have thought it best to refer you to them until we undertake a new discussion of their books. [Col. 0505C]

# CHAPTER XI  
## ON THE ABSURD WORSHIP WHICH CAECILIUS MOST FALSELY ALLEGES THAT CHRISTIANS RENDERED TO AN ASS’S HEAD AND TO A PRIEST’S GENITALS

### ARTICLE I  
#### How false this accusation is; whether the Jews or Gnostics gave occasion for it; and whether they were the first of all actually to worship an ass’s head

The pagans brought against the Christians another charge, as absurdly fabricated as it was impious, which Caecilius puts to Octavius in these words: “I hear that, through some persuasion unknown to me, they venerate the consecrated head of an ass, that most shameful [Col. 0505D] animal.” Tertullian explains this ridiculous accusation more clearly and discloses its occasion and cause in these words: “You have dreamed,” he says, addressing the pagans, “that an ass’s head is our god. Cornelius Tacitus first introduced this sort of suspicion. For in the fifth book of his *Histories*, beginning his account of the Jewish War from the origin of the nation, and arguing as he pleased about that nation’s origin, name, and religion, he relates that the Jews, having departed from Egypt—or, as he thought, having been driven from it—and being tormented by thirst in the vast and extremely waterless regions of Arabia, used wild asses as guides to a spring, because those animals happened to be thought to be seeking water after grazing; and that, in gratitude for this, they consecrated the likeness of such a [Col. 0506A] beast. And thus, I suppose, it was inferred that we too, as near relatives of the Jewish religion, are initiated into the same image” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 16, p. 17). He repeats this in another book as well (idem, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 11, p. 57 ff.). How rightly Tertullian said that this absurd slander should be laid at Cornelius Tacitus’ door may be judged from Tacitus’ own words in the cited fifth book of the *Histories*: “But nothing distressed the Jews as much as the scarcity of water. They were already lying prostrate over the whole plain, not far from destruction, when a herd of wild asses left their pasture and withdrew to a rock shaded by woodland. Moses followed them and, inferring from the grassy ground, uncovered abundant veins of water. . . . They consecrated in their sanctuary the image of the animal by whose guidance they had dispelled their wandering and thirst [Col. 0506B], sacrificing a ram, as if in insult to Ammon” (Cornelius Tacitus, *Histories*, book V, beginning). Although Tertullian calls Tacitus “the most loquacious of liars”—at least in this matter not without justification—Plutarch nevertheless subscribed to that false opinion when speaking about the same Jews as follows: “They now regard it as a religious prohibition both to kill and to eat a pig. And perhaps this accords with reason: Ὡς τὸν ὄνον δὲ ἀναφήναντα πηγὴν αὐτοῖς ὕδατος τιμῶσι, ‘Just as they honor the ass that revealed to them a spring of water,’ so they revere the pig as their teacher in sowing and ploughing” (Plutarch, vol. II, *Table Talk*, book IV, p. 670).

What of the historian Democritus, who, according to the account of the *Suda*, handed down these assertions about the Jews: Χρυσῆν ὄνου [Col. 0506C] κεφαλὴν προσεκύνουν κατὰ τριετίαν ξένον ἀγρεύοντες προσέφερον, καὶ κατὰ λεπτὸν τὰς σάρκας δίεξαινον, καὶ οὕτως ἀνῄρουν: “They worshipped a golden ass’s head; every third year they captured and offered a foreigner, sliced his flesh into small pieces, and thus killed him” (*Suda*, s.v. Ἰούδας, “Judas/Jew”). Perhaps this is why Zeno of Alexandria, formerly a Jew, as the same *Suda* reports (idem, s.v. Ζήνων, “Zeno”), renounced his religion and, in mockery of this absurd though wholly fictitious cult, led a white ass through their synagogue on the Sabbath day.

Josephus, however, replying to Apion when he advances the same slander, demonstrates its falsity by various arguments. His words should be quoted; although absent from the Greek text, they nevertheless appear in the printed editions in Latin [Col. 0506D] as follows: “Apion presumed to declare that the Jews had placed an ass’s head in the sanctuary, worshipped it, and deemed it worthy of such great religious reverence. He affirms that this was exposed when Antiochus Epiphanes plundered the temple and found that head fashioned from gold and worth a great sum of money. To this, then, I reply first that, since he was an Egyptian, even if there had been anything of the sort among us, he ought by no means to have reproached us, because an ass is no worse than the shrews, goats, and other creatures that are gods among them. Next, how did he fail to understand that his incredible lie was refuted by the facts? For we have always followed the same laws, in which we have steadfastly remained. And although various disasters have afflicted our city, as they have others, [Col. 0507A] and Theos, Pompey the Great, Licinius Crassus, and finally Titus Caesar conquered it in war and took possession of the temple, they found nothing of this sort” (Josephus, *Against Apion*, book II, p. 1065). Josephus therefore makes two replies to this tasteless falsehood, so often repeated and refuted. First, he turns the argument against Apion himself, an Egyptian, and shows that his complaint about this charge is grossly unjust: for the Egyptians worshipped and venerated beasts with no less absurd and impious a cult. Second, he proves that the allegation is not merely false but utterly incredible: for the Jews, who always held every form of idolatry—and consequently the worship of an ass’s head, however golden—in the highest hatred, always lived under the same laws. [Col. 0507B] Then Josephus shows that what Apion records about Antiochus Epiphanes is wholly at variance with the truth. For neither Pompey the Great nor any other Roman commanders, upon entering the Temple at Jerusalem after winning victories over the Jews, ever found in it any image of an ass’s head.

Nor could Tacitus deny this. Tertullian refutes and assails him as a liar forgetful of his own testimony: “But indeed this same Cornelius Tacitus”—these are Tertullian’s own words—“that most loquacious of liars, relates in the same *History* that Gnaeus Pompey, after capturing Jerusalem and consequently entering the temple to inspect the mysteries of the Jewish religion, found no image there. [Col. 0507C] And surely, if the object worshipped was represented by some image, it would be displayed nowhere more than in its own sanctuary; all the more so because that worship, however vain, had no fear of foreign observers: for only the priests were permitted to enter, and even the sight of it was forbidden to all others by a curtain drawn before it” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 16, p. 17). We read the same points transmitted by him again, though in other words, in the first book *To the Nations* (chap. 11). And rightly so; for Cornelius Tacitus had actually written the following in the book cited: “Gnaeus Pompey was the first of the Romans to subdue the Jews, and by right of victory he entered the temple. It then became generally known that there was no image of God within”—that is, none painted or fashioned—“and that the seat was empty and the mysteries void” (Tacitus, *Histories*, book V, shortly after the beginning).

[Col. 0507D] From what has been discussed thus far, who does not see that no credence whatsoever should be given either to this Cornelius Tacitus or to the other wicked slanderers who most shamelessly lied that divine worship had been accorded to an ass’s head, first by the Jews and then by the Christians?

Besides the explanation already offered by us, Bochart (*Sacred Animals of Scripture*, vol. I, chap. 18, p. 221 ff.) and Vossius (*On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, book III, chap. 75, p. 565) have given various accounts of the origin of so foul a fable. Since anyone may easily read these in their books, we shall add only what Tertullian records concerning a certain Jew, a thoroughly depraved man. [Col. 0508A] For after renouncing the Jewish religion, this man, says that learned African writer, “displayed a picture bearing an inscription of this kind: THE GOD OF THE CHRISTIANS ONOCHOETES” (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 11, p. 57 ff.). He then adds that this fictitious god was represented with ass’s ears, one hoofed foot, carrying a book, and wearing a toga (idem, *Apology*, chap. 16, p. 17), as we shall explain in its proper place. Moreover, Epiphanius, when discussing the Gnostics, those most notorious Christian heretics, committed the following to written record: “As regards Sabaoth, some consider him to have the form of an ass, others that of a pig” (Epiphanius, *Heresy* XXVI, § 40, p. 91). But whether this or any other fabricated story gave any occasion for accusing Christians of so wicked a [Col. 0508B] cult, our Minucius certainly denies utterly and completely that either the malicious imposture of that apostate from the Jewish religion or the supremely absurd cult of the Gnostics pertained in any way whatsoever to true Christians. For he openly and steadfastly asserts that this false rumor concerning the worship of an ass’s head was scattered and spread abroad by demons, and that no sane person could ever have believed it. “For who,” he says, “is so foolish as to worship this? Who is more foolish still as to believe that it is worshipped?” He therefore declares it to be mere slander and a most shameless lie, utterly incredible to anyone unless he is wholly bereft of sense. He does not refute it in more words, since he considered that by denying it once he had sufficiently confuted it.

### ARTICLE II  
#### How Minucius proved that the pagans were guilty of this same crime, or one similar or worse, since they impiously worshipped and venerated asses together with Epona and Isis, as well as the heads of cattle and wethers

[Col. 0508C]

Our author makes manifest the extent of the madness of the pagans who accused Christians of ass-worship by showing that they themselves could most certainly be convicted, not once but very frequently, of the same or a similar offense. He proves this by so many examples that the pagans dared neither answer anything in reply nor even open their mouths. He therefore begins with the very worship they rendered to asses and Epona: “You consecrate even whole asses [Col. 0508D] together with your”—the Royal manuscript adds “or”—“Epona.” Most scholars, however, believe that a scribal error lies here, which each has attempted to correct according to the sharpness of his own ingenuity. Some have removed the word “or” and wished the printed editions simply to read “together with your Epona.” Yet some of these have suspected that someone wrote “with Hippona” in the margin. These, however, are mere conjectures supported by no authority or testimony. Others directed their edition to read, “with your or its Epona.” Although contrary to the manuscript’s authority, they try—wrongly—to confirm this conjecture by words of Tertullian soon to be cited. Finally, some think it should be emended to “with Vesta, or Epona,” [Col. 0509A] because the pagans were persuaded that the ass was Vesta’s favorite. Yet even if the ass truly had been Vesta’s favorite, it would still have to be proved that asses were consecrated to her, and why Minucius said “or Epona.” Moreover, he ought to have written “and Epona,” especially since he immediately added that asses were sacrificed to Isis. What, then, if the particle *vel* (“or”) is used by our author, as by many others, to mean “also”? Would not his expression then be Latin and clear, with nothing requiring alteration in his text?

Plutarch, following Agesilaus (Agesilaus III Italicus), explains who Epona was in these words: “Fulvius Stellus, estranged in mind from women, had intercourse with a mare. In due time she gave birth to a most beautiful daughter, to whom her father gave the name Epona, ὠνόμασεν Ἱππώναν, ‘he named her Hippona’; [Col. 0509B] and she is the goddess who presides over the breeding of horses” (Plutarch, vol. II, *Parallel Stories*, p. 312).

That her image was customarily placed in stables is inferred from these words of Juvenal:

> . . . He swears  
> Only by Hippona and the faces painted by the foul-smelling mangers.  
> (Juvenal, *Satires* VIII, v. 157.)

Some, however, wish *Epona* to be read there instead of *Hippo*. Apuleius also says the following of the image of Epona, or Hippona, placed in stables: “I looked toward the central pillar supporting the stable’s beams, and almost exactly at its midpoint there was an image of the goddess Hippona, or Epona, resting in a shrine, which had been carefully crowned with garlands of roses—and fresh ones at that” (Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, book III, near the end).

[Col. 0509C] Moreover, when our Minucius reproaches the pagans with that impious worship of Epona, he appears to follow in the footsteps of Tertullian, who had argued similarly against them: “Yet you will not deny that all beasts of burden, and even whole geldings along with their Epona, are worshipped by you. Perhaps we are reproved for this: that among the worshippers of all livestock and wild beasts, we alone are worshippers of asses” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 16, p. 17; and *To the Nations*, chap. 11, p. 58). He also repeats the same point in another book, for the most part in the same words. Accordingly, Prudentius rejects this asinine divinity in these verses:

> No one grants the goddesses Cloacina or Epona  
> A throne above the stars, although he pays out a solid incense-box  
> And, with sacrilegious hands, probes the meal and entrails.  
> (Prudentius, *Apotheosis*, § II.)

[Col. 0509D] After this Octavius continues to rebuke Caecilius and the other pagans as follows: “You devoutly consecrate [*devotatis*] the same asses together with Isis.” The Royal manuscript has “devour [*devoratis*],” but nearly everyone considers this a scribal error. Some therefore thought that *devotatis* should be read—that is, “you show them to be consecrated.” But they ought to have proved where the verb *devotare* is used in that sense. Others conjectured that *denotatis* (“you designate”) had been written. But what would that contribute to Octavius’ purpose? Others maintained that it should be emended to *devocatis* (“you call down”), in the sense in which good authors say that Socrates “called philosophy down” from heaven. But what, pray, would be the point of calling asses down together with Isis? Finally, others judge that it should be corrected to [Col. 0510A] *decoratis* (“you adorn”), because they simultaneously worshipped both the image of the ass carrying the goddess Isis and that of Isis herself, adorned with various ornaments. This conjecture does not seem wholly inept; for we have just heard Apuleius say that the image of the goddess Epona had been carefully crowned with fresh rose garlands. In another book he declares no less clearly that the image of Isis was customarily carried by her priests through huts, villages, and camps, covered with a silken mantle, and was worshipped by every pagan who met it (Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, book VI, near the end). Consider, therefore, whether this is our Minucius’ meaning and whether *decoratis* should be read in his text. For we believe that nothing should be decided with certainty without the authority of a complete manuscript.

[Col. 0510B] Let us therefore pass to other matters. “You both sacrifice,” he adds, “and worship the heads of cattle and the heads of wethers.” Herodotus speaks as follows concerning the worship and sacrifice of cattle: “All Egyptians θύουσι, ‘sacrifice,’ clean bulls and bull-calves. But they are not permitted θύειν, ‘to sacrifice,’ the females, since these are consecrated to Isis. For the image of Isis is female and provided with bovine horns, just as the Greeks portray Io. Καὶ τὰς βοῦς τὰς θηλείας Αἰγύπτιοι πάντες ὁμοίως σέβονται προβάτων πάντων μάλιστα, ‘And all Egyptians alike venerate female cattle far more than all other livestock’; hence no Egyptian man or woman would kiss a Greek man on the mouth, or use his knife, spit, or pot, or eat the flesh even of a clean ox cut with a Greek knife” [Col. 0510C] (Herodotus, book II, § 41).

What then, someone will argue? Is it not entirely false that the Egyptians displayed any veneration toward the ox, since they sacrificed it to the gods? Eusebius, indeed, most explicitly denied that the Egyptians regarded oxen as gods, since they sacrificed those same oxen to the gods. These are his words: Ὅτι δὲ οὐδὲ τὰ ζῶα θεοὺς ἡγοῦνται, δηλοῖ τὸ πολλαχοῦ βοῦς ἀναχθέντας θεοῖς ἐν ταῖς ἱερομηνίαις: “That they—the Egyptians—did not regard brute animals as gods is clear even from the fact that in many places they customarily sacrificed oxen consecrated to the gods during solemn festivals and religious observances” (Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book III, chap. 12, p. 117). Yet one should consider whether the knot of this difficulty is not untied by Herodotus’ distinction [Col. 0510D] between male and female cattle.

For no one will deny that the Egyptians sometimes showed reverence and honor to the ox, since, besides many others, Philo the Jew left this written concerning them: Ἐκλαθόμενοι τῆς πρὸς τὸ ὃν ὁσιότητος, ζηλωταὶ τῶν αἰγυπτιακῶν γίνονται πλασμάτων· εἶτα χρυσοῦν ταῦρον κατασκευασάμενοι, μίμημα τοῦ κατὰ χώραν ἱερωτάτου ζώου δοκοῦντος εἶναι, θυσίας ἀθύτους ἀνήγαγον: “The Israelites, forgetting the piety owed to the One who is, began to emulate Egyptian fabrications; then, having made a golden bull in imitation of the animal regarded as most sacred in that country, they offered unhallowed sacrifices and performed dances, and so forth” (Philo, *Life of Moses*, book III, p. 677). Jerome likewise says: [Col. 0511A] “It seems to me that the people of Israel made for themselves in the wilderness a calf’s head to worship, and that Jeroboam son of Nebat fashioned golden calves, so that they might preserve in their superstition what they had learned in Egypt: that Apis and Mnevis, worshipped in the form of oxen, were gods” (Jerome, on chap. XLVII of Hosea, v. 15 [citation as printed]). Earlier Lactantius likewise said of the same Israelites: “Having fallen into luxury, they turned their minds to the profane rites of the Egyptians. For when Moses their leader had ascended the mountain and remained there forty days, they fashioned a golden head of the ox called Apis, to precede them as a standard” (Lactantius, book IV, chap. 10, p. 376). But we shall discuss the ox Apis a little below.

[Col. 0511B] Nor should anyone be troubled because both Lactantius and Jerome said “a calf’s head,” just as our Minucius speaks of “heads of cattle.” For by this manner of speaking they signify nothing other than the whole calf or oxen themselves. If you wish, you may consult Bochart on this subject (*Sacred Animals of Scripture*, book II, chap. 34, p. 346).

In precisely the same sense our author added “and heads of wethers.” No one, surely, is unaware that a wether is a castrated male sheep. Elsewhere we have noted that the Samians actually rendered worship to a sheep, and for what reason (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation 1, chap. 9, art. 3, p. 768). But that did not prevent others from sacrificing wethers to the Lares, as Cicero testifies: “Nor [Col. 0511C] is it necessary,” he says, “for us to explain . . . what kind of sacrifice is made to the Lar with wethers” (Cicero, *On the Laws*, book II, p. 340). Lambinus confirms this in his observations on this passage of Cicero with the following inscription of the Larval Brethren: LARIB. VERB. N. II. MATRI. LAR. OV. N. II. [“To the Lares, two wethers; to the Mother of the Lares, two sheep.”]

He then relates that, because the name Augustus had been conferred upon Octavian Caesar and in commemoration of that event, “a wether” was sacrificed in the temple of Jupiter by the flamen on the Ides of January. For this reason Ovid sang:

> On the Ides, in the temple of mighty Jove, the pure priest  
> Offers in the flames the entrails of a half-male sheep.  
> Every province was restored to our people,  
> And your grandfather was given the name Augustus.  
> [Col. 0511D]  
> (Ovid, *Fasti*, book I, v. 346 ff.)

### ARTICLE III  
#### How Minucius further proved that the pagans worshipped and adored gods compounded of goat and man and bearing the faces of lions and dogs; likewise the ox Apis, serpents, crocodiles, and other birds and beasts, and even onions and the breaking of wind

Minucius continues and, in order to shame the pagans for the utterly senseless slander by which they falsely charged Christians with ass-worship, says: “You dedicate as gods beings compounded of goat and man, and faces of lions and dogs as gods.” Of the first kind he mentions, Pan undoubtedly belonged; Herodotus relates that he was represented as follows by painters and sculptors [Col. 0512A]: Γράφουσι δὲ δὴ καὶ γλύφουσι οἱ ζωγράφοι, καὶ οἱ ἀγαλματοποιοὶ, τοῦ Πανὸς, καθάπερ Ἕλληνες, τὤγαλμα αἰγοπρόσωπον, καὶ τραγοσκελέα: “Painters paint and sculptors carve the image of Pan, as do the Greeks, with a goat’s face and a he-goat’s legs” (Herodotus, book II, § 46). He adds, however, that they believed Pan resembled the other gods, but that they depicted him in this manner for certain reasons which, he declares, it was by no means agreeable for him to relate. Satyrs too were imagined with a human but horned head, goats’ feet, and a hairy body. Lucian says that they, like Pan and Silenus, were remarkable for their monstrous appearance: Ὧν ὁ μὲν κέρατα ἔχων, καὶ ὅσον ἐξ ἡμισείας, ἐς τὸ κάτω αἰγὶ ἐοικὼς, καὶ γένειον βαθὺ καθειμένος . . . . . οἱ δὲ σάτυροι ὀξεῖς [Col. 0512B] τὸ ὦτα, καὶ αὐτοὶ φαλακροὶ, κεράσται, οἷα τοῖς ἄρτι γεννηθεῖσιν ἐρίφοις τὰ κέρατα ὑποφύεται: “The one, bearing horns, resembling a goat from the waist downward, and letting a deep beard hang down, differs little from a he-goat. . . . The satyrs have pointed ears, are themselves bald and horned, with horns like those that begin to sprout on newborn kids” (Lucian, *The Council of the Gods*, p. 1096). Hence Propertius, book III, Elegy 16:

> Goat-footed Pans will sing through the open reed.

And Lucretius, book IV, before the midpoint:

> The neighboring peoples imagine that goat-footed Satyrs and Nymphs  
> Possess these places, and they say that Fauns exist.

If you desire other witnesses to a matter not unknown, you may consult Eusebius, Servius, Vossius, Natalis Comes, and not a few others (Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book [Col. 0512C] III, chap. 4, p. 124; Servius on Virgil, *Eclogue* 2, v. 31; Vossius, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, book I, chap. 8, p. 31, and book VII, chap. 3, p. 359).

Those gods who bore the faces of lions and dogs, and other monstrous figures of gods, are mentioned not infrequently by various writers. First, we read the following report from Porphyry in Eusebius: “In fashioning images of the divine powers, they used every kind of animal, sometimes mingling wild beasts with human beings, and sometimes combining the bodies of birds and humans.” Εἴκασται γάρ τις παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς μέχρι τραχήλου ἀνθρωποειδής, τὸ δὲ πρόσωπον ὀρνέου, ἢ λέοντος, ἢ ἄλλου τινὸς ζώου κεκτημένος· καὶ πάλιν αὖ κεφαλὴ [Col. 0512D] ἀνθρώπειος, καὶ ἄλλων τινῶν ζώων μέρη, πῇ μὲν ὑποκείμενα, πῇ δὲ ἐπικείμενα: “Among them one may see certain images human in form as far as the neck, but with a face resembling now a bird, now a lion, or any other animal; and others again with a human head, while the remaining parts of the body, whether lower or upper, display the form of other living creatures” (in Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book III, chap. 4, p. 93). Eusebius had borrowed this from the fourth book of Porphyry’s Περὶ ἀποχῆς ἐμψύχων (*On Abstinence from Animal Food*), and from it, while combating the pagans, turns back against them the same weapon they had directed against the Christians, as Minucius does (Porphyry, book IV, before the midpoint, p. 372).

[Col. 0513A] Tertullian too, arguing in the same way against those same pagans, had carried the torch before our author in these words: “But they ought immediately to have worshipped a two-formed deity, since they have accepted gods compounded with dogs’ and lions’ heads, horned from goat and ram, goats from the loins, serpents from the legs, and winged on sole or back” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 16). Again in another book: “Among you—that is, the pagans—there are gods with goats’ heads and lions’ heads, derived from ox, ram, and he-goat; caprine gods, goat-born or serpent-shaped, and winged on sole, forehead, and back” (idem, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 14).

These points may be further confirmed from other authors, and especially by the following very words of Macrobius: “A city adjoining Egypt, which boasts Alexander the Great of Macedon as its founder, [Col. 0513B] observes the worship of Serapis and Isis with a veneration bordering on stupefaction. Yet it declares that all this veneration is rendered to the sun under that name, whether when they attach to his image a three-headed creature, whose central and largest head presents the likeness of a lion; on the right rises that of a dog, with the gentle appearance of one fawning; while the left side of the neck ends in the head of a ravening wolf. A serpent connects these animal forms with its coils, its head returning toward the god’s right hand, where the monster is seen” (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, book I, chap. 20).

But let us, if you please, follow our author: “Do you not both worship and feed the ox Apis together with the Egyptians?” If you seek a witness to the feeding and worship of this prodigious divinity, you will find Strabo, [Col. 0513C] who relates that Memphis was the royal city of the Egyptians, where there was a temple of Apis, who was the same as Osiris, and of the Sun [the transmitted Latin syntax is uncertain]: Καὶ τὸν βοῦν τὸν Μνευῖν, ἐν σηκῷ τινι τρεφόμενον, ὃς παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς νενόμισται Θεὸς, ὥσπερ καὶ ἐν Μέμφει ὁ Ἄπις: “And the ox Mnevis, which is fed in a certain enclosure and is regarded by the Heliopolitans as a god, just as Apis is by the Memphites” (Strabo, *Geography*, book XVII, p. 803 ff.). He adds that certain marks indicate which other ox, when this one dies, is fit to succeed it. If you want still more, consult Diodorus Siculus, who also relates how that ox Apis was buried after death and another installed in its place, by whom it was fed, and where it was stationed and honored. You may also consult Herodotus, Aelian, Mela, Porphyry as cited by Eusebius, Cicero, and, if you wish, what we reported in the first volume of our *Apparatus* (Diodorus Siculus, *Library*, book I, p. 53 ff.; Herodotus, book III, § 27; Aelian, *On Animals*, book X, chap. 28; Mela, *Description of the World*, chap. 9; Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book III, chap. 13, p. 117; Cicero, *Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 207; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1090 ff.).

[Col. 0513D] Minucius further faults the Romans because they not only worshipped the ox Apis together with the Egyptians, but also did not condemn the laws those Egyptians had established concerning beasts, birds, crocodiles, and serpents—namely, that anyone who killed one of them should be punished with death. Very many writers have mentioned this impious Egyptian custom. To select a few from their great number, let [Col. 0514A] Cicero come first; in these few words he explains how great the superstition of those peoples was: “The Egyptians consecrated nearly every kind of beast” (Cicero, *Nature of the Gods*, book III, p. 245, line 25; ibid., book I, p. 207, line 18). Elsewhere he records that this superstition went so far that it had never even been heard said that they harmed a crocodile, ibis, or cat. Indeed, “Even if they have inadvertently done any harm to one of them,” as he says in another book, “they refuse no punishment” (*Tusculan Disputations*, book V, p. 189, line 25). Herodotus speaks no less clearly about the penalty for harming a sacred animal: “If anyone kills one of these beasts,” he says, “if intentionally, he is punished with death; if unintentionally, he pays whatever penalty the priests have determined” (Herodotus, book II, § 65). [Col. 0514B] Tertullian, in whose footsteps our author still seems to stand, attests this concerning the same Egyptians: “No one, not even a human being, will wish to be worshipped against his will. And therefore even the Egyptians have been permitted the power of so vain a superstition: consecrating birds and beasts, and condemning to death those who kill any god of this kind” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 24, p. 26).

We briefly touched upon this ridiculous Egyptian worship in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus*. But if this does not suffice for you, we shall refer you to Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus, Strabo, Aelian—who gave Plutarch as his guarantor—Cyril of Jerusalem, Jerome, and Theodoret (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 496 and 636; Herodotus, book II, § 65 ff.; Diodorus Siculus, *Library*, book I, pp. [Col. 0514C] 52, 54 ff.; Strabo, *Geography*, book XVII, p. 811 ff.; Aelian, *On Animals*, book X, chap. 13, and book XVII, chap. 5; Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechesis* 6, p. 61; Jerome, *Against Jovinian*, book II, p. 59; Theodoret, Discourse 4, *On Angels and Demons*, p. 521). As for Minucius’ additional assertion that these Egyptian superstitions were not condemned by the Romans, anyone will readily concede it who remembers that the Romans either adopted or tolerated the religions of all other nations, however absurd. For, as Caecilius himself said: “By adopting the sacred rites of all nations, they also earned dominion.”

[Col. 0514D] Still more absurd was what Minucius next adds: that the Egyptians, together with very many Romans, feared the pungency of onions and the noises of the belly. That this was no less true and certain than the foregoing may be learned from these verses of Juvenal:

> Who does not know, Volusius Bithynicus, what monsters  
> Mad Egypt worships? One district adores  
> The crocodile; another fears the ibis gorged with serpents.  
> The golden image of the sacred long-tailed monkey gleams;  
> . . . . . . . . . .  
> Some towns worship blue fishes, others a river fish; elsewhere  
> Whole cities venerate a dog, but no one Diana.  
> It is sacrilege to violate the leek and onion or break them with a bite.  
> O holy peoples, in whose gardens such  
> Divinities grow!  
> (Juvenal, *Satire* XV, beginning.)

We read similar lines sung later by Prudentius:

> Do you pray to Venus? Pray also to the ape.  
> Does the sacred asp of Aesculapius please you?  
> Why then do the crocodile, ibis, and dogs displease you?  
> Set religious little altars beside your couches;  
> [Col. 0515A] Venerate the bitter onion and pungent garlic.  
> (Prudentius, Hymn in Honor of Saint Romanus, v. 256 ff.)

Accordingly, Pliny had said without hesitation concerning the onion: “Egypt includes garlic and onions among its gods when taking an oath” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book XIX, chap. 6, p. 593).

If you ask the causes of this ridiculous veneration and worship, we shall give them in Plutarch’s words: Οἱ δὲ ἱερεῖς ἀφοσιοῦνται, καὶ δυσχεραίνουσι, καὶ τὸ κρόμμυον παραφυλάττοντες, ὅτι τῆς σελήνης φθινούσης μόνον, εὐτροφεῖν τοῦτο καὶ τεθηλέναι πέφυκεν· ἔστι δὲ πρόσφορον οὔτε ἁγνεύουσιν, οὔτε ἑορτάζουσιν· τοῖς μὲν, ὅτι διψῆν, τοῖς δὲ, ὅτι δακρύειν ποιεῖ τοὺς προσφερομένους: “The priests religiously abstain from the onion and regard it with aversion, taking care to avoid it because this plant alone naturally thrives and flourishes while the moon is waning. The use of onions is appropriate neither for those practicing ritual purity nor for those celebrating festivals: for the former, because it creates thirst; for the latter, because it causes those who partake of it to shed tears” (Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris*, p. 853). [Col. 0515B] Aulus Gellius indeed reported the first of these explanations from the same Plutarch, but asserts that the Egyptian priests said this about the people of Pelusium (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book XX, chap. 8). And not entirely without good reason. For Lucian relates that the Pelusians were devoted to this ridiculous and absurd cult, just as others were devoted to another: Μεμφίταις μὲν ὁ βοῦς Θεὸς, Πηλουσιώταις δὲ κρόμμυον, καὶ ἄλλοις ἴβις, ἢ κροκόδειλος, καὶ ἄλλοις κυνοκέφαλος, ἢ αἴλουρος, ἢ πίθηκος: “For the Memphites the ox is a god; for the Pelusians, the onion; for others, the ibis or crocodile; and for still others, the dog-headed ape, cat, or monkey” (Lucian, *Jupiter Tragoedus*, p. 699 ff.). [Col. 0515C] Hence Jerome says: “Compel a Pelusian, if you can, to eat an onion” (Jerome, *Against Jovinian*, p. 59).

Elsewhere the same Jerome speaks as follows both of that same onion and of the breaking of wind, which our author mentions second: “Most of their towns—the Egyptians’—derive their gods from beasts . . . not to mention the fearsome and horrible onion and the breaking of a swollen belly’s wind, which is the religion of Pelusium” (idem, on Isaiah 46). Pseudo-Clement supports Jerome, speaking as follows about these and the other matters discussed by our author: “Some of the Egyptians handed down the ox called Apis as an object of worship; others, the he-goat; others, cats; some, the ibis; certain people, a serpent; also a fish and onions, the breaking of wind, sewers, and the limbs of brute animals—and [Col. 0515D] they submitted themselves to a thousand other most loathsome obscenities” (Pseudo-Clement, *Recognitions*, book V, § 20, p. 466). He mentions similar things elsewhere; if you have the leisure, you may consult him (idem, *Homilies* 10, p. 657). What, then, must be said of the Romans, who, as we have stated, either did not condemn or actually followed those insane rites, yet patiently endured their ridicule by the poets?
