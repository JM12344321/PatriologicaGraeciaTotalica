But consider, I ask, what human beings are capable of when deceived by the mere semblance of false piety. “Infants,” Minucius says, “were sacrificed by their parents, who stifled their crying with caresses and kisses, lest a weeping victim be sacrificed.” Here he follows Tertullian, who, as we have already noted, had said: “Parents offered their own children; they responded willingly and caressed the infants, lest they should be sacrificed while weeping” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 9). But if they then suppressed their children’s crying and tears, how could they themselves, while killing them, restrain their own sighs, groans, and every feeling of a father’s heart? Yet with that wholly perverse fortitude they stifled their children’s groans and tears, [Col. 0492C] lest a mournful and tearful victim—and therefore one by no means pleasing to the gods—be slaughtered. For it was indeed an evil omen if a victim was sacrificed while resisting and unwilling. If you desire a witness, hear Macrobius: “Those performing sacrifices observed the rule that, if the victim being led to the altars resisted too violently and showed itself unwilling to be brought to them, it should be removed, because they believed it was being offered to an unwilling god.” Or, as Pliny says: “The gods are not appeased by a victim belonging to another, nor by one that draws back from the altars” (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, bk. III, ch. 5; Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. VIII, ch. 45, p. 221). For this reason, according also to Ammianus Marcellinus, they considered it an ill omen that, during the sacrifice of Julian the Apostate, the tenth bull broke its bonds and escaped, and was recovered only with difficulty (Ammianus Marcellinus, bk. XXIV, [Col. 0492D] ch. 6, p. 409). Likewise, “the flight of the victims from the very altars,” as Valerius Maximus says, appeared to portend some disaster for Pompey (Valerius Maximus, bk. I, ch. 6, § 12); or, as Lucan sang:

> Brought near the gods, the bull broke loose and fled the altar,  
> And headlong cast itself upon the Emathian fields;  
> Nor was any victim found for the ill-omened rites.  
> (Lucan, bk. VII, v. 156)

Yet it was not only by these examples that the Gentiles were convicted of homicide and parricide, but by many others still, which we must examine.

## ARTICLE III. In What Further Manner Minucius Shows That Guests Were Sacrificed by the Taurians of Pontus and by Busiris the Egyptian; Human Beings to Mercury by the Gauls; and a Greek Man and Woman, a Gallic Man and Woman, and Others to Jupiter Latiaris by the Romans

[Col. 0493A]

Just as it was a most inhuman custom among certain peoples of Africa to sacrifice infants to Saturn, so, Minucius says, “among the Taurians of Pontus and under Busiris the Egyptian, guests were sacrificed to their fictitious gods.” Concerning the Taurians, Clement of Alexandria committed the following to writing in Greek: “The Taurians, the people who inhabit the region about the Tauric Chersonese, immediately sacrifice to Tauric Artemis any strangers whom they capture among those who have lost their way at sea.” [Col. 0493B] (Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Gentiles*, p. 27). You will find these words of Clement of Alexandria copied by Eusebius. Athenagoras relates precisely the same thing more briefly, Origen more clearly, and Athanasius more fully than either of them (Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, bk. IV, ch. 16, p. 157; Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, p. 29; Origen, *Against Celsus*, bk. V, p. 249; Athanasius, *Oration Against the Greeks*, § 25, p. 2). Before all these, however, Naso thus sang of that utterly savage cruelty, more characteristic of wild beasts than of human beings:

> Those whom you hear delighting in human blood  
> Dwell almost beneath the same zone of cold.  
> [Col. 0493C] Nor far from us is the place where the dread Tauric altar  
> Of the quiver-bearing goddess is sprinkled with slaughter.  
> (Ovid, *Tristia*, bk. IV, Elegy IV, v. 325 ff.)

Juvenal harmonizes with Ovid in these verses:

> What comparable excuse does Egypt have, more savage than the Maeotian altar?  
> For Taurica, the inventor of that unspeakable rite,  
> Nevertheless sacrifices human beings—if now you believe  
> The trustworthy testimony handed down by poems. . . .  
> (Juvenal, *Satires*, XV, v. 115 ff.)

Prudentius likewise subscribed to both writers in these words:

> In vain do we now habitually condemn the Tauric rites:  
> Human blood is shed in the service of Jupiter Latiaris.  
> (Aurelius Prudentius, *Against Symmachus*, bk. I, on the spectacles, etc.)

Lucan too, and others soon to be cited, [Col. 0493D] relate things not unlike these. But Diodorus Siculus gives the reasons for this most dreadful custom; you may read them in his work if you wish, and may in addition consult Seneca and Gregory Nazianzen (Diodorus Siculus, *Library*, bk. IV, p. 173; Seneca, at the end of *Octavia*; Gregory Nazianzen, Oration 39, *On the Holy Lights*, p. 829).

But as much as Diodorus supports Minucius in that case, so much does he appear to disagree with him concerning Busiris. For he declares that it is a mere fable that foreigners were slaughtered through the cruelty of Busiris. Hear, I ask, his words: “They say that in ancient times human beings of the same color as Typhon—that is, red-haired—were sacrificed by the kings at the tomb of Osiris. Now only a few Egyptians are found to be red-haired, but many foreigners are. Hence the tale about Busiris’s cruelty in slaughtering foreigners [Col. 0494A] gained strength among the Greeks—not because any king was named Busiris, but because the tomb of Osiris bore that designation in the native language” (Diodorus, bk. I, p. 53 ff.). Yet in the same book he does not entirely deny that there were Egyptian kings called by the name Busiris, for he mentions two who bore that name (ibid., bk. I, p. 29). Accordingly, he appears to assert only that not human beings but cattle were sacrificed at the tomb of Osiris, and not by Busiris.

[Col. 0494B]

Strabo appears to support Diodorus in these very words: “In the Busirite nome are the city of Busiris and Cynopolis. Eratosthenes says that the custom of expelling foreigners is common to all barbarians; but that the Egyptians are accused because of the fables told about Busiris in the Busirite nome, later writers wishing to malign the inhospitality of this region, although, by Zeus, there was neither a king nor a tyrant named Busiris.” [Col. 0494C] (Strabo, *Geography*, bk. XVII, p. 802).

But although these writers denied the matter, Minucius nevertheless has more numerous supporters, foremost among them Ovid, who sings in these verses:

> Egypt is said to have lacked the rains that aid the fields,  
> And to have been dry for nine years.  
> When Thrasius approaches Busiris and shows that Jupiter  
> Can be appeased by the shedding of a guest’s blood.

And elsewhere:

> Therefore I subdued Busiris, who defiled his temples  
> With the blood of foreigners.  
> (Ovid, *Art of Love*, bk. I, v. 650; *Metamorphoses*, bk. IX, v. 293)

Virgil spoke no less clearly:

> [Col. 0494D] Who does not know of harsh Eurystheus,  
> Or the altars of the infamous Busiris?  
> (Virgil, *Georgics*, bk. III, vv. 4–5)

Servius observes on this passage: “Busiris was a king of Egypt who used to sacrifice the guests he received. He was killed by Hercules when he wished to kill Hercules also. Isocrates wrote an encomium of him.” For this reason Statius somewhere called them:

> The pitiless altars of Busiris.

Eusebius transmitted the following concerning Busiris: “Busiris, son of Neptune and Libya, the daughter of Epaphus, exercises tyranny in the regions neighboring the Nile, killing passing guests in a cruel crime” (Eusebius, *Chronicle*, at the year 546). Augustine, among others, supported him: [Col. 0495A] “At that time,” he says, “that is, in the time of Hercules, Busiris, whether king or tyrant, sacrificed his guests to his gods. He is said to have been the son of Neptune by his mother Libya, daughter of Epaphus” (Augustine, *City of God*, bk. XVIII, ch. 12). You see that several authorities have voted with Minucius concerning that savage ferocity of Busiris.

We likewise possess very many reliable witnesses confirming what Minucius adds afterward: “The Gauls slaughter human—or rather inhuman—victims for Mercury.” Cornelius Tacitus is guarantor and witness of so great an inhumanity, when, among many other statements concerning the customs of the Gauls, he relates the following: “Of the gods they worship Mercury above all; on certain days they consider it lawful to propitiate him even with human victims.” Caesar, moreover, to whom the Gallic nation was especially well known, [Col. 0495B] writes that when afflicted by more serious illnesses or engaged in battles, they sacrificed human beings as victims, or vowed that they would sacrifice them, and employed Druids as ministers at these sacrifices (Tacitus, *On the Customs of the Germans*, shortly after the beginning; Caesar, *Gallic War*, bk. I, shortly after the beginning). Caesar does not, however, explain there to which god they presented these victims. But since he, like Strabo, asserts in the same place that the Gauls especially worshiped Mercury, can it not be said with our Minucius that this cruel sacrifice was made chiefly to that god, and also to Hesus, as will soon be stated? (Strabo, *Geography*, bk. IV, p. 198). The same judgment must undoubtedly be passed concerning Diodorus Siculus, Mela, Strabo, Cicero, and Solinus, who likewise testify generally that the Gauls sacrificed human beings to the gods [Col. 0495C] (Diodorus, *Library*, bk. V, p. 214; Mela, bk. VI, where he treats Gaul; Strabo, *Geography*, bk. IV, p. 193; Cicero, *Oration for Marcus Fonteius*, p. 222; Solinus, ch. 21, p. 41). Finally, Lucan sang the following about them and about Tauric Diana, whom we discussed a little earlier (Lucan, *Pharsalia*, bk. I, v. 444 ff.):

> And you, by whom pitiless Teutates is appeased with dreadful blood,  
> And Hesus, grim at savage altars,  
> And Taranis, whose altar is no gentler than that of Scythian Diana.

Lactantius supports Lucan. Suetonius further attests that this impious and detestable sacrifice was prohibited by the emperor Augustus and afterward abolished by Claudius: “He utterly abolished,” Suetonius says, “the religion of the Druids among the Gauls, a religion of dreadful cruelty, which under Augustus had been forbidden only to citizens” (Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, [Col. 0495D] bk. I, ch. 21, p. 112; Suetonius, bk. V, § 25).

Undoubtedly no less cruel was the Roman sacrifice next mentioned by our Minucius, in which they buried alive beneath the Forum Boarium a Greek man and woman and a Gallic man and woman. We have received from Livy the following written account of this utterly inhuman sacrifice: “On the authority of the Books of Fate, several extraordinary sacrifices were performed. Among them a Gallic man and woman and a Greek man and woman were lowered alive beneath the earth in the Forum Boarium, into a place enclosed by stone, which had previously been drenched with human victims in a rite particularly alien to Roman religious practice” (Livy, third decade, bk. II, p. 195). Plutarch relates the same things, adding, however, that those rites were performed in November. Pliny openly declares that they persisted even to [Col. 0496A] his own age (Plutarch, vol. I, *Life of Marcellus*, p. 299; vol. II, *Roman Questions*, p. 283; Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. XXVIII, ch. 1, p. 537).

Indeed, as our author continues, Jupiter Latiaris is honored by these same Romans with homicide; or, as he wrote earlier: “When Jupiter is Jupiter Latiaris, he is drenched with blood.” Tatian firmly asserts that he was an eyewitness of this horrible sacrifice and of another offered to Diana: “When I saw these things . . . among the Romans, Jupiter Latiaris being appeased by human gore and by blood shed through acts of homicide, and Diana, not far from Megalopolis, delighting in similar sacrifices” (Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, p. 165). For this reason Theophilus of Antioch was wholly justified in reproaching the Gentiles [Col. 0496B] with the wantonness of the Mother of the Gods and the cruelty “of Jupiter Latiaris, who thirsts”—*dipsōntos* (διψῶντος)—“for human blood” (Theophilus, *To Autolycus*, bk. III, p. 122). Tertullian not only expressly confirms this in his *Apology*, as has been noted, but repeats it in another book in these words: “The age permitted the Scythians’ Diana, the Gauls’ Mercury, and the Africans’ Saturn to be appeased with human victims; and in Latium, even to the present day, human blood is given to Jupiter to taste in the middle of the City” (Tertullian, *Scorpiace*, ch. 7, p. 624). Thus, in his time that horrible custom persisted, and it continued still later. For Lactantius similarly writes: “Nor indeed were the Latins free from this cruelty, since even now Jupiter Latiaris is worshiped with human blood” (Lactantius, [Col. 0496C] bk. I, ch. 21, p. 113). Who would dare to deny credence to so many and such reliable witnesses?

Whether so monstrous a sacrifice was performed long afterward is not so well established. Athanasius certainly did not believe that it was, since he speaks of it as follows: “The Romans of old likewise worshiped the one they call Jupiter Latiaris with human sacrifices” (Athanasius, *Oration Against the Greeks*, § 25, p. 23).

We have elsewhere discussed this detestable rite and custom of slaughtering human beings for the gods. In addition to the writers cited by us there, you may also read Philo Judaeus, Cornelius Tacitus, Pliny, Suetonius, Porphyry, Eusebius, and Cyril of Alexandria, to whom Tertullian and [Col. 0496D] Lactantius may be added; but we shall treat them in their proper place (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, dissertation 1, ch. 9, art. 2, p. 757; Philo, *On the Offering of Victims*, p. 853; Tacitus, *Annals*, bk. XIV, around the middle; Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. XXX, ch. 1, p. 728; Suetonius, *Life of Claudius*, § 25; Porphyry, *On Abstinence from Animals*, bk. XII, p. 223; Eusebius, *Oration in Praise of Constantine*, p. 646; Cyril of Alexandria, *Against Julian*, bk. IV, p. 128; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 34, art. 1; and *On Tertullian*, ch. 20, art. 2 ff.).

## ARTICLE IV. On Other Crimes Arising from That Savage Custom of Slaughtering Human Beings: Namely, Catiline’s Conspiracy Sealed with Human Blood; the Rites of Bellona Drenched in Human Gore; the Remedy for Epilepsy Sought by Drinking It; and Beasts Fattened on It and Then Devoured by Human Beings in the Arena

[Col. 0497A]

Minucius suspects that three horrible crimes flowed, as from a most corrupt fountain, from the custom thus far explained of sacrificing human beings to fictitious gods: namely, Catiline’s criminal conspiracy, sealed by a compact of human blood; the rites of Bellona drenched by the drinking of human blood; and a remedy for epilepsy sought in that same human blood. Would you like to hear him [Col. 0497B] speak? “It was this practice itself, I believe,” he says, “that taught Catiline to conspire under a covenant of blood; taught Bellona to drench her rite by drinking human gore; and taught men to cure epilepsy with blood—that is, to cure one disease with a still more grievous disease.” You have already heard the words of Tertullian cited by us, in which he had previously committed the same matters to writing.

Not everyone, however, relates in the same way how Catiline’s conspiracy was formed. Sallust transmitted the following concerning it to posterity: “At that time there were some who said that, after delivering a speech, when Catiline was binding the associates in his crime by an oath, he passed around in a bowl human blood mixed with wine. Then, after an imprecation, when all had tasted it, as was customary in solemn rites, he is said to have disclosed his plan” (Sallust, *The Conspiracy of Catiline*, [Col. 0497C] ch. 8). Sallust therefore reports that this was the opinion not of everyone, but only of certain persons, and he himself pronounces nothing definite about it. Florus, however, asserted without hesitation that the event occurred in this way (Florus, *Roman History*, bk. IV, ch. 1).

Cassius Dio, however, testifies in these words that it occurred differently: “He compelled them to bind themselves by an unlawful oath. For he sacrificed a certain boy and, after swearing the oath over his entrails, then ate them together with the others” (Cassius Dio, *Roman History*, bk. XXXVII, p. 48 ff.). Plutarch transmitted that they tasted the flesh of the sacrificed man (Plutarch, *Life of Cicero*, p. [Col. 0497D] 866). Yet the authority of these latter writers, being Greek, will perhaps undoubtedly appear to some less weighty than that of the former. Not without reason, therefore, our author and Tertullian support these Latin writers (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 9, p. 10).

Concerning the rites and festivals of Bellona customarily celebrated in her honor, Lactantius gives the following words, describing the bloody human sacrifice: “There are other rites of Valor, whom they also call Bellona, in which the priests sacrifice not with another’s blood, but with their own. For, cutting their shoulders and brandishing swords in both hands, they run, are carried into frenzy, and rave” (Lactantius, bk. I, ch. 21, p. 116). Lampridius [Col. 0498A] says of the emperor Commodus: “In his zeal for cruelty, he ordered the servants of Bellona actually to cut their arms” (Lampridius, *Commodus*, p. 49). For this reason Lucan sang:

> . . . Those whom Bellona’s rite excites  
> When they have cut their arms.  
> (Lucan, *Pharsalia*, bk. I, after the middle)

And again in another book:

> . . . Wherever she wanders,  
> Bellona seems to brandish a bloodstained scourge.  
> (Ibid., bk. VII, after the middle)

You may consult Montfort’s observations on these verses of Lucan, Casaubon’s on Lampridius, and Caecilius’s scholia on these verses of Juvenal: [Col. 0498B]

> . . . Behold, the chorus of raging Bellona  
> And of the Mother of the Gods enters.  
> (Juvenal, *Satires*, VI, around the middle)

You have surely observed that some authorities transmitted that the shoulders were cut in these mysteries, others the upper arms, and others the arm. Seneca, however, mentions all these parts of the body together in these words: “When someone skilled in cutting his own upper arms makes his arms and shoulders bleed with suspended hand” (Seneca, *On the Happy Life*, ch. 27). Who does not see that he is there alluding to the rites of Bellona?

Why, then, someone will ask, did Tertullian mention only the cutting of the thigh? “Today,” he says, “in this very place, blood caught in the hollow of the hand from a cut thigh and given as food marks those consecrated to Bellona”—the manuscripts read “one consecrated”— (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 9, p. 10). Why should he not have been permitted to mention only [Col. 0498C] the thigh, just as others mentioned only the shoulders or upper arms? For neither he nor the others thought that all the limbs cut during Bellona’s rites had to be enumerated. But something more must be said below about these mysteries and that passage of Tertullian (below, ch. 20, art. 3).

Yet I would have you observe that our Minucius did not merely say that blood was drawn from the initiates’ limbs in these rites, but that these rites were drenched by drinking human gore. He mentions only this second act because it was more grievous, although the initiates did not drink human blood unless it had first been drawn from their limbs. You have just heard Tertullian, who appears to have mentioned both the drawing and the drinking of the blood on that occasion. But we shall discuss it later (*Dissertation on Tertullian*, [Col. 0498D] ch. 20, art. 4).

Finally, our author justly rebukes those who, when suffering from epilepsy, eagerly drank the blood of gladiators wounded in the arena, believing they could be cured by it. For Pliny himself, although a Gentile, inveighs no less sharply against them: “Epileptics drink the blood of gladiators, as though from living cups—a sight that horrifies us even when wild beasts do the same in that very arena. But, by Hercules, they consider it most effective to suck from the human being himself while the blood is warm and steaming, and to drink the very life from the wound by placing their lips upon it, though it is not considered lawful for human mouths to be brought even to the wounds of wild beasts. . . . As though it could be regarded as health to turn from a human being into a beast, and as though a person were not deserving of disease in the very remedy—[Col. 0499A] an excellent frustration, by Hercules, if it does no good. It is considered impious to look upon human entrails; what, then, of eating them? Who invented these portents? My dispute is with you, destroyer of human law and craftsman of monstrosities, whoever you were who first devised them, I suppose so that human life would not forget you. Who invented the eating of human limbs one by one? By what conjecture was he led to it? What origin could this medicine have had? Who made poisons more innocent than remedies? Grant that barbarous and foreign rites invented them; did even the Greeks make these practices their own?” (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. XXVIII, ch. 1, p. 54). You may also consult Celsus, the already cited Tertullian, and others (Celsus, bk. III, ch. 23, on epilepsy; Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 9, p. 10).

Finally, anyone may readily learn from Apuleius and the already cited Tertullian that wild beasts fattened on the blood, entrails, and limbs [Col. 0499B] of human beings were devoured by other human beings who fought against them in the circus, as we shall demonstrate in the following dissertation on Tertullian’s books (Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, bk. IV, before the middle, p. 58 ff.; *Dissertation on Tertullian*, ch. 20, arts. 4–5).

# CHAPTER X. ON THE THIRD CHARGE, NAMELY THE INFAMOUS FORNICATION, ADULTERY, AND INCEST AFTER THE BANQUET OF WHICH CHRISTIANS WERE ACCUSED

## ARTICLE I. How Greatly Christians Abhorred This Crime, How Chaste Their Marriage Was, and How Many Persevered in Perpetual Virginity

[Col. 0499C]

In another slander no less shameless than the former, the Gentiles attacked the Christians, accusing them of infamous fornication, adultery, and incest after their banquets and feasts. Caecilius pursued this charge with a disposition no fairer and with no less vehemence than the preceding one. For although this charge was plainly fictitious and fabricated, he nevertheless attempts to prove it by two arguments. He derived the first from the testimony and authority of Fronto; the second from the fact that, as he said, “honorable things always rejoice in publicity,” whereas Christians always concealed their mysteries with great care.

[Col. 0499D] But Octavius crushes and refutes both this entire slander and the argument for it with two replies. For he boldly and openly declares that this crime attributed to the Christians was fabricated by the customary lies of the demons and was never proved by anyone. Secondly, he shows that the Gentiles themselves were in fact guilty of this very detestable offense that they wrongly charged against Christians.

He therefore emphatically pronounces: “It is a monstrous fable which the conspiracy of demons has falsely devised against us for a twofold reason: first, that it might stain the glory of chastity by directing foul infamy against it.” But several scholars consider the word *aversione* (“by aversion” or “by directing away”) corrupt: some suspect that *aspersione* (“by aspersion” or “by besmirching”) should be read, others *accessione* (“by accession”). [Col. 0500A] Yet who would dare to decide anything certain without consulting another, more accurate manuscript? For what if Minucius took the word *aversione*, or *adversione*, in the sense in which it is used only by jurists, for deflecting something onto another person, or for action taken without the matter being properly perceived or investigated? The meaning of our Minucius, the advocate, would then be: the demons devised this fable so that, by casting foul infamy upon Christians—or by allowing it to go unexamined and uninvestigated among the Gentiles—they might stain the glory of Christian chastity. Whether we have struck the mark must be left to the judgment of others. Our author’s second reason, by which the first appears to be confirmed, is this: “That before the truth had been investigated, the demons might, through terror inspired by an unspeakable belief, turn people away from us and from examining the Christian religion.”

[Col. 0500B] Octavius then very easily weakens the authority of Fronto that had been interposed: “Your Fronto,” as he continues, “did not give testimony as one affirming a fact, but hurled abuse as an orator.” Who does not see that the word “your” clearly declares that the Fronto of whom we spoke above was a Gentile orator, whose testimony against Christians could therefore carry no weight?

But so that this might appear more clearly, Octavius exposes that shameless slander of the Gentiles and the exceptional innocence of Christians from their sober banquets and especially from their supreme chastity, which he sets forth as truthfully as simply in the following manner: [Col. 0500C] “We preserve modesty not merely in the face, but in the mind. We willingly adhere to the bond of a single marriage; in the desire for procreation, we know either one wife or none.” Such, therefore, was the chastity of Christian spouses in Minucius’s time, and such, by Christ’s command, it should always have been. But, from zeal for greater perfection, many who were never bound by any marriage bond remained in perpetual virginity. Yet it is better again to hear our author speaking about it: “Very many enjoy, rather than boast of, the perpetual virginity of an undefiled body. So far removed are they from the desire for incest that, for some, even a chaste union is a cause for shame.” In the words “They enjoy, rather than boast of,” he incidentally censures the Gentiles, among whom the Vestal Virgins and the priests of the Mother of the Gods boasted of their virginity more than they actually enjoyed it. [Col. 0500D] But of these matters below.

Tertullian likewise answers that utterly false accusation by the Gentiles in a similar manner: “A most careful and faithful chastity has fenced us off from that eventuality; and just as we are kept from fornication and from every excess after marriage, so are we kept from the chance of incest. Some, much more securely, repel the whole force of this error by virginal continence—boys grown old” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 9, p. 10). By “boys grown old” he means those who preserved the virginity of childhood even to old age. Justin Martyr had earlier declared the same in these words: “Indeed, very many men and women sixty and seventy years old, who were instructed in Christ from childhood, remain incorrupt and celibate; and I boast that I can point out such persons [Col. 0501A] among every race of humankind” (Justin Martyr, *First Apology*, p. 62).

Moreover, concerning not only these virgins but also Christian spouses, Justin afterward transmitted the same teaching as Tertullian and our Minucius: that Christians either entered marriage solely for the sake of procreating and educating children or, having rejected it, remained in a state of perpetual continence. Athenagoras repels the unjust accusation of the Gentiles in the same manner and by an equally public declaration: “Each of us regards the wife whom he has married according to the laws approved among us [Col. 0501B] as intended for no purpose other than the procreation of children. For just as the farmer, after entrusting seed to the earth, awaits the time of harvest and does not sow more upon it, so among us the measure of desire is defined by the procreation of children. You may find many among us, both men and women, who grow old in celibacy because they know that in this state they will be more closely united with God” (Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, p. 36 and [Col. 0501C] following).

Finally, to omit others, Origen, in refuting the frivolous quibbles of Celsus, plainly shows that men and women, after professing the Christian religion, became as much more chaste as they had formerly been impure and unchaste: “From the time when they received the Word, they have in some measure become more equitable, more dignified, and more steadfast; so much so that some of them, through love of surpassing purity and in order to worship God more purely, do not engage even in the sexual relations permitted by the law” (Origen, *Against Celsus*, [Col. 0501D] bk. I, p. 21).

In another book, after showing that Gentile philosophers had wallowed in every mire of impurity, wantonness, and disgrace, he adds the following about Christians: “But those whom they despise as uneducated, and call fools and slaves, if only they entrust themselves to God and receive the teaching of Jesus, are so far removed from wantonness, uncleanness, and every indecency in sexual intercourse that, after the manner of perfect priests who turn away from all intercourse, many of them live wholly chastely and purely, refraining even from lawful intercourse with women . . . Among Christians, complete virginity is practiced not for honors among human beings, not for monetary rewards, nor for petty glory; and because they have determined to retain God in their knowledge, they are preserved by [Col. 0502A] God in a sound mind and in the performance of their duties, filled with all righteousness and goodness” (ibid., bk. VII, p. 364 ff.).

[Col. 0502B] There he contrasts Christians living in chastity or perpetual virginity with the Athenian high priest, who used hemlock in order to perform solemn ceremonies chastely, and with certain—but very few—Gentile virgins, who, like that high priest, appeared to remain virgins for the sake of religion, honor, or reward. In other dissertations we have discussed the continence and virginity preserved by very many Christians throughout the entire course of their lives, and the most chaste use of marriage among all the rest (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, dissertation I, ch. 5, art. 3, p. 708; ibid., dissertation II, ch. 10, art. 4, p. 1021). But something more will have to be said below about each of those Christian virtues (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 27, arts. 5–6).

Furthermore, when our author said, “We willingly adhere to the bond of a single marriage,” [Col. 0502C] do not, I ask, allow yourself to suppose that he condemns a second marriage. For he added “willingly” to signify that a Christian man could indeed marry a second wife, but that this was conceded to him by the Apostle as an indulgence. See, if you wish, what we have observed elsewhere on this subject (vol. I, *Apparatus*, loc. cit., p. 2015 ff.).

## ARTICLE II. How Minucius Turns Caecilius’s Argument Back Against the Gentiles and Demonstrates from Their Own Records That They Contract Marriages with Their Mothers and Sisters and That Their Gods Are Defiled by Fornication, Adultery, and Incest

[Col. 0502D]

It was not enough for our Minucius to repel the Gentiles’ slander and disclose the innocence of the Christians. He proceeds and demonstrates, by arguments of the greatest force, that the Gentiles themselves are truly guilty of the fornication, adultery, and incest of which they maliciously accused Christians. For, as he says, “among the Persians it is lawful to have intercourse with their mothers; among the Egyptians and Athenians, marriages with sisters are legal.”

Concerning the Persians, in addition to Tertullian (*Apology*, ch. 9, p. 11), Tatian writes that the Greeks consider it abominable for sons to be joined to their mothers, but that among the Persian Magi this is regarded as especially praiseworthy (Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, p. 164). Origen asserts that there are many laws by which the Persians [Col. 0503A] are not restrained, “neither mothers from marriage with their sons nor fathers from marriage with their daughters”: “They do not prohibit mothers from marrying their sons, nor fathers from marrying their own daughters” (Origen, *Against Celsus*, bk. V, p. 248). How greatly that which appears wholly execrable to other human beings was regarded as praiseworthy and approved among the Persians you may learn from these words of Philo Judaeus: “Persians of the highest rank marry their own mothers, and they consider the offspring born from these unions to be of the noblest birth and worthy, as they say, of the highest kingship” (Philo, *On the Special Laws*, p. 778).

Besides these witnesses, after Eusebius (Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, bk. I, ch. 4, p. 11), we have Theodoret, [Col. 0503B] who testifies that this most impious law was enacted by Zaradas and was immediately abolished after those same Persians received the Christian faith: “The Persians,” he says, “who formerly lived according to the laws of Zaradas, had intercourse with their mothers, sisters, and even daughters, and regarded the grossest violation of law as a lawful ordinance. But after they obeyed the laws of the fishermen, they trampled upon the laws of Zaradas as lawless and embraced evangelical continence” (Theodoret, vol. IV, discourse IX, *On Laws*, p. 614). We have already discussed this abominable law and custom of the Persians (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, dissertation II, ch. 24, art. 1, p. 1239 ff.).

[Col. 0503C]

If we turn to the Egyptians and Athenians, Philo Judaeus declares the marriage practices of both to be utterly depraved: “It is commanded that not even a sister is to be married—a truly honorable precept and one suited to continence—although among the Athenians Solon permitted marriage with sisters who had the same father, but prohibited marriage with those who had the same mother. The Spartan lawgiver, conversely, permitted marriage with sisters born of the same mother but prohibited it with those born of the same father. The Egyptian, however, mocking the scruples of both as though their regulations were only half-complete, gave free rein to lust and lavishly increased in bodies and souls the incurable evil of intemperance, granting permission to marry all sisters: those related through [Col. 0503D] either one parent or the other, and those related through both; not only younger sisters but elder and same-aged sisters as well. For twins too have often been born, whom nature separated and divided from each other at the very moment of birth, but whom licentiousness and sensuality summoned into an association that admits no association and into an inharmonious union.” [Col. 0504A] (Philo, loc. cit., p. 779 ff.).

Diodorus Siculus had previously said that the laws of the Egyptians permitted marriage between sisters and brothers, since this had turned out happily for Isis: “They marry their sisters because of the success Isis experienced in such a union” (Diodorus Siculus, *Library*, bk. I, p. 16).

Pausanias mentions the same most unjust law where he speaks of Ptolemy as follows: “This Ptolemy, having fallen in love with Arsinoë, his full sister, joined her to himself in marriage. In doing so he acted not at all according to Macedonian custom, but according to the law of the Egyptians over whom he ruled” [Col. 0504B] (Pausanias, bk. I, p. 6). Aemilius Probus—or the author of the little book *On Illustrious Men* addressed to Atticus—is likewise said to have related the same thing concerning Cimon’s marriage at Athens to his sister Elpinice. Plutarch, however, says that Cimon married her because he had not found a bride equal to the dignity of his family (Plutarch, vol. I, *Life of Cimon*, p. 481). Athenaeus, by contrast, asserts that this was done contrary to the laws (Athenaeus, *Deipnosophists*, bk. XIII, p. 589). But we believe we have thus far sufficiently proved what our author transmitted concerning these criminal and abominable marriages of both the Athenians and the Egyptians.
