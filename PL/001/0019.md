But come, let us also hear the far more numerous scholars who unanimously acknowledge that the book *On Baptism* was written before Tertullian’s separation [Col. 0152B]. Among these, the error of those such as Pamelius is foolish: following Epiphanius, as it were (*Haer.* 42, nos. 1–2), they suppose that the Quintilla who furnished the occasion for composing this book belonged to the Montanist school. Since Tertullian opposed this woman even with insults, it would follow that, when he wrote this book, he still stood with the party of the purer Church. That argument would certainly settle the entire matter, had not Epiphanius—a man most careless in expounding the opinions of heretics—here too surrendered himself, in his usual fashion, to error. For Tertullian himself says in chapter 1 that this woman belonged to the sect of the Cainites, whose doctrines and customs differ greatly from Montanist discipline; nor do we ever read that baptism was rejected by the Montanists, [Col. 0152C] but rather that it was defended in many places by Tertullian as a Montanist, as in *On Modesty*, chapter 19.

Dupin, however (*Nouvelle bibliothèque des auteurs ecclésiastiques*, vol. I, p. 91), has two other arguments by which he places this book in the catalogue of writings of sounder doctrine. The first is that no Montanist errors are scattered through the book; the second, that Tertullian says the power of conferring baptism belongs only to bishops and that it is even unlawful to grant it to women. These words of Tertullian appear to him contrary to Montanist discipline. Yet anyone can easily evade the former argument by saying that this book was not directed against the doctrines of the orthodox Church. It would therefore be nothing surprising if Tertullian professed no disagreement with that Church here, [Col. 0152D] since he did not do so in other works either when he saw no need to contend with the Psychics, as in the book *On the Pallium* and the other works discussed above.

But in Dupin’s second argument falsehood is mixed with truth. Accordingly, we shall discuss later what may rightly be drawn from it in support of the uncorrupted doctrine of this book, and here we shall note only one error of that learned man. He evidently suspects that the Montanists gave women the power to teach publicly; and because Tertullian denies them this license in our treatise, he thinks that the work is not among the Montanist writings. In this he is undoubtedly mistaken. We would establish the contrary here from Tertullian’s own Montanist-infected writings—for example, from *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 9—had not that discussion already been undertaken by the distinguished Theophilus Wernsdorf (*Commentatio de Montanistis saec. 2. vulgo creditis haereticis*, [Col. 0153A] Gdańsk, 1751, § 19).

We must therefore explain what moves us to think that judgment concerning our book should incline toward the more honorable rather than the worse conclusion. There are two grounds for this judgment. First, when Tertullian treats the question of those who possess the right to confer baptism (chapter 17), he says that the chief priest, who is the bishop, possesses that power in the highest degree, and after him the presbyters and deacons, though not without the bishop’s authority, because of the honor of the Church. “Otherwise,” he continues, “the right belongs even to laymen”—but since these matters belong to superiors, laymen should not assume for themselves the office of the episcopate, which is assigned to bishops.

From this it follows that Tertullian wrote our book while he belonged to some sacred assembly which both regarded bishops as the highest [Col. 0153B] governors of sacred affairs and granted laymen the right to wash someone in the sacred laver only when they found themselves in the utmost necessity. But a very different custom appears to have prevailed in the conventicles of the Montanists. For although I do not deny that they too had their own presbyters and deacons, Jerome bears witness that a different order was observed in appointing their teachers (*Letter to Marcella*, no. 27 in the edition of Jean Martianay, vol. IV, part II, p. 65):

> Among us, he says, the bishops hold the place of the apostles; among them, the bishop ranks third. For they have first the patriarchs of Pepuza, second those whom they call *Cenones*, and thus bishops are relegated to the third—that is, almost the last—place.

If anyone wishes to say that this Montanist custom was invented by Jerome [Col. 0153C] or was later than Tertullian’s age, I neither see what could have moved Jerome to invent things that could not be charged against the Montanists as a grave offense, nor is it in any way improbable that those who had separated themselves from the common Church by their doctrines or institutions should also have established an order of priests different from common custom—especially since Montanus himself had already called Pepuza, a town in Phrygia, Jerusalem (see Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 18).

And now observe how much honor Tertullian claims for bishops in our treatise, since he derives from them all power of baptizing with sacred water and teaches that the honor of the Church itself is most closely joined to their dignity. But in the book *On Modesty*, that most vehement assault upon the custom of the orthodox Church, [Col. 0153D] how sharply, I ask, does he ridicule (chapter 1) “the Supreme Pontiff, the Bishop of Bishops,” and his peremptory edict! How little he grants to so great a man—to one upon whom, as he says here, the dignity of the Church depended!

And if anyone thinks—as I do not deny—that Tertullian brought great emotional agitation to the cause in that book, I shall offer another passage from the undoubtedly Montanist book *On the Exhortation to Chastity*. This passage, as it seems to me, grants laymen far greater license to confer baptism than he had defended in our book, as I now see that a great man, whom I name with honor, Justus Henning Böhmer, acutely observed (*Dissertatio IX Juris Ecclesiastici antiqui*, § 5). There, after Tertullian had said that repeated marriages were permitted neither to the priests of the Old Testament nor to the priests of the Christian [Col. 0154A] Church, he says that laymen ought not to contract them either, since they themselves had been constituted priests by Christ, and for that reason they too possessed the power of offering and baptizing and were priests to themselves.

Although he says that this should not be done except where no assembly of the ecclesiastical order is present, nevertheless he neither introduces any mention of the authority of bishops or the other public teachers, as he does in our book, nor derives this power of laymen from the reciprocal character of duties and rights, as he does here:

> **Note [Col. 0154D].** Thus in *On Baptism*, chapter 16: “Laymen too possess the right to confer baptism; for what is received on equal terms may be given on equal terms.”

Rather, he derives it entirely from the mystical priesthood of all Christians. From this, unless I am mistaken, a strong suspicion may be drawn that Tertullian’s discipline when writing those books infected by the errors of Montanus was entirely different from his discipline when he applied his mind to writing our book. [Col. 0154B]

And to confirm this further, I shall propose here a conjecture of my own—not because I think that I cannot be mistaken in it, but in order to offer those capable of judging the matter something by which they may determine whether it has any force in commending our case to learned men. In chapter 17 of our book Tertullian refutes an argument which the persons against whom he was contending were accustomed to take from certain *Acts of Paul*, in order to claim for women the power of teaching publicly. If I can show what these *Acts of Paul* were and who their author was, then it seems to me that Tertullian could scarcely have rejected them if he had written this book *On Baptism* after already devoting himself to Montanist discipline.

I suspect that these *Acts* were those which we know existed among the ancients under the title *Acts of Paul and Thecla*. They were not, indeed, in the condition and form in which they appear in the edition published by Johann Ernst Grabe (*Spicilegium SS. Patrum et Haereticorum*, vol. I, section 1, p. 95 ff.), for those texts were undoubtedly corrupted in later times. Nevertheless, in Grabe’s version there are certain traces or fragments, as it were, of those *Acts of Thecla* which were famous among the ancients—for example, on p. 115, the passage in which Paul confers upon Thecla the power to communicate Christ’s doctrine publicly to others. [Col. 0154C]

This very circumstance, I think, shows that in the passage cited Tertullian had these *Acts of Thecla* before his eyes, because he mentions a certain fictitious writing which falsely claimed the name of the apostle Paul and in which women were granted this license to teach. We shall confirm this further below. But if it is asked who fabricated these spurious acts under the name of the apostle Paul, I have no doubt that the heretic Leucius—whom people commonly, but wrongly, consider a Manichaean—was the author of the *Acts* cited by Tertullian. The reasons for this judgment should be explained briefly.

First, it cannot be denied that Leucius fabricated certain *Acts* of Paul, just as he did of the other apostles. This is attested by Photius (*Bibliotheca*, section 114, p. 156 in my edition), who had often examined such writings. Second, the exceptionally learned and highly discriminating Isaac de Beausobre demonstrated admirably that Leucius belonged not to the Manichaean but to the Docetic or Encratite school (*Histoire critique de Manichée et du Manichéisme*, book II, chapter 2, § 2 ff.). [Col. 0155A] Their principal error consisted in contempt for marriage; and the entire purpose of the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* is to extol with the highest praises a life lived without marriage. Many other works were likewise fabricated by Leucius under the names of the apostles in order to confirm that error by the apostles’ own teaching and practice.

Furthermore, Beausobre demonstrated by many arguments, in the place cited, that Leucius already existed in the second century; and Epiphanius (*Haer.* LI.6, p. 427) most plainly calls Leucius a disciple of the apostle John. Therefore these *Acts of Paul*, which we have said had Leucius as their author, existed in Tertullian’s time; and [Col. 0155B] it is highly probable that Tertullian intended to designate none other than the *Acts* of Leucius. For he says that the author of these *Acts* was a presbyter who, after being convicted of fraud in the matter, was removed from his place—that is, deposed from his sacred office.

If anyone adds to this Jerome (*Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, chapter 7, p. 104), who expressly says that according to Tertullian this presbyter was convicted before John and removed from public office because of the fraud he had committed, he will surely see that the author of the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* was subject to the authority of the apostle John—which, as I have just shown, can very well be said of Leucius.

Finally, it happened most conveniently for me that I found some indication of this matter also in Philastrius (*On Heresies*, chapter 55, Fabricius’s edition, p. 112), in the passage where he discusses the heretic Seleucus and his [Col. 0155C] doctrine. All acknowledge that the heretic Seleucus and our Leucius are the same person. Now observe, I ask, what Philastrius says about Seleucus and his followers:

> They do not use baptism because of this saying spoken by John the Baptist: “He will baptize you in spirit and fire.”

Nothing could be more similar to the errors that Tertullian refutes in the book *On Baptism*. The heretics against whom he argues there denied that Christians ought to be baptized in the sacred laver; and one of them adapts John’s own words about baptism through spirit and fire to his position. What, therefore, is more probable than that Tertullian’s adversaries adhered to the opinion of Leucius, who, according to Philastrius, had said precisely the same thing?

Let no one object to me that Seleucus, or Leucius, of whom Philastrius speaks was a Manichaean and was different from Leucius, the disciple of the apostle John; or that Philastrius was a man of no authority, [Col. 0155D] and that therefore we ought not trust this witness, whose credibility walks so unsteadily. For Beausobre, in the place cited, § 3, has sufficiently maintained against Fabricius that this distinction is fictitious and nonexistent. Nor am I unaware that Philastrius is indeed a trifler in very many matters, even in enumerating Seleucus’s errors themselves, not all of which can certainly be attributed to Leucius, the disciple of John. Yet who would dare to say that no seeds of truth lie concealed among those Philastrian trifles?

Let this therefore be established: when Tertullian rejected the *Acts of Paul* in our book, he rejected a writing fabricated by Leucius, who defended in every way the righteousness of a life passed without marriage. I now wish to appeal to Pacian, bishop of Barcelona [Col. 0156A] in the fourth century, who writes (*Letter I to Sympronian the Novatian*, published in the *Bibliotheca maxima Patrum*, Lyon edition, vol. IV, p. 305G):

> The Phrygians—that is, the Montanists—falsely claim that they were inspired by Leucius.

Nor does that testimony appear to depart from the truth, because it is certain that the Montanists, just like the Encratites, to whose school Leucius belonged, attributed excessive merit to those who recoiled from marriage. From this I believe the following conclusion may be reached: if Tertullian had written our book while established among the disciples of Montanus, he would have spoken more cautiously about a book whose author was Leucius and would not have accused him of fraud, since Leucius himself agreed with the Montanists.

But if anyone replies that the force of our case escapes us because it is not sufficiently established whether Tertullian knew the author of these *Acts*—although ignorance of this is scarcely credible in so inquisitive an investigator of all antiquity—I shall grant that, if one prefers. Yet in turn everyone should grant me this: unless someone establishes the contrary position, this suspicion should at least prevent us from casting this book of Tertullian into the class of works that he composed after his separation from the orthodox. [Col. 0156B]

## § 5.—The Two Books *To His Wife* Written Before Tertullian’s Fall

I now come to the books that Tertullian addressed to his wife. That they were published together is placed beyond doubt by the beginning of the latter book:

> **Note [Col. 0156C].** “Most recently I explained to you … what a holy woman ought to pursue.”

Although these books contain things that appear consonant with Montanist errors, I would nevertheless venture to place them rather among the works that he wrote before that defection. This appears to me to be confirmed, first, by the fact that Tertullian nowhere boasts of his Paraclete in matters in which that figure’s pronouncements would have possessed great authority. For, to use this example, at the end of chapter 7 of book I, where he argues that it is better to avoid second marriages, he does not invoke the Paraclete—whom he never fails to invoke in books proceeding from the Montanist school—but appeals solely to the authority of the Church’s discipline and the law of the Apostle, by which, he says, those who had contracted a second [Col. 0157A] marriage were excluded from public office.

> **Note [Col. 0156C].** Unless I am mistaken, this passage, and that other passage cited from *On the Exhortation to Chastity*, chapter 7—not to mention many others—plainly teaches that the ancient Christian Church understood Paul’s words in 1 Timothy 3:3 concerning a bishop, “μιᾶς γυναικὸς ἀνδρι (“a husband of one wife”),” in such a way that Paul was speaking of the second marriages of teachers.
>
> For in that passage of Tertullian, after he had said that such marriages were forbidden to the priests of the Old Testament, he says: “For Christ was reserving, in this matter as in the others, [Col. 0156D] the fullness of the law. Hence among us it is prescribed more fully and explicitly that those chosen for the priestly order must be of one marriage. Indeed, I remember some twice-married men being deposed from their place.”
>
> Here he neither intends this to be understood of priests who used some prostitute in addition to a wife, as Heumann thought in *Erklärung des N. T.*, part IX, p. 179, nor speaks in the manner of the Montanists. For Tertullian’s entire purpose opposes Heumann’s interpretation: he plainly speaks of the curtailed license to marry repeatedly, and in chapter 6 of God’s twofold dispensation—the first, “Increase and multiply,” and the second, “The time is now shortened,” etc.—by which God abolished the former.
>
> Nor does he mention an opinion peculiar to the Montanists, but one common to them and the Catholics. For first, in similar passages of *On Monogamy*, chapter 11, he convicts his adversaries of the inequity of repeated marriages by saying that they request such a marriage from persons who are not permitted to have one: from a monogamous bishop; from presbyters and deacons under the same sacramental rule; from widows as well; and from laymen. He also argues that the Apostle forbade such marriages because he orders the entire rank of the Church—that is, the priests—to be drawn from the once-married. [Col. 0157D]
>
> All these things show that the Catholics rejected second marriages among priests, since from the Catholics’ own opinions he concludes that marriages of this kind cannot be permitted even to laymen. And in chapter 12 he says most plainly that the Catholics argued as follows: “He bound only those who are in the clergy to the yoke of monogamy; therefore he does not prescribe it for everyone.”
>
> Next, he attributes this institution, by which second marriages were abolished, not to the Paraclete but to Christ, and adduces the Apostle’s words, who prescribed more fully and explicitly than in the Old Testament that those chosen for the priestly order must be of one marriage. Then he immediately adds: “Indeed, I remember twice-married men being deposed from their place.” This must be understood of Catholics, for the Montanists would not have tolerated even a layman guilty of a second marriage.
>
> In our present passage, however, he speaks as follows: “How greatly second marriages detract from faith, how greatly they obstruct holiness, is declared by the discipline of the Church and the Apostle’s prescription, since it does not permit the twice-married to preside, and does not allow a widow to be chosen for the order unless she has been the wife of one man.”

I am inclined to think that these words are less suited to a Montanist writer than to one who followed the doctrines of the more rightly believing Church. Next, in the same section, he does not say that his wife would act wrongly if she entered a new marital union; and he adds, in accordance with the thought of the apostle Paul, not that second marriages are dishonorable, but that they nevertheless bring considerable anxiety and misfortunes. Nor in book II, chapter 1 does he commend abstinence from a new marriage on any other ground than that it brings greater advantage. This is excessive leniency in a Montanist writer, whose discipline ranked every second marriage among the gravest vices.

Finally, in book II he permits his wife to enter a new union after his death, and imposes no stricter law than that she marry a Christian; and in chapter 1 he says that difficult circumstances readily obtain pardon. [Col. 0157B] But how did the Montanists receive those who returned to marriage?

> “We keep the twice-married outside,” says Tertullian at the end of *On Modesty*, chapter 1; “we fix the same boundary for adulterers and fornicators as well … they will receive from the Church no more than the public exposure of their disgrace.”

This, evidently, is what it means to grant pardon readily.

It is now not difficult to judge whether such clear passages, which support the case we have proposed, can be overthrown by some resemblance between certain statements in these books and the positions that Montanus’s more severe discipline persuaded Tertullian to adopt elsewhere. Hoffmann, in the place cited, § 12, has only one argument: that these books were written after the question of flight in danger on account of religion had been debated, and after Tertullian, together with the Montanists, had denied that anyone ought to seek safety by that means. Yet this single argument is so far from moving us to adopt his opinion that it draws us still further from it. For the very passage that he produces from book I, chapter 3—

> “Even in persecutions it is better to flee from the town, when permitted, than to be caught, tortured, and deny”—

sufficiently teaches that Tertullian did not consider every flight in such a crisis unlawful. It therefore confirms that, at the time when he composed these books, he agreed less with the Montanists than with the others. [Col. 0157C]

# ARTICLE V  
## The Fourth Class of Books, in Which Nothing but Montanism Is Evident

## § 1.—The Book *On Patience*, Written in A.D. 200 or 201

[Col. 0157D]

For this reason let us remove these books as well from the number of Montanist works [Col. 0158A] and consider those which may more properly be referred to that class. Among them I do not hesitate to place first the golden book *On Patience*. For although Tillemont (*Mémoires pour servir à l’histoire ecclésiastique*, vol. III, p. 262) wishes to join it to those works that precede Tertullian’s defection to the Montanists, because it neither rejects flight amid public calamities inflicted upon Christians on account of religion nor abandons great moderation, I consider neither argument at all suitable for establishing this conclusion.

As for that moderation, I do not see why Tertullian could not have employed it even after he had adopted hostility toward the orthodox, since it is not a virtue peculiar to the rightly believing. And the other argument does not even possess the truth attributed to it. For in the passage from which Tillemont attempts to derive Tertullian’s permission to flee (chapter 13), flight is by no means permitted. Rather, patience itself is affirmed to supply strength to human weakness, and fire, the cross, wild beasts, and the sword are said to be conquered by steadfastness itself. [Col. 0158B]

Yet even in this splendid little book there are several things which show that Tertullian’s mind was by no means lightly tinged with Montanist errors; indeed, almost all the errors of the Montanists are presented there. The indication of the Paraclete is admittedly rather obscure, yet there is some indication in this passage, chapter 1, where the grace of divine inspiration alone works toward comprehending and fulfilling these things.

Further, the excellence of marriages not repeated is presented in approximately these terms in chapter 13:

> “Patience also ministers continence of the flesh to holiness; it keeps the widow, dedicates the virgin, and raises the voluntary eunuch to the kingdoms of heaven.”

Likewise, in chapter 12 he stigmatizes second marriages with the name of adultery. The highest praise of fasting undertaken by one’s own decision appears in the following passage, chapter 13:

> “The affliction of the flesh is a propitiatory victim to the Lord through the sacrifice of humiliation, when, amid squalor and restricted nourishment, it makes an offering to the Lord, content with simple food and a pure draught of water; when it joins fasts together; when it becomes habituated to ashes and sackcloth.”

I have already spoken of flight. Finally, is the denial of pardon to sinners excluded from the sacred fellowship not contained in the words of chapter 12, where he places all the sinners’ hope not in pardon obtained from members of the Church, but in that salvation which Christians, awaiting their amendment, would either desire or obtain by prayer? Beyond this, nothing was to be expected from the Montanists’ sacred community (see [Col. 0158D] *On Modesty*, end of chapter 1).

Although these Montanist errors can be discerned in this book only through certain coverings, as it were, nothing follows from that except that this little book belongs to a period when Tertullian’s zeal in defending Montanist doctrines had either not yet blazed up or had somehow subsided. The date of this work is therefore insufficiently certain. I would not greatly doubt that it was written when danger to the Christians was already imminent, since a not unreasonable ground for this opinion is to be sought in the book’s very subject. But whether Tertullian wrote it amid the fears aroused by Scapula against the Christians during Caracalla’s reign, as Allix believed, or at the beginning of the public calamities under the emperor Severus—when that equally calm and temperate treatise *On Flight in Persecution* also appeared—no one could easily say. [Col. 0159A] Let us therefore pass to another work.

## § 2.—The Book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, Which Is Very Probably the First of the Montanist Books

This is the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, which I scarcely doubt belongs among the first works filled with Montanus’s errors. The extremely frequent mention of the Paraclete and of his new discipline does not allow us to hesitate about it; thus it is not even necessary to cite either the similarity of this book to the works *On the Dress of Women*, which belong to the Montanist school, or its rather obscure defense of single marriage in chapter 3.

For what is signified by those revelations by which, as Tertullian says in chapter 17, [Col. 0159B] the Lord measured out the dimensions of the veil? What is that novelty of correction which, while the rule of faith contained in the Apostles’ Creed remains fixed, the other matters of discipline and conduct admit through the grace of God working and advancing to the end (chapter 1)? What is that Paraclete whom the Lord sent so that discipline might gradually be directed and brought to perfection (ibid.)? What is that account of the ages of Christian discipline, in which righteousness is finally to be brought to maturity through the Paraclete? What, I ask, do all these things attest except that Tertullian was a disciple of Montanus?

Nor is it any more obscure that this was approximately the first offspring he produced after joining the Montanists. For—to begin with this—among all his Montanist books there is scarcely one in which he employs greater force to defend Montanist discipline against the charge of novelty than the book under consideration, especially in sections 1 and 2. [Col. 0159C] This certainly provides reasonable grounds for suspecting that Tertullian composed it as soon as he joined the Montanists, in order to remove from himself the accusation of innovation.

Nor in any other work does he seem to defend more strongly the Paraclete and the divine origin of his discipline than in this one. Since elsewhere (*Against Praxeas*, chapter 1) he says that not only his acknowledgment of the Paraclete but his defense of him separated him from the Psychics, it is easy to understand that our book, in which he certainly defends him vehemently, was written during the opening period of his life among the Montanists.

This may be confirmed by many arguments not lacking in probability. First, the books *On the Dress of Women* were written at precisely that time, as we argued elsewhere, and they treat almost the same subject as our book. Second, although Tertullian had a sufficiently suitable opportunity to inveigh more severely against the custom—contrary to his own—followed by at least some adherents of the orthodox Church in the veiling of virgins, he nevertheless argues here far more moderately than in his other works directed against the orthodox. [Col. 0159D] Anyone who has learned elsewhere of Tertullian’s extremely vehement emotional agitation in defending Montanism will say that this is difficult to understand unless our proposal is admitted.

Finally, although it would have greatly benefited the cause whose defense he had undertaken in our book if he had mentioned the public calamities and dangers to which Christians were exposed by the worshipers of the gods—and although Tertullian, who habitually omits nothing useful to his cause, employed that argument in the books *On the Dress of Women*, which resemble our book—he nevertheless does not say here, even in a single word, that such calamities existed. From this it may be concluded that it was written at a time when Christians were not oppressed by such evils. [Col. 0160A]

But since we have said that it was composed after Tertullian joined the Montanists, it is evident that our book cannot be earlier than A.D. 200, because we demonstrated elsewhere that Tertullian went over to the Montanists in that year. Nor can it be much later, since by A.D. 202 the calamities stirred up by Severus had already begun. We have nevertheless declined to place that book among those whose date is most certainly established, because the circumstances of its birth could be determined more by conjecture than by the employment of entirely certain arguments. [Col. 0160B]

## § 3.—The Book *On the Exhortation to Chastity*, Written in A.D. 201

Not much later, it appears, were written those books in which Tertullian sought to vindicate the Montanists’ distinctive doctrines from the contempt and assault of the opposing party: the treatises *On the Exhortation to Chastity*, *On Monogamy*, *On Fasts*, and *On Modesty*. No one doubts that all these works are full of Montanus’s errors; and it can scarcely be doubted any more that they appeared at approximately this time—that is, near the beginning of Septimius’s Montanism. We shall demonstrate both points briefly by surveying these individual books, [Col. 0160C] lest this part of our work, although also treated by others, appear to have been omitted.

If I see the matter correctly, these books were published in the same order in which we have listed them, so that the first of them may be regarded as *On the Exhortation to Chastity*. It is true that Jerome (*Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, chapter 53) does not include it among the books that Tertullian composed in defense of Montanus’s doctrines. There are also things in this book which might persuade someone who had not examined the matter sufficiently that Tertullian did not yet belong to that school at the time; but those considerations are comparatively slight.

The reason Jerome does not include our book in his catalogue of these writings may be that he intended to enumerate only those books that Tertullian designed to oppose to the orthodox. But the book *On the Exhortation to Chastity* was not directed against them; rather, it was written to instruct some brother or other, whom Tertullian wished not so much to refute when he was contemplating remarriage as to exhort to refrain from it. [Col. 0160D]

Moreover, I certainly do not deny that there is considerable moderation here—indeed, more than in the other books that discuss some doctrine of the Montanists. In this book Tertullian himself recommends one marriage and calls it useful and honorable more than he defends an absolute necessity of abstaining from second marriages. From this someone might conclude that he was perhaps inclining toward the Montanists but had not yet attached himself to their party, just as we said above concerning the books *To His Wife*.

Yet this moderation [Col. 0161A] and leniency appears even in other books undoubtedly written at a time when he had already devoted himself to Montanus’s discipline. Nor is it particularly surprising in a book of this kind, which has the character of a letter of persuasion, in which we customarily employ a more subdued and tranquil style. It also appears to have been written not to a Montanist, who would have delighted in a more severe discipline, but to a certain man belonging to the orthodox Church.

There is a clear indication of this at the beginning of the book, where he says that this brother was undoubtedly contemplating an end to his solitary state—that is, the restoration of marriage. I am inclined to think that this would not apply to someone who, together with the Montanists, regarded second marriages as unlawful.

Nor am I unaware of Tillemont’s scruple (place cited, p. 202). He both imagines a great difference between this work and *On Monogamy*—a difference that scarcely exists for anyone who attends correctly—and is surprised that Montanus is not mentioned. But, even without invoking the passage that Rigaltius restored because it was not printed in our books (*note ad hunc locum*, p. 114), which contains the highest praises of Priscilla, the Montanist prophetess, it would not have been prudent for Tertullian to praise Montanus to a man who appeared to reject his discipline. [Col. 0161B]

But if any other doubt remains, one may oppose to it not only the extraordinarily close similarity, extending even to the wording, between this book and *On Monogamy*—which one could almost call the παράφρασιν (“paraphrase”) of our book—but also the following statement in chapter 6:

> “In the beginning God cast forth the seed of the race, the reins of marriage having been loosened, until the world should be filled, until material for the new discipline should advance; but now, at the ends of the ages, he has restrained what he had released and recalled what he had permitted.” [Col. 0161C]

Nor is it more remote from the truth to call our book approximately the first of those whose very subject consists in defending Montanist errors. We cannot suppose the book *On Monogamy* to have been written at the same time as our book because, as we have said, it treats precisely the same subject. It therefore appears that Tertullian wrote our book before the other. Had *On Monogamy* already existed when he set about writing this one, there would have been no need to write the present book; instead, he could have referred that brother to the treatise *On Monogamy* as a publicly published discussion.

But if we judge that our book was written first, it could not have been commended to the Psychics, since it was not public but addressed to a private individual. [Col. 0161D] Finally, we shall show below that the book *On Monogamy* was written approximately at the beginning of the calamities stirred up by Severus himself in A.D. 202, and that the other works deliberately directed against the Psychics are later than *On Monogamy*. It is therefore necessary to believe that our book *On the Exhortation to Chastity* precedes them in date.
