Why then, you will say, does he declare so openly that his birth was perfect not from eternity but at the beginning of the world, and that he then received his form, adornment, [Col. 0848A] sound, and voice? But shall we say that Tertullian descended into such madness as to suppose that the generation of the Son of God was at some time imperfect or merely begun, and that afterward it assumed form and adornment and was thus perfected and completed (ibid.)? He therefore called it imperfect not in that sense, but in the manner he himself explains in the same place: although it had been perfect from eternity, it nevertheless lay hidden within the bosom of God and was known to no one except God himself alone. But when, in creating the world, he brought forth this Word of his outside himself and, as it were, emitted him from the womb and matrix of his heart—as Tertullian likes to speak—then that eternal Word assumed sound and voice, and therefore an outward adornment and form. For then he was recognized by rational creatures. The noun *species* among good authors indeed has the same [Col. 0848B] meaning as the manifestation of some thing. And indeed, Nonius says that *species* derives from seeing. Therefore, when the Word of God, or the eternal Son of God the Father, was emitted outside him at the beginning of the world, he then received form and adornment, or sound and voice, and his birth was perfect—not, indeed, with respect to himself, but with respect to the creatures who recognized it.

No further hearing, therefore, should be given to those who again object that the same Tertullian said: “God was not possessed of Word from the beginning” (ibid., chap. 5). For he immediately added: “but God was rational even before the beginning; and because the Word himself also subsists in Reason, he shows that Reason to be prior, as his substance.” But if God was rational before the beginning, or from eternity, he also from eternity brought forth Reason, which, [Col. 0848C] as has often been said, is his Word and Son (ibid., chap. 8). Then afterward, he says, “this Word was constructed of spirit, and, so to speak, the body of the Word—that is, his substance—is spirit.” He acknowledges, however, that both, although they are one and the same, can in some manner be distinguished (ibid., chap. 26): “For spirit,” he says, “is the substance of the Word, and the Word the operation of the spirit, and the two are one.” But this Word of God the Father, or this very operation of his spirit, did not become known to creatures before the world was founded. The knowledge of this operation, or generation, of the Son of God is called by Tertullian his “perfect birth,” because what had previously been known to God the Father alone was then disclosed to rational creatures.

Petavius and others, however, still object to us these words of Tertullian concerning the same manifestation of the Son [Col. 0848D] (Petav., vol. II, *Theolog. dogmat.*, book I, chap. 5, p. 26; Tertull., *Against Praxeas*, chap. 12): “But in the preceding works of the world, how is it written? First, while the Son had not yet appeared: ‘And God said, Let there be light, and light was made’—the Word himself immediately being the true light which enlightens the man coming into this world, and through him the light of the world also. From then on, however, with the Word, Christ, assisting and administering, God willed things to be made, and God made them . . . through whom all things were made.” From this they conclude that it is there manifestly demonstrated that the Son and the Spirit, the second and third persons of the Trinity, did not subsist from eternity, but only when God willed to make the world and actually made it.

[Col. 0849A] But it must be observed that with these words Tertullian was answering the heretic Praxeas, who objected to Catholics that God, to whom nothing is impossible, could make himself both Father and Son at once. Tertullian readily acknowledges that all things could indeed be done by God, but that not everything he could do was done by him. Praxeas therefore had to demonstrate clearly that the Father had in fact made himself both Father and Son, or that the Son was none other than the Father himself. Tertullian, on the contrary, affirms all the more confidently that this can never be proved by Praxeas, because he so readily heaps up various testimonies of Scripture by which, he says, “among us the Father and the Son are demonstrated distinctly—distinctly, I say, not separately” (ibid., chap. 21); since the Son of God, as we have said so often, proceeded from the Father and is therefore [Col. 0849B] indeed distinct from him, but not divided or separated, because the substance of both is the same. Then, on the authority of the same Scripture, he shows that there are three persons of the Trinity joined by a simple unity. It was there that he uttered the words objected to us, and afterward concluded that all things were made through God’s Word, or through the Son of God, who is himself also God. Therefore, because he is God’s Word, he is distinguished from him and is called another “in the name of person,” he says, “not of substance; for distinction, not division.” But how is his substance one with that of the Father, and how is he one God with him, unless he existed from eternity, just as the Father did? Therefore, when he said that the Son of God had not appeared before the beginning of the world, he signifies plainly enough that the Son was then known to no creature. But when, as he says in the same place, it was first said, [Col. 0849C] “Let there be light,” light was immediately made and the true light appeared—that is, the Word of God—not indeed because he then existed for the first time and had not existed before, since he had been begotten from eternity, but because he who is the true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world, then began to appear to men. So far, then, is this answer of our Tertullian from favoring Petavius and the others that it instead overturns their entire argument from its foundations.

We know, indeed, that his words are twisted by Bull into another sense. He admits that they are very obscure, but nevertheless suspects that they should be understood of the Son’s manifestation as follows: the Son of God, he says, insofar as he is eternal Reason and Wisdom, first began to appear when God began to dispose, order, and adorn by his wisdom the unformed and confused [Col. 0849D] matter of things. God’s Wisdom and Sophia had not yet shone forth from the confused and unordered mass of things called chaos. But it shone forth most clearly when the primeval light, as an image of the Son of God, who is the splendor of eternal light, illuminated matter and clothed each thing with its own form and adornment. Yet what seems to conflict with this interpretation is that the Word and Son of God shines forth no less from the creation of unformed and confused matter than from the creation of the primeval light, since through him every light and all other things were created. If, nevertheless, anyone should rightly prove that the explanation Bull offers of Tertullian’s words is true, we shall not greatly resist it, provided [Col. 0850A] that one can also infer from it that Tertullian acknowledged the eternal splendor of the light—that is, of the Son of God—and the eternal generation so constantly asserted and defended by him, as we have seen.

## ARTICLE IX. Another argument is resolved, derived from the fact that Tertullian seems to have said that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist

By another argument, drawn from Tertullian’s book *Against Hermogenes*, Petavius thinks it can still be proved that the eternity of the Son of God was entirely unknown to him. For our African author, as it seems to Petavius, declares most explicitly that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist. That learned man thinks that Tertullian asserts [Col. 0850B] the sheer and unadulterated fiction of Arius and his followers when he refutes the arguments of Hermogenes, who babbled that matter was coeternal with God. For that heretic attempted to demonstrate the eternity of matter from the fact that God had never not been Lord. But, he argued, God could not always have been Lord unless there had been matter of which he was always accounted Lord. Tertullian, however, refutes this argument of Hermogenes and denies that God was always Lord (Tertull., *Against Hermogenes*, chap. 3): “For ‘God,’” as he says, “is the name of the substance itself—that is, of divinity—whereas ‘Lord’ is the name not of substance but of power. He was therefore Lord only from the time when those things began to exist which God held in his power.” [Col. 0850C] He then continues as follows: “Because God is also Father, and God is Judge, he is not therefore always Father and Judge merely because he is always God. For he could not be Father before the Son, nor Judge before the offense. But there was a time when neither the offense nor the Son existed, the one to make him Judge, and the other to make him Father; so too he was not Lord before those things existed whose Lord he would be, but was only destined sometime to become Lord, just as Father through the Son and Judge through the offense, so also Lord through those things which he would make to serve him.” From this Petavius concludes that Tertullian clearly and plainly defines that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist.

Nor should it be answered that Tertullian is speaking not of God’s Son but of the son of any human being. For when he discusses God’s Sophia, or the Son of God himself, he cites and interprets concerning him these words of Proverbs (Prov. [Col. 0850D] VIII, v. 22 ff.): “The Lord created me at the beginning of his ways; before the ages he established me for his works . . . Before the abyss I was begotten.” From them he argues as follows (Tertull., *Against Hermogenes*, chap. 18): “Let Hermogenes therefore acknowledge that God’s Sophia is proclaimed as born and created precisely so that we should believe nothing to be unbegotten and uncreated except God alone. For if that which was from the Lord and in him was not without a beginning—namely, his Sophia, born and created from the time when it began to be stirred in God’s thought for the ordering of the works of the world—much less can it be admitted that anything outside the Lord was without a beginning.” Here, they will say, Tertullian plainly asserts that Sophia, or the Son of God, was not without a beginning, but received one, and that [Col. 0851A] Sophia was then born and created when it began to be stirred in God’s thought for the ordering of the works of the world.

We have reported this entire argument and the complete words of our author lest anyone complain that we have concealed or omitted anything. Nor shall we conceal that these passages of Tertullian seem so obscure and difficult to some that they have thought nothing else should be answered to the argument drawn from them except these words of Jerome, with which Jerome answers Helvidius when the latter adduces the authority of this same Tertullian for another purpose (*Against Helvidius*): “Of Tertullian, indeed, I say nothing more than that he was not a man of the Church”—that is, that he fell into various errors of the heretics. But by this answer the knot of the difficulty is cut, not untied. For Tertullian wrote this [Col. 0851B] book while he was still a Catholic and a man of the Church. Moreover, even if he fell into various errors and, to use Hilary’s words, “although the man’s error detracted authority from writings otherwise worthy of approval” (Hilary, *Commentary on Matthew*, chap. V), we nevertheless demonstrated above that the Catholic doctrine of the divine and eternal generation of the Son of God was constantly asserted and defended by him, whether orthodox or heretical.

Perhaps, therefore, the response of others will appear more probable: they say that Tertullian, a man of extremely keen intellect, was carried somewhat further than was proper, in the not-unusual manner of disputants, during his very great contest with the heretic Hermogenes concerning the eternity of matter (Bull, *Defense of the Nicene Faith*, sect. 3, chap. 10, no. 4). For no one even moderately acquainted with reading his [Col. 0851C] books can fail to know that, in order to establish some proposition, he gathers arguments from every quarter, including even some that carried little weight. Therefore, they say, one should not judge from the cited words of this book what he thought about the eternal generation of the Son of God, but from other passages where, with a settled and tranquil mind, he explained his own doctrine—and that of the whole Church—concerning the supreme divinity and eternity of the Son of God, as we have seen.

But let us examine more carefully, if you please, whether in the passages objected against us he actually transgressed the limits of the true doctrine that he himself established. In the former passage he said that God was not always Lord, just as he could not be Father before the Son or Judge before the offense. Yet the question there concerned neither Father nor Judge, but only [Col. 0851D] Lord. They are therefore examples that he employs to refute Hermogenes. Examples involving any father or judge, whether created or uncreated, served his purpose equally well.

Granted, however—and let us concede, if you please—that he is speaking of the true God the Father and his Son: could he have denied that the Son was coeternal with his Father? Has it not been proved more than sufficiently how clearly and frequently he proclaimed that the Word, or Son of God, who is his Father’s Reason, was always in him and was never separated from him, and consequently is eternal? Accordingly, when he says that there was a time when he did not exist, he undoubtedly did not intend to deny his eternity, which he asserted and defended more frequently elsewhere.

[Col. 0852A] What, then, you will ask, is the meaning of his words, by which he seems so openly to deny it? You will readily persuade us that he intended nothing other than what he meant in the other words explained above, where he had said that the Son’s birth was perfect when God began to create the world. For although this Son of God had previously and from eternity been begotten, as we have shown, he had not yet come forth from the bosom or intellect of the eternal Father, as from his matrix, womb, and heart. Before the creation of things, therefore, neither his being the Son nor his having a Father was known. But when the Father emitted him outside himself, men then began to recognize his generation and that he truly was the Son of God. Therefore, in order to overturn more effectively Hermogenes’ frivolous argument drawn from the word “Lord,” Tertullian [Col. 0852B] thought it could be said that there was a time when men recognized neither that God was Father nor that his Son existed, because his generation and birth had not been ascertained and recognized by them. But when they recognized it, this generation was then perfect, as we saw him say. He therefore appears to be speaking of this perfect generation in the sense we have noted.

That this was indeed his meaning can be established from the cited words of Tertullian himself against Hermogenes: “His Sophia,” he says, “was born and created from the time when it began to be stirred in God’s thought for the ordering of the works of the world” (Tertullian, *Against Hermogenes*, chap. 18); because this very Sophia, who, as he admits and as we have said, was always in God the Father, appeared to men as born from him and was manifested to them. For he did not say [Col. 0852C] that she then first arose and was begotten. Indeed, in this very passage, where he rejects the subtleties of Hermogenes, he most expressly proclaims that she arose from eternity: “If matter is necessary to God for the works of the world,” he says, “as Hermogenes thought, God possessed a matter far worthier and more suitable, not to be sought among the philosophers but understood among the prophets—namely, his Sophia. She alone, in short, knew the mind of the Lord. For who knows the things of God, and the things within him, except the spirit who is within him? But Sophia is spirit.” From this he concludes: “Who would not rather commend her as the source and origin of all things, a matter of matters not subjected to him, not different in state, not restless in motion, not formless in condition, but innate and his own, composed and beautiful, such as God could have employed, having need [Col. 0852D] of what was his own rather than another’s?” Who does not understand that Tertullian here asserts that God’s Sophia was begotten by her Father from all eternity; that she is spirit only, and God the Father’s counselor; that she is not different from him in state—that is, as we noted above, in substance—but is his own, was always inherent in him, and was the only thing, and no other, that he needed for the creation of the world, if indeed God can ever be said to have needed anything?

Having established these points, he answers Hermogenes, who babbled that God needed matter and that matter was therefore coeternal with him: “God’s Sophia is therefore also proclaimed as born and created, so that we should believe nothing unbegotten and uncreated except God alone” (ibid.). Thus he says that Sophia, or the Word and Son of God, is proclaimed to have been born at a certain time, [Col. 0853A] and to have begun to be stirred, according to the prophetic saying he cites: “My heart has uttered a most excellent word.” “And thus,” he says, “the Word of God, who is also his Sophia, was born,” because he then came and went forth from the bosom of his Father, in which he had lain hidden, begotten from eternity; and thus, as we have said, his birth was perfect, because it was then, and not before, disclosed and demonstrated to men. Moreover, just as this generation of the Son of God, so also the dominion—and indeed the fatherhood—of his divine Father began to be recognized by rational creatures at the beginning of the world, and God began to be called by the names of Father and Lord alike. Furthermore, that this is Tertullian’s meaning is established by the fact that he is there disputing against Hermogenes, who contended that God was not Lord from eternity unless he had matter outside himself of which [Col. 0853B] he truly was Lord. Yet although Tertullian may not have entirely extricated himself from every difficulty in this argument, no one can therefore rightly conclude that he denied the eternity and divine nature of the Son of God. For who does not know that certain doctrines most difficult to understand are asserted without hesitation by many who cannot entirely untie all the knots of the difficulties in which they are entangled? But we have shown most clearly how constantly, how explicitly, and without any verbal evasions Tertullian asserted and defended the eternity and divine nature of the Son of God. He proclaims it no less plainly elsewhere as well, as we shall see in the following chapter.

# CHAPTER IX. On Christ as God and Man

[Col. 0853C]

## ARTICLE I. On Christ’s human birth from a virgin, foretold by the sacred prophets; why he is said to be a man mingled with God; and why his advent upon earth cannot be rejected by the Gentiles, who had fabricated certain similar tales to destroy its truth

We have demonstrated sufficiently and abundantly, unless we are altogether mistaken, how true and orthodox was Tertullian’s opinion and doctrine concerning the supreme divinity of the Word, or Word and Son of God. But further light can be shed on these matters by what he taught concerning the same Son of God clothed in human flesh. To the Gentiles [Col. 0853D] he explains briefly, in this manner, what Christians believed concerning him: “This ray of God”—that is, the Son of God, who proceeded from his Father’s bosom like a ray from the sun—“as had always been proclaimed in ancient times” by the ancients, namely, the prophets, “descended into a certain virgin, and, fashioned as flesh in her womb, is born a man mingled with God” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 399). But in another book he expounded this in the following clearer words: “Accordingly, the derivation of each substance displayed him as man and God: on the one side born, on the other unborn; on the one side fleshly, on the other spiritual; on the one side weak, on the other exceedingly strong; on the one side dying, on the other living. This distinctive property of the conditions—the divine and the human—was duly assigned by the truth of each nature, with equal assurance regarding spirit and flesh: the miracles proved the Spirit [Col. 0854A] of God, the sufferings the flesh of man” (idem, *On the Flesh of Christ*, chap. 5). He therefore teaches lucidly that both the divine and the human nature were altogether perfect in Christ. No less openly does he declare that those two natures of Christ were not confused, but were so distinguished from one another that their properties are entirely different.

Accordingly, when in the *Apologeticum* he said, “He is born a man mingled with God” (passage cited above), he undoubtedly did not signify that his two natures were blended and confused, as the Eutychians later dreamed, but that they were coupled and joined to one another—as we have explained elsewhere—so that Christ is both true God and true man (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 17, art. 3).

He teaches, however, that Christ was made man [Col. 0854B] not in the ordinary manner of men, but “descended,” he says, “into a virgin,” who bore him without the agency of any man (*Apologeticum*, passage cited). But because this was extremely difficult to believe, or could be mocked by the pagans, Tertullian deliberately warns everyone that it had been foretold by the sacred prophets, whose authority, as we said above, can be undermined by no one. Hence he had said shortly before: “(Therefore he came who was foretold as destined to come from God to reform and illuminate it—the grace of the Jews transmitted to the Christians—namely, Christ, the Son of God.) The arbiter and master of this grace and discipline, the illuminator and guide of the human race, was announced as the Son of God; not, indeed, begotten in such a way that he should blush at the name of son or at his father’s seed . . . The Son of God has no mother through sexual misconduct; [Col. 0854C] even the one whom he seems to have had had not married” (ibid.). All these words, in precisely this form, are found in our manuscripts and in the old editions down to Rigaltius. But Rigaltius, whom others afterward followed, decided to cut away completely the first phrase of this passage, enclosed in parentheses. He nevertheless gave no reason for this excision—not even the slightest word. Yet even if this phrase appeared to him wholly useless and altogether improperly inserted into Tertullian’s text, the reader should at least have been notified of this addition.

Moreover, Tertullian’s meaning can be obscure to no one: he most manifestly declares that the Son of God descended into a virgin, from whom he received a human body. He said “virgin” because this Son of God, he says, [Col. 0854D] “has no mother through sexual misconduct; even the one whom he seems to have had had not married.” But if any woman became a mother without the stain of sexual misconduct and without marital intercourse, she certainly gave birth to Christ while preserving the integrity of her virginity. Yet by what reasoning, someone will ask, could it be said, “she had not married”? Do not almost all assert that she had actually married Joseph? Some answer that by the words “she had not married,” nothing else is signified than that Mary had not known a man. For she herself answered the angel: “How shall this be, since I know not a man?” (Luke 1:34). But they needed to prove who uses the verb *nubo* in this sense. Others approve the opinion—which they believe to be Jerome’s—that when Mary conceived Christ [Col. 0855A] she was Joseph’s betrothed, or, to use the Evangelists’ word, she had indeed been espoused to him, and married him after a short interval following the conception. But no one can disclose Tertullian’s meaning more certainly than Tertullian himself (Petav., vol. V, *Theolog. dogmat.*, book XIV, chap. 3, §10; Jerome, *Against Helvidius*). In his book *On the Flesh of Christ*, however, he wrote the following concerning the Virgin Mary: “Although she conceived as a virgin, in giving birth she married, by the very law of the body being opened,” which he there cites: “Every male that opens the womb shall be called holy to the Lord” (idem, *On the Flesh of Christ*, chap. 23). You see plainly that he here takes the verb *nubere*, “to marry,” to mean the virgin’s delivery, by which her womb was opened. We are not now inquiring in what sense he said it was opened, a matter treated in our observations on Ambrose. For our present purpose it is enough [Col. 0855B] that the verb *nubere* is taken by him to mean the opening of a woman’s womb in childbirth (*Notes on Ambrose’s Exposition of the Gospel according to Luke*). Just as we cannot deny that this is by no means the proper meaning of this verb, so certainly all must confess that Mary’s inviolate virginity is asserted by him most clearly (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, diss. 2, chap. 6, art. 9, p. 973). For he establishes and confirms this teaching not only in his *Apologeticum* but also in another book, as we have seen elsewhere, as something received by the universal Church of his time and previously by broad consensus. We discussed it in our preceding dissertations (ibid., book II, diss. 6, chap. 7, §5, p. 597; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 17, art. 3; vol. I, *Apparatus*, passage cited, and [Col. 0855C] book III, diss. 2, chap. 6, art. 4, p. 597 ff.; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 8, art. 1).

Tertullian further openly professes that this birth of Christ from a virgin and his advent upon earth were foretold by our sacred seers. But he remained entirely silent about what their oracles were in this book, in which he was aiming at brevity. He reported many, however, in his book *Against the Jews*, and others reported many in other books; we have already treated them in those same preceding dissertations of ours. Yet we cannot omit the testimony of Justin Martyr, which agrees excellently with Tertullian’s statements and confirms them wonderfully. For when that most holy martyr cites several predictions of the sacred prophets concerning Christ’s future advent, and especially this one of Isaiah, “Behold, a virgin shall conceive,” he there shows that this gave the pagans no opportunity to excuse their Jupiter, who had begotten several children in adulterous intercourse. For that prophecy of Isaiah—[non-Latin text omitted]—means that a virgin was to conceive without sexual intercourse. For if she had consorted with anyone, she would no longer have been a virgin. But a power coming upon the virgin overshadowed her and, while she remained a virgin, caused her to carry a fetus in her womb (Justin, *First Apology*).

Even if no one should venture to declare with certainty that Tertullian was aiming at these words of Justin, no one can doubt [Col. 0856A] that Cyprian had regard to this passage of our Tertullian when he committed the following to writing: “The arbiter and master of this indulgence, grace, and discipline, the Word and Son of God, is therefore sent, who throughout earlier times had been proclaimed by all the prophets as the illuminator and teacher of the human race . . . He glides into the virgin, puts on flesh through the cooperation of the Holy Spirit: God is mingled with man” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*). He is said by Cyprian, as by Tertullian, to be “mingled,” not by a confusion of the two natures, divine and human, fabricated by the Eutychians, but because Christ, as we noted shortly before, has both perfect natures joined and coupled to his divine person.

Then our author, addressing the Gentiles themselves, demonstrates as follows [Col. 0856B] that these things cannot be rejected by them: “Accept for the moment this tale—or accept it as a tale; it is similar to your own—while we show how Christ is proved. Those among you who furnished rival tales beforehand for the destruction of this truth knew it” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 399). In our manuscripts, however, and in the editions before Rigaltius, the words after “is proved” are written differently, as follows: “and who among you furnished rival tales of this sort beforehand for the destruction of truth of this sort.” Why Rigaltius altered these words, you should expect no reason from him. Tertullian argues against the pagans as follows: You can object to me that what I have said about Christ, his birth from a virgin, and his human life is a mere tale. Yet even if I granted you that, [Col. 0856C] you should nevertheless not reject but accept it, since it is similar to the tales you have fabricated. For we show how Christ’s birth from a virgin is proved, and that there are several writers among you who furnished similar tales beforehand to destroy the truth. Tell me, then, I ask, whether the meaning of Tertullian’s words as transcribed in our manuscripts is any more obscure than it is in Rigaltius’s edition.

Moreover, Justin Martyr had previously argued against the same Gentiles in an entirely similar manner: [non-Latin text omitted]. “And when we proclaim that Christ was born of a virgin, let this too be something you have in common with us because of Perseus” (Justin, *First Apology*). For everyone knows the tale in which the Gentiles invented that [Col. 0856D] Jupiter, transformed into a golden shower, descended to Danaë and begot Perseus from her. In the same place the holy martyr mentions other pagan tales, which he shows to resemble what is truly narrated concerning Christ. Afterward he adds that such tales were celebrated by pagan poets under the prompting and through the deceits of demons, so that Christ’s wondrous deeds might be regarded as tales and poetic fictions.

There is no one who, even if we remained silent, would not understand how closely these things agree with Tertullian’s statements and how they confirm them. For he touched briefly not only on the single tale concerning Jupiter’s wicked intercourse with Danaë but also on several others in these words: “The Son [Col. 0857A] of God was announced as not begotten in such a way that he should blush at the name of Son or at his Father’s seed; he did not endure as his father a god who, through incest with a sister, violation of a daughter, or adultery with another man’s wife, became a scaly, horned, or feathered lover, or one transformed into gold. These are your names for the Jupiter of Danaë” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 396). Thus these words are read in our manuscripts and older printed editions, except that the latter placed a full stop after the name “Danaë” and read “gold” in place of “into gold.” But Rigaltius removed the name “Danaë” on his own authority, as is evident, and against the testimony of all the manuscript and printed copies. We discussed these transformations of Jupiter for the violation of virgins and women in our preceding dissertations, especially those on the books of Arnobius [Col. 0857B] (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 9, art. 4).

## ARTICLE II. The same advent of Christ upon earth cannot be denied by the Jews unless they are afflicted with blind obstinacy

After Tertullian had argued against the Gentiles in the manner we have explained, he then immediately proves that what Christians taught against the Jews concerning the advent of Christ the Lord was true. For he continues thus: “The Jews too knew that Christ was to come, since it was to them that the prophets spoke. For even now they await his advent, and there is no greater contention between us and them than their refusal to believe that he has already come. For since two advents of his were indicated—the first, which has already been discharged,” or accomplished and completed, “in the humility of the human condition; the second, which threatens at the close of the age, in the sublimity of manifested divinity,” that is, visible and manifest, “by failing to understand the first and hoping for the second, which was more manifestly proclaimed, they supposed there was only one” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 400). Cyprian, in his customary fashion, drank from this source of Tertullian and adapted it to his own use as follows: “The Jews too knew that Christ was to come. For he was always announced to them by the warnings of the prophets. But because a twofold advent of his was indicated—one in which he would perform the office and furnish the example of a man, the other which would confess him as God—by failing to understand the first advent, which secretly preceded in suffering, they believe only in the one that will be manifest in power” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*).

Both, therefore, acknowledge that Christ’s advent in the assumed flesh [Col. 0857D] of a man was real. For it was so openly foretold by the ancient sacred prophets, as we noted shortly before, that the Jews could not have been ignorant of it. But because Christ lived a mortal life in the humility of the human condition, the Jews stubbornly denied that he was the true Messiah and, swollen with pride, await another who is to come at the end of the world with the full glory of divine majesty. Elsewhere, however, we have shown that this error of the Jews was voluntary, and that it was utterly overthrown not only by our Tertullian and Cyprian but also by Lactantius (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 17, art. 5).

The cause of so great an error and so great a blindness among the Jews, Tertullian says, was their transgression, [Col. 0858A] as Isaiah had foretold. But our author himself must again be heard: “For their failure to understand Christ’s former advent—since they would have believed if they had understood, and would have attained salvation if they had believed—was the deserved consequence of their transgression. They themselves read it written thus: that they were punished by the loss of wisdom and understanding, and of the fruit of their eyes and ears” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 400). Cyprian once again followed closely in his tracks: “But the fact that the Jewish people could not understand this was the deserved consequence of their transgressions. They were so punished with blindness of wisdom and understanding that, being unworthy of life, they had life before their eyes and did not see it” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*).

Both were undoubtedly aiming at and tacitly citing these words of Isaiah: “You shall say to this people: Hear while hearing, and do not understand; and see a vision, and do not recognize it. Blind the heart of this people, make its ears heavy, and close its eyes, lest perhaps it see with its eyes and hear with its ears and understand with its heart and be converted, and I heal it” (Isa. 6:9–10). You will find these words cited not only in Matthew’s Gospel and the Acts of the Apostles, but also by John the Evangelist in this form: “Therefore they could not believe, because Isaiah said again: He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart, so that they should not see with their eyes and understand with their heart and be converted, and I heal them” (Matt. 1:14; Acts 28:26; John 12:39–40). But we have already discussed in our dissertation on the books of Lactantius the Jews who were blinded by an utterly intolerable vice of obstinacy and who are therefore said to have been no longer able [Col. 0858C] to believe; and something further must still be said below in Article III (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 16, art. 2).
