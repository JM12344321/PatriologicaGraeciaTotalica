# TWO BOOKS TO HIS WIFE

[Col. 1273]

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, TO HIS WIFE [Note: [Col. 1273D] The Franeker edition adds: *On Single Marriage*] (C)

## BOOK ONE

[Col. 1273D] **SUMMARY.—**Whether warned by advancing age or illness, or because he was now about to bid farewell to worldly affairs so that he might profess Christian discipline more rigorously and be raised to the heights of the sublime priesthood, Tertullian drew up for his wife what were, as it were, the tablets of a will—namely these two books. In the first he urges his wife, should he himself depart to the Lord before her, to abstain [Col. 1274D] from a second marriage; in the second he instructs her, should she wish to marry, to unite herself only with a Christian man. Such is the outline of the whole work.

## CHAPTER I

## CHAPTER II

We certainly do not deny that the union of man and woman was blessed by the Lord [Note: [Col. 1277B] *God*, other editions], and was devised, as we know [Note: [Col. 1277B] “As a nursery for the human race,” Rigaltius], for the human race, the replenishment of the world, and the ordering of temporal life, and was thereafter permitted—yet only one such union. For Adam was Eve’s one husband, and Eve his one wife: one woman, one rib.

It is true that among our ancestors and the patriarchs themselves it was lawful not merely to marry, but even to enter marriages in several forms; there were concubines as well. But although this passed over figuratively [Note: [Col. 1277B] *A figure*, Junius, Franeker; *figuratively*, Rigaltius] into the synagogue and the Church [Note: [Col. 1277B] “Intervened in the synagogue as the Church,” Rigaltius], nevertheless—to interpret it simply—it was necessary to institute practices that would afterward deserve either to be cut away or to be moderated. For the law was going to supervene; the grounds for fulfilling the law had to run in advance. The same [Note: [Col. 1277C] *For the same*, Franeker, Paris; *likewise*, Rigaltius] Word of God was soon to come to the law’s aid by introducing spiritual circumcision. Thus, through the unrestricted license [Note: [Col. 1277C] “Unrestricted license”: multiple marriages permitted indiscriminately. RIGALTIUS] of that time, material was furnished in advance for the subsequent reforms, whose excesses the Lord afterward cut away by his Gospel and then, at the ends of the age, the Apostle either excised where redundant or arranged where disordered.

## CHAPTER III

Yet I would not for that reason have begun by discussing the freedom of antiquity and the correction [Col. 1278A] of later times, or have laid the foundation for claiming that Christ came to separate marriages and [Note: [Col. 1277C] *And* is absent; *abolish*, Rigaltius] destroy unions, as though I were here and now prescribing an end to marrying. RHENANUS]. They deny him who borrowed the woman from the male and then joined the two bodies—taken from a common share in the same substance—back into one through the compact [Note: [Col. 1277C] *Computation*, Rigaltius] of marriage.

In short, we nowhere read that marriage is altogether forbidden, since it is, of course, a good. Yet we learn from the Apostle what is better than that good: although he permits marriage [Note: [Col. 1277C] “Although he permits marriage.” Marriage is permitted on account of weakness, but abstinence is considered preferable to it; thus St. Ambrose, book 1 *On Virgins*: “She who marries is not censured; she who abstains is praised.” And Blessed Jerome, on the Apostle’s words, “But if you take a wife, you have not sinned,” says: “For it is one thing not to sin, another to do well.” LE PRIEUR], he gives preference to abstinence—the former because of the snares of temptation, the latter because of the constraints of the times. Once the rationale of each pronouncement is examined, it is readily discerned that the power to marry was granted to us by necessity; [Col. 1278B] and what necessity supplies is itself thereby depreciated. For, after all, Scripture says, “It is better to marry than to burn” (1 Cor. 7:9). What sort of good, I ask you, is one commended by comparison with an evil? Marriage is better only because it is worse to burn. Yet how much better it is neither to marry nor to burn!

Even [Note: [Col. 1277C] Semler and Leopold include *even*; others omit it] in persecutions [Note: [Col. 1277D] “Even in persecutions it is better, by permission, to flee from one town than, when arrested, to deny.” The well-known judgment of Paul writing to the Corinthians is that it is better to marry than to burn. Tertullian argues and contends that it is far better neither to marry nor to burn. To prove this, he employs a comparison with another maxim, which teaches that in persecutions it is better for a Christian to flee than to deny Christ; to this he immediately adds that it is far better neither to flee nor to deny.

This passage circulates in the printed copies in so corrupted a state that the force of the argument, being insufficiently understood, appears to droop. Pamelius printed it thus: “In persecutions it is better, by permission, to flee from town to town than, when compressed and twisted, to deny. But what blessedness is that—to escape the confession of blessed testimony for this reason, lest one deny? I can say: what is permitted is not good. Why must I die? If I weep, it is good; if I fear. What is permitted has a suspect reason for its permission.”

This passage greatly tormented Rhenanus, and he printed it in the following form, probably because the manuscripts pointed that way: “It is better, by permission, to flee from town to town than, when compressed and twisted, to deny—and by that fact it is blessed. Or what do those who deny lose by the confession of blessed testimony? I can say: what is permitted is not good. Why [Col. 1278D] must I die, if when I weep it is good; if I fear. What is permitted has a suspect reason for its permission.” These words are undoubtedly devoid of good sense.

Let us see whether the author’s meaning can be recovered with the aid of our manuscript. It reads: “In persecutions it is better to flee from a town than, when arrested and twisted, to deny; and therefore more blessed are those able to depart through the blessed confession of testimony. I can say that what is permitted is not good. Why must I die, if when I weep it is good? But if I fear what is permitted, it has a suspect reason for its permission.” So reads Agobard’s manuscript throughout. It too requires some assistance, but very little, in my opinion, as follows: “Even in persecutions it is better, by permission, to flee than, when arrested and twisted, to deny; and yet more blessed than that are those able not to fail in the blessed confession of testimony. I can say: what is permitted is not good. What then? I must die. If I weep, what is permitted is good. [Col. 1279C] But if I fear what is permitted, it has a suspect reason for its permission.”

Tertullian says that when Christians are sought by a tyrant in order to make them defect, they are indeed permitted to provide for their safety by flight, and that it is better to avail themselves of the permission and flee than to be arrested and driven by torture into apostasy. He then adds that it is far better to stand firm, await and endure the tyrant, hooks, and crosses, and not violate the sacramental pledge of faith given to Christ.

The stern censor continues and obstructs the flight he had said was permitted. “I can say,” he declares, “that what is permitted is not good. What then? The sword hangs over my neck, the furnace blazes, the instruments of torture crack: I must die. If, cowardly with weeping and wailing, [Col. 1279D] I beg to be spared torture and death, what is permitted—namely flight—is good. But if I fear the very thing that is permitted, because conscience dictates that flight from so blessed a death is unbecoming to a Christian, the very fact that my mind does indeed suggest permitted flight, though not without fear of disgrace, sufficiently proves that the reason for that permission is suspect: namely, a spirit insufficiently courageous and insufficiently tenacious of faith—the fault of weakness, for which evil flight was granted as a remedy. Thus, if it was good for someone to flee so as not to fall into the fault of weakness, it would certainly have been better not to flee, but, once arrested, bravely to endure torture and not deny.” RIGALTIUS] it is better [Note: [Col. 1278B] Thus the Codex Agobardinus supplied the text to Rigaltius, with the words “by permission” omitted; Rigaltius nevertheless restored: “and yet more blessed than that are those who—do not fail in the confession.” Rhenanus: “when compressed and twisted, to deny, and by that fact it is blessed. Certain things are not therefore to be sought merely because they are not forbidden, even though in a manner they are forbidden when others are preferred to them; for preferring higher things [Note: [Col. 1279B] Semler correctly conjectures that *higher things* was written instead of *higher*.—*And*, Franeker and Rigaltius; *of the weak*] is a dissuasion from weaker things [Note: [Col. 1279B] *Lower things*, Franeker, Rigaltius]. A thing is not good merely because it is not evil; nor is it therefore not evil [Note: [Col. 1279B] “It is not therefore good, nor is it therefore not evil,” Venetian edition] because it does no harm. Rather, that is plainly [Note: [Col. 1279C] *Fully*, Semler] the prior good which not only does no harm but, in addition, does benefit. Accordingly [Note: [Col. 1279C] *For*, Semler; “Accordingly, that,” Franeker, Paris; Rigaltius], you ought to prefer what benefits to what merely does no harm. For every contest strives toward first place; second place has consolation, but not victory.
