## § I

If the sure knowledge of the times at which the ancient teachers committed their books to writing had always been sought by learned men with an eagerness and diligence proportionate to its evident usefulness, I do not doubt that sacred history would today abound in far fewer difficulties and would be furnished with a more orderly arrangement. But our ancestors, who first wiped away the filth of barbarism, labored rather to restore the utterly prostrate condition of letters than to render them distasteful to themselves and others through what they considered minute details. A different mind indeed belonged to those who followed those divine restorers and who, with Scaliger, Baronius, Petavius, Calvisius, and others leading the way, are known to have made great progress in these studies. [Col. 0537C] Yet both the age of our fathers and our own have produced men of the highest distinction in this field: Noris, Pagi, Lloyd, Dodwell, Tillemont, Nourry, and many members of the Benedictine family who edited the writings of the Fathers. Although I detract nothing from the praises due to any of them, I nevertheless do not deny that they left many things undone; that they treated many subjects more learnedly than successfully; and that many matters, because they burned with zeal for a sect or excessively favored some novel opinion, were entangled rather than unraveled. If no other testimonies to this fact existed, the very writings in which they attack one another and the books in which they repeatedly establish new calculations would themselves speak.

Especially in the history of the calamities through which the earliest of our brethren passed, it would have been of great [Col. 0537D] benefit if the dates of the books which we understand to have been presented on their behalf to emperors and provincial governors, and of the other writings composed while those persecutions were raging, had been carefully determined. For in this way we would possess a surer and fuller knowledge of the sequence of events, the magnitude of the evils inflicted, the number of confessors, and the unconquered strength of mind displayed by those most holy men. No one whose judgment has reached maturity can fail to recognize the remarkable utility which such knowledge can afford in every case.

I certainly wonder that, when Henry Dodwell—a man otherwise worthy of immortality—was perhaps with good intentions expelling whole armies of martyrs from the sacred commonwealth, none of those who came forward against him exercised himself in this arena. [Col. 0538A] Yet from this arena something could have been obtained than which nothing can be more certain or more useful for breaking down such boldness. So long as the words of the Fathers conflict with passages in other authors, an opportunity for escape will almost always be available. If no other refuge is at hand, either some stigma is branded upon the witnesses or some new kind of criticism is summoned to bring aid to a desperate cause. But if the intervals of time are carefully marked, and the order of successive events diligently explained, one must either incur the disgrace of brazen effrontery or else renounce one’s error.

In truth, learned men have been wanting in this cause: whether because they fled from the vexation inherent in such business, because they did not perceive its exceptional advantage clearly enough, or finally because they thought that they should take account not so much of the circumstances as of the events themselves. There were indeed some, [Col. 0538B] I admit, who chose to undertake part of this labor. Yet I do not know how it happened that they acquitted themselves either too carelessly or too unsuccessfully. It may have been haste, weariness, partisan zeal, or something else I cannot identify; but it is certain that there was something which caused them sometimes to reckon their calculations very badly.

For these reasons, although I feel myself far inferior to them, I began some time ago to form the plan of rolling this stone, and I conceived the idea of a new history of the Christian afflictions—one that would be distinguished, among other things, particularly by a more diligent notation of dates. A specimen was provided not long ago by our *Dissertation on the Date of Athenagoras* [Note: [Col. 0538D] It appears in the theological, historical, and philological library published at Bremen, vol. II, fascicle.], and a reading of it will attest that I inflicted considerable [Col. 0538C] damage upon the arguments of the celebrated Dodwell. Although thorny, the labor also enjoyed the good fortune of not displeasing those whose authority carries the greatest weight with me and whom all prudent men acknowledge as discerning judges of these matters.

I have therefore resolved now to test my strength again and to attempt whether I might, with the same dexterity, discover the date of the celebrated *APOLOGY* left to us by TERTULLIAN, the most ingenious of the Fathers. I was moved to impose this burden upon myself sooner than I had intended by the most elegant edition of that *APOLOGY*, which we owe to the labors of Sigibert Havercamp, a man of extraordinary learning and among my closest friends. When I saw it endowed with such brilliance, fortified with such varied and elegant learning, and splendid and spotless in every part, [Col. 0538D] I immediately wished above all that not only this work but also the more important books of the other Fathers might appear in the same dress. For I judged that, if a great part of the weariness associated with these studies were removed in this fashion, sacred antiquity would no longer be found almost abandoned and bereft of cultivators.

There was nevertheless something I missed in so polished a work: namely, an inquiry into its date and circumstances. I understood, indeed, that the most learned man promised to meet this desideratum also in the preliminary material to be prefixed to TERTULLIAN’s remaining books against the nations (*Preface*). [Col. 0539A] Yet so far was I from resting content with these promises that they instead spurred me to relieve him of this burden. For I immediately reasoned with myself as follows: “The wait for the works he is preparing will be shorter if even a very small part of the labor is transferred to someone else.” Added weight was given to these reflections by the encouragement of that excellent man, which drove me, still hesitant, to complete what I was attempting. Why say more? I immediately set aside my other work, applied my hand to this labor, and committed to paper what I present here.

It will now be for you to judge, most learned HAVERCAMP, whether my little effort is worth enough to allow you to dispense with the trouble of composing a new dissertation of this kind. But if my work should please you less, you will approve my intention—I know your fairness. And what was that intention? To serve the interests of learning, to explain the history [Col. 0539B] of the evils heaped upon the Christians under the emperor SEVERUS, and—if you permit this addition—to leave you somewhat freer to complete other works. Though perhaps rash in a man who is not exceedingly learned, it is nevertheless honorable. If I deserve less well of letters and of TERTULLIAN, I can in no way appear to have deserved ill of you. The common people, of course, judge plans by their outcome; good men judge them by their motive.

## § II

When, as was proper, I surveyed what learned men before me had thought about this same matter, I found it entirely abandoned by some and handled with little skill by others. Generally speaking, I saw disagreements and divisions of opinion reigning here as well. I heard something asserted by certain men, but in such a way that they forgot to provide reasons. I observed others sitting more diligently over their calculations, [Col. 0539C] though their efforts were not attended by equal success. I saw that very many men of great name had preferred to follow leaders rather than torment themselves over these reckonings.

Even this variety of opinions moved me to examine which one accorded with the truth. I shall attempt to cast as much light upon the matter as its intricate nature permits, and for that reason I shall first establish two classes of opinion. Let the year 200 of the era be the boundary separating the two. One class, therefore, will comprise those who seek the birth date of our book before that year or in that year itself. The other will comprise those who judge that it was composed after the completion of that two-hundredth year. I shall discuss the latter first, since it is both further removed from the truth and more easily refuted. [Col. 0539D] The former, whose arguments demand greater labor and must be drawn from the very hidden places of history, I shall reserve for later.

## § III

[Col. 0539D] Among those whom we have said must be heard first, let William Cave, a man of the greatest reputation and industry, give his opinion. He contends for the year 202. “It seems more probable to me,” he says (*Literary History of Ecclesiastical Writers*, vol. I, p. 42b, Geneva ed.), “that it was written during the sixth persecution, about the year 202, when SEVERUS forbade anyone to become a Christian under a severe penalty.” To this he adds certain things by way of proof which I would repeat if I had any desire to harass that good man. One part tends to show that the Christians’ peace was indeed disturbed during SEVERUS’s reign; another shows that the evils inflicted upon them were not slight. [Col. 0540A] Yet since all these points have never been called into doubt by anyone, I am wholly at a loss to understand how they confirm the date he undertook to defend.

It would therefore have been better to omit the conclusion he appends: “Accordingly, we can assign the birth year of this work to no other epoch with a greater appearance of truth.” Its foundation is situated not in everything he had premised, but in this point alone: that before the year 202 SEVERUS did not trouble the Christians’ fortunes by his decrees. How slippery and infirm this is will soon become evident. TERTULLIAN certainly wrote when the brethren were already beset on every side by evils. Nevertheless, that is not sufficient unless it is also demonstrated that he did not take pen in hand before the emperor’s edicts were promulgated. If the contrary can be shown, [Col. 0540B] this entire computation is to be valued at nothing.

Nor did the learned man attend to this point: even if his arguments possessed great force, the date of the book would nevertheless have to be deferred rather to the year 203. For only when everything had already been ablaze for some time with the slaughter and punishment of Christians did our author emerge as the defender of his sect. It is therefore not sufficiently credible that this defense was committed to writing at the very beginning of the calamities, in the same year in which they arose.

Henry Dodwell, however, was not remote from Cave’s view. For although, so far as I know, he nowhere states openly what he thinks about the date of the *APOLOGY*, nevertheless what he intended can be gathered without any dispute from his discussion of the persecutions of Christians under SEVERUS. He persuades himself that before the year 202 no one had disturbed the peace [Col. 0540C] of the sacred assembly, and he endeavors to establish this by certain arguments which we shall presently examine (*Cyprianic Dissertations*, XI, § 41, p. 68, Bremen ed.). Thus it befitted one to whom nothing seemed less tolerable than an abundance of martyrs.

If, therefore, as is beyond doubt, TERTULLIAN addressed the governors of the provinces only after the calamities had begun to increase, it is also certain, according to Dodwell’s opinion, that this was done after the year 202 or toward its close. And why is there any need for conjecture? More explicit words of the learned man are available: “Hence,” he says (*Dissertation* cited, § 42, p. 69), “at precisely this time”—he is speaking of the year 202—“or perhaps even somewhat later, there appeared that new representation, as TERTULLIAN calls it, of the Christians’ God, with this inscription: ‘The Christians’ God, *onochoites*, with ass’s ears, [Col. 0540D] one hoofed foot, carrying a book, and wearing a toga.’” The passage to which he appeals is found in the *APOLOGY* (chapter XVI, p. 169 of the edition of the celebrated Havercamp, which I use throughout), the work presently under discussion. What could show more clearly that Dodwell agreed with Cave and others about the date of the book?

Today this man’s opinion concerning the small number of martyrs has spread more widely and taken possession of the minds of very many learned men. There can therefore be no doubt that this opinion concerning the date of the *APOLOGY* has become fixed in many minds. Yet since their leaders have been identified, I do not think it necessary to review them at greater length here.

I turn instead to Antoine Pagi, who, although he spent almost his entire life among chronological calculations, nevertheless committed himself to the same opinion. [Col. 0541A] When he composed his *Hypatic Dissertation*, no one judged better than he did, for he expressly pronounced in favor of the year 198. Hear his words (*Hypatic Dissertation*, part II, chapter III, § 12, p. 117; Lyon, 1682, quarto):

> TERTULLIAN composed his *APOLOGY* in the year 198, when SEVERUS celebrated his quinquennalia and departed from Rome, for which reason he addressed it to the Roman magistrates. For although no edict against the Christians had yet been promulgated by that emperor, Plautianus nevertheless, by whose authority everything at Rome was conducted, was beginning to persecute the Christians.

It is certainly excellent that he recognizes that no law against the Christians had then been enacted by the emperor. What he adds about Plautianus he owes to a conjecture of Baronius (*Ecclesiastical Annals*, vol. II, at the year 200, nos. VII–VIII, p. 198; Cologne ed., 1624, folio), [Col. 0541B] which he himself later demolished (*Critique on the Annals of Baronius*, vol. II, at the year 199, no. IV, p. 92). His proof is vague and overly general, and could also fit other years in which we know that Severus was absent from the city. But let it have been altogether too weak: I nevertheless wish the learned man had persisted in that opinion.

When correcting Baronius, however, he judged that he must reject it and transferred the date of the *APOLOGY* to the year 205. “The persecution of the Christians,” you hear Pagi say (at the year 199, no. VI, p. 93), “was not decreed before the year 202. Therefore, since Tertullian wrote his *Apology* while persecution had already been raging everywhere for a long time, as is gathered from the *Apology* itself, that most excellent work was published much later—namely, as one may conjecture, in the year 205, when Antoninus celebrated the tenth anniversary [Col. 0541C] of his imperial power and when the persecution raged more fiercely.”

No one ever changed his mind under worse auspices. While wishing to correct another man’s slighter error, he polluted himself with a graver one. I know, indeed, that when our author composed his work, nations estranged from the worship of the true Deity were raging against the Christians. But what conclusion follows from this? “Therefore the book was written after Severus issued his decrees.” As if he had been so ignorant of events as not to know that our brethren had often been tortured under the authority of previously enacted laws. Or, if this fact had been less clearly known to him, as if this very *Apology* could not have taught him that it happened at that time. But I shall now discuss this matter more fully.

## § IV

We have seen that those who wish the book to have been committed to writing after the year 202 were chiefly misled by [Col. 0541D] their belief that the Christians’ affairs remained entirely peaceful until that year. This conviction arose from their having learned from many testimonies that, in the year I have named, decrees against the Christians were proposed by SEVERUS. The weakness of this reasoning was evidently not apparent to them: “In the year 202 SEVERUS enacted laws against the Christians; therefore they could not have been persecuted before that time.”

I am readily willing, however, to except Dodwell here, since he also took refuge in other subtleties because he perceived that no protection for his position lay in this argument. Since matters stand thus, there will be no surer way to refute these men than to demonstrate that, long before the emperor’s laws were enacted, very many evils had already weighed upon the Christians’ necks.

This whole issue was passed over by Thierry [Col. 0542A] Ruinart, who undertook to correct Dodwell. He bears it heavily and with great vexation that Dodwell compresses the entire affliction into the span of a single two-year period (*Preface to the Authentic Acts of the Martyrs*, section II, § 12, p. 48, Wetstein ed.). Yet he adduces against him only more general considerations, for which the latter might perhaps have found parallels had he sought them. You will look in vain for the more diligent investigation and precise notation of dates which the nature of the matter uniquely demands.

Pagi applied greater industry, since he possessed extensive knowledge of these matters (*Critique on the Annals of Baronius*, vol. III, at the year 201, no. VII, p. 2, and at the year 207, no. III, p. 7). Nevertheless, he could not touch this point because, as I have shown, he did not differ from his opponent in assigning the beginning of the affliction. [Col. 0542B] He was therefore occupied solely in demonstrating that from the year 202 until the death of SEVERUS peace was not restored to the holy assembly.

What he and many others left unattempted, I shall now undertake to complete. We are not at present concerned with the precise year that gave rise to these evils. It will be possible to derive it without great difficulty from what we shall establish beyond doubt concerning the date of the *APOLOGY*. My sole purpose here is to demonstrate that, before the year 202—in which I concede that SEVERUS’s decrees against the Christians were published—an immense mass of afflictions had been inflicted upon them.

Let our own TERTULLIAN hold first place among the witnesses. Let him give testimony from the very book whose date we are determining here. It appeared—as no one denies—when Christians were already being put to death by the most cruel punishments. [Col. 0542C] Yet in more than one passage he explicitly indicates that nothing at all had then been heard concerning edicts of the emperor.

I now pass over a conjecture, though I deny that it deserves contempt: namely, that the work’s being addressed to the governors of the provinces is evidence that they alone, and not the emperor, were the authors of the evils. The matter will be decided by more evident testimonies.

Let this be the first (chapter XXXV, p. 300):

> Nor are there any who more loudly demand the Christians than the common people. Plainly, the other orders, in proportion to their rank, are religious out of loyalty: nothing hostile breathes from the senate itself, from the equestrian order, from the camps, or from the palaces themselves.

I certainly do not know what could make more evidently for our position. He excludes the palaces themselves, in which the emperor resided with his sons, from the number of the Christians’ enemies. [Col. 0542D] With what effrontery could he have written this if the laws already possessed authority for persecuting Christians?

The entire discussion concerning laws found in chapters IV, V, and VI confirms the same point clearly. The Christians’ opponents advanced the laws which commanded that they be treated with cruelty. But what laws? Those recently issued? By no means. They were continually invoking edicts enacted by emperors now dead, which they said they were not permitted to violate. The acute author splendidly meets these feeble little arguments. Before everything else, he demonstrates by reasoning that no law is so sacred that it must not immediately be abolished if it contains any injustice. He then shows that this principle is not lacking in examples, since the Romans had removed all authority from even the most ancient decrees once they recognized their injustice, and SEVERUS himself [Col. 0543A] had abrogated the Papian law.

“Do not you also,” he says (chapter IV [the source prints “XV”], p. 49), “daily, as experience casts light upon the darkness of antiquity, prune and cut down that entire old and squalid forest of laws with the new axes of imperial rescripts and edicts?” Observe, however, which laws are under discussion. They are ancient laws, as is evident, to which he opposes new ones.

He proceeds to the authors of the laws against the Christians (chapter V, p. 56), all of whom he contends were marked by the infamy of shameful and criminal acts and were exceedingly like Nero. I do not inquire how widely this assertion extends. It is enough for me that it makes clear that he did not regard SEVERUS as belonging to the number of those who had enacted decrees against the Christians. The logic of the matter itself is witness to this. For who would be so foolish as to persuade himself [Col. 0543B] that a wise man would have attacked the laws by this argument if it were established that the man who then possessed supreme power had bound himself by the same crime? A charming defender indeed—one who would number the emperor whose mind he wished to soften among the vilest of mortals, surpassed by none in wickedness!

On the contrary, our author praises SEVERUS several times and calls him the most steadfast of emperors (chapter V, p. 52). So far was he from thinking that Severus had ordered the sword to be drawn against the brethren.

But let TERTULLIAN explain himself, as he addresses the Romans in these words (chapter V, p. 62):

> Yet from among all the emperors since then down to the present—men wise in divine and human matters—produce a single assailant of the Christians.

Who is that “present” emperor? SEVERUS, I think. Yet he denies that Severus should be numbered [Col. 0543C] among the assailants of the Christians. What could be clearer?

I pass over the famous passage from the same dissertation (chapter V, pp. 63, 65):

> What kind of laws, then, are these, which only the impious execute against us—which no Hadrian, no Vespasian, no Pius, no Verus enforced?

There are manuscripts which read *Severus* here. But I agree with you, most learned Havercamp, and with the distinguished men who, with you and through the benefit of better manuscripts, restored *Verus* for us. Although Dodwell disapproves of this reading (*Cyprianic Dissertations*, XI, § 44, p. 69), he nevertheless could not deny that the names Severus and Verus are frequently confused in ancient monuments.
