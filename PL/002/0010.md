So far is he from bringing upon his own head an offering to idolatry—[Col. 0098A] indeed, I would now say, to Christ, since “the head of the man is Christ” (1 Cor. 11:3)—a head as free as Christ himself, subject not even to a covering, much less to a binding. Moreover, that which is subject to a covering, the woman’s head, is already occupied by that very covering and has no room for an additional binding; it bears the burden of its humility. If she ought not to be seen with her head uncovered because of the angels, much more, perhaps, would she then scandalize those crowned ones by wearing a crown (1 Cor. 11:10).

[Note: [Col. 0097C] “Much more, perhaps, would she then scandalize those crowned ones.” He alludes to those twenty-four elders crowned with golden crowns, Rev. 4:4. If a woman with uncovered head scandalizes the angels, much [Col. 0097D] more would she with crowned head scandalize those crowned elders. RIG.]

For what is a crown upon a woman’s head but a procuress of beauty, the supreme badge of wantonness, the uttermost denial of modesty, and a compound of enticement? For this reason, too, from the Apostle’s point of view a woman will not be adorned too elaborately, so that she may not even be crowned by an artful arrangement of her hair. Yet he who is both the man’s head and the woman’s countenance (1 Tim. 2:5), Christ Jesus, what garland, I ask you, underwent on behalf of both sexes (Matt. [Col. 0098B] 27:29)? One, I think, of thorns and thistles, as a figure of the sins which the soil of our flesh brought forth for us, but which the power of the cross removed, blunting every sting of death through the endurance of the Lord’s head (Isa. 53:5). Certainly, apart from the figure, the insult is manifest, and the shame and dishonor, together with the savagery entwined in them, which then both befouled and lacerated the Lord’s temples.

This was so that you might now be crowned with laurel, myrtle, olive, some splendid foliage, and—what is more serviceable—with hundred-petaled roses gathered from the garden of Midas,

[Note: [Col. 0097D] “And—what is more serviceable—with hundred-petaled roses gathered from the garden of Midas.” Rhenanus’ first edition reproduced the reading of the Dijon manuscript: *Centauriis quoque roris et hortomi delectis*, which no one doubts is corrupt. The manuscript of Agobard, eaten away in this section, preserved only: *et quod magis v . . . . narint quoq. rosis de horto lectis*. Greater credence should indeed be given to this reading, since that manuscript is on parchment far older than the Dijon copies. We therefore follow Rhenanus’ conjecture and judge that it should be read: *Centenariis quoque rosis de horro Midae lectis*—“with hundred-petaled roses gathered from the garden of Midas”—which comes closer to that ancient reading. Tertullian called the roses “hundred-petaled” from their hundred petals, just as in the *Apologeticum*, p. 7, he speaks of “hundred-thousand-sesterce dinners,” and in the book *On the Pallium*, p. 138, of “platters of a hundredweight.” Most appropriately, moreover, he said that a crown made from roses—a thing for sight and breath, [Col. 0098C] that is, delighting the eyes by its color and the nostrils by its scent—is more serviceable than one made from laurel, myrtle, olive, or any other splendid foliage, which feeds only the eyes. RIG.]

and with lilies of both kinds,

[Note: [Col. 0098C] “And with lilies of both kinds”: white and purple. Concerning these there are famous verses in Ovid, *Metamorphoses* X, where he speaks of Hyacinthus:

> Brighter than Tyrian purple  
> A flower arises and assumes the form of lilies, were not  
> The color purple in these, and silver in those.

The poets therefore tell how purple lilies were born from Hyacinthus’ blood; moreover, they bear the letters AI, AI, marks of mourning and lamentation. But as for the common tradition that those lilies are meant in that very well-known verse of Virgil, “Tell me in what lands,” etc., if we may be permitted a short digression, we shall demonstrate that it is false. And why should it not be permitted to weave ribbons into Tertullian’s little work *On the Crown*?

I certainly laugh at the grammarians clinging to the cross which Virgil, jesting with Asconius Pedianus, revealed that he himself had once set up for them. And who would not laugh at Servius, who, transmitting that story [Col. 0098D] from Asconius, perversely torments himself with the most elaborate trifles?

There are pastoral contests in *Eclogue* III, where Damoetas and Menalcas alternately test against one another what each can achieve in song; at last, wearied by competition, they conclude their humorous quarrel by submitting the dispute to Palaemon’s judgment. Damoetas asks Menalcas:

> Tell me in what lands—and you shall be my great Apollo—  
> The breadth of heaven extends no more than three cubits.

Menalcas strikes back at Damoetas with a similar question:

> Tell me in what lands flowers grow  
> Inscribed with kings’ names; and have Phyllis for yourself alone.

Damoetas had asked where on earth heaven extended only three cubits. Certainly no such place exists, nor can it happen according to the nature of the universe that anywhere on earth the breadth of the heavens above the earth should be contained within a space of three cubits. Menalcas therefore does not solve the proposed question but refutes it with another: “Tell me,” [Col. 0099A] he says, “where on earth flowers grow inscribed with the names of kings?”—of Romulus, that is, Numa, Tullus, Tarquin, and others. Flowers of that kind certainly grow nowhere.

In the third book *Against the Academics*, Augustine says that this is called a Tuscan quarrel, when a proposed question appears to be met not by its solution but by the counterproposal of another. The former jest, “from impossibilities” (*ἐκ τῶν ἀδυνάτων*), is wittily refuted by the latter, “from non-existent things” (*ἐκ τῶν οὐκ ὄντων*).

And perhaps, at the same time, Virgil expressed his longing for the recently lost Roman republic—Suetonius reports that Augustus even entertained plans for restoring it—concealing it beneath the simple jests of rustic men, under this guise: flowers inscribed with the name of kings, whom the Roman people abhorred through hatred of tyrannical license, grow no more truly than there can be found a region of the earth where the [Col. 0099B] breadth of heaven extends only three cubits.

There is no reason to suspect that something more learned is concealed here; nor am I credulous toward the grammarians’ comments, as though Virgil himself had revealed to Cornificius or Asconius that his intention in the verse,

> The breadth of heaven extends no more than three cubits,

was to lash a certain Coelius of Mantua. I do not think so frigid a jest ever entered Virgil’s mind. And the writings about Virgil by those whom they produce as witnesses to their story of Coelius—Asconius and Cornificius, of course—have perished.

“But they are cited by Philargyrius; they are cited by Servius.” We shall presently discuss Philargyrius. Certainly, in the extant Servian material, the passage reads precisely as follows: “Asconius Pedianus says that he heard Virgil say that in this passage he had set up a cross for the grammarians, wishing to test which of them would be found more learned; intending Coelius of Mantua to be understood.”

First, then, what confidence can be placed in a grammarian who offers us these trifles along [Col. 0099C] with solecisms? Next, how absurd is: “wishing to test which of them would be found more learned.” Who could believe that Asconius ever spoke in this way? No: from the Philargyrian material it should be read: “Pedianus says that he heard Virgil say that in this passage he had set up a cross for the grammarians, who would ask whether anything more learned was concealed there.” This, certainly, is Asconius—and nothing further. But some petty grammarian immediately added from his own invention: “The poet, however, means Coelius of Mantua.” In the Servian material too, “intending Coelius of Mantua to be understood” was added by the same hand.

Now the Philargyrian material which we possess is generally purer than the Servian; but here, at any rate, it was interpolated with no greater wisdom. For on the verse, “The breadth of heaven extends three,” etc., it has this note: “Among the ancients there was a very deep well, into which they descended to perform sacred rites. The circle of this well—that is, its uppermost rim, from which they ascertained the year’s yield—measured no more than three cubits.” These are sheer ravings and the dreams of a sick man; no sounder are the explanations that follow concerning a cavern [Col. 0099D] near Etna, or “heaven” meaning the entire world, or “the breadth of heaven” meaning the shield of Achilles. After trifles of this sort, who could promise himself anything better thereafter?

It continues: “Cornificius says that he heard from Virgil himself that he touched upon a certain Coelius of Mantua, who, having exhausted all his resources, left himself nothing except a plot of three cubits for burial. Likewise, Ovid Naso: *torificium*—only so much heaven seems open as the *torificium* is open.” I do not see what good anyone can fashion from these scraps of foolishness.

I rather embrace the opinion of those who judge that there is nothing of Cornificius here, but that the name slipped in through similarity of sound with the word *torificii*, or *orificii*, occurring shortly afterward. And we have already demonstrated from the Servian and Philargyrian materials, whatever their quality may be, that it cannot rightly be inferred that Asconius transmitted something about Coelius of Mantua which he had received from Virgil. What? Did this become known to Cornificius but not also to Asconius, who was among the eminent poet’s intimates and who published a book [Col. 0100A] against his detractors?

Let us therefore reject that stale tale about Coelius of Mantua, admitting only the final note of Philargyrius: “Asconius Pedianus says that he heard Virgil say that in this passage he had set up a cross for the grammarians, who would ask whether anything more learned was concealed there.” What follows—“The poet, however, means Coelius of Mantua”—is no longer Asconius’, but some foolish glossographer’s.

So much for the breadth of heaven. As for the flowers inscribed with the names of kings, the grammarians are mistaken here as well, though much more simply. They all agree in thinking that the hyacinth flower is meant, born first from the blood of that Hyacinthus whom Apollo loved, and afterward from Ajax’s blood. They take these men for kings, although neither answers to the title of king. That is the first point.

Next, [Col. 0100B] no poet’s boldness in inventing myths ever went so far as to say that Hyacinthus’ name was written upon that flower. “Read the Hyacinthus,” says Philostratus, “for it is inscribed” (*Ἀναγνώθι τὸν Ὑάκινθον … γέγραπται* [text uncertain]). But what is read upon it? A king’s name? The name of Hyacinthus? By no means. It is the opening of mourning and lamentation: AI, AI. Thus Ovid, where he speaks of Apollo mourning the boy:

> He himself inscribes his groans upon the petals, and AI AI  
> The flower bears inscribed, and the mournful letter is traced.

Moschus, in the *Epitaph for Bion*:

> Now, Hyacinthus, speak your letters, and yet more AI, AI  
> Take upon your petals.  
> (*Νῦν, Ὑάκινθε, λάλει τὰ σὰ γράμματα, καὶ πλέον AI, AI / Λάμβανε τοῖς πετάλοισι.*)

It is therefore established that what is read upon the hyacinth flower is not Hyacinthus’ name but the beginning of a mournful song, “the prelude to the lament” (*προοίμιον τῆς παρηγορίας* [reading uncertain]), as Palaephatus says, or “the letters belonging to the dirge” (*τὰ ἐπὶ τῷ θρήνῳ γράμματα*), as Pausanias says.

Hence, in this Virgilian verse,

> Tell me in what lands flowers grow  
> Inscribed with kings’ names,

those who wish the names of Hyacinthus and Ajax to be meant [Col. 0100C] must now allow the name of one of their kings to be struck out. They must henceforth defend themselves by Ajax alone and say that Virgil used the plural “names of kings” for “a royal name,” and called a man king who was never king. That this is Virgilian seems to me scarcely probable.

But what if we also strike Ajax’s name from the flower? Ovid appears to stand in the way when he says that a letter common to Hyacinthus and Ajax, boy and man, had been inscribed upon the petals—a mournful letter, that is, or a lamenting inscription, AI, AI—so that for Hyacinthus it is a sign or letter of lamentation, but for Ajax a letter of his name. Yet it will not be difficult to remove this objection if we reflect that those letters denoted mourning or lamentation before Ajax; indeed, that even in Ajax’s case they are employed upon those flowers only by way of mourning, since Ajax’s very name signifies lamentation. From this came Ovid’s opportunity to prophesy that the time would come when the bravest hero would add his mournful name to this already mournful flower. It thus follows that nothing mournful is read upon the Hyacinthus [the source reading is contextually suspect], and certainly nothing [Col. 0100D] royal.

Pliny, book XXI, chapter 11: “The hyacinth is attended by the story of the mourning for the man whom Apollo loved, and of Ajax, its veins running in such a way that the shape of the Greek letters AI, AI, is read inscribed upon it.”

On the mournful name of Ajax, Ajax himself says in Sophocles:

> AI AI! Who would ever have thought that my name  
> Would so accord by name with my misfortunes?  
> For now it is possible for me to cry *aiai* twice  
> And three times.  
> (*AI AI, τίς ἄν ποτ᾽ ᾤεθ᾽ ὧδ᾽ ἐπώνυμον / Τοὐμὸν ξυνοίσειν ὄνομα τοῖς ἐμοῖς κακοῖς; / Νῦν γὰρ πάρεστι καὶ δὶς αἰάζειν ἐμοὶ / Καὶ τρίς.*)

Thus, in the interpreter of Theocritus, *Idyll* X, Euphorion, in mourning for the descendant of Aeacus, addresses the purple hyacinth as “wailing through what is written upon it” (*γεγραμμένα κωκύουσαν*). He was reading upon the hyacinth not the royal name of the descendant of Aeacus, but the mournful song AI AI.

Nor do I think the same letters, AI, should be understood in any other sense upon an ancient gem which displays the image of a human figure changing into a flower and which is kept by that most eminent man Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc. From these considerations, I think, it will become sufficiently clear how empty is the conjecture of [Col. 0101B] those who suppose that what is read inscribed in Greek letters, AI AI, upon the purple lily—the poets’ hyacinth—constitutes “the names of kings” implicitly meant by the little verse of Virgil’s shepherd. RIG.—“With lilies of both kinds and with all violets.” Thus at the end of the book *On the Spectacles* he said that the spectacles were more delightful than “either seating section and every stadium.” The white lilies belong to confessors, the purple to martyrs. RIG.]

[Col. 0099A] and with every kind of violet; perhaps even with gems and gold, so that you may also emulate that crown of Christ [Col. 0100A] which afterward came to him, since he also tasted honeycomb after gall (Luke 24:42). Nor was he greeted by the heavenly beings as King of Glory before he had been condemned to the cross as King of the Jews; first made by the Father a little lower than the angels, and thus crowned with glory and honor (Ps. 8:6; Heb. 2:7).

If because of these things you owe him your head, repay him, if you can, with one such as he offered for yours; or else do not be crowned with flowers if you cannot be crowned with thorns, because you cannot be crowned with flowers.

## CHAPTER XV

Keep God’s own possession undefiled for him. He will crown it if he wishes—indeed, he does wish to; in fact, he invites you: “He who conquers,” he says, “I will give him the crown of life.” You too must “be faithful unto death” (Rev. 2:10). You too must “fight the good contest,” whose crown the Apostle rightly trusts has been laid up for him (2 Tim. 4:7–8).

The angel too receives the crown of victory as he goes forth upon a white horse to conquer (Rev. 6:2). Another is adorned with the encircling rainbow (Rev. 10: [Col. 0101B] 1), a meadow of heaven.

[Note: [Col. 0101B] “And another was adorned with the encircling rainbow, a meadow of heaven.” So the old copy reads. Septimius elegantly and appropriately called the rainbow a heavenly meadow because of its appearance, green like an emerald. For so they are described in the Apocalypse: “and a rainbow round about the throne, in appearance like an emerald” (*καὶ ἶρις κυκλόθεν τοῦ θρόνου ὁμοία ὁράσει σμαραγδίνῳ*). RIG.]

The elders also sit crowned (Rev. 4:4), and with the same gold the Son of Man himself shines above the cloud (Rev. 14:14). If such are the images in vision, what will the realities be when made present? Gaze upon those; smell those.

Why do you condemn your head to a little wreath or a *dracontarium* [Col. 0102A] when it is destined for a diadem?

[Note: [Col. 0101C] “Your head to a little wreath or a *dracontarium*.” The ancients used thinner crowns, Pliny says, calling them *strophia*, whence *strophiola*. Furthermore, *dracontarium* appears to have been a word of that age for a little crown. [Col. 0102B] RHEN.—I, however, think *dracontarium* means a crown made either from the plant *dracontia*, an antidote against poisons mentioned by Pliny, book XXIV, chapter 19, or from the *draconitis* gems discussed by the same Pliny, book XXXVII, chapter 10. For Christ Jesus has also made us kings to God and his Father (Rev. 1:6; 5:10).

[Note: [Col. 0102C] “For he has also made us kings to God and his Father.” Septimius very frequently insists upon the exalted dignity of the Christian family, which Justin also extolled splendidly against Trypho the Jew: “We are God’s true high-priestly race” (*ἀρχιερατικὸν τὸ ἀληθινὸν γένος ἐσμὲν τοῦ Θεοῦ*).]

What have you to do with a flower destined to die? You have the flower from the stem of Jesse, upon whom the whole grace of the divine Spirit rested (Isa. 11:1), a flower incorruptible, unfading, everlasting. By choosing him, the good soldier too

[Note: [Col. 0102C] “The good soldier too.” He is speaking about that soldier who had carried the crown in his hand rather than upon his head and was dragged to prison for that reason. RHEN.]

advanced in the heavenly order through divine favor. Blush, his fellow soldiers: you are now to be judged not by him but by some soldier of Mithras. When that soldier is initiated in a cavern—in a camp that is truly one of darkness—a crown is offered to him upon the point of a sword, as though in a parody of martyrdom; it is then fitted to his head, but he is instructed to push it away from his head with his hand and, if possible, transfer it to his shoulder, saying that Mithras is his crown. From that time onward he is never crowned, and he possesses this as his token of authentication [Col. 0102B] if he is ever tested concerning his sacrament: he is immediately believed to be a soldier of Mithras if he has cast away the crown and declared that it is in his god. Let us recognize the devil’s ingenious devices: he lays claim to certain things belonging to God precisely so that he may confound and judge us through the faithfulness of his own servants.

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIANUS ON FLIGHT IN PERSECUTION (C)

## ARGUMENT

[Col. 0101C]

—The book *On Flight in Persecution*, written in the year 202, when persecution was already raging, is still a sign of Tertullian’s very obstinate disposition. [Col. 0101D] In it he openly upholds the Paraclete of Montanus, although he does not yet assail Catholics as “Psychics.”

Not implausibly, recent critics judge this treatise to be the same work which, in *On the Crown*, chapter 1, he promised to publish *On Questions Arising from Confessions*, namely, against pastors who abandoned their flock during times of persecution. A dispute having arisen among Christians as to whether it was permissible during persecution to flee, or to buy off harassment with gold or silver, Tertullian, after discussing something of this question orally, was questioned by a certain intimate acquaintance named Fabius and eventually composed this treatise. In it he attempts at length to prove against the orthodox that God’s ministers must neither flee during persecution nor buy off persecution [Col. 0102C] by paying money. Among other things he argues as follows:

“If it is established from whom persecution comes, [Col. 0102D] we can now take up your question and determine from this very discussion that one must not flee during persecution. For if persecution comes from God, a twofold reason opposes flight: because what comes from God ought neither to be avoided nor can be escaped. It ought not to be avoided, because it is good; for everything that has seemed good to God must necessarily be good.”

—I am reluctant to pursue at greater length the other things which he adds in confirmation of his opinion. Anyone can easily understand that Tertullian is raving there with so foolish an argument, which is most openly contradicted by Christ’s example, by the Gospel in which flight is commanded during persecution, and by the authority of holy men. LUMP.

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 0103A]

You recently asked, brother Fabius,

[Note: [Col. 0103D] “Brother Fabius.” He calls him brother here by the name common among Christians. Who he was, however, is uncertain. LE PR.]

whether or not one ought to flee during persecution, because some report or other was being announced. There on the spot I replied with something suited to the place and time; but, because of the importunity of certain persons, I carried the only half-treated subject away with me, and shall now report it more fully in writing, since both your inquiry had commended it to me

[Note: [Col. 0103D] “To him,” Semler and Oberthür; “to me,” Rhenanus; “and which,” Junius.]

and the present condition of the times had now imposed it on its own account. For the more frequently persecutions loom over us, the more necessary it is to conduct an examination of how faith ought to receive them.

The examination, however, must be conducted by you who, by not accepting the Paraclete as the guide into all truth, are perhaps deservedly still liable to other questions as well. Therefore [Col. 0103B] we also impose an order upon your inquiry, perceiving that it is first necessary to determine the status of persecution itself: whether it comes from God or from the devil, so that how it should be met may more readily be established. For the examination of every matter is clearer once its author is known.

It is indeed sufficient to lay down as a preliminary rule that nothing happens without God’s will. Yet we shall not immediately, by this proposition, give scope to the other reconsiderations, lest we be drawn away from the present issue if someone replies: “Then evil too is from God, and sin is from God; nothing now belongs to the devil, and nothing even to ourselves.”

The present question concerns persecution. Concerning this, for the moment, I would say that nothing happens without God’s will, considering, first of all, that persecution is worthy of God and, so to speak, necessary—namely, for the approval or rejection of his servants. For what is the outcome [Col. 0103C] of persecution, what other effect does it have, except the approval and rejection of faith, by which the Lord surely examined his own?

Persecution is all an act of judgment, through which a person is adjudged either approved or rejected. Judgment, moreover, belongs to God alone. Persecution is that winnowing shovel

[Note: [Col. 0103D] “That winnowing shovel.” Rhenanus rightly interprets the *pala* as an agricultural implement. For he alludes to Matt. 3, according to a similar version used by blessed Cyprian: “because the Lord will come bearing the winnowing shovel in his hand and [Col. 0104D] will cleanse his threshing floor.” PAM.]

which even now cleanses the Lord’s threshing floor—that is, the Church—by winnowing the confused heap of the faithful, the grain of the martyrs

[Note: [Col. 0104D] “Ripe grain,” Rhenanus.]

and the chaff of the deniers. These are also

[Note: [Col. 0104D] “These too,” Semler, Oberthür, and Leopold.]

the ladders which Jacob dreams of, showing to some an ascent to the heights, to others a descent to the depths.

Thus persecution also begins to be understood as an athletic contest. By whom is the contest proclaimed, except by him who offers the crown and the prizes? You read the proclamation of this contest in the Apocalypse: with what rewards it invites men to victory, especially those [Col. 0103D] who have conquered specifically in persecution, having prevailed in the struggle—[Col. 0104A] in truth, “not against flesh and blood, but against the spiritual powers of wickedness” (Eph. 6:12). You will thus recognize that the control of the contest belongs to the same contest-master who invites men to the prize.

Everything enacted in persecution is the glory of God, who approves and rejects, raises up and casts down. But that which pertains to God’s glory will surely occur by his will. And when is God more believed in than when he is more feared, unless

[Note: [Col. 0104D] “Unless when—in time of persecution the Church,” Latinus.]

in a time of persecution? Then the Church is in a state of alarm; then faith too, on active service, is more watchful and disciplined in fasts, stations, prayers, humility, mutual solicitude and love,

[Note: [Col. 0104D] “In mutual solicitude and love.” By these words he means all the duties of Christian piety toward God and neighbor, and the affections of the faithful toward one another as those who cared for and loved one another—things which even pagans praised and admired, in the *Apologeticum*. RIG.]

holiness, and sobriety. For there is leisure for nothing except fear and hope. Accordingly, from this very fact it is also shown to us that persecution, which makes God’s servants better, cannot be attributed to the devil. [Col. 0104B]

## CHAPTER II

If

[Note: [Col. 0104D] “Since,” Latinus.]

iniquity is not from God but from the devil, while persecution consists in iniquity—for what is more unjust than that the priests of the true God and all

[Note: [Col. 0104D] “All,” Semler, Oberthür, and Leopold.]

the followers of truth should be treated like the most criminal of men?—then persecution therefore appears to come from the devil, by whom the iniquity of which persecution consists is performed.

We must know that, insofar as there is neither persecution without the devil’s iniquity nor a testing of faith without persecution, the iniquity necessary for testing faith provides persecution not with authorization but with service. For God’s will regarding the testing of faith—which is the rationale of persecution—comes first; the devil’s iniquity follows as the instrument of persecution, which is the rationale of the testing. For elsewhere too, to the same degree that iniquity is the rival of righteousness, it is material for bearing witness to that of which it is the rival, so that righteousness may be perfected in iniquity just as power is perfected in weakness (2 Cor. 12:9). For the weak things of the world were chosen by God to confound the strong, and its foolish things to confound the wise (1 Cor. 1:27). Thus iniquity too is employed so that righteousness may be proved, confounding iniquity.

Therefore, because the ministry belongs not to command but to service—the command, namely, is the Lord’s: persecution for the testing of faith; while the ministry

[Note: [Col. 0104D] The words “the ministry—for the instrumentality” were omitted for some unknown reason by Semler and his follower Oberthür.]

is the devil’s iniquity for the instrumentality of persecution—we believe that persecution may perhaps occur through the devil, but not from the devil. [Col. 0104D]

Satan will be permitted nothing against the servants of the living God unless the Lord permits it, [Col. 0105A] either so that he may destroy Satan himself through the faith of the elect, victorious in temptation,

[Note: [Col. 0105D] “Victorious against temptation,” Semler and Oberthür.]

or so that he may expose certain people as having belonged to him—

[Note: [Col. 0105D] “Or so that he may expose certain people as having belonged to him.” That is, so that he may show and demonstrate that those who have forsaken God and deserted to the devil were the devil’s people. RIG.]

those who have deserted to him.

You have the example of Job, upon whom the devil could inflict no temptation unless he had received authority from God—not even against Job’s property, unless the Lord had said: “Behold, I give everything he possesses into your hand; but do not stretch out your hand against the man himself” (Job 1:12). And indeed he did not stretch it out until, after the devil had requested this too, the Lord said: “Behold, I deliver him to you; only preserve his life” (Job 2:6).

Thus he also requested the power to tempt the apostles, since he did not possess it except by permission. For in the Gospel the Lord says to Peter: “Behold,” he says,

[Note: [Col. 0105D] “Satan asked to sift you.” *Cernere* in the sense of “to sift,” which the Greek word *σινιάσαι* means at Luke 22, is also employed by Pliny, book XVIII, chapter 11. PAM.]

“Satan asked to sift you like wheat; but I prayed for you, that [Col. 0105B] your faith might not fail” (Luke 22:31)—that is, lest so much be permitted to the devil that faith would be imperiled. By this it is shown that both things are in God’s hands: both the shaking of faith and its protection, since both are requested from him—the shaking by the devil and the protection by the Son.

And since the Son of God surely has in his own power the protection of faith which he requests from the Father, from whom he receives all power in heaven and on earth (Matt. 28:18), how could the devil possess the shaking of faith in his own hand?

But in the prescribed Prayer,

[Note: [Col. 0105D] “But in the prescribed Prayer.” He calls the prayer which Christ, the expert in heavenly law, dictated to us the “prescribed prayer.” [Col. 0106D] Thus Augustine called it the “prescribed petition,” Sermon 125, *On Time*. RIG.]

when we say to the Father, “Do not lead us into temptation” (Matt. 6:13)—and what temptation is greater than persecution?—we profess that it comes from him from whom we pray for deliverance from it. For this is what follows: “But deliver us from the evil one” (*ibid.*); that is: do not lead us into temptation [Col. 0105C] by permitting us to the evil one. For we are delivered from the devil’s hands when we are not delivered to him in temptation.

Not even over the herd of swine did the devil’s legion possess power unless it had obtained that power from God—so far is it from possessing power over God’s sheep. I can say that even the swine’s bristles were then numbered before God, much more the hairs of the saints.

The devil appears perhaps to possess a power already his own over those who do not belong to God, once they have been reckoned by God as a drop from a bucket (Isa. 40:15), the dust of a threshing floor,

[Note: [Col. 0106D] “And as spittle, the nations being reckoned.” As worthy contemptuously to be spat upon. RIG.]

and as spittle—the nations thus exposed to the devil as a kind of vacant possession. But over members of God’s household he is permitted nothing by his own power, because examples recorded in the Scriptures demonstrate when he is permitted anything—that is, for what causes.

Either the right of temptation is granted to him for the sake of testing, when he has been challenged or is himself challenging, as in the examples above; or, for the sake of rejection, [Col. 0106A] a sinner is delivered to him for punishment, as to an executioner, as Saul was: “And the Spirit of the Lord,” it says, “departed from Saul, and an evil spirit from the Lord shook him and choked him” (1 Sam. 16:14 [cited here as 2 Kings 16:2]). Or it is for the sake of restraint,

[Note: [Col. 0105D] “Cohabitation,” Semler and Oberthür.]

as the Apostle relates (2 Cor. 12:7), that a stake, an angel of Satan, was given to him so that he might be struck with fists.

[Note: [Col. 0106D] “A stake was given to him.” A pale, “a thorn in the flesh” (*σκόλοπα τῇ σαρκί*). Thus, in book V *Against Marcion*, he says that the Creator appointed an angel of Satan to strike his apostle with fists. RIG.—“A stake was given to him”: a thorn, *σκόλοπα*. LE PR.]

Not even this form of power is permitted to the devil for humbling the saints through affliction of the flesh unless it is permitted at the same time so that the power of endurance may be perfected in weakness. For the Apostle himself also delivered Phygelus and Hermogenes to Satan so that they might be corrected and cease blaspheming (2 Tim. 1:15). You now see that the devil more readily receives power even from God’s servants—so far is he from possessing it by right of ownership.

## CHAPTER III

Since, therefore, these examples occur especially during persecutions—[Col. 0106B] seeing that we are then especially approved or rejected, and then especially humbled or corrected—these things must necessarily take place universally by the permission or command of him by whom they also take place in particular; namely, by him who says: “I am he who makes peace and creates evils” (Isa. 45:7), that is, war. For this is the contrary of peace. And what war is there against our peace other than persecution?

If the outcomes of persecution above all bring either life or death, either a wound or healing, you have the author of both: “I shall strike, and I shall heal; I shall make alive, and I shall put to death” (Deut. 32:39). “I shall burn them,” he says, “as silver is burned;

[Note: [Col. 0105D] “Silver, gold,” the Franeker edition.]

and I shall test them,” he says, “as gold is tested” (Zech. 13:9). For when we are burned by the heat of persecution, then we are tested concerning the constancy of our faith.

These will be the devil’s fiery darts [Col. 0106C] (Eph. 6), through which faith’s burning and refining are administered—yet by God’s will. Who could doubt this I do not know, unless it is a plainly frivolous and cold faith which overtakes those who assemble timidly in church.

For you say:

[Note: [Col. 0106D] Rigault adds “he says.”]

“Because we assemble without order,

[Note: [Col. 0106D] Semler and Oberthür add “and.”]

assemble all together, and gather in great numbers at the church, the nations seek us out, and we fear that the nations may be stirred up.”

Do you not

[Note: [Col. 0106D] “Do you,” Rhenanus.]

know that God is Lord of all? If God wills it, then you will suffer persecution; but if he does not will it, the nations will remain silent. Surely you should believe this—if indeed you believe in that God without whose will not even a sparrow worth one penny falls to the earth (Matt. 10:29). We, however, I think, are worth more than many sparrows.
