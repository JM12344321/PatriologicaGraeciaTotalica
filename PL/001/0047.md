## CHAPTER XXV

**ARGUMENT.—**It is therefore without justification that the vast expansion of the Roman Empire is reckoned a reward for the Romans’ piety toward the gods—as though those gods had been able to confer it upon them and thus were the only gods. Yet these gods are ridiculous, shameful, or even foreign; and, if they possessed any power, they ought instead to have defended their own peoples against the Romans—a point pursued here with much wit. He also says that some of their gods had been kings, although at the time neither human beings nor those kings themselves had been without gods. Moreover, the gods were formerly worshipped with meager rites. It therefore appears that the Romans did not become great because of their religious devotion, but became religious because of their greatness. Indeed, they are not to be regarded as religious at all, since they became great by injuring religions: for they conquered the gods of other peoples together with the peoples themselves, devastated their temples, cast down their statues, and abolished their rites. Yet those peoples had been no less devout toward their own gods; nevertheless they were conquered by the Romans and absorbed into the sum of their empire.

I believe that I have sufficiently proved the nature of false and true divinity, since I have shown how the proof is established not only by discussions and arguments, but also by the testimony of the very beings whom [Col. 0422B] you believe to be gods. Nothing more, therefore, need be reconsidered in this case.

Yet since particular mention has arisen of the Roman name, I shall not omit the encounter invited by the presumption of those who say that, as the reward of their most scrupulous religious devotion, [Note: [Col. 0422C] *Of their most scrupulous religious devotion.* Thus Rigaltius and others correctly read from the manuscripts; the Leiden manuscript also reads thus. Some manuscripts, [Col. 0422D] as well as certain printed editions, have *religionis diligentissime*. The Fulda manuscript also has *of religion*, as though the phrase ought rather to refer to the gods, who had exercised particular care for the Roman commonwealth. In *To the Nations* II.17—which should be compared with Augustine, *City of God* IV, and Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, as well as with this chapter—the phrase is: “because by their observance of religious duties they earned dominion over the world.” Here too he presently repeats “to ascribe it to the merits of their religious devotion.”

Concerning this religious devotion of the Romans and the favor they believed they consequently received from the gods, there survives a splendid testimony of Cicero, Oration XXX, *On the Response of the Haruspices*, chapter 19:

“For who is so senseless that, when he looks up to heaven, he does not perceive that gods exist, but thinks that those things which are accomplished by such great intelligence that scarcely anyone can, by any art, follow the order and succession of events, happen by chance? Or who, once he understands that gods exist, does not understand that by their divine power this mighty empire was born, enlarged, and preserved? However much we may wish to love ourselves, Conscript Fathers, we have surpassed neither the Spaniards in numbers, nor the Gauls in strength, [Col. 0423A] nor the Carthaginians in cunning, nor the Greeks in arts, nor even the Italians and Latins themselves in that native and domestic intelligence peculiar to this race and country; but we have surpassed every people and nation in piety, religion, and this one wisdom—the recognition that all things are ruled and governed by the divine power of the immortal gods.”

Thus Cicero speaks as an orator. Polybius, however, called it rather what it was—superstition—adding the following political digression in book VI, chapter 54, which it will suffice to give in Latin:

“But,” he says, “I think that in no respect do the Romans surpass other peoples so much as in the opinion they hold concerning the gods. Indeed, the very thing that among other mortals is counted a fault appears to me to hold the Roman commonwealth together: I mean superstition. For, to a degree that many may deservedly find astonishing, superstition has been magnified among them and introduced both into private life and into public counsels as far as it possibly could be; and I believe they did this for the sake of the multitude. For if a commonwealth could be composed solely of wise men, that entire class of things would perhaps be unnecessary. But since every multitude is fickle, filled with unlawful desires, seething with headlong anger, and driven by rage to violence, nothing remains but that the common people be restrained by hidden terrors and terrifying fictions of this sort.”

True religion is constituted otherwise: worshipping God not from fear of punishment but from love of the good, it possesses a favorably disposed Deity of its own accord. Hence in Deuteronomy IV.6–8 Moses even makes Israel’s enemies proclaim its happiness, saying: “This people truly is wise and great.” For, as he himself adds: “What people is so great that its gods are so near to it?” etc.

The Jews may therefore argue from religious devotion to success in affairs: Rabbi Lipmann in book N, section 122, p. 158, and Rabbi Albo, discourse III, chapter XXV, *Sepher Ikkarim*, p. 14, from Genebrard’s translation: “Truly great is the people of God, the ruler of the world, since all kingdoms must be subjected to the Lord, whose scepter, sent forth from Zion, lays waste the boundaries of the Moabites and conquers the sons of Seth.”

Here, then, that enormous crowd of certain and uncertain gods whom Varro records—those eminent and chosen gods too, together with their king Jupiter himself, brandishing thunderbolts, formidable in counsel and power, as their worshippers declare, and terrible in arms—here, here they ought to have done something, if not for the world, at least for themselves, lest, indeed, one crucified man should destroy them. That they did perish we ourselves know, and the prophet David confirms it, saying: “Their memory has perished with a crash, but the Lord remains forever.” These are the words of Prosper of Aquitaine, [Col. 0423D] *Promises and Predictions* III.38.

Accordingly, our author rightly says of the Romans below in this chapter: “But how vain it is to ascribe the summit of the Roman name to the merits of religious devotion.” He refutes the same claim at the end of the chapter: “It certainly cannot be consistent with faith that those should appear to have grown through the merits of religion who, as we have suggested, either grew by injuring religion or injured it by growing.” HAV.]

the Romans were raised to such a height of greatness [Col. 0423A] that they occupied the world. [Note: [Col. 0423D] *That they occupied the world.* That is, because of this religious devotion of theirs. Havercamp refers to this an elegant relief in which the mother of the descendants of Aeneas is seen among the victorious standards of her Romans, accompanied by the gods Jupiter, Neptune, Vulcan, and Mercury, who appear to confirm for her the empire of the world.

Also worthy of notice is this ancient inscription, recorded by Gruterus, p. 83, no. 45, and Boissardus, part III, *Roman Antiquities*, plate 120:

> IN THE HAPPY AGE, PHISIAS, PRIEST OF ISIS THE SAVIOR. AT THE CONSECRATION OF THE PONTIFF, MAY THE GODS FAVOR THE VOWS, [Col. 0424A] THE SECRET POWERS OF THE ROMAN COMMONWEALTH AND THE DEFENSES OF THE CITY. MAY THOSE BY WHOSE WILL KINGDOMS HAVE YIELDED TO ROMAN RULE GIVE THEIR FAVOR.

Hence, in that elaborate letter which Symmachus wrote to the emperors Valentinian, Theodosius, and Arcadius in support of dedicating the Altar of Victory, Rome is introduced speaking thus: “This worship brought the world beneath my laws.”

Plutarch, however, was not so credulous: he ascribes it to the vicissitude of events [Col. 0424B] and attributes it to Fortune. Thus book I, chapter 4: “For what is peculiar to our work, and what has happened in our own time as a cause of wonder, is this: just as Fortune inclined nearly all the regions of the entire inhabited world in one direction and compelled all things to converge upon one and the same goal, so by this history we set before our readers, as though under a single view, the manner in which Fortune herself conducted herself toward the completion of this universal work.”

Rather, it was their pursuits in war and peace that placed them at so lofty a summit, together with their integrity of character, as Augustine argues in *The City of God* V.1. Indeed, Prudentius judges more reverently and truly that God softened the barbarians through the Romans and bound all peoples together by that single bond of Roman dominion, so that they might be subjected together to Christ the Lord, the stone cut out without the work of hands, and that their pride might be crushed by him. His verses in the *Passion of Saint Lawrence*, from verse 419 onward, deserve notice, as do these verses *Against Symmachus* II.582:
