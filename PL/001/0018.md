The work *On Prayer* may undoubtedly be said to belong to the first class. Unless I am greatly mistaken, it is the earliest of all Tertullian’s works that have survived; and by the broad consensus of all—if Hoffmann is excepted (*Dissertat. citat.* § 11, p. 37 sq.), whose interest it was to refer all Tertullian’s writings to his Montanist period—it is numbered among the works of purer doctrine. And even if the testimony of Hilary of Poitiers is perhaps suspect, though he indicates clearly enough that this little work was written before Tertullian’s defection, [Col. 0145C] nevertheless the book itself contains so many indications of opinions differing from those which he later defended in writings infected with Montanist errors that the doctrine of this book cannot be difficult to ascertain for anyone willing to compare the opinions expressed in the two groups of writings.

For first, how great are the praises bestowed here upon the work of Hermas entitled *The Shepherd*! When Tertullian shows that the practice of sitting down after prayer has ended, as observed by Hermas, does not conflict with his own position, he calls the work “almost Scripture,” and later—lest that “almost” furnish anyone with grounds for resenting our conclusion—he calls it “Scripture.”

> [Note: [Col. 0145D] Ch. 16. We use the edition of Lodovico Antonio Muratori, who published our little work, previously mutilated, in complete form from a manuscript of the Ambrosian Library in his *Anecdota Latina*, vol. III, p. 9 sq.]

Although I do not understand this, with Muratori (*loc. cit.*, note 3, p. 31) and others, as though Tertullian were placing the books of Hermas among the canonical books of the New Testament—for he is speaking more according to the customary language of his opponents than according to his own—he nevertheless does not scorn the authority of Hermas, [Col. 0145D] but brings it into agreement with his own position. This is very different from what he does in *On Modesty*, chs. 10 and 20, where he not only rejects the pronouncements of Hermas but calls that work spurious by universal consent, and calls him “the Shepherd of adulterers.”

I am therefore surprised that Hoffmann found this argument in Allix surprising. He supposed that Tertullian did not assail the *Shepherd* of Hermas in our little work because the authority of Hermas had not obstructed him so greatly as it did when he wrote *On Modesty*, and that there had consequently been no need to depreciate its worth. For even if we do not deny that the authority of Hermas was more adverse to Tertullian [Col. 0146A] in the matters discussed in *On Modesty*, his judgment concerning Hermas in our *On Prayer* is not merely much gentler but is even expressed in such a way that he himself appears not to have scorned the authority of Hermas altogether.

Moreover, the phrase “almost Scripture,” which Hoffmann thinks diminishes the force of this argument, has been explained by him quite ineptly, as though Tertullian had meant rashly to say that the title *Shepherd* was given to the little book of Hermas although it in no way deserved that title. For “almost Scripture” must certainly be understood to mean that the authority of the little work of Hermas was regarded as almost equal to that of the sacred books. This is clear from the fact that he afterward calls it “Scripture” without qualification; nor, in my opinion, would he have called it so if Hermas had been as contemptible to him as he was [Col. 0146B] at the time when *On Modesty* was written.

But there are also other statements of Tertullian here that differ greatly from those which he advanced in his Montanist works, and these show most clearly that Tertullian’s discipline was then very different from what it became afterward. In section 18 (p. 35 Murat.), for example, when disputing against those who, while abstaining from food, were also accustomed not to receive others with a kiss, he says that only on the day of Passover, on which there is a common and, as it were, public observance of fasting, may the kiss rightly be denied to others. This certainly shows that Tertullian then belonged to that sacred society which observed no day with public and common abstinence from food except the day on which Christ had died; for the Day of Passover is the day consecrated to the commemoration of Jesus’ death.

> [Note: [Col. 0146D] See, for example, *Against the Jews*, ch. 10.]

Indeed, the ancient Christians in Tertullian’s time [Col. 0146C] commonly observed only this day by fasting, because they said that the bridegroom had then been taken away. Yet Tertullian, in *On Fasting*, ch. 2—which undoubtedly belongs among his Montanist works—severely censures this custom of observing this one day alone through the common religious practice of fasting; and it is sufficiently clear that the Montanists observed several other days besides the anniversary of Christ’s death. From this the following conclusion is elicited: when Tertullian composed *On Prayer*, he had not yet withdrawn to the Montanist party.

This can be confirmed by a new argument drawn from the two little works. If anyone can reconcile their passages in any way other than that which we have stated—namely, from the difference in Tertullian’s discipline at the times when the two works were written—we shall hear him. For when, in [Col. 0146D] *On Prayer*, ch. 25, he turns to the question of the times at which prayers should be offered to God, he says that it is indeed good to assign certain fixed and settled hours, as it were, to prayer—namely the third, sixth, and ninth hours, which were also observed by the Apostles. But he adds that this is done without any prescribed observance, and that no prayers are commanded by law except the prescribed prayers, that is, those uttered at the rising of daylight and of night. And at the end of ch. 23 he says: “Concerning the times of prayer, absolutely nothing has been prescribed, except, of course, that one should pray at every time and in every place.”

But in *On Fasting* he assails with great bitterness the adherents of the orthodox Church [Col. 0147A] (ch. 2), because they said that abstention from food should be undertaken by free choice, not by command of the new discipline, according to the circumstances and reasons of each person, and because they generally concluded their prayers at the ninth hour, following Peter’s example. He adds expressly in ch. 10 that, while the liberty to pray always and everywhere and at every time remained entirely intact, those three hours were nevertheless more solemn occasions for divine prayer. And in ch. 13 he appeals to the very law given by the Paraclete, by which the latter had confirmed this Montanist custom. All these points undoubtedly prove that Tertullian’s opinion was entirely different when he composed *On Prayer*.

Finally, to omit many other considerations, this seems to me a powerful argument for assigning to our book orthodoxy: although he frequently discusses fasts and stations in this book, [Col. 0147B] he makes no mention of xerophagies, which he defends with such ardor in *On Fasting*, chs. 2, 5 sq. I doubt whether any other explanation of this omission can be given than that, when our little work was written, he belonged to that Church which, as he says in *On Fasting*, ch. 2, called xerophagies “a novel name for an affected duty, bordering upon pagan superstition.”

All these considerations seem to me far more worthy of attention than those which Hoffmann advances in defense of his opinion, to which we ourselves could, if necessary, add some others not wholly devoid of weight. He says, for instance, that the precepts given here in ch. 22 concerning the veiling of virgins are very similar to those defended by Tertullian in his book on that subject. But the case is quite otherwise. [Col. 0147C] For although he wishes virgins to be covered with a veil in both books, the mere agreement of the books on this point does not demonstrate that both proceeded from the same discipline. If anyone compares the passages of the two works carefully, he will find a great disagreement within Tertullian himself, one that confirms our judgment much more than Hoffmann’s.

In *On the Veiling of Virgins*, he defends this custom vehemently and above all from the law of the Montanists. But in our little work *On Prayer*, ch. 22, p. 43, he does not command this custom to be observed and says that even a law imposing the veil upon virgins can be excused; while at the beginning of that discussion, ch. 21, he treats as an uncertain question requiring reconsideration what is variously observed throughout the Churches: whether virgins ought to be veiled or not. The learned man’s argument is therefore slight. [Col. 0147D] He could more properly have cited ch. 6 as testimony for his opinion. There, after saying that the fourth petition of the Lord’s Prayer is to be understood not literally but figuratively of the sacred supper, Tertullian adds: “But also because this expression is understood carnally, it cannot be fulfilled without regard for religion and spiritual discipline.” Here “spiritual discipline” might give rise to some suspicion of a Montanist author, since the Montanists called themselves “spiritual” and others “psychic.”

Tertullian, however, could also have meant by “spiritual persons” any upright people and Christians, and I prefer this interpretation because not much later he explains these persons as “the faithful.” He contrasts them not with the orthodox, or “psychics,” but with the nations—that is, those who did not believe in Jesus. Nor have I found that Tertullian, even in his Montanist [Col. 0148A] books, denied the name “faithful” to the orthodox.

Since it is now established that this book belongs among those which contain the purer doctrine, it makes no difference what particular place one assigns it among Septimius’ writings of this kind. Since it has offered us not even the slightest suspicion of Montanist errors, we think it best to place it first within this class.

## § 3.—The Book *On Baptism* Written by Tertullian While a Catholic. The Arguments of Those Who Disagree Are Refuted

But it is more difficult to say what doctrine should be assigned to the book *On Baptism*. I am indeed aware that everyone judges the orthodoxy of this book to be placed beyond all doubt, with Hoffmann offering only slight opposition, as in the other cases. [Col. 0148B] Yet in this matter the levity of many learned men is astonishing. They have attributed to Tertullian the Montanist other books whose subject provides a definite indication of purity, while safely excluding from that class this book, even though its passages are so obscure that, if one searches in them for even the chief opinions either of the Montanists or of the orthodox, the mind, after examining everything, can scarcely see in which direction it should turn.

Nevertheless, so far as possible, let us determine whether the doctrine prevailing in our book inclines more toward the one side or the other. First I propose to examine the passages which create a suspicion of Montanism concerning this book, and then those—though more rarely advanced—which point in the contrary direction. To omit the objections hesitantly advanced by Baronius, whom Allix sufficiently refuted (*in Vit.* [Col. 0148C] *Tert.*, p. 29 sqq.), Hoffmann brings forward the following arguments in favor of the former position.

First, he argues that in *On Baptism* Tertullian uses statements similar to those he defended in *On Modesty*, since he assails those who assumed for themselves the power to grant pardon for offenses, though that power belonged to God alone. Unless I am mistaken, Hoffmann finds an indication of this in the words: “Neither does anyone forgive sin nor grant the Spirit except God alone.” I do not perceive how these words could have been uttered only by an author devoted to the discipline of Montanus. For the members of the orthodox Church did not deny this, nor was it a matter disputed between them and the Montanists. Rather, although those orthodox Christians assigned the power of forgiving sins to God alone, they did not think that peace should be denied to those who had been [Col. 0148D] cast out of the sacred society on account of sins, provided that they promised to reform their lives.

The learned man next says that this book belongs to a time filled with calamities inflicted upon Christians by the worshipers of the gods. This is certainly a slight conjecture. For the statement in ch. 16 of our book, which he calls as a witness—“We also have a second washing, namely that of blood”—in no way indicates that danger was then immediately present. Even if such danger had actually been mentioned by Tertullian, the book could not on that account be relegated among the writings filled with the errors of Montanus. For there are many writings of Tertullian which, as we showed in Dissertation I, were contemporary with these calamities but were composed before his defection.

Nor does the very manner of speaking by which Tertullian calls suffering a kind of baptism immediately reveal a Montanist, unless we wish to say that Origen also and others—whose passages are collected by Dodwell (*Dissert. Cyprian.* XIII, on the second baptism of martyrdom, especially § 2)—though they were never accused of Montanism, must on this account be regarded as adherents of the Montanist society. [Col. 0149A]

> [Note: [Col. 0149B] But someone might say that Christian authors who lived after Tertullian, once the highest esteem had arisen for those who had publicly confessed Christ by undergoing either death or torture, adopted this form of speech, like many other things, from the Montanists. Consequently, their passages would not be suitable for freeing this language from suspicion of Montanism, especially since it first appears in Tertullian and in the writings of other Montanists, as in the *Acts of Perpetua and Felicity* (§ 22).
>
> I may perhaps dispel this suspicion with another conjecture, if I show that Marcion himself had already employed the same manner of speaking. Epiphanius, *Haeres.* XLII, § 3, Cologne ed., p. 304, says that Marcion defended three baptisms (οὐ μόνον παρ` αὐτῷ ἓν λοῦτρον δίδονται, ἀλλὰ καὶ ἕως τριῶν λούτρων, “with him not only one baptism is given, but as many as three”).
>
> Although, in relating this, we grant that Epiphanius [Col. 0149C] mingled falsehoods with truth—as when he says that Marcion devised a threefold baptism in order to purify himself from an outrage committed with a virgin, which, as many have shown, is sheer calumny; and also when he says that Marcion administered baptism three times, a statement which in no way agrees with the argument by which this threefold baptism was confirmed, as will become clear below—I nevertheless judge that Epiphanius’ testimony should not be wholly despised. It will therefore be worthwhile briefly to show what this threefold baptism of Marcion was.
>
> I. There are many features in Marcion’s discipline similar to Manichaean discipline, and it is established that Manes divided his followers into Hearers and Elect. Therefore I am inclined to think that the same division may perhaps be assigned to the Marcionites, since Tertullian, *Against Marcion*, book V, ch. 7, appears to indicate that Marcion denied permission to marry only to the more holy among his followers, in these words: “Marcion removes all sexual intercourse from the Faithful; as for his catechumens, they may see to themselves.” Here he could have called the less perfect “catechumens” in the same manner in which Epiphanius and others also use this name for the Hearers of the Manichaeans. Nor is it credible that Marcion abolished all marriage universally among his followers, since, as is established, he regarded the author of marriage not as the evil God but as that intermediate God whom he called just. [Col. 0149D]
>
> From this, II, it may reasonably be conjectured with Beausobre (*Histoire du Manichéisme*, vol. II, p. 122) that one baptism of Marcion was conferred upon everyone, while the Marcionites admitted to the inner circle were initiated by a second. This is especially so because Epiphanius expressly adds: ἔξεστι διδόναι, “it is permitted to give” (namely, λοῦτρον, “a baptism”) παρ` αὐτοῖς τῷ βουλομένῳ, “among them, to anyone who wishes”. This would conflict with all Tertullian’s passages on that subject, in which he says that everyone wishing to be washed in the sacred laver by the Marcionites had to renounce marriage (book IV, *Against Marcion*, ch. 11; book I, ch. 29; etc.), unless we admit this double baptism.
>
> But now, III, it remains to say what Marcion understood the third baptism to be; and this is the matter properly at issue for us. The manner in which it should be understood is taught most clearly by the argument with which Epiphanius says Marcion confirmed his threefold baptism. It was drawn from the passage in Luke (ch. XII, 50) where Christ undoubtedly speaks of his own suffering and of a certain baptism still impending over him—a passage which we see was also adduced by others to defend a second baptism of martyrdom. Moved by this consideration, I conclude without any doubt that Marcion also gave the name of a kind of baptism to the sufferings undertaken by Christians after the example of Jesus Christ.]

Finally, Hoffmann says that in the same passage Tertullian indicates clearly enough that the Montanists were wrongfully expelled from the society of the universal Church, since they and the other Christians had one God, one baptism, and finally one Church in heaven, while there was no fellowship with the other heretics (ch. 15). This argument certainly lends no support to his opinion. For Tertullian does not say a single word about the agreement of the Montanists with the orthodox in that doctrine. Rather, he contrasts that faith of the Christians with the heresy of the Caians, and shows that the latter were spurious Christians because communion with the other Christians had been taken away from them. [Col. 0149B]

And if what Hoffmann supposes were true, Tertullian would be so far from defending himself against the wrong inflicted upon the Montanists that he would instead have confessed that he and the Montanists were heretics. For in that very ch. 15 he says that heretics are outsiders—that is, non-Christians—as the very removal of communion testifies. Therefore, if Tertullian had indicated in this passage that the orthodox had expelled the Montanists from the Church, he would certainly have called himself and his associates heretics, which is wholly inconceivable. [Col. 0150A]

Here we must also address another difficulty advanced by Allix against the accepted opinion concerning the doctrine prevailing in this book (*Diss. de Tertulliani vita et scriptis*, ch. 4, p. 33). He says that we might suspect that Tertullian’s mind was already inclined toward the discipline of Montanus because he called Christians “little fishes” and Christ “the FISH” (ΙΧΘΥΣ, “FISH”). These names, he says, were drawn from the Sibylline books, [Col. 0150B] composed by some Montanist or even by Montanus himself. Moreover, in ch. 20 Tertullian mentioned certain charisms, which seemed to savor of Montanism, particularly when those statements are compared with what is taught in ch. 29 of *On the Spectacles*. But Allix did well not to regard this suspicion itself as excessively subtle or acute.

For even if we granted that the books now passed off under the name of the Sibylline books were fabricated either by Montanus or by one of his disciples—though I would rather assign them to some Gnostic—and that Tertullian also derived from them this name “Fish” given to Christ, nevertheless this argument could no more make Tertullian suspect of Montanism when he composed this book than it could Justin Martyr, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, and others [Col. 0150C] who frequently cite certain Sibylline books as testimony. And even if those books existed in general form at this very time, who can guarantee that they were not later interpolated so as to assume the form which they now possess?

This is especially to be feared in the acrostic of the eighth book, Ιησοῦς Χρίστος Θεοῦ υἱὸς σώτηρ, “Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior”, from which the common people suppose that this very surname ἰχθύος, “of the FISH” was taken. For it was not set forth by any of the ancients before Constantine the Great (*Oration to the Assembly of the Saints*, ch. 18). From the very fact that he says Virgil sang of the birth of Jesus Christ (ch. 19 sqq.), it is clear how easily he could be deceived by men fabricating religion.

> [Note: [Col. 0150C] It may perhaps seem strange that our most learned Author, so highly knowledgeable in the ancient discipline, should hesitate [Col. 0150D] anxiously, as it were, upon an open road and doubt uncertainly whether the well-known token ΙΧΘΥΣ, “FISH” was more peculiar to Christians or to Montanists; and even that he should assert that the same symbolic acrostic had been set forth by none of the ancients before Constantine the Great.
>
> Many things occur that might be said on the contrary side. Until we reach the book *On Baptism*, however, it is better to postpone their treatment to the proper place. For now it is pleasing to anticipate only a few points, briefly and in readiness.
>
> Indeed, more than enough innumerable monuments answer the first point—monuments fully sufficient, of unquestionable antiquity, and genuinely produced and dedicated by Christian hands. In them the aforesaid token stands out, whether cut by a chisel, cast in bronze, carved in ivory, depicted in colors, expressed in letters, or adorned with verses; and, as generally happens, it is mingled with other signs and sigla from which one may infer the pure and unalloyed integrity of the sounder faith. Thus the fish is very frequently depicted with the dove (Aringhi, vol. I, 580; vol. II, 258), or joined to an anchor or to Christ’s monogram, which, as everyone knows, had come into common use long before the Constantinian *labarum* (Boldett., 370, 366, 453; Aringhi, I, 520; II, 327, 620; Bottari, III, 19). At one time it is placed at the feet of figures in prayer, [Col. 0151C] at another beside the Good Shepherd (Bold., 573; Bolt., pl. XXXV. See also and compare Costadoni, *dissertazione sopra i pesce come simbolo de primi cristiani*; *Raccolta d’opuscoli scientifici da Calogera*, vol. XLI, 1749.—Lupi, *dissert. ad Severae Epitaphium*, Palermo, 1734, pp. 103, 136, 145, 153, 165, 185.—Fabretti, *Inscript. antiq.*, book VIII, p. 569, nos. 124–126; p. 590, no. 107.—Münter, *Sinnbilder und Kunstvorstellungen der alten Christen*.—Thiersch, *Jahres-Berichte der Koeniglich Bayerschen Akademie der Wissenschaften*. Munich, 1829.)
>
> Nor are the written and sculpted testimonies proving the use of the Sibylline acrostic to be far older than Constantine the Great either fewer in number or of suspect credibility. Indeed, one may marvel at how great a host of witnesses concerning this matter rises from the apostolic age down to our own times. They seem, as it were, to join hands with certain traditions from the earliest age of humankind, diffused in every direction and, once the Church had arisen in the midst of the ages, renewed and revived within her own bosom; until, when peace had been established throughout the world, the fish and the dove, and all the other symbolic signs or sigla that had flourished in concealment, came openly into the light. [Col. 0151D]
>
> For now, therefore, concerning the ΙΧΘΥΟΣ, “of the FISH” acrostic, let it suffice merely to refer to certain Fathers: namely Clement of Alexandria in four passages, vol. I, pp. 260, 312, 289, and 787, Potter; and our own Tertullian in *On Baptism*, *On the Resurrection*, and *On the Spectacles*, as there will be an opportunity to discuss more fully below.
>
> Nor should we omit to add two ancient marble inscriptions recently excavated and published: one through the care of D. Raoul-Rochette, *Mémoires de l’Académie royale des inscriptions*, vol. XIII, p. 98, and D. Thiersch, at the place cited; the other dug up from the most ancient cemetery of St. Peter near the city of Autun, beside the paved road, and printed in the work entitled *Annales de philosophie chrétienne*, vol. XIX.
>
> Compare further Costadoni, §§ 23–27, 30, 33; Secchi, *epigramma greco cristiano de’ primi secoli trovata preso l’antica Augstoduno*, Rome, 1840.—Franz, *Christliches Denkmal von Autun erkloït*, Berlin, 1841. EDD.]

And [Col. 0151A] even if I concede everything he wishes, not even the points gathered in this learned man’s argument can stand or agree with one another. The earliest Christian writers, as William Beveridge (*In codice canonum Ecclesiae primitivae*, book I, ch. 14, § 4 sqq.) and William Whiston demonstrate at length, acknowledge that the books they call Sibylline are exceedingly ancient.

> [Note: [Col. 0151D] In easily the best of his books: *A Vindication of the Sibylline Oracles*, Proposition 10, p. 36 sq.]

From this it follows necessarily that those books were very different from the writings which falsely claim their name in our time, and which appear even to our opponent to have been composed in the second century after the birth of Christ. What is more, the very Tertullian about whom we are inquiring, at the time when he was very near to his defection, calls the Sibyl’s pronouncements exceedingly ancient in ch. 12 of the books *To the Nations*. Could he therefore have thought the Sibylline books to have proceeded from Montanus or from anyone belonging to that flock? [Col. 0151B]

Nor does the second point which Allix adds concerning a charism prove anything. I confess that in the cited ch. 20 I do not find a sufficiently clear mention of it. Even if it were found, it would no more follow that *On Baptism* had issued from the workshop of Tertullian the Montanist than the same could be said of *On the Spectacles*, which was undoubtedly written before his defection, as I demonstrated elsewhere.

And lest we pass over in silence anything by which someone might in some way lend truth to this opinion adverse to our book *On Baptism*, he might perhaps appeal to ch. 20 of this little work, where Tertullian says that abstinence from food is highly useful for avoiding the solicitations of sin. This might seem to agree with the praises of fasting [Col. 0151C] which he celebrated in *On Fasting*. Still more suspect is his statement at the end of ch. 8: “The person who renews his offenses after baptism is destined for the fire.” [Col. 0152A]

Yet neither of these two propositions was so alien to the discipline of those who held the correct faith as to be peculiar to the Montanists. For in the former there is nothing that could not be admitted, even if in other passages Tertullian attributes too much to this abstinence. The latter was either defended—though in an inept sense—even by certain adherents of the orthodox Church, as will be shown later, or it could properly be said of those who, after baptism, had fallen into an impious life and gave no thought to reforming it.
