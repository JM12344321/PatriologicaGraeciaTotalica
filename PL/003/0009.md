[Col. 0011A] III. More certain is the argument drawn from the authority of the ancients who wrote about Saints Perpetua and Felicitas. For, besides Victor of Vita’s affirmation that they “were buried at Carthage in the greater basilica” [Note: [Col. 0011D] Victor of Vita, *On the Vandal Persecution*, book I, chapter III, p. 5, Paris ed., 1694], Prosper in the Labbean *Chronicle*, Bede in his book *On the Six Ages of the World*, and indeed also in his authentic *Martyrology* (although the work circulated under his name reads otherwise), Regino, Rabanus in his *Martyrology*, and very many other martyrologies, both printed and manuscript—as may be seen in the Bollandists under March 7—expressly assert that these holy martyrs suffered at Carthage.

Nor is it helpful to reply that Augustine, in Sermon XXXII as edited by Sirmond, which is CCCXLV in the new edition, calls these martyrs “the Tuburbitan women.” For in the manuscript codex from which [Col. 0011B] that sermon was taken, one reads not *Tuburbitanarum*, as Sirmond corrected it, but *Suburbitarum*. Someone might perhaps say that they were called *Suburbitanae* because they suffered in a suburb of Carthage. But even if it be granted that this passage was correctly emended by Sirmond, nothing can be inferred from it against Valesius’s opinion. Valesius indeed admits that Augustine is speaking here of the Tuburbitan martyrs, but he flatly denies that they are Perpetua and Felicitas. Elsewhere, when Augustine often discusses Perpetua and Felicitas, he never calls them Tuburbitan, but always calls them by their proper names, Perpetua and Felicitas. The learned man instead maintains that by “the Tuburbitan women” Saint Augustine here meant Maxima, Donatilla, and Secunda, who are said to have suffered at Tuburbium under the emperors Valerian and Gallienus. And indeed [Col. 0011C] this answer of Valesius could be confirmed from the ancient Carthaginian calendar published by Mabillon, in which the feast of the holy Tuburbitan women is commemorated on July 30; on that day Perpetua and Felicitas are nowhere commemorated, whereas Maxima and her companions are reported in all the martyrologies to have died on that date.

IV. We shall not dwell long upon determining the time at which these blessed martyrs completed their passion. The Acts and martyrologies which report that they suffered under Valerian and Gallienus are proved false by the very fact that Tertullian, who mentions Perpetua in his book *On the Soul*, departed this life long before the time of those emperors. Thus the authentic *Martyrology* of Bede and other ancient sources more correctly assign this martyrdom to the reign of the emperor Severus; the genuine Acts [Col. 0011D] agree with them, since there [Note: [Col. 0011D] *Acts of the Passion*, etc., chapter 16] the holy martyrs were exposed to the beasts on the birthday of Geta Caesar. We believe this occurred in the year of Christ 202, or the following year, on the Ides of March. DOM THÉODORE RUINART.

V. Finally, we must inquire who was the author—or rather the compiler—of these Acts, since no one can deny that the greater part of them was written by Perpetua and Saturus. The author of a letter written from Bourges to Henri de Valois attributes that work to Tertullian. To prove this, he compared many passages in the Acts with other works of Tertullian and showed that precisely the same style, the same forms of expression, and very often even the same words are found in both. Whether this entirely proves the matter, however, others will judge.

VI. But I cannot agree with Valesius, who thinks that these Acts were compiled by a follower of Montanus, whoever he may have been. Nevertheless, that most learned man thinks that he can infer this from entirely certain indications. Those indications are that the author professes to accept new prophecies and greatly prefers them to the prophecies of the Old Testament. He proves this from the compiler’s own words, who speaks as follows in the preface: “Therefore we also acknowledge and honor new visions, equally promised beforehand, just as we do prophecies.” And a little earlier he had said: “But let those who judge the one power of the one Holy Spirit according to the ages of the times look to it, since all more recent things, as being the very latest, must be regarded as greater.”

Yet, although at first sight these words may seem to savor of Montanus’s errors, if they are examined somewhat more carefully, you will find that they contain nothing except what is orthodox and entirely consonant with the catholic faith. For this author does not compare the most recent visions with the canonical Scriptures, as Valesius thought, but only with the visions and revelations of the ancient martyrs. He asserts that events occurring in his own time should be held in no less veneration than those which had occurred in ancient times, that is, in the first or second century of the Church. Indeed, he says that in some measure these new revelations should be received with greater attention, since examples are more effective the more recent they are; and, furthermore, because these latest visions, which would one day be ancient, needed to be supported by some authority so that posterity would believe them. This is clear from the same author’s entire preface, in which there is not even a single word about comparing Sacred Scripture with the martyrs’ visions:

“If ancient examples of faith,” he says, “have been set down in writing . . . so that by reading them, as though through a renewed representation of the events, God may be honored and man strengthened, why should new testimonies, equally suited to both purposes, not also be set down? etc.”

Moreover, even if this author believed that these martyrs’ visions were among those which God had promised through Joel, it does not follow that he equated them with Sacred Scripture. Nor did the Church count the faithful’s spoken or written words among these canonical Scriptures, although it did not doubt that they proceeded from Him by whom to some [Col. 0012D] are given various kinds of tongues, to others prophecy, to others the word of wisdom, and to still others other gifts—gifts which, it is certain, had been foretold and promised in the cited testimony of Joel.

VII. But lest the words of the author’s preface, who praises these visions as though divinely inspired, should perhaps disturb anyone, Augustine must be heard on the matter. Although, as they say, he expressly excludes these visions of Perpetua from the canon of Scripture [Note: [Col. 0012D] Augustine, book I, *On the Soul and Its Origin*, to Renatus, chapter 10, no. 12; and book III, to Vincentius, chapter 9, no. 12], he nevertheless calls them “divine revelations” and asserts that the faithful should honor them with religious reverence [Note: [Col. 0012D] The same, Sermon I; new ed., 280, vol. V, p. 1134].

“We heard,” he says, “their exhortations”—those of Perpetua and Felicitas—“in divine revelations, [Col. 0013A] and the triumphs of their passions, when these were read; and we perceived all those things, arranged and illuminated by the lights of words, with our ears, beheld them with our minds, honored them with religious reverence, and praised them with charity.”

Augustine discusses these same visions in several other passages and testifies that they were publicly read in church [Note: [Col. 0013C] The same, Sermon 282]. But finally, to settle the matter in a single word, how could a heretic and follower of Montanus exhort his own people to maintain communion with these women, whom they knew to have died in communion with the Catholics whom they called “Psychics”? Nor could those to whom this author wrote have been ignorant of this, since he addresses those who had been present at the martyrdom: “We proclaim it,” he says, “to you, brothers and little children, so that you also who were present may remember the glory of the Lord, etc.” But enough of this. We must now [Col. 0013B] say a few words about the praises of these martyrs uttered by various authors [Note: [Col. 0013C] Mabillon, vol. I, *Museum Italicum*].

VIII. The memory of Saints Perpetua and Felicitas has always been renowned in the Church, especially in the African Church. Possidius testifies in his *Index* that Saint Augustine delivered three discourses on their anniversary; these are Sermons CCLXXX, CCLXXXI, and CCLXXXII in the new edition. For some time I hesitated over whether to reproduce those sermons here in full. But since Augustine’s works are in almost everyone’s hands, it seemed sufficient, after omitting them, to record in notes upon the text those remarks which relate more particularly to the Acts. The same holy Doctor discusses these martyrs and their companions in many other passages. Tertullian mentions “the most steadfast Perpetua and her fellow martyrs” in *On the Soul*, chapter 55, as we have already said. [Col. 0013C] All the martyrologies assign their feast to March 7, on which day it continues to be celebrated among us.

[Col. 0014A] Their names are also commemorated in the canon of the Roman Mass and in the extremely ancient Gallican Sacramentary published by our Mabillon. They likewise occur in the canon of the Mass in the Gregorian Sacramentary, although the feast of these saints does not appear among the other feasts recorded there, perhaps because it falls during Lent. It is also absent from Mabillon’s very ancient calendar of the African Church. But since it is certain from Augustine that their feast was celebrated in his day, it must be said that, unless they are designated in it by the name “Tuburbitan women,” that calendar is not complete; for it actually lacks twelve days of February and the whole months of March and April.

That their feast was also celebrated at Rome in the earliest times is clear from the missal of Gelasius and from the ancient Bucherian martyrology, where the following appears: “On March 7, Perpetua and Felicitas, in Africa.” The other martyrologies, both ancient and more recent, have the same. They are celebrated with their companions in the Greek *Menologion* of the emperor Basil on February 2.

Their relics are said to have been brought from Africa to Rome during the Vandal persecution. Later, in the ninth century, Rodulfus, archbishop of Bourges, received them from there and deposited them in the monasteries of Dèvres and Beaulieu, which he himself had founded. Certainly even today the Benedictine monks who, after the destruction of Dèvres, migrated to Vierzon, glory in possessing the relics of blessed Perpetua.

But the Acts themselves must now be presented. Anyone who wishes to compare them with the printed editions will readily notice how much more accurately corrected they appear on the basis of our codices. [Col. 0014C] Certainly it is no insignificant achievement that with their aid we have restored the name and martyrdom of Saturninus. Thus far the distinguished RUINART.

Moreover, the fact that our martyrs are commemorated as having fought so vigorously when they had scarcely yet been taught the rudiments of the Christian faith and were novices in our religion does not diminish but increases their glory. That is: “Let the one who has been deemed worthy of martyrdom rejoice with joy in the Lord; and even if he is a catechumen, let him depart without sorrow. For the suffering endured for Christ will be for him a more genuine baptism, because he himself dies to the Lord in actual experience, whereas the others do so in figure.” HOLSTE.]

## CHAPTER ONE

[Col. 0016B] **SUMMARY.—**The saints having been arrested, Saint Perpetua overcomes her father, is baptized with the others, and is thrust into a foul prison. Anxious about her infant, she understands from a vision granted to her—in which a ladder is raised toward heaven, and Saint Saturus and she herself ascend it and a morsel of food is offered—that martyrdom will occur very soon.

I. The young catechumens Revocatus [Col. 0017A] [Note: [Col. 0017D]

**Married as a matron.** The circumstance of marriage, especially in a woman, seems to increase the merit and worth of martyrdom because of the particular obstacles which that state presents in such a crisis. Hence Tertullian, book I, *To His Wife*, says that in times of persecution the burdens of marriage are exceedingly inconvenient and dangerous to faith: “For why did the Lord pronounce woe upon pregnant and nursing women, except because the impediments of children, etc.”—indicating that, whether women bear offspring in the womb or in their arms, they are greatly restrained by love of them from the readiness necessary at such a time to offer themselves for death.

Therefore, the fact that these two holy women, Perpetua and Felicitas, had children, one in her womb and the other at her breasts, demonstrates the heroic courage of each all the more. This was perhaps the reason why, although Revocatus, Secundulus, and the others completed their martyrdom on the same occasion, the Church nevertheless honored the memory of Perpetua and Felicitas alone with particular distinction.

But to return to Tertullian: in the same passage, considering the obstacles which marriage presents to women when martyrdom is possible, he proceeds to discourage his wife from marrying again after his death, because, as he says, “Widows are better unencumbered for enduring every affliction and persecution, with no marital burden swelling in the womb or at the breasts.” Therefore, women who broke through so many restraints and ran free and unencumbered toward the trophy of the Cross, trampling upon love for father, husband, and offspring, were regarded as having offered a far more praiseworthy example of heroic courage.

It may be asked here why no mention is made of Perpetua’s husband in these Acts. Was it because he was a Christian, as it seems possible rightly to infer from Perpetua’s own words a little below? Or because, when the woman had been found guilty of a graver crime, the marriage was then considered dissolved, as seems to be implied by law 5, *Digest*, *On the Property of Condemned Persons*? HOLSTE.

**An infant at the breast.** An infant is said to be “at the breast” on the same principle as “a servant at the threshold,” “a boy at hand,” “at one’s feet,” etc. Such children are called *sububeres* in Latin. Isidore’s glosses: “*Sububeres*: infants who are still beneath the breast”; in Greek, ὑποτίτθια, as in the Septuagint at Hosea 14:1; and at Joel 2:16, νήπια θηλάζοντα μαστούς, “little children sucking the breasts.” By others they are called ὑπομάζια or ὑπομάστια and ἐπιμάζια. Thus the Greeks called mares ὑπόπωλοι, αἷς πῶλοι ὕπησαν, “those beneath which there were foals,” as Homer says, *Iliad* XI. HOLSTE.

**II.—While we were still with the persecutors, etc.** How was Saint Perpetua, who wrote this, not “with the persecutors,” when at that very time she was being guarded by them in prison? Shall we say that she intended to indicate the change that occurred in her, which she shortly afterward expresses in these words: “We were taken into prison, and I was terrified because I had never experienced such darkness. O bitter day! There was intense heat because of the crowds, and extortion by the soldiers,” etc.? But after suffering such anguish for some time because of the weakness of nature, and then receiving a richer influx of grace, she says: “Immediately I recovered, and I was relieved of my distress, and the prison suddenly became a palace to me, so that I preferred to be there rather than anywhere else,” etc.

A passage from Tertullian’s book *To the Martyrs* may be relevant here. Comparing life in the world with life in prison, among the many advantages of the latter he includes these: “You are free from scandals and temptations, from evil recollections, and now also from persecution. Prison gives the Christian what the desert gave the prophets. Let us remove the name ‘prison’; let us call it a retreat.” HOLSTE.

**And persisted in casting me down out of his affection, etc.** The word “cast down,” transferred from boxers and the wrestling-ground, is extremely frequent among Christian writers in the sense of tempting and perverting. To omit Tertullian, I shall cite a single example from Cassian, *On the Spirit of Vainglory*, chapter 7: “The one whom it cannot cast down through honor, it trips through humility.” HOLSTE.

**Thus I too cannot call myself anything other than what I am—a Christian.** For it belongs to the upright and truthful, as the Greek iambic line has it, τὰ σῦκα σῦκα, τὴν σκάφην σκάφην λέγειν, “to call figs figs, and a trough a trough.” Tertullian rightly complains in the *Apology* that the pagans tortured Christians for confessing what they were, whereas under the laws people were customarily tortured when they denied what they were. Eusebius, book V, chapter 1, seems to allude to this in the words: ἀλλ’ οἱ μὲν ὁμολογοῦντες ὅπερ ἦσαν [Col. 0019B] συνεκλείοντο, ὡς Χριστιανοί, “But those who confessed what they were were shut up in prison as Christians.” HOLSTE.]

**But he only maltreated me.** The verb *vexare* is used here in the milder sense of a violent assault short of severe injury, although elsewhere it generally denotes, as Asconius Pedianus observes on the second *Verrine Oration*, the experience of a tremendous calamity. He confirms this from Virgil, who wrote that Scylla “maltreated the Dulichian ships.” Nevertheless, as is particularly relevant to our passage, the word *vexare*, according to Gellius, book I, chapter 6, is too mild and denotes too slight a hardship to match so dreadful an atrocity as people’s being suddenly seized and torn apart by a monstrously savage beast. He therefore maintains that the poet spoke precisely and pointedly there of the tossing of ships without shipwreck. “For one who is borne and seized,” he says, “and dragged this way and that is properly said to be *vexed*.” Thus I understand that Saint Perpetua was here battered with blows and kicks and dragged about by her enraged father.

Tertullian also includes wounds under “maltreatment,” since in *On Patience*, chapter 3, he says that Malchus was maltreated by Peter, who had of course wounded him with a sword. And in the final chapter of *To Scapula* he said that the Christian name was being maltreated when it was oppressed by a most severe persecution. HOLSTE.

**He was defeated together with the devil’s devices.** Thus Antonius in the *Life of Saint Symeon Stylites*, chapter 6: “When he made the sign of the cross”—before setting foot in the chariot offered him by the demon—“the devil immediately disappeared altogether; he vanished together with his argument,” etc. And Anastasius the Librarian, in *Benedict III*: “He conquered the flesh and the prince of the world, and all his most wicked arguments.” HOLSTE.

**Because I had been deprived of my father, I gave thanks to the Lord, etc.** Pontius in the *Life of Saint Cyprian*: “For us, ‘fatherland’ is a less cherished, ordinary name, since we abhor even our own parents if they counsel anything against the Lord.” HOLSTE.

**His absence refreshed me.** This explains what in Greek would be called ἀνέπαυσεν or δι­ανέπαυσεν—that is, “gave rest, assisted, brought consolation.” This is read in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VIII, chapter 10. HOLSTE.

**And the Spirit instructed me to ask nothing else from the water except endurance of the flesh.** The Holy Spirit, clearly, urges the baptized to ask for the grace of martyrdom. For this reason Tertullian, at the end of *On Flight in Persecution*, calls him “the Paraclete, the exhorter to endurance.”

Moreover, Cyprian speaks splendidly in accordance with the meaning of this passage in his *Letter to the Martyrs Designate*: “Nothing now occupies your hearts and minds except divine precepts and heavenly commandments, by which the Holy Spirit has always encouraged you to endure suffering.” By “from the water,” she means after receiving Baptism, or after the bath of saving water. Thus Tertullian more than once in *On Repentance*: “If we cease sinning from the waters onward.” And in *On Baptism*: “Therefore we too, someone will say, ought rather to fast from the bath onward.” Shortly afterward: “Then the Lord, after the water, withdrew into the desert.” Likewise book I, *To His Wife*: “For how many are there who immediately after the bath seal their flesh?” And in *Exhortation to Chastity*: “A second virginity from the second birth, [Col. 0020A] that is, from the bath.”

Tertullian likewise often calls Baptism simply “water,” as at the beginning of *On Baptism*: “Blessed is the sacrament of our water.” Saint Cyprian, moreover, teaches at length in Letter LXIII that whenever water alone is named in the Holy Scriptures, Baptism is proclaimed.

Furthermore, by this utterly remarkable example of Saint Perpetua the power of Baptism against the new sectarians is confirmed in a marvelous way. By the final words, “except endurance of the flesh,” Saint Perpetua signifies that, instructed by the Spirit whom she received in Baptism, she understood how necessary the endurance not merely of the soul but also of the body would be.

Tertullian speaks excellently on this subject in *On Patience*, chapter 13. After discussing up to that point patience situated solely in the soul, he adds that it also “labors in many ways through the body to win the Lord’s favor, since it was manifested by the Lord himself in the body’s endurance as well. For,” he adds, “the governing soul readily shares with its own dwelling the things brought in by the spirit.” And a little afterward: “What proceeds from the soul’s virtue is perfected in the flesh. Finally, the patience of the flesh does battle in persecutions. If flight presses upon it, the flesh serves amid the hardships of flight. If prison also oppresses it, the flesh is in chains, the flesh in the stocks, the flesh upon the ground, both in that poverty of light and in that deprivation of the world. But when it is brought forth for the trial of blessedness, for the occasion of a second immersion, for the very ascent to the divine seat, nothing is more necessary there than the patience of the body. If the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak for want of patience, where is the salvation of the spirit and of the flesh itself? Yet when the Lord says this of the flesh, pronouncing it weak, he shows what it needs in order to be strengthened: patience, of course, against every preparation for overthrowing or punishing the faith, so that it may endure with the utmost constancy blows, fire, the cross, beasts, and the sword.” For, as he adds below in chapter 15, “patience alone completes martyrdom.”

Cyprian follows his master when, in the same sense but different words, he says: “No consolations are more helpful than those of patience to persons who in the contest of persecution must abandon their estates, undergo prison, bear chains, spend their lives, and endure the sword, beasts, fires, crosses, and, finally, every kind of torture and punishment through the faith and strength of patience.” HOLSTE.

**We were taken into prison.** In the *Proceedings Concerning the Vindication of Caecilian and Felix*, p. 279: “Let him be taken into prison.” Likewise the *Passion of Saints Dativus, Saturninus*, etc., p. 290: “The longed-for prison received Christ’s martyrs.” Ulpian, law 1 of the *Digest*, *On the Custody of Defendants*: “The proconsul is accustomed to determine, regarding the custody of defendants, whether a person should be received into prison, handed over to a soldier, entrusted to sureties, or even committed to his own recognizance. He ordinarily makes this determination either according to the nature of the charge, or because of the person’s distinction or very ample resources, or because of the innocence of the person, or the rank of the accused.” Thus far Ulpian. Raevardus wrote most learnedly on the different kinds of custody in connection with this law, *Various Readings*, book III, chapter 12. HOLSTE.

**And I was terrified, etc.** You see how this most steadfast martyr was terrified by the horror of prison, although a little afterward she received the proconsul’s sentence and death itself not merely with a brave and fearless but even with a joyful spirit. From this it appears that the sufferings of prison were preliminary exercises for her future martyrdom and—to use Eusebius’s word—προκαταικίσματα, “preparatory ordeals,” which through the practice and habitual experience of evils strengthened body and soul for the endurance of torments and death. HOLSTE.]
