### IV.—At the last supper, which they call the “free supper”

Those condemned to the beasts ate an especially sumptuous supper openly on the day before the contest.

“They ate an agape.” We shall not describe what learned men have observed in various places concerning the agapes of the ancient Christians; among others, Albaspinaeus, Book I, Observation 18. The same subject is treated by Chrysostom, Homily 27 on 1 Corinthians 11; Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chapter 39; Cyprian, *Epistles*, Book III; and the Council of Gangra, canon 11. There were three kinds: birthday, wedding, and funeral agapes; Gregory Nazianzen, *On the Distinctions of Life*. Moreover, this form of expression was also customary for Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chapter 39: “Thereafter they depart, not into bands of cutthroats, but with the same concern for modesty and chastity, as persons who have partaken not so much of a supper as of discipline.” [Col. 0049D] HOLST.

“Friends today, enemies tomorrow.” Saturus says this to the pagans who were curiously watching the martyrs while they took part in the free supper on the day before the spectacle. He means that those who were then watching peacefully and almost amicably would on the following day demand their death with frenzied shouts. For when the beast-fighters were brought into the arena, the people changed from spectators into adversaries, as Seneca says in Book I of *On Anger*, chapter 2; see him also in *On Tranquillity*, chapter 10. HOLST.

### Chapter VI.—I. “The day of their victory dawned,” etc.

So Pontius the Deacon, who more than once echoes these Acts: “At last another day dawned—that appointed, promised, divine day.” HOLST.

“They came forth from prison.” Thus Pontius concerning Saint Cyprian: “He therefore came forth, now certain that what had long been delayed would be fulfilled: he came forth with a lofty and upright spirit, displaying cheerfulness in his face and courage in his heart.” HOLST.

“Joyful, into the amphitheater as though into heaven.” That is, [Col. 0050B] as Cyprian says on page 166: “Confident of the heavenly reward, the crown of martyrdom, and the kingdom of God.” HOLST.

“Comely in countenance.” Eusebius, Book V, chapter 1, concerning the martyrs of Vienne and Lyons: *οἱ μὲν ἱλαροὶ προσήεσαν, δόξης καὶ χάριτος πολλῆς ταῖς ὄψεσιν αὐτῶν συγκεκραμένης*: “They came forward joyfully, a certain splendor, suffused with abundant grace and beauty, shining in their faces.” Or, as Rufinus translates it: “They were brought forth from their torments rejoicing, displaying something somehow divine in their very countenances.” Conversely, those who had denied Christ were *κατηφεῖς καὶ ταπεινοὶ καὶ δυσειδεῖς, καὶ πάσης ἀσχημοσύνης ἀνάπλεοι*: “Sad, downcast, dreadful even in appearance, and disfigured by every deformity of shame.” HOLST.

“With radiant step.” Thus Xenophon in his *Apology of Socrates*, when Socrates, already condemned to death, was departing from the judges’ presence: *ἀπῄει καὶ ὄμμασι καὶ σχήματι καὶ βαδίσματι φαιδρός*: “He departed radiant in his eyes, bearing, and gait.” HOLST.

“From blood, from the midwife.” Hence Law 2 of the Code, *Concerning Parents Who Sell Their Children*, calls infants “bloodstained.” Juvenal likewise, Satire 7, calls them “still red from their mother.” And Law 1 of the Theodosian Code, *Concerning Exposed Children*: “Still drenched in the blood of the maternal womb, and newly born.” HOLST.

“To be washed in a second baptism.” That is, the baptism of blood and martyrdom. Celerinus the confessor, in Cyprian, Epistle 21: “Unless you have first washed in that holy blood for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.” HOLST.

“They were compelled to put on the costume—the men, indeed, that of the priests of Saturn” [Col. 0050D]. For, as Lactantius says in Book VI, chapter 20, the beast-hunts called *munera*, “spectacles,” were assigned to Saturn. Tertullian, *Scorpiace*, chapter 7, testifies that the Saturn of the Africans was customarily appeased in that age with human victims; he explains this more clearly in the *Apologeticum*, chapter 9, writing thus: “In Africa children were openly sacrificed to Saturn down to the proconsulate of Tiberius.” Condemned persons seem to have been substituted for the children, lest Saturn be deprived of a victim of human blood. Hence the same Tertullian teaches in *On the Testimony of the Soul*, chapter 2, that the priests of the bloodthirsty god wore a scarlet cloak; there, distinguishing the various costumes of pagan priests, he says: “And bound with Ceres’ fillet, and scarleted with Saturn’s cloak, and clad in Isis’ linen,” etc. Then in *On the Pallium*, chapter 4: “The ambition of the broader purple and the superstition of helmeted redness commend Saturn.” HOLST.

“But the women, that of those consecrated to Ceres.” Just as men condemned to the beasts were clothed in the scarlet cloak, which, as has been said, was the distinctive garment of Saturn’s priests [Col. 0051B], so women destined for the same punishment were bound with a fillet. For we have just heard from Tertullian that the priestesses of Ceres were bound with fillets; and he confirms the same in *On the Pallium*, chapter 4: “Why do you not look upon those costumes which falsely claim religion on the basis of their own novelty? For by their entirely white apparel, by the distinguishing fillet, and by the privilege of the cap, they are initiated into the rites of Ceres.” Women thus initiated were called “consecrated to Ceres.” An ancient inscription in Gruter, page CCCIX, nos. 2 and 3, presents Fabia Aconia Paulina as such a woman, consecrated to Ceres and Kore. ## CHAPTER VI

**SUMMARY.—**They are led joyfully from prison [Col. 0049B] into the amphitheater, especially Perpetua and Felicitas; they all refuse to put on profane garments; they are scourged; they long [Col. 0050A] for the beasts and are exposed to them; Saturus is twice unharmed; Saints Perpetua and Felicitas are thrown down; they are recalled to the Gate of Life. ### I

“That noble constancy resisted to the very end.” Tertullian, in *On Idolatry*, chapter 18, teaches, among the other kinds of idolatry, that defiled garments must also be refused by the faithful, since, having been dedicated to idolatry from the beginning, they bear the stain of their profanation. “No one,” [Col. 0051C] he says, “can appear clean in unclean things. These are his words: “Your head remained free from the impious and criminal veil with which the captive heads of those sacrificing were veiled there; a forehead pure with the sign of God cannot bear the devil’s crown.” Thus Cyprian. Moreover, the beast-fighters were adorned in these garments to embellish the games with their appearance, being led forth in procession before they were exposed to the beasts. Quintilian, *Declamation* 9: “The bodies of those about to perish, already displayed throughout the arena, had led the procession of their own death.” HOLST.

“We came to this of our own accord.” Thus Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chapter 49, refutes the pagans who vainly taunted Christians: [Col. 0051D] “As though,” he says, “everything you can do against us were not subject to our own choice. “Lest our freedom be obscured.” In an expression customary among Africans, *obducere* means to cloud, obscure, diminish, violate, or injure. “We have bound our lives over.” Perhaps one should read *addiximus*, “we have bound over,” rather than *adduximus*, “we have brought”—that is, we have subjected them to capital judgment. “The people, enraged.” Seneca, Book I of *On Anger*, chapter 2: “What of gladiators? “That they be tormented with scourges.” By the governor, that is, who could summarily inflict punishment with rods or scourges. Digest, Book IX, §3, *Concerning the Duties of a Proconsul*. HOLST.

“Before the line of beast-fighters.” That is, by the beast-fighters who stood ready equipped with scourges. Hence Tertullian, *To the Martyrs*: [Col. 0052C] “Others have walked between the hunters’ oxhide lashes with shoulders most enduring.” The same, Book I of *To the Nations*: “If a woman has exulted beneath the lashes, she too, after lately traversing the line among the beast-fighters, has measured out this path.” This passage from our Acts, the two passages we have adduced from Tertullian, and this entire *διαμαστίγωσις* (*diamastigōsis*), or scourging in the arena—which has hitherto not been adequately explained—are splendidly illuminated by a truly remarkable passage in Eusebius, Book V, chapter 1: *ὑπέφερον πάλιν τὰς διεξόδους τῶν μαστίγων τὰς ἐκεῖσε εἰθισμένας*: “They again passed through the line of scourges customary there.” Or: “According to custom, they again underwent scourging before the line of beast-fighters.” Here you see that “before the line” means “in front of the line,” or “while passing along the line,” etc. They plainly stood arranged in a long line with scourges; the naked beast-fighters were compelled to walk before them, each man striking the sufferers’ naked limbs with the oxhide lash he held in his hand. I think Damasus had this form of punishment in view in poem 5 on Saint Agatha, when he said: “Thereafter, more joyful and more ardent, she exultantly ran through all the scourges.”

Furthermore, it could easily have happened that, as we have said, the raging people demanded this scourging from the governor, since he presided over the spectacle. For, as Lipsius teaches in *On the Amphitheater*, page 15, the president of the spectacle watched from the podium—that is, from the first or lowest row of seats, where his tribunal or platform stood, as Lipsius likewise observes on page 27, at no great distance from the arena. “They rejoiced that they had obtained some share in the Lord’s sufferings,” etc. Namely, because they remembered that Christ had been scourged, they received the scourges with joy. Thus Dionysius of Alexandria, in Eusebius, Book VI, chapter 34, says that the martyr Nemesius, who was burned with robbers, was honored by a likeness to Christ’s Passion. Many similar passages occur in Tertullian and others. HOLST.

“When he had been bound fast for the bear.” And a little above: “He had fastened him to the boar,” etc. These words signify that the beast-fighters were customarily tied up so that they could not flee before the sight of the wild beasts that were to be sent against them. ### III.—“A most ferocious cow”

“Clothed in nets.” Thus Saint Blandina, after various kinds of torture, *εἰς γύργαθον βληθεῖσα ταύρῳ παρεβλήθη*: “Was cast into a net and exposed to a bull.” Rufinus, in his edition, noted the same as a novel and unusual punishment: “Then,” he says, “another kind of cruelty was devised: that, bound in a net, she should be exposed to a fiercely goaded bull. “Mindful more of modesty than of pain.” Tertullian, in *On Modesty*, chapter 1, calls chastity “the foremost discipline of the Christian name,” which, he says, “the world itself bears witness to this extent: whenever it strives to punish chastity in our women, it does so by defilements of the flesh rather than by torments, wishing to take from them what they value above life.” [Col. 0054C] See his *Apologeticum*, chapter 50; Victor of Vita concerning Dionysia; and Saint Hilary, page 289.

### IV.—“The soldier Pudens”

[Col. 0056A] Washed and saved!” Plainly he who had shone forth in this spectacle was indeed saved [Note: [Col. 0056B] “Had just washed,” Holstenius.].

Then he said to the soldier Pudens: “Farewell, and remember my faith. Do not let these things disturb you, but let them strengthen you.” At the same time he asked for the loop [Note: [Col. 0056B] “Ring,” Salisbury manuscript.] from his finger, dipped it into his wound, and returned it to him, leaving him the inheritance of a pledge and a memorial of blood [Note: [Col. 0056B] The Salisbury manuscript has: “He returned it to him as a blessed inheritance, leaving him a pledge and a memorial of such great blood.”].

Then, already unconscious, he was laid down with the others in the customary place for execution. And when the people demanded that they be brought into the center, so that, as the sword penetrated their bodies, they might add their eyes as accomplices in the homicide, they rose of their own accord and moved to the place the people desired. First, however, they kissed one another, so that they might consummate their martyrdom through the solemn rite of peace.
