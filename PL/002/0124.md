For one willing to be washed. For I greatly prefer this reading to “unwilling,” which my codex has; for although at first he had refused, doubtless as being unworthy, yet soon afterward he assented all the more readily.

They were destined. For after the Resurrection Christ was going to command the Apostles: “Go, teach all nations, baptizing them,” etc. But Christ’s Baptism was the gateway to the Church, and a nearer and more [Col. 1179B] direct way to salvation than John’s baptism.

He was baptized. And indeed with John’s baptism, which in others was nothing but a baptism of repentance unto the remission of sins (Mark 1:4); but now, through Christ its institutor, it was being elevated to the character of a Sacrament, as our theologians more commonly hold.

It was not necessary. For it should not be said that the Apostles—being, of course, human beings—were at that time free from every sin that could be washed away through Baptism, and hence that they were not themselves subjects for the baptism of repentance.

Rivals [Aemuli], i.e., adversaries, enemies, or the envious, whom shortly afterward he calls those opposing the Lord. Thus also Our Author, in the *Apology*, ch. 21, speaks of fables as “rivals bent upon the destruction of the truth”; and similarly elsewhere quite often. It is established, however, that in other places this word is also taken in a favorable sense, for zealous imitators.

It is suggested, i.e., an argument is supplied—namely, one taught from the contrary, almost as though, according to Jesus Christ’s formulation, “the exception confirms the rule in [Col. 1179C] the contrary case.”

The discipline of religion, i.e., according to the discipline, rite, or ordinance of Religion. To discipline, moreover, belong all things ordained for common observance in the worship of God, whether by human beings appointed to govern the Church or by God himself; although those things that are only of human ordinance are called matters of mere discipline. And although in Tertullian elsewhere *disciplina* also means doctrine, nevertheless, in the stricter sense, that which proceeds from one who teaches seems to be called doctrine, while that doctrine, once received by learners and formed into their conduct, is called discipline.

Through patience. That is, the forbearance and permission by which he endures, or allows, these things to occur. In this sense Cicero says in the eighth action against Verres: “I shall not bear it, I shall not endure it, I shall not permit it”; and in Book I of *On Duties* he speaks of those who allow the people whom they ought to protect to be abandoned.

At the last. This is a Hellenism; for the Greeks customarily say ἐπ’ ἐσχάτων, “at the last,” which Latin writers commonly express by *tandem*, *ultimato*, or *denique*: “at length,” “finally,” or “lastly.” [Col. 1179D]

Provided only that it refers. For in John 13:10 Christ said to Peter: “He who has been WASHED needs only to wash his feet”; perhaps showing that we need only one washing of the body, or baptism, and that Baptism, once received, cannot afterward be repeated.

To deem. He seems here to use the verb *aestimare* in the sense of judging or deciding—as though it would be rash to make any unfavorable judgment concerning the salvation of the Apostles, even if perhaps they had not received Baptism.

Of enrollment. Or, of admission into the Apostolate.

A compendium of Baptism. That is, that singular prerogative would serve them in place of Baptism and, as by a compendium and shorter way, would supply it in advance.

Could confer. Junius judges that this entire passage was astonishingly mangled by an unskilled scribe through an inept transposition of words, and that it should accordingly be restored thus: “And thence the prerogative of inseparable familiarity WITH HIM [CUM ILLO] [Col. 1180A] could confer a compendium of Baptism. I suppose they followed him,” etc. Although I do not think this remedy for the passage should be rejected at all, I nevertheless think the received reading can also be sustained, so that the sense of the new sentence is: “With that”—namely, with that compendious supplement for Baptism—“they followed him,” etc.

Made whole. For he had used these words to the woman whom he delivered from the issue of blood, Matthew 9:22: “Take heart, daughter; your FAITH has made you whole.” He also addressed them to the Samaritan cleansed of leprosy, Luke 17:19, and shortly afterward, in ch. 18:42, to the blind man of Jericho whose sight he restored. And these obtained through that most living faith not merely bodily health, but far more the salvation of the soul—a faith to which salvation had already elsewhere and repeatedly been promised by Christ, as in John 3:16: “God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son, that everyone who believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.” And in the same place, v. 36: “He who believes in the Son of Man has eternal life.” Nor is this surprising, since salvation had also been promised to Christ’s Baptism only when received [Col. 1180B] with faith: “He who believes and is baptized shall be saved; but he who does not believe”—even if already baptized—“shall be condemned.”

Yet not baptized. For nowhere do we read that they had previously been baptized by Christ or by the Apostles.

Whose faith. That is, in what other persons, or among what others, faith is to be required. He immediately adds examples.

Raised up. Junius reads: “He who by one word,” etc. And indeed the context seems to require that the little word “who” at least be understood, in this way: “He who was raised up by one word of the Lord,” etc.

The tax booth. Τελώνιον, or, as Suidas has it, τελωνεῖον, strictly taken, is the place ἐν ᾧ, as he says, καθέζεται ὁ τελώνης, “in which the tax collector sits.” The latter, or *telonarius*, is so called ἀπὸ τοῦ τέλους—because among the Greeks τέλος sometimes also means a tax or tribute—and from ὠνεῖν, which among them means “to buy.” For in those times they generally held the taxes by purchasing the right to collect them for an annual payment; hence they were properly called τελῶναι, “purchasers of taxes,” [Col. 1180C] and among the Romans *publicani*, as persons charged with public money—money, namely, to be paid into the public treasury or farmed from it—or collectors acting under such officials. This latter class of men, because of their importunity and greed and the very wicked practices associated with them, was hated almost everywhere, but especially by the Jews, who more than others considered themselves freeborn and born for liberty, crying out against Christ himself in John 8:33: “We are Abraham’s seed; we have never served anyone.” Hence tax collectors or publicans of this kind were regarded as public sinners, indeed as heathens, from whose fellowship one ought likewise to abstain. Hence the Pharisees rose up against Christ and his disciples, Matthew 9:11, saying: “Why does your Teacher eat with publicans and sinners?” And in the same Evangelist, ch. 18:17, Christ himself, speaking to his disciples about fraternal correction, says: “If he will not hear the Church, let him be to you as a heathen and a publican.”

Father. He alludes here to Peter and Andrew, to whom [Col. 1180D] Christ said: “Come after me, and I will make you fishers of men.” And they immediately left their nets and followed him, Matthew 4:20. Most especially, when he called James and John, they immediately left their nets and their father and followed him, ibid., v. 22. Indeed, they had also left their mother, who afterward, in Matthew 20:20, was so deeply concerned on their behalf about securing primacy in the kingdom of heaven.

He disregarded. Clement of Alexandria, in Book III of the *Stromata*, believed this to have been St. Philip the Apostle, who, when the Lord called him in Matthew 8:21–22, said: “Lord, permit me first to go and bury my father.” But Jesus said to him: “FOLLOW ME, and leave the dead to bury their dead.” Yet what prevents us from appending here that entire passage of Tertullian from *On Idolatry*, ch. 12, which also agrees with the examples cited above? “Even then,” he says, “it was demonstrated to us that pledges, crafts, and occupations must be abandoned for the Lord’s sake: when James and [Col. 1181A] John, called by the Lord, leave both father and boat; when Matthew is raised from the tax booth; when even burying one’s father was too slow for faith.” I promise, moreover, that one will not regret reading this entire chapter.

Which he heard. For the earlier calling of the disciple had already occurred previously—namely, Matthew 8:21—whereas only afterward, in Matthew 10:37, was this saying, “He who … father,” etc., pronounced by Christ.

## Notes on Chapter XIII

They provoke, i.e., stir up or draw forth.

Unless. “But only if” would be clearer.

Pleased. “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness,” Genesis 15:6. Upon these very words the Apostle says at Galatians 3:6: “Know, therefore, that those who are of faith are the sons of Abraham,” etc., etc. This faith, however, was certainly not a Sacrament in the proper sense.

They prevail. This is in the nature of a reply to the argument raised. It rests on the commonly received rule of law: “Later provisions derogate from earlier ones.”

[Col. 1181B] Enlarged, i.e., amplified and extended more widely, namely to more objects.

And his Resurrection. Namely, as things no longer future, but already past.

Sacrament, i.e., mystery, or mystery of faith.

The sealing of Baptism. For by Our Author himself, in *On the Shows*, ch. 24, it is called the “SEAL OF FAITH.” And in *On Repentance*, ch. 6, he says: “That washing is the sealing of faith”; and thus faith is reciprocally the sealing of Baptism. For, as St. Basil says in *On the Holy Spirit to Amphilochius*, ch. 12: “Faith is perfected through Baptism, while Baptism rests upon the foundation of faith,” etc. And in Book III *On the Holy Spirit against Eunomius*, he also calls Baptism the “SEAL OF FAITH.”

A certain garment of faith. Thus St. Gregory Nazianzen, in Oration 40, *On Baptism*, calls this Sacrament ἀφθαρσίας ἔνδυμα, “the garment of incorruption,” and, as he says a little afterward, αἰσχύνης κάλυμμα, “a covering of shame.” St. Cyril of Jerusalem also calls it a white garment, both because of the whiteness it imparts to the soul when the soot of sins has been wiped away [Col. 1181C], and because of the rite by which the baptized were immediately clothed in a white garment, which they were to wear for a fixed period. Even now the Rituals prescribe this in a certain manner both in the Baptism of infants and in that of adults, and the custom is still retained in the Church.

Previously. That is, before, namely in the Old Testament.

Bare. For that faith of the Old Law was in itself bare, empty, and void, and destitute of all intrinsic power for eternal salvation.

Power, i.e., force and efficacy.

Without its law. Namely, before that Old Law was fulfilled by the new Law of Christ.

Into the name. Matthew 28:19, where the Greek text reads altogether explicitly εἰς τὸ ὄνομα, “into the name,” while the Vulgate has “in the name.” It is well known, however, that among the Greeks εἰς is sometimes used for ἐν, as in Aeschines: εἰς τὴν ἐκκλησίαν καθέζομαι, “I sit in the assembly”; and no less so in Demosthenes and Xenophon. In this sense, therefore, our Vulgate [Col. 1181D] Translator understood εἰς τὸ ὄνομα, and theologians and interpreters generally have followed him.

It might, however, be asked here whether a Baptism conferred with this formula would be valid: “I baptize you into the name of the Father,” etc. I answer that it could at least appear doubtful, and therefore that, without grave sin—since the matter is likewise grave—the words “in the name” cannot be changed to “into the name”; hence such a Baptism should at least be repeated conditionally.

Some, however, think that an error lies hidden here, and that in other copies ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι, “in the name,” is actually found, so that the entire difficulty disappears of itself. Moreover, that law of Baptism was imposed by Christ not only upon the Apostles, that they should administer it, but also upon the nations, that they should receive it, Mark, last chapter, v. 16, in these words: “He who believes and is baptized shall be saved”—the sense being that in the New Law salvation can be obtained in no other way than through Baptism.

## Notes on Chapter XIV

[Col. 1182A]

The household of Stephanas. For shortly beforehand he had said: “I give thanks to GOD that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius. I also baptized the household of Stephanas,” 1 Corinthians 1:14, 16. This Stephanas had been among the disciples of St. Paul.

He assigns, i.e., he ascribes his Baptism to him as though to its author or bestower. In nearly this sense he says in *Exhortation to Chastity*, ch. 16: “It was the old dispensation, which is assigned to the Gospel.” And Solinus: “Because of the odd-numbered days, January is allotted to the gods above; February, as being ominous, is allotted to the gods below.”

Apollo. Since some in the Corinthian Church had been baptized by Paul, others by Peter, and others by Apollo, a dispute and something like a schism had arisen among them, while some preferred their baptism to that of others as having been given by a more eminent minister. They said: “I indeed am of Paul; but I of Apollo; and I of Cephas; and I of Christ,” 1 Corinthians 1:12. But Paul reproved them all rather sharply, insisting that the efficacy of Baptism should be ascribed not to its ministers, [Col. 1182B] but to Christ alone as its sole author and institutor: “Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?” And he presses these points much more forcefully in ch. 3:4ff.

Moreover, this Apollo—as he is described in Acts 18:24ff.—was a certain Jew, an Alexandrian by birth, an eloquent man and mighty in the Scriptures. Although he had not yet been initiated into Christ, but only with John’s baptism, nevertheless, being fervent in spirit, “he spoke and carefully taught the things concerning Jesus”; afterward Aquila and Priscilla explained to him the way of the Lord more carefully (Acts 18:25–26), and probably also initiated him with Christ’s Baptism. Accordingly, when he subsequently proceeded into Achaia and Corinth (ibid., v. 27, and ch. 19:1), and there joined many to the Church through Baptism, some of the Corinthians used this occasion to contend more zealously for his glory than was proper. This so displeased Apollo himself that, offended by it, he instead withdrew [Col. 1182C] to Crete with Zenas the lawyer, as St. Jerome testifies in his commentary on the Epistle to Titus, ch. 3; nor would he return until the dissenting Corinthians had been reconciled with one another. He is believed afterward to have been appointed their bishop, although others maintain that he became bishop of Caesarea, Iconium, Dura, or elsewhere.

Moreover, this name *Apollo* is not the same as that of the god Apollo. For the latter in Greek is Ἀπόλλων, ωνος, of the fifth declension, while the former is Ἀπολλώς—as it is written in the Greek copies—of the fourth Attic declension, whose genitive is Ἀπολλώ. Indeed, in the accusative too it is found retaining the same ending in Acts 19:1: Ἐγένετο δὲ ἐν τῷ τὸν Ἀπολλὼ εἶναι ἐν Κορίνθῳ, “And it happened while Apollo was at Corinth”; and Titus 3:13: Ζηνᾶν τὸν νομικὸν καὶ Ἀπολλὼ σπουδαίως πρόπεμψον, “Diligently send forward Zenas the lawyer and Apollo.” Or perhaps it was altogether indeclinable.

Peaceable. For his correction had the sole purpose of restoring peace and concord of mind among the Corinthians.

[Col. 1182D] To defend. Here it is readily taken to mean “to vindicate” or “to attribute.” It can also be explained as meaning “to defend himself and his own cause”; lest, therefore, he might appear to be doing this, he gives thanks to God that he baptized no one. See above, the note “The household of Stephanas.”

To baptize. For in Matthew, last chapter, v. 19, Christ himself commanded the Apostles: “Go therefore and TEACH all nations, BAPTIZING,” etc. And in Mark, last chapter, v. 15: “PREACH the Gospel to every creature. He who believes and is BAPTIZED,” etc. Teaching is therefore prior in the dignity and authority of institution, and baptizing subsequent; in other respects, however, Baptism has the greater excellence, as being that for which preaching itself serves solely as preparation.

## Notes on Chapter XV

Is discussed, i.e., whether anything further can be raised against Baptism, beyond those questions, namely, which we have already debated and examined.

I shall resume. *Retexere* here does not mean to undo a weaving again, as we read that Penelope formerly used to do, but to weave again or continue weaving—that is, to proceed in a matter once begun [Col. 1183A] and interrupted for a time.

I omitted. I think this refers to ch. 12, where he had begun to speak about the unity of Baptism. There he omitted, or interrupted, it, in the sense in which Horace said in the *Art of Poetry*:

> Let him defer most things and omit them for the present.

To cut through. That is, lest I should entirely separate the thoughts close at hand, or what remained to be said, and thus break off the whole web, as though cutting through it.

Baptism. Just as the Greeks say τὸ βάπτισμα, so certain Latin writers—not only our Tertullian, but also St. Ambrose throughout—customarily say *Baptismum, -mi* in the neuter gender.

One Church in heaven. He doubtless alludes to what the Apostle says in Ephesians 4:5: “One LORD, one FAITH, one BAPTISM.” For, as the same Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 12:13, “in ONE SPIRIT we were all baptized into ONE BODY.” Hence Clement [Col. 1183B] of Alexandria also says in Book IV of the *Stromata*, p. 326 of my edition: “The Lord washes believers through one Baptism into a wholly perfect communion, he who also comprehends the many baptisms of Moses in one Baptism.”

Our Author has, however, used a certain transposition of words here, mentioning Baptism in the second place and Faith in the third. Faith he seems to denote through the Church, of which the former is, as it were, the soul, while the latter is the proper seat of true Faith and Religion.

Nor is it surprising that he spoke of the Church “in heaven,” since throughout the Gospels it is called the Kingdom of heaven, as in Matthew 13:44: “The Kingdom of heaven is like a man who sowed,” etc., etc.; and v. 31: “The Kingdom of heaven is like a grain of mustard seed,” etc. And so repeatedly in the same chapter, where it is certain that our Church is designated: ordained by God in heaven from eternity, planted by Christ on earth, united in the unity of Baptism, and finally consummated in heaven through vision and the love of fruition.

[Col. 1183C] For it was issued to us. Some understand it thus: “For that saying of Holy Scripture”—namely, about the Unity of Baptism, as immediately above—“was directed to us,” or has reference to us, Christ’s faithful.

Of communion. From this passage alone it is evident that the custom of abstaining from communion with heretics is most ancient in the Church. Add also *On Prescription*, ch. 30.

Us and them. He here lashes Cerdon, Marcion, Apelles, Hermogenes, and others of the same bran, who produced from their deranged brains two Gods—or indeed, as Valentinus did, a whole swarm of them. See *On Prescription*, chs. 7, 30, 33, and 40 at the end, and throughout the Appendix, ch. 49.

That is, the same. Here he attacks Valentinus in particular, who most impiously asserted that Christ was brought forth from the Demiurge and Sophia. See again *On Prescription*, ch. 7.

They do not possess. There are indeed some who strive to clear Tertullian here of every stain of error, as though he had spoken only of those heretics who adulterated the [Col. 1183D] legitimate form of Baptism, such as the Gnostics, Marcites, Cataphrygians, and others in Tertullian’s time. But it is clear that he speaks of heretics universally, and he confirms the same thing in *On Modesty*, ch. 19: “Among us,” he says, “the heretic, being equal to a heathen—indeed worse than a heathen—is admitted through the Baptism of truth,” that is, true Baptism, “after being cleansed of both persons,” namely, of that heathen and of the superimposed heretic—admitted, that is, to the Church.

It remains, therefore, that Tertullian erred seriously in the passage before us; nor does his argument establish its conclusion. For although those heretics discussed above erred monstrously concerning God and Christ, nevertheless, so long as they did not err in administering the Sacrament itself, but employed the same matter and form that our true Christ instituted in his true Church, this was the same Baptism, consisting of the same essential parts, as we call them, [Col. 1184A] and having the same Author. Our Baptism can therefore be conferred even outside our Church, if it is conferred duly and in the same manner as in our Church. For, as St. Augustine reasons in Book V *Against the Donatists*, ch. 26: “Just as it is said, ‘One Church, One Baptism,’ so it is said, ‘One Hope.’” The holy Doctor has in view Ephesians 4:1: “One body and One Spirit, as you were called in the One Hope of your calling,” and what immediately follows: “One Lord, One faith, One Baptism,” etc. “And yet,” he continues, “those who did not have One Hope possessed One Baptism; for how did those who said, ‘Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die,’ asserting that there was no resurrection of the dead, have One Hope with the holy and the righteous?”

Therefore, those who do not have one faith and are not in one Church with us can nevertheless possess and confer one Baptism. For Baptism is the gateway and door of the Church, through which alone entrance into it lies open; it is therefore given to a person not because he already is, but so that he may be, in the Church. Just as a wicked minister of Baptism [Col. 1184B] does not have grace in himself and yet confers it upon another through Baptism, so too a heretic, even if perhaps he himself has not validly received Baptism, can nevertheless confer it validly upon another, open for him that door of the Church, and admit the other through it, while himself remaining outside the door. But we shall speak more conveniently and precisely about this matter in its proper place.

Meanwhile, however, what has been said makes it evident that the contrary opinion arose in the Church of Carthage as the second century was drawing to its close, and was afterward adopted by Agrippinus, its bishop, and much more forcefully confirmed by St. Cyprian. The history of those times is full of this matter, which our dogmatic theologians customarily present at greater length.

Yet a new doubt arises here: whether Agrippinus derived this error from Tertullian, or Tertullian from Agrippinus, for it is established that they were contemporaries. Although this matter cannot be defined with sufficient clarity amid such uncertainty about when Agrippinus began his episcopate, [Col. 1184C] we nevertheless judge it more probable that Tertullian was led into this opinion by Agrippinus, and consequently that Agrippinus was the first author of that error in Africa known to us.

Indeed, first, Vincent of Lérins, a writer of the fifth century, expressly testifies concerning Agrippinus in ch. 9 of his *Commonitorium* in these words: “First of all mortals”—in Africa, I understand, for it is established that the custom of rebaptizing heretics had already flourished in the East long before—“contrary to the divine Canon,” etc., “he judged that they should be rebaptized.”

Secondly, St. Cyprian also, in his Letter to Jubaianus, mentions Agrippinus as the very first: “Among us,” he says, “it is not a new and sudden practice to judge that those who come from the heretics to the Church should be baptized, since many years and a long age have now passed from the time when very many bishops, assembling together under Agrippinus, decreed this,” etc.

Thirdly, St. Augustine, speaking in Book II *On Baptism*, ch. 7, about the ancient custom of the Churches, says: “It is more truly believed to have begun to be corrupted through Agrippinus than to have been corrected.” But this corruption should instead have been imputed [Col. 1184D] to Tertullian if he had publicized this error before Agrippinus.

Fourthly and finally, Tertullian himself, at the very beginning of this chapter, sufficiently signifies by the words “for it was issued to us” that he had received this as something established by a solemn decree in the African Churches, and consequently had not invented it by his own ingenuity.

Others object, however, that since Tertullian wrote his book *On Baptism* while still a Catholic and, as we said in the *Critical Questions on On Prescription*, Question VII, around A.D. 197—when perhaps Agrippinus had not yet been appointed to the see of Carthage—and since the persecution begun against the Christians in 202 continued nearly until 213, a council certainly could not have been celebrated before that year—because during the persecution public assemblies of Christians had been prohibited with extreme severity by imperial edict—and hence only about eighteen years after the publication of the book *On Baptism*, when peace had finally been restored to the Church.

[Col. 1185A] Yet what prevents us, amid such uncertainty concerning the African bishops, from assigning the beginning of Agrippinus’s episcopate and the Council itself under Agrippinus before the year 196 [text uncertain: printed “166”]? This certainly seems more consistent with Cyprian’s testimony cited above. For in the Letter to Jubaianus already cited, he expressly testifies that “many years” and “a long age” had passed since the bishops assembled under Agrippinus, etc. But if this Council was held only after the persecution, namely in the year 215, [Col. 1186A] and that Letter was written only in 256, not very many years—only forty-one—and consequently not a long age, would have elapsed since that Council of Carthage under Agrippinus. But if we place this same Council in A.D. 194, it will be at least sixty-two years removed from the time when Cyprian wrote, a span that can finally constitute an age. It is better, however, to bring the whole matter, according to Pagi’s chronology—which we already adopted elsewhere in *On Prescription*—under a single view.

[Col. 1185B] You see, therefore, benevolent Reader, that in this system Agrippinus is neither placed too close to Cyprian nor removed an unduly great distance from him, and that the intervening interval could readily have been filled by Donatus, the intermediate bishop—perhaps, moreover, one or two others intervened as well.

Finally, just as this error brought no stain upon St. Cyprian and Agrippinus, so neither did it upon Tertullian, since the dogma that heretics should not be rebaptized had not yet been defined by the Church. Indeed, other Fathers distinguished by holiness of life also thought that this matter pertained solely to the discipline of the Church. Hence, even after the deaths of St. Stephen the Pontiff and St. Cyprian, this opinion and practice of rebaptizing heretics, or those coming from heresy to the Church, did not immediately fall into disuse; nor even after the First Council of Arles, held in 315, and the Council of Nicaea, celebrated in 325—although one or the other of these is believed to have been that plenary Council at which St. Augustine says, in Book I *On Baptism*, ch. 7 and elsewhere, that this controversy was fully settled. For even [Col. 1185C] St. Basil, who died only in 379, left the matter free to each Church, while himself still following the practice of the rebaptizers in his own Church, as Tournely admits in his *Treatise on the Sacraments in General*, q. 7, art. 2, p. 100 of my edition and following. But we shall deal with this matter elsewhere in due course.

In Greek. There can be no doubt that Tertullian was also highly proficient in the Greek language, and that he had previously written another book on Baptism in that language, in which he dealt chiefly with Baptism invalidly received from heretics and therefore to be repeated when they wished to come to our Church. Moreover, in *On the Soldier’s Crown*, ch. 6, he testifies that he had also treated the subject of the shows in Greek for the sake of those fond of spectacles; and immediately at the beginning of the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, he clearly intimates that he had already treated this very argument in Greek. It is regrettable, however, that these and his other Greek works, long since consumed by age, have perished. [Col. 1186B]

Sins are washed away. Through Baptism, namely; sins committed afterward, however, must certainly be washed away by the tears of Repentance.

Jewish Israel. ISRAEL, as Our Author interprets it in Book IV *Against Marcion*, ch. 39, means “growing strong with God,” or, according to others, as though “he has prevailed over God.” The context of Genesis 32:28 itself also agrees very well, where the Angel says to Jacob: “Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but () ISRAEL; because if () YOU HAVE BEEN STRONG, or have prevailed, () [Col. 1186C] AGAINST GOD, how much more shall you prevail against men!”

He is defiled. Innumerable washings were prescribed for the Jews in both Exodus and Leviticus, each of which it would certainly be superfluous labor to enumerate. Yet all these washings were of themselves powerless to cleanse the soul; they remedied only legal and bodily stains and impurities. They also had many other washings derived from the emptiest traditions and customarily observed even to the point of ostentation, as stated in Mark 7:3ff.: “The Pharisees and all the Jews do not eat unless they have frequently washed their hands, holding the tradition of the elders; and when they come from the marketplace, unless they are baptized, they do not eat; and there are many other things handed down to them to observe, the baptisms of cups and pitchers,” etc. Against these, therefore, Christ himself inveighs in the same place, v. 6: “Isaiah rightly prophesied about you hypocrites, as it is written: This people honors me with its lips, but its heart is far from me. . . . For leaving the commandment of God, you hold the tradition of men, the baptisms of pitchers and cups,” etc.

But in Hebrews 9:9ff., the Apostle, speaking of the old tabernacle and the gifts and sacrifices offered in it, says: “These cannot perfect the worshiper according to conscience, consisting only in foods and drinks, and various baptisms and ordinances of the flesh,” etc. [Col. 1187A]

Which does not make sport of sinners. The sense appears to be: which does not mock sinners with an ineffectual result, namely by disappointing those who place their hope in it and deservedly exposing them to ridicule, as happened through those sordid washings of the Pharisees.

It defiles. Superstitious washings of this kind washed the body, but at the same time infected the soul with a new stain, hypocrisy. [Col. 1187B] See the note “He is defiled.”

## Notes on Chapter XVI

One and the same. For it cannot be repeated.

To be baptized with a baptism. Luke 12:50, where he speaks of his impending Passion, which in Mark 10:38 he also designates by the name of Baptism, saying thus to the sons of Zebedee: “Can you drink the cup that I am to drink, or be baptized with the baptism with which I am baptized?”

He wrote. 1 John 5:6: “This is he who came by WATER and BLOOD, Jesus Christ; not in Water only, but in WATER and BLOOD.” Here some codices have δι’ ὕδατος, καὶ αἵματος, καὶ πνεύματος, i.e., “through water and blood and Spirit.” Others judge this last word superfluous and rightly omitted, because there immediately follows: “And it is the Spirit who testifies, because Christ is the Truth. For there are Three who bear testimony in heaven: the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit; and these Three are One. And there are three who bear testimony on earth: [Col. 1187C] the Spirit, the Water, and the Blood; and these three are one.”

Might be glorified. For thus St. Peter, reproaching the Jews for their faithlessness in Acts 3:13, says: “God glorified his Son Jesus, whom you delivered up . . . and you killed the Author of life, whom God raised from the dead,” etc. Those words that St. Ambrose summarized so elegantly in his *Commentary on Luke*, ch. 22, accord wonderfully with these: “There are three witnesses, Water, Blood, and Spirit: Water for the washing; Blood for the price; Spirit for the resurrection.” Likewise, in Book I *On the Holy Spirit*, ch. 6: “Water is the witness of burial; Blood is the witness of death; Spirit is the witness of life.”

He doubtless drew these statements from the Apostle himself, who speaks thus in Romans 6:4: “For we were buried with him through Baptism into death, so that, just as Christ rose from the dead, we too might walk in newness of life.” And in Colossians 2:12 he addresses his Colossians thus: “You were buried together in Baptism, in which you also rose again through faith in the operation of God, who raised him from the dead.” Thus it was only after his Passion and the shedding of his blood that Christ was glorified in the Resurrection, [Col. 1187D] as he himself declared to the disciples going to Emmaus, Luke 24:26: “Was it not necessary for Christ to suffer these things and so enter into his glory?”

The elect. He certainly alludes to Matthew 20:16 and 22:14: “Many are called, but few are chosen.”

Had washed, i.e., had washed away their sins. Observe, however, the difference—if we wish to speak strictly—between *lavare*, “to wash,” and *lavari*, “to be washed.” “In baths,” says Varro in Book VIII *On the Latin Language*, “we both wash and are washed. But since usage was content with one of the two, we use ‘we are washed’ of the whole body, but ‘we wash’ of the parts, as when we say, ‘I wash my hands,’ and likewise the feet and other parts. Hence in the baths people do not correctly say, ‘I washed’; ‘I washed my hands’ is correct, but because ‘I am washed’ in the baths, the expression is ‘I have been washed.’”

[Col. 1188A] Might drink the Blood. To me, at least, there is no doubt that the Sacrament of the Holy Eucharist is indicated here, in which we drink the Blood of Our Lord Jesus Christ, not a figure of blood; for we are not said to be given figures of liquids to drink. Tertullian therefore judged that in that Sacrament we truly drink the Blood itself—the very Blood that flowed forth in Christ’s Passion, chiefly from his most sacred side. For concerning this he himself said in John 6:55ff.: “My flesh is truly food, and my blood is truly drink. He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me,” etc. Also worthy of citation here is the passage from *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 8, where Our Author says: “The flesh feeds upon the body and blood of Christ, so that the soul may be made fat upon God.” He supposes the same thing in *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 13.

Baptism. For when the mouth is given the Eucharistic Blood to drink, the soul is also inwardly washed. But here he means rather the Baptism of martyrdom, in which our blood is poured out and, by virtue of Christ’s Blood shed for us in his Passion and upon the Cross, cleanses the soul from every stain of guilt and [Col. 1188B] punishment, no less than the Baptism of water itself. “The shedding of blood for Christ,” says St. Thomas, *Summa Theologiae*, III, q. 66, art. 12, in the body of the article, “and the inward operation of the Holy Spirit are called Baptisms insofar as they produce the effect of the Baptism of water; while the Baptism of water has its efficacy from the Passion of Christ and the Holy Spirit.”
