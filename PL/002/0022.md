## CHAPTER XXI

“Therefore,” you say, “if you prejudge that [Col. 0216B] all things were made from nothing because it is not expressly related that anything was made from preexistent matter, take care [Note: [Col. 0216D] “Lest the opposing party.” Above: “Hence the opposing party strives to support its suspicions by faith.” RIG.] lest the opposing party contend that all things were made from matter precisely because it is likewise not openly stated that anything was made from nothing.”

Some arguments can certainly be turned back easily; but they are not therefore to be admitted as equally valid where the grounds of the case differ. For I say that, even if Scripture did not expressly declare that all things were made from nothing, just as it did not declare that they were made from matter, there was not so great a necessity for expressly indicating that all things were made from nothing as there would have been if they had been made from matter. For what is made from nothing is shown, by the very fact that it is not shown to have been made from anything, to have been made from nothing; [Note: [Col. 0216D] “And there is no danger that it may be thought to have been made from something,” etc. He uses this construction with the verb *periclitor* elsewhere also, in imitation of the Greeks, who use the verb κινδυνεύω in this way. [Col. 0217C] “There is no danger,” that is, “it is absent; there is no risk.” RHEN.] and there is no danger of its being thought to have been made from something when that from which it was made is not demonstrated.

But unless something made from something is expressly declared to have been made from that thing, then, because that from which it was made is not shown [Note: [Col. 0216C] *Non* is absent from Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür.], it will first risk appearing to have been made from nothing, because that from which it was made is not stated. Next, even if its condition is such that it cannot appear not to have been made from something, [Note: [Col. 0216C] “To appear to have been made from something,” Semler, Oberthür. Junius reads: “so that it can now appear to have been made from something.”] it will likewise risk appearing to have been made from something entirely different from that from which it actually was made, because the source from which it was made is not set forth.

Thus, even if Scripture had not added that God made all things from nothing (2 Macc. 7:28), supposing that God could not have made them from nothing, it ought in every way to have declared that he made them from matter, if he had indeed [Note: [Col. 0217C] *Etsi* is absent from Semler and Oberthür.] made them from [Note: [Col. 0217C] “Even if from,” Semler, Oberthür.] matter; for the former could be understood absolutely even if it were not expressly indicated, whereas the latter would remain doubtful unless it were indicated.

## CHAPTER XXIII

But he argues from what follows, because it is written: “The earth, however, was invisible and unformed.” [Col. 0218B] [Note: [Col. 0218D] “For he also reduces the name ‘earth.’” Here he approaches more closely to Hermogenes’ error, whose origin he shows to have lain in his belief that matter was designated by the name “earth.” LE PR.] For he also reduces [Note: [Col. 0218C] Rhenanus conjectures “And in the earth’s.”] the name “earth” to matter [Note: [Col. 0218C] *In* is absent from the Frankfurt and Paris editions.], because earth is what was made from it. [Note: [Col. 0218D] “Invisible and crude.” Shortly before he had said “unformed” for the Greek ἀκατασκεύαστος; here it is called “crude,” as in Ovid, *Metamorphoses* I: “A crude and undigested mass.” LE PR.] He calls it “invisible and crude” because he maintains that matter was formless, confused, and unordered.

I shall indeed refute these opinions of his one by one; meanwhile, however, I wish to answer him as follows. Let us suppose [Note: [Col. 0218C] Junius conjectures, “Let us suppose.”] that matter is demonstrated by these clauses. Yet does Scripture, because it existed before all things, indicate that anything of this kind was made from it? On the contrary, it indicates no such thing. Let matter have existed, to the extent that it—or rather Hermogenes—is permitted this concession; it could both have existed and yet God have made nothing from it, either because it was unfitting for God to have needed anything, or certainly because he is not shown to have made anything from matter.

“Then it would have been purposeless,” you say. Not [Col. 0218C] altogether without purpose. For even if the world was not made from it, a heresy was made from it; and the heresy is all the more shameless because the heresy was not made from matter, but rather the heresy itself made matter.

## CHAPTER XXIV

[Col. 0219A]

I now return to the individual clauses through which he thought matter was signified. First I shall demand an account of the names. For we read one of these, namely “earth”; we do not find the other, namely “matter.” I ask, therefore: since the designation “matter” does not occur in Scripture, how is the appellation “earth” also applied to it, when that appellation is already known in another genus of substance?

All the more should the designation “matter” also have appeared, accompanied by the appellation “earth,” so that I might know that “earth” was a name shared with matter, and so that I would not claim it exclusively for that substance to which it is proper and in connection with which it is better known [Note: [Col. 0219C] “It is known,” Oberthür and Semler, following Rhenanus, who indeed suspected that the reading should be “it is known.”]; or so that, if I wished, I might not be able to share it with any other kind whatsoever, and certainly not with every kind of matter. For when the proper term for the thing to which a common term is assigned does not exist, inasmuch as the thing to which it is assigned does not appear, it will be possible to assign that term to anything else whatsoever. Thus, even if Hermogenes could show that matter was named, he would still have to prove that it was also surnamed “earth,” so that he might thereby claim both terms for it.

## CHAPTER XXV

## CHAPTER XXVI

[Col. 0220C]

For us, however, there is one God and one earth, which God made in the beginning. Scripture, beginning to trace the sequence concerning it, first declares that it was made and then explains its quality; just as it first professes that heaven [Col. 0221A] was made—“In the beginning God made heaven”—and then adds its arrangement: [Note: [Col. 0221D] “And he separated between the water that was beneath the firmament.” Here the author plainly takes *infra* to mean “underneath” or “under”; for it can be understood in no other way. The Greek is ὃ ἦν ὑποκάτω τοῦ στερεώματος, “that which was beneath the firmament,” especially since the same expression is translated in Hebrews 2 as “beneath his feet,” just as other translators render it here, “beneath the firmament.” PAM.] “And he separated between the water that was beneath the firmament” (Gen. 1:7) “and that which was above the firmament, and God called the firmament heaven”—the very heaven that he had made at the beginning.

Likewise concerning man: “And God made man; in the image of God he made him” (Gen. 1:27). Then Scripture relates how he made him: “And God fashioned man from the mud of the earth, and breathed into his face the breath of life, and man became a living soul” (Gen. 2:7).

And surely this is the proper way to begin a narrative: first to preface, then to pursue; to name, and then to describe. Otherwise it would be absurd if Scripture suddenly disclosed the form and condition of a thing of which it had made no prior mention—not even its name—namely matter: [Note: [Col. 0221C] “It discloses the condition before it relates what it was, before it shows whether it existed; it delineates the shape of the deformed thing, but conceals its name.” Junius thinks it should be read thus.] it first relates what it was like before showing whether it existed; it displays the shape of the deformed thing but conceals its name.

How much more credible is our account: that Scripture appended the arrangement of the very thing whose creation and designation it had already set forth. How complete, indeed, is the sense: “In the beginning God made heaven and earth. The earth, however, was invisible and crude” (Gen. 1:1–2)—the earth, namely, which God made, and which Scripture had just expressly mentioned. [Note: [Col. 0221C] “Had declared,” Semler, Oberthür.]

For the very word “however” (*autem*), like the clasp of a connective particle, has been attached [Note: [Col. 0221C] “Placed beside,” Semler, Oberthür.] to bind together the narrative: “the earth, however.” By this word Scripture returns to the earth of which it had spoken above and binds the sense together. [Note: [Col. 0221D] “Thus remove ‘however’ from here,” etc. A fine interpretation of what the particle “however,” when added in Holy Scripture, signifies. ## CHAPTER XXVIII

On the contrary, we shall prove not only that this condition belonged to this earth, but also that it could not have belonged to that other one. For if naked matter [Note: [Col. 0222D] “If matter was naked, subject to God,” Semler, Oberthür. “If naked in this way,” Junius.] lay exposed to God, with no element obstructing it—since as yet nothing [Col. 0222B] existed apart from matter itself and God—it certainly could not have been invisible.

For even if Hermogenes wishes darkness to have existed in the substance of matter—to which clause we shall have to respond in its proper order—even to a human being darkness is visible. The very fact that darkness exists is seen, much more so by God. And surely, if matter were invisible, its quality could in no way be known. How, then, did Hermogenes ascertain that it was formless, confused, and unordered, [Note: [Col. 0222D] “Restless,” Semler, Oberthür. [Note: [Col. 0222D] “Or if this was revealed by God.” A passage to be noted against the heretics whom some call “spiritual” or “ecstatic,” who teach that Scripture may be disregarded and reliance placed solely upon revelations. I also demand to know whether it could have been called “crude.” For surely what is crude is imperfect. Surely nothing can be imperfect unless it has been made. For what has been made insufficiently is imperfect.

## CHAPTER XXIX

From that point it accordingly also became visible, when God said: “Let the water be gathered together into one gathering, [Note: [Col. 0223C] “In one gathering,” Rhenanus.] and let the dry land be seen.” He says, “Let it be seen,” not “Let it be made.” For it had already been made, [Note: [Col. 0224D] “But until then, being invisible, it waited to be seen.” That is, it expected. RIG.] but until then, being invisible, it waited to be seen. The dry land, however—what it was to become through the separation of moisture—was nevertheless earth. “And God called the dry land earth,” not matter.

So too, after it attained perfection, it ceased to be regarded as crude, when God declared: [Note: [Col. 0223C] “Let it bear fruit,” Paris.] “Let the earth put forth the herbage of grass [Col. 0223C], sowing seed according to kind and according to likeness, and the fruitful tree producing fruit, whose seed is within it according to likeness.” Likewise: “Let the earth produce a living soul according to kind: quadrupeds, creeping things, and beasts of the earth according to kind.”

Divine Scripture therefore completed its sequence. To the earth which it had previously declared invisible and crude, [Col. 0224A] it restored both visibility and perfection. But no other matter was invisible and crude. Therefore [Note: [Col. 0223C] An old manuscript of Wouwerius inserts “not.”] matter will afterward be visible and perfect. I therefore wish to see matter, for it has become visible. I wish also to recognize it in its perfected state, so that from it I may pluck the herbage of grass, and from it gather the fruitful tree, [Note: [Col. 0223C] Rhenanus and Semler read: “the fruit tree, and from it.” Rhenanus conjectured [Col. 0224C] “the fruit of the tree”; but Franeker and Paris read, “the fruitful tree producing fruit”; Rigault, however, “the fruitful tree, and from another.”] and so that animals from it may serve my use.

But matter is nowhere; the earth, however, is here [Note: [Col. 0224C] “This,” Franeker, Paris, Pamelius.]—that is, before me. I see it and enjoy it, now that it has ceased to be invisible and crude. Isaiah speaks most clearly of it: “Thus says the Lord who made heaven” (Isa. 45:18): “This is the God who displayed the earth and made it.” Surely he displayed the same earth that he made. How did he display it? Clearly by saying, “Let the dry land be seen.” Why does he command it to be seen unless because previously it was not seen? Thus too, by making it visible and thereby serviceable [Note: [Col. 0224C] “And habitable,” Latinius.], he did not make it to no purpose.

Thus it is proved to us in every respect that the earth which we inhabit is the very same earth that was both made and displayed by God; that no other earth was crude and invisible than the one which was both made and displayed; and therefore that the words “the earth, however, was invisible and crude” refer to the earth which God separated along with heaven.

## CHAPTER XXX

In the same way, what follows will also appear to support Hermogenes’ conjecture: “And darkness was over the abyss, and the Spirit of God was borne over the waters” (Gen. 1:2), as though these confused substances too pointed to evidence of that massive bulk. On the contrary, by defining separately the darkness, the abyss, the Spirit of God, and the waters, Scripture allows nothing confused, nor anything uncertain within a confusion, to be inferred from so distinct [Note: [Col. 0224C] “Different,” Junius, from the Vatican manuscript.] an account [Col. 0224C] of definite and distinguished elements.

More than this: when it assigns their respective positions—darkness over the abyss, the Spirit over the waters—it denied any confusion of substances; for by demonstrating their arrangement, it also demonstrated their distinction.

It is supremely absurd [Note: [Col. 0224C] *Vanitimum*, Rhenanus, Junius, Semler; Junius suspects that some Greek term lies behind it.] that matter, which is introduced as formless, [Col. 0225A] should be asserted to be formless despite the names of so many forms, without stating what that body of confusion actually is—a body which must certainly be believed to be single if it is formless, since that which is formless is uniform. A formless thing, however, which is confused through variety, must necessarily have one appearance; yet it has no appearance, while from many it has one appearance [the transmitted wording is uncertain].
