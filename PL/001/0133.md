Now, as we run through the causes of impatience, the other precepts too will answer to them in their proper places. If the mind is aroused by a loss of property, it is admonished in almost every passage of the Lord’s Scriptures to despise the world; nor is there any greater inducement to contempt for money than the fact that the Lord himself is found possessing no riches. He always justifies the poor and condemns the rich beforehand [Note: [Col. 1260D] “Condemns the rich beforehand”—as though in anticipation of the Last Judgment. RIG.]. Thus, by his contempt for opulence [Note: [Col. 1260C] “Of losses,” Wouwer’s MS.] [Note: [Col. 1260D] “Thus the loss of patience,” etc. The sentence is obscure; it appears that it should be understood and punctuated as follows: “Thus the Lord furnished beforehand for patience the loss”—understand, “of property”—“the loathing of opulence, the contempt of riches”; the phrases are appositional. The sense would thus be: the Lord was the first to show that the loss of property must be endured patiently, since he teaches that riches are to be despised. In this way the clause that follows will cohere suitably. RHEN.], he furnished patience beforehand against loss, demonstrating [Col. 1260C] through the rejection of riches that their losses also [Col. 1261A] [Note: [Col. 1261C] “Their losses also,” etc. By *injuries* he means losses or diminutions, when something of our property [Col. 1261D] is lost. He could have said *losses*, but preferred a novel term. RHEN.] are to be reckoned as nothing. Therefore, what we have no need at all to seek—because the Lord did not seek it either—we ought not to endure reluctantly when it is diminished or even taken away.

Through the Apostle, the Spirit of the Lord declared covetousness to be the root of all evils (1 Tim. 6:10). Let us not interpret it as consisting only in desire for what belongs to another; for even what seems to be ours belongs to another: nothing is ours, since all things belong to God, to whom we ourselves also belong. Accordingly, if, when afflicted by loss, we react impatiently [Note: [Col. 1261C] Thus Rigault and Semler. “We shall be impatient,” Paris and Leopold.], grieving over the loss of what is not [Note: [Col. 1261C] “What is not ours,” Rigault.] ours, we shall be detected as close relations of covetousness. We seek another’s property when we reluctantly endure the loss of another’s property [Note: [Col. 1261C] Franeker adds “grieving.”]. Whoever is aroused by impatience at loss, preferring earthly things to heavenly, sins directly against God. For, on account of a worldly possession, he disturbs the spirit that he received from the Lord [Col. 1261B]. Let us therefore willingly [Note: [Col. 1261C] “Willing,” Franeker and Rigault.] lose earthly things and preserve heavenly things. Let the whole world perish, provided that I gain patience.

Anyone who has resolved not steadfastly to endure some trifling loss through theft, violence, or even negligence—I do not know whether he could easily or sincerely lay hands upon his own property for the sake of almsgiving. For how can one who cannot bear at all to be cut by another apply the knife to his own body [Note: [Col. 1261C] “To his own body,” Rigault.]? Patience amid losses is training in giving and sharing. One who does not fear to lose is not reluctant to give. Otherwise, how will a man who has two tunics give one of them to the naked, unless he is also the same man who can offer his cloak as well to one who takes away his tunic (Matt. 5)? How shall we make friends for ourselves from mammon [Col. 1261C] (Luke 16), if we love it so greatly that we cannot bear its loss? We shall perish along with what has perished. What do we find here, where we possess only so as to lose [Note: [Col. 1261D] “What do we find here, where we possess only so as to lose?” He alludes to Christ’s words in Matt. 10:39: “He who finds his soul will lose it, and he who loses his soul for my sake will find it.” RIG.]?

It belongs to the Gentiles to meet every loss with impatience, for they perhaps place monetary property [Note: [Col. 1261C] “Money,” Franeker.] before their souls. Indeed [Note: [Col. 1262C] “What,” Latinus.], they do so when, in their lust for gain, [Col. 1262A] they undertake the profitable perils of commerce at sea; when, for money’s sake, they do not hesitate to undertake even in the forum anything that carries the risk of condemnation; when, finally, they hire themselves out to the arena and the camp [Note: [Col. 1261D] “When, finally, to the arena and the camp.” He means the gladiatorial school, whose master was called the *lanista*; by “camp,” military service. RHEN.] [Note: [Col. 1261D] “They hire themselves out.” This was a most disgraceful custom of acquiring profit; for even men born to respectable station, when they had fallen into poverty, hired and bound themselves to the gladiatorial arena. Seneca mentions this custom in Epistle 87: “Whether he hires himself out to the sword or to the knife.” No wonder: for what will poverty not bring about? On this, see Cyril of Alexandria, Homily 5, *On the Paschal Festivals*. LAC.]; and when they turn brigand along the road, adopting the habits of beasts [Note: [Col. 1261D] “They turn brigand along the road, adopting the habits of beasts.” Pithou’s codex reads, “through the way into those mindful.” These are traces of the true reading, which we think was something like this: “Through trackless places [Col. 1262C] they turn brigand after the manner of beasts.” Thus Tertullian himself in *On the Soul*: “Because it too has cast to the beasts those whom it has slaughtered in forests [Col. 1262D] and trackless places.” “Into the habits of beasts” means “after the manner of beasts,” “in the way of beasts.” RIG.]. But in accordance with the difference between them and us, it is fitting for us to surrender not life for money, but money for life, whether voluntarily in giving or patiently in losing.

## CHAPTER VIII

In this world we carry our very soul and body exposed to every injury, and we submit patiently to that injury; shall we be hurt by the relinquishment of lesser things [Note: [Col. 1262C] “By the tasting,” Latinus, Pithou, and Rigault. They judge that one could write “concerning liberation.” Latinius emended it to “concerning libation.”] [Note: [Col. 1262D] “Shall we be hurt by the relinquishment of lesser things?” The old books most consistently preserve the reading *minorum*, “of lesser things,” that is, of money. *Deliberatione*, however, appears to have been used with the force of *liberatione*, “relinquishment.” It is also a tenet of Christian philosophy that riches are counted among impediments. In the same sense it might also be read: “Shall we be hurt at being freed from lesser things?” This also commended itself to Pithou. RIG.]? Far be such a stain from a servant of Christ: that patience, prepared for greater trials, should fail in trivial matters [Col. 1262B].

If anyone has tried to provoke you with his hand [Note: [Col. 1262C] “To be provoked,” Rhenanus.], the Lord’s admonition [Note: [Col. 1262D] “The Lord’s *monela*.” He uses *monela* from *monere* (“to admonish”) for “admonition,” just as *sequela* comes from *sequi* (“to follow”). RHEN.] is at hand: “To him who strikes you,” he says (Matt. 5:39), “on the face, turn the other cheek also.” Let wickedness be wearied by your patience. Whoever [Note: [Col. 1262C] “Whoever,” Franeker, Paris, and Rigault.] dealt that blow, being bound over to pain and disgrace, is beaten more severely by the Lord [Note: [Col. 1262D] “Is beaten more severely by the Lord”—by a counterblow, or by the rebound of divine vengeance. RIG.]. By enduring, you strike that wicked man more heavily; for he will be beaten by the one for whose sake you endure.

If the bitterness of a tongue breaks out in a curse or insult, look to the saying (Matt. 5:12): “When they curse you, rejoice.” The Lord himself was accursed under the Law (Deut. 21:23), and yet he alone is blessed (Gal. 3:13). Therefore, let us servants follow the Lord and patiently suffer curses [Col. 1262C], so that we may be blessed.

If I hear with insufficient composure some insolent or wicked remark directed against me, I must either repay bitterness in kind or be tormented by silent impatience. Therefore, when, having been cursed, I have struck back [Note: [Col. 1262C] “With a curse,” Franeker and Paris; “accursed,” Wouwer’s MS.], how shall I be found to have followed the Lord’s teaching, by which it is handed down (Mark 7:15, 18) that a person is defiled not by the uncleanness of vessels [Note: [Col. 1262D] “Not by the uncleanness of vessels.” What is read more than once in Matt. 15, *κοινοῖ τὸν ἄνθρωπον* (“defiles the person”), Tertullian translates as *communicat* (“makes common”), just as Aurelius Augustine also read it. *Κοινοῖν* means “to make common.” And because the Jews call what is impure “common,” it came about that *κοινοῖν*, or “to make common,” means for them “to defile,” as the Old Latin version of the Gospels has it in certain manuscripts, especially ours. Erasmus very elegantly translates it “to render impure.” RHEN.], but by those things that [Col. 1263A] issue from the mouth [Note: [Col. 1263C] Others read “to be defiled.”]? Likewise (Matt. 12), that the guilt of every vain and superfluous word remains upon us? It follows, then, that what the Lord restrains us from doing, he also admonishes us to endure calmly when done by another.

Here, now [Note: [Col. 1263C] Junius adds “also.”], is the pleasure of patience. For every injury, whether inflicted by tongue or hand, when it encounters patience, will be canceled by the same outcome [Note: [Col. 1263D] “Will be canceled by the same outcome.” *Dispungere* means to delete and erase; the metaphor is taken from wax tablets containing names, on which entries that have been settled are struck through and erased with a stylus. For the jurist Ulpian teaches that this verb pertains to accounts when he says, “To strike through is to compare receipts and expenditures,” *Digest*, *On the Meaning of Words*. RHEN.] as some missile hurled [Note: [Col. 1263C] “Against a rock,” Junius.] against a rock of utterly steadfast hardness [Note: [Col. 1263D] “As some missile.” Here he takes *librare* to mean “to hurl with great force,” imitating Virgil, *Aeneid* IX: “He was poising the missile from beside his ear.” Otherwise its proper meaning is quite different: “to weigh.” PAM.], and blunted. For it will fall there, its effort vain and fruitless, and sometimes, rebounding against the one who launched it, it will rage back with reciprocal force.

Indeed, a person injures you precisely so that you may suffer, because the offender’s profit lies in the pain of the injured party. Therefore, when by not suffering you destroy his profit, he must himself suffer from the loss of his profit. Then you depart not merely uninjured [Note: [Col. 1263C] “You will depart,” Rhenanus.]—which by itself is enough for you [Col. 1263B]—but, beyond that, delighted by your adversary’s frustration and defended by his pain. This is the usefulness and pleasure [Note: [Col. 1263C] “Will,” Rhenanus.] of patience.

## CHAPTER IX

Not even that species of impatience arising from the loss of our own people is excused, where some affection pleads in defense of grief [Note: [Col. 1263C] “Assertion,” Rhenanus; perhaps: “affection pleads in defense of grief.”]. For precedence must be given [Note: [Col. 1263C] “We shall set before ourselves,” Wouwer’s MS.; “must be preferred,” Rhenanus.] to regard for the Apostle’s injunction, when he says (1 Thess. 4): “Do not grieve over anyone’s falling asleep, as the nations do who have no hope.” And rightly so. For, believing in Christ’s resurrection, we believe also in [Note: [Col. 1263C] “In” is absent from Junius.] our own, for whose sake he both died and rose again.

Therefore, since the resurrection of the dead is certain, grief over death is idle, and so is impatience with grief. Why, then [Note: [Col. 1264C] “For,” Franeker.], should you grieve if you do not believe that one has perished [Col. 1263C]? Why should you endure impatiently the temporary withdrawal [Col. 1264A] of one whom you believe will return? What you think to be death is a departure. One who goes ahead is not to be mourned, though certainly he is to be missed. That longing too must be governed by patience. For why should you bear immoderately the departure of one whom you will soon follow?

Moreover, impatience in such cases both casts an evil omen upon our hope and betrays the faith. And we injure Christ when we do not calmly accept those summoned by him, as though they were to be pitied. “I desire,” says the Apostle (Phil. 1), “now to be received [Note: [Col. 1263D] “I desire,” says the Apostle, “to be received.” The common text very properly has “to be dissolved,” for the Greek is *ἀναλῦσαι* (“to depart”). La Cerda defends this interpretation, “to be received,” with an ingenious but insufficiently true explanation. Septimius follows this version elsewhere, as in chapter 28 of *On the Spectacles* and chapter 5 of Book I of *To His Wife*. LE PR.] and to be with the Lord.” How much better [Note: [Col. 1263D] “How much better displays the desire of Christians.” One must repeat from the preceding words: “than the nations who lack hope.” RIG.] does this display the desire of Christians? Therefore, if we impatiently grieve that others have attained this desire [Note: [Col. 1264C] Junius punctuates: “How much better does it display the desire? Therefore, the desire of Christians,” etc.], we ourselves do not wish to attain it.

## CHAPTER X

There is also another supreme spur to impatience: the lust for vengeance [Col. 1264B], which serves the interest either of glory or of malice. But glory is certainly vain, and malice is never anything but hateful to the Lord—especially in this instance [Note: [Col. 1264C] Franeker adds “when.”], when, provoked by another’s [Note: [Col. 1264C] “By another’s,” Franeker.] malice, it sets itself above him in exacting vengeance [Note: [Col. 1264C] “Sets itself above him in exacting vengeance.” To establish oneself as superior in vengeance will be to conduct oneself like a ruler and to arrogate to oneself the authority of a judge. LAC.], and, by repaying wickedness, doubles what was done only once.

Among those in error [Note: [Col. 1264D] “Among those in error”—among the heathen. Thus, “with the truth” means “with Christ.” RIG.], vengeance appears to be a consolation for pain; with the truth, it is certainly convicted of malignity [Note: [Col. 1264C] “Certainly,” Wouwer’s MS.; “certain,” Rhenanus.] [Note: [Col. 1264D] “It is certainly convicted of malignity.” Rhenanus printed: “With the truth, it is convicted of certain malignity.” The emendation “a contest is convicted of malignity” is attractive. Augustine confirms this emendation and appears to have had Septimius’s words before his eyes when he wrote thus on Psalm 36: “Contend with the evil man, but in goodness; for this is the true contention, or rather the salutary contest: that one should be good against an evil man, not that there should be two evil men.” RIG.]. For what difference is there between the provoker and the provoked, except that the former [Note: [Col. 1264D] “The former is found first in wrongdoing.” For to render evil for evil, or *ἀνταποδοῦναι* (“to repay”), what else is it than *ἀντιδικῆσαι δεύτεραν* (“to retaliate a second time”)? Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* VII.] is found first in wrongdoing and the latter afterward? Yet each is guilty before the Lord of injuring a human being—the Lord who both forbids and condemns every [Note: [Col. 1264C] “Every” in the feminine, Franeker and Paris.] wicked deed.

There is no consideration of order in wrongdoing; position does not separate what similarity joins. It is therefore commanded without qualification [Note: [Col. 1264D] “It is therefore commanded without qualification.” Indeed, Ambrose, Sermon 10, says that this is the sum of Christian discipline: “For this is the sum of our Christianity: that we repay mutual love to those who love us and patience to those who injure us.” LAC.] that evil is not to be repaid with evil (Rom. 12). An equal deed [Col. 1264C] has equal desert [Note: [Col. 1264C] “Also has desert,” Wouwer’s MS.]. How shall we observe this [Col. 1265A] if, having first been scorned [Note: [Col. 1265C] “If, having first been scorned, we shall not be engaged in the scorn of vengeance?” Thus it is read in Ursinus’s codex. Others read, “If, being scornful, we shall not be engaged in the scorn of vengeance,” with a very feeble sense. Accordingly, we read: “If we have harmed the one scorning us with the scorn of vengeance.” We are scorned by an adversary who hates us; if we hate and scorn him in turn, we have now scorned the one who scorned us, repaid evil with evil, and failed to observe the Lord’s precept. In this passage Septimius said “to harm the one scorning,” just as he says “to harm the one being born” in *On the Exhortation to Chastity*. RIG.], we do not engage in scornful vengeance [Note: [Col. 1265B] “Being scornful, in scorn,” Rhenanus.]?

What honor, moreover, shall we offer as a sacrifice to the Lord God if we arrogate to ourselves the decision concerning our defense? We—mere wineskins [Note: [Col. 1265B] “Corrupt,” Rhenanus.], earthen vessels—are gravely offended when our little servants take vengeance upon fellow servants on their own authority; and those who offer us their patience, mindful of their lowliness and servitude and cherishing their master’s right to honor, we not only approve, but also grant them more ample satisfaction than they would have presumed to take for themselves. Is this uncertain for us in the Lord, who is so just in assessing and so powerful in accomplishing? Why, then, do we believe him to be judge, if we do not believe him also to be avenger?

He promises us this in return, saying (Deut. 32): “Vengeance is mine [Note: [Col. 1265C] “Vengeance is mine.” Consider the passage of Gregory of Nyssa in his letter to Flavian, where, speaking of an injury done to him, he first states that the insolence of the enemy must not be pardoned, but rather that his pride must be beaten down and his empty arrogance restrained. After saying this, so that the reader might suppose Gregory about to aspire to avenge himself, he splendidly confounds his readers by adding: *ὅπως δ’ ἂν γένοιτο θεῷ μελήσει*, “But how that is to be done will be God’s concern.” That is, the injury done to me has God as its avenger; I entrust it to him who hears my complaints. LAC.], and I will avenge”; that is: “Give patience to me, and I will reward patience.”

For when he says (Matt. 7) [Col. 1265B], “Do not judge, lest you be judged,” does he not demand patience? For who will refrain from judging another except one who will patiently forgo defending himself [Note: [Col. 1265C] “Being defended,” Rhenanus; Junius prefers “to be defended.”]? Who judges for the purpose of pardoning [Note: [Col. 1265C] “Would judge,” Franeker.]? And even if he does pardon, he has nevertheless provided for the judge’s impatience [Note: [Col. 1265C] “Patience,” Latinus.] and has taken away the honor of the sole judge, that is, God [Note: [Col. 1265D] “He has nevertheless provided for the judge’s impatience.” That is, he has employed the impatience of the person judging, or prosecuting an injury, in a judicial action. He has provided against his impatience over the injury by undertaking legal action concerning it, even if he did not pursue it to completion. He has furnished security for impatience, as though the vengeance deposited with God were at risk; and by that security he has taken away the honor of the sole judge, that is, God. Tertullian said *cavere impatientiam* (“to furnish security for impatience”) in the same construction in which Ulpian and Scaevola say *cavere chirographum* (“to secure by a written bond”), Book V, *Digest*, *Commodatum*, and Book LXXXIX, *On Payments and Releases*; that is, “to secure by means of a written bond.” Thus *cavere* [Col. 1266C] *impatientiam* means “to secure by means of impatience,” to provide vengeance for oneself through impatience at an injury. RIG.].

How many disasters of this sort impatience is accustomed to incur [Note: [Col. 1265C] “Accuse,” Rhenanus.] [Note: [Col. 1265C] “Had been accustomed,” Rhenanus.]! How often has self-defense been regretted! How often has its insistence become [Note: [Col. 1266C] “Its insistence became worse than its causes.” It pressed its claim more sharply and harshly than was fair. There was no cause underlying such savage and atrocious or obstinate insistence. It exacted more than any law conceded to pain. RIG.] worse than its causes [Note: [Col. 1266B] “Was found,” Rhenanus.]! For nothing undertaken in impatience knows how to be carried through without violence; and nothing done violently fails either to give offense, to collapse, or to rush headlong to ruin.

Now, if your defense is too slight, you will go mad; if too abundant, you will be burdened. What have I to do [Col. 1266A] with vengeance, whose measure I cannot govern because of my impatience with pain? But if I devote myself to patience [Note: [Col. 1266C] “If I devote myself to patience.” *Incubare* means “to sleep upon”; the metaphor is taken from precious things that, lest we lose them, we place beneath ourselves for safekeeping even while we sleep. Cyprian, *On Almsgiving*: “Why do you brood alone over your riches?” Tertullian similarly uses it in *On the Prescription* [Col. 1266D] and *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*. RHEN.], I shall not suffer pain; if I do not suffer pain, I shall not desire vengeance.

## CHAPTER XI

After setting forth, as best we could, these principal materials of impatience, why should we wander among the rest? The things at home, the things abroad [Note: [Col. 1266D] Broad and diffuse is the operation of evil. Thus in *On Idolatry*, chapter 2, he said that the devil’s ingenuity does not remain within narrow bounds and that Christians are armed against the devil’s breadth. RIG.]? Broad and diffuse is the operation of evil [Note: [Col. 1266B] Others punctuate: “The operation. Of evil.”]: manifold are the incitements of the spirit that hurls its darts, now very small, now very great. But you may despise the small because of their insignificance and yield to the greatest because of their overwhelming force [Note: [Col. 1266C] “Abundance,” Paris.]. Where the injury is smaller, there is no necessity for impatience [Note: [Col. 1266C] “Patience,” Latinus.]; but where the injury is greater, there patience [Note: [Col. 1266C] “Of patience,” Franeker and Paris.] is the more necessary remedy for injury [Note: [Col. 1266D] “There the more necessary.” An unusual comparative. Thus he used *necessarius* in the neuter in *On the Flesh of Christ*: “As though,” he says, “they brought something necessary.” RHEN.].

Let us therefore strive to endure what is inflicted by the evil one, so that the rivalry of our composure may frustrate [Col. 1266B] the enemy’s zeal. But if we ourselves bring certain things upon ourselves, whether through imprudence or even deliberately, let us equally submit with patience to what we impute to ourselves. And if we believe that some things are struck upon us by the Lord, to whom should we show patience more than to the Lord? Indeed [Note: [Col. 1266C] “Who moreover,” Junius.], he teaches [Note: [Col. 1266C] “It is fitting,” Latinus.] us in addition to give thanks and rejoice in the honor of divine chastisement. “Those whom I love,” he says, “I chastise.”

O blessed servant, whose correction the Lord pursues! With whom he deigns [Note: [Col. 1266D] “With whom he deigns to be angry.” Splendidly said; for God’s acts of anger toward us proceed from his sheer condescension and mercy, so that we may be corrected. The wife of Julian the Apostate, a pious and holy woman, when she saw her impious husband punished by God, said to him, as recorded in Theodoret, Book III, chapter 12: *οὐδὲ γὰρ ἂν ἔγνως τίς ὁ παρὰ σοῦ πολεμούμενος, εἰ τῇ συνήθει μακροθυμίᾳ χρησάμενος ταύτας σοι θεηλάτους πληγὰς οὐκ ἐπήγαγεν*—“Indeed, you would never have known who it was whom you attacked, had he, employing his customary forbearance, not inflicted upon you these divinely sent blows.” LAC.] to be angry, whom he does not deceive by withholding admonition!

From every side, then, we are bound to the duty of exercising patience. From whatever quarter the occasion for that duty comes upon us—whether through our own errors, the evil one’s snares, [Col. 1267A] or the Lord’s admonitions [Note: [Col. 1267C] “Whether through our own errors, the evil one’s snares, or the Lord’s admonitions the occasion comes upon us.” We intervene against our errors or the evil one’s snares when we oppose them—not by consenting, but rather by avoiding them. Thus formerly a tribune interposed his veto against decrees of the senate. But we intervene in the Lord’s admonitions when we obey them. RHEN.]—great is its reward: blessedness, of course.

For whom did the Lord call blessed, if not the patient, when he said: “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven” (Matt. 5)? Certainly no one is poor in spirit unless he is humble; and who is humble unless he is patient? For no one can subject himself without first possessing patience for the very act of submission.

“Blessed,” he says, “are those who weep and mourn.” Who endures such things without patience? Accordingly, consolation [Note: [Col. 1267C] “To such people consolation and laughter are promised.” *Advocatio*, Greek *παράκλησις*, means “consolation.” Thus “words of consolation”; “to console the languishing,” that is, to comfort [Col. 1267D] the sick; “consoler of the poor.” RIG.] and laughter are promised to such people.

“Blessed are the gentle.” By this designation the impatient certainly cannot be reckoned at all. Likewise, when he marks peacemakers with the same title of blessedness and calls them sons of God, can the impatient have any affinity with peace? Only a fool would think so [Note: [Col. 1267C] “This,” Franeker and Paris; Junius prefers “this.”].

And when he says, “Rejoice and exult whenever they curse and persecute you [Col. 1267B], for your reward is very great in heaven,” he certainly does not promise that reward to the impatience of exultation, because no one has exulted [Note: [Col. 1267C] “Will exult,” Franeker and Paris.] amid adversities unless he first despised them, and no one will despise them unless he has practiced patience.

## CHAPTER XII

As for the discipline of peace, which is most pleasing to God, what man wholly born to impatience will forgive [Note: [Col. 1267C] “Forgives,” Rhenanus.] his brother even once—not to say seven times, but even [Note: [Col. 1267C] “Or,” Latinus and Junius.] seventy-seven times (Matt. 18)? Who, while arranging a settlement with his adversary (Luke 6; Matt. 5), will resolve the matter by agreement [Note: [Col. 1267D] “Will resolve the matter by agreement”—that is, by a settlement, an amicable compact. RIG.], unless he first cuts away his heart’s pain, hardness, bitterness—the poisons, of course, of impatience?

How will you forgive, and be forgiven, if through the absence of patience you cling to an injury? [Col. 1267C] No one whose mind is enraged against his brother will complete his offering [Col. 1268A] at the altar unless, by first being reconciled to his brother, he has returned to patience. If the sun sets upon our anger, we are in danger. We are not permitted to remain without patience for even a single day.

But since patience governs every form of saving discipline (Eph. 4), what wonder is it that it also ministers to repentance, which is accustomed to assist the fallen? When a marriage has been severed for that cause which permits either the man or the woman to be sustained in continued widowhood, patience waits [Note: [Col. 1267D] “It desires this, entreats this.” Patience not only waits and desires, but even wrests forth repentance—that is, it compels those whom it thus calmly tolerates to repent, so that they may eventually enter upon salvation. And because he adds, “which is accustomed to assist the fallen,” he appears to be speaking of Christians, whether men or women, who join themselves in marriage to heathens. Here it sometimes happened that a Christian wife at last persuaded her heathen husband to continence, which he embraced and maintained; the woman, meanwhile, performed repentance and expiated her former deed before the Church according to the rite of that time. And this is what follows: “How much good it confers upon both! It does not make the one an adulterer; it corrects the other.” RHEN.], desires, and entreats [Note: [Col. 1268C] “Exhorts this,” Paris.] for the repentance of those who will one day enter upon salvation. How much [Note: [Col. 1268C] “Salvation? How much,” etc., Junius.] good it confers upon both! It does not make the one an adulterer [Note: [Col. 1268C] “An adulterer it does not,” Franeker, Paris, and Junius.] [Note: [Col. 1268C] “It does not make the one an adulterer.” Thus Rhenanus in his final edition; thus Pamelius; thus others. But the oldest copies read, “It does not send the one to another,” which we are readily led to believe is Tertullian’s own style. When a marriage, he says, has been severed on account of adultery, patience intervenes and confers very much good upon both: it does not “send the one”—that is, the spouse who has repudiated the adulterous spouse—“to another” marriage. That is certainly the sense: “It does not send,” “does not dispatch,” “does not permit to go.” So in *On the Pallium*: “Which makes its way from Libya to that man,” that is, “comes.” RIG.]; it corrects the other.

Thus, in those examples from the Lord’s holy parables, patience is also present. The shepherd’s patience [Note: [Col. 1268D] “The shepherd’s patience”—that is, Christ’s, who is the most patient shepherd. Ignatius the Martyr, *Letter to the Philadelphians*: *ποιμένος πραοτάτου*, “of the gentlest shepherd.” Gregory of Nazianzus, Oration 1, among Christ’s praises, also has this. Likewise, in Peter Chrysologus, Sermon 6, Christ is called the merciful shepherd, and he ascribes to him pastoral discipline and gentleness. LAC.] seeks and finds the wandering sheep. For impatience would readily have despised a single one [Col. 1268B]. But patience undertakes the labor of the search, and the patient bearer additionally carries the abandoned sinner upon his shoulders.

The father’s patience also receives that prodigal son, clothes him, feeds him, and defends him against the impatience of his angry brother. Therefore, the one who had perished is saved because he entered upon repentance. Repentance does not perish [Note: [Col. 1268C] “Did not perish,” Junius.], because it has found patience.

For love is the supreme mystery of faith, the treasure of the Christian name [Note: [Col. 1268D] “Love is the supreme mystery of faith”—because it alone comprehensively (*συλλήβδην*) displays in itself all the Christian virtues. RIG.]. The Apostle commends it with all the power of the Holy Spirit; but by whose discipline is it instructed, if not patience’s?

“Love,” he says, “is great-souled” [Note: [Col. 1268D] “Love,” he says, “is great-souled.” Tertullian renders the Pauline word *μακροθυμεῖ* (“is patient”) as *magnanimis est*, “is great-souled,” a phrase unusual in Latin; but he is accustomed to speak in his own fashion. Cyprian, an imitator of Tertullian but of purer style, says, “Charity is great-souled.” He likewise rendered *μακροθυμίαν* as “greatness of soul” in *On Almsgiving*. Others prefer “long-suffering” and “long-sufferingness.” RHEN.]. Thus it assumes patience.

“It is beneficent”; patience does no evil.

“It does not envy”; that certainly is patience’s own property.

“Nor does it savor insolence” [Col. 1268C]; it has drawn modesty from patience.

“It is not puffed up; it does not trample others” [Col. 1269A] [Note: [Col. 1269C] “Does not act insolently,” Franeker and Paris.] [Note: [Col. 1269C] “It is not puffed up; it does not trample others.” La Cerda explains this of the pride of those who arrogate too much to themselves and trample upon others. Others read “has not acted insolently.” Erasmus translates it not badly as “is not brazen,” that is, as he interprets it from certain Greek scholia, “does not exalt or display itself.” LE PR.]; for that does not belong to patience.

“It does not seek its own,” since it offers its own [Note: [Col. 1269C] “But offers,” Latinus.], provided that it benefits another.

“It is not provoked”; otherwise [Note: [Col. 1269C] “Otherwise indeed,” Latinus.], what would it have left for impatience?

Therefore, he says, “Love sustains all things, tolerates all things”—certainly because it is patient. Rightly, then, it will never fail; for the other things will be made void, will be brought to an end. Tongues, forms of knowledge, and prophecies are exhausted [Note: [Col. 1269C] “Will be exhausted,” Junius.]; faith, hope, and love remain: faith, which Christ’s patience introduced; hope, for which human patience waits; love, which, with God as teacher, patience accompanies.

## CHAPTER XIII

Thus far, finally [Note: [Col. 1269C] “Only,” Junius.], we have treated patience as simple and uniform, and as established only in the soul. Yet that same patience also labors in many ways in the body to win the Lord’s favor [Col. 1269B], since it was manifested by the Lord himself in the strength of the body as well; for the governing soul readily shares the things brought in by the spirit [Note: [Col. 1269C] “Unconquered,” Wouwer’s MS.] with its dwelling.

What, then, is the activity of patience in the body? First of all, the affliction of the flesh, an appeasing victim offered to the Lord through the sacrifice of humiliation: when it offers the Lord squalor together with straitened nourishment [Note: [Col. 1269C] “When the straitness,” Wouwer’s MS.], content with simple food and a drink of pure water; when it joins fasts to these; when it becomes habituated to ashes and sackcloth [Note: [Col. 1269D] “It becomes habituated to ashes and sackcloth.” That is, through custom it renders repentance familiar to itself and, as it were, natural. The metaphor is taken from trees that, when grafted and budded into other trees, become accustomed to food and nourishment drawn up and attracted through the trunk. LE PR.].

This patience of the body commends prayers and strengthens supplications; it opens the ears of Christ God [Note: [Col. 1269C] “Of Christ and God,” Junius.] [Note: [Col. 1269D] “It opens the ears of Christ God.” It renders God kindly and accessible to us, so that he does not close his ears to our petitions and prayers. LE PR.], disperses severity, and draws forth mercy (Dan. 4). Thus that Babylonian king, after offending the Lord and being exiled from human form amid seven years of squalor and filth, by sacrificing the patience of his body both recovered his kingdom [Col. 1269C] and—what is more desirable for a human being—made satisfaction to God.

Now, if we set forth the higher and happier degrees of bodily patience, that same patience also provides for holiness the continence [Note: [Col. 1270C] “Continence,” Petavius and Ciacconius.] of the flesh (Matt. 19). It preserves the widow, appoints the virgin, and raises the voluntary eunuch [Col. 1270A] to the kingdoms of heaven. What proceeds from the virtue of the soul is perfected in the flesh.

Finally, the patience of the flesh does battle in persecutions. If flight presses upon us, the flesh struggles against the hardships of flight. If prison too bears down [Note: [Col. 1270C] “Comes beforehand,” Franeker.], the flesh is in chains, the flesh in the wooden stocks [Note: [Col. 1269D] “The flesh in the wood”—in wooden fetters. Thus in Acts 16: *τοὺς πόδας αὐτῶν ἠσφαλίσατο εἰς τὸ ξύλον*, “he secured their feet in the wood.” The Vulgate rendered it in Latin: “He fastened their feet in the stocks.” RIG.], the flesh upon the ground [Note: [Col. 1269D] “The flesh upon the ground”—in solitude, in the sorrow of desolation; [Col. 1270C] or on the bare earth, upon naked flint. The same.], both in that poverty of light and [Note: [Col. 1270C] “Patience,” Rhenanus.] in that deprivation of the world [Note: [Col. 1270C] “In that deprivation of the world”—in destitution of all things. *Ἐν ἐρημίᾳ* (“in abandonment”), which Arrian, in his book on Epictetus, most elegantly calls *κατάστασίς τις ἀβοηθήτου*, that is, “the condition of a person bereft of aid.” RIG.].

But when it is led forth to the trial of blessedness, to the opportunity of a second immersion [Note: [Col. 1270D] “To the opportunity of a second immersion”—of a second baptism, that is, martyrdom. RIG.], to the very ascent to the divine throne, nothing avails more there than patience of the body. If the spirit is willing but the flesh weak without patience (Matt. 26), where is the salvation of the spirit and of the flesh itself?

Yet when the Lord says this of the flesh, declaring it weak, he shows what is needed to strengthen it—namely, patience against every apparatus intended to overthrow or punish [Note: [Col. 1270D] “To punish the faith”—that is, to exercise and test it by punishments and torments. RIG.] the faith [Note: [Col. 1270C] “Prepared,” Paris.]—so that it may most steadfastly endure blows [Col. 1270B], fire, the cross, beasts, and the sword: the things that the Prophets and Apostles conquered by enduring them.
