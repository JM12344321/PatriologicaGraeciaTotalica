### MENTION OF THE BOOK *ON BAPTISM*, FROM THE BOOK OF THE SAME TITLE (chapter 15)

[Col. 1121]

[Col. 1121B] But what rule ought properly to be observed concerning heretics is a matter someone might worthily reconsider, for the present work was published for us. Heretics, however, have no fellowship in our discipline, since the very deprivation of communion proves them to be outsiders. I ought not to recognize among them what has been commanded to me, because we and they do not have the same God, nor one Christ—that is, the same Christ. Consequently there is not one baptism either, because it is not the same. Since they do not possess it rightly, they undoubtedly do not possess it at all; what is not possessed cannot be counted. Thus they cannot receive it either, because they do not possess it. But we have already set out this subject more fully in Greek.

#### [NOTE]

[Col. 1121B] These words also recall the principal subject matter of the Greek work *On Baptism*. Since, moreover, we promised to discuss here what Tertullian’s opinion was concerning the baptism of heretics, we certainly cannot deny that he held the same view as Blessed Cyprian. Perhaps, therefore, someone might say that this little book was suppressed when the opinion of certain African bishops was reconsidered by the Council of Nicaea.

Nor should it be surprising that Tertullian embraced that paradox, since the council under Agrippinus, bishop of Carthage, which Blessed Cyprian mentions in Epistle 71 to [Col. 1122B] Quintus, appears to have been held at almost the same time. That council also drew both him and the contemporary African bishops into this opinion. Cyprian appears to indicate this sufficiently in Epistle 70 to Januarius, when he says that the opinion had long before been established by their predecessors; and more expressly in Epistle 73 to Jubaianus, where he says that many years had passed since numerous bishops under Agrippinus had made this decree.

Yet approximately fifty years intervened between the time when Tertullian, before falling into any heresy, composed the book *On Baptism*, and the first African council of seventy-two bishops concerning the baptism of heretics, which was held in the year of the Lord 255. This is demonstrated more fully in the author’s Life.

## Fourth Appendix [Col. 1121]

### FRAGMENTS, MENTIONS, AND BRIEF NOTICES OF THE LOST WORKS OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIANUS. (C, S)

### I. BRIEF NOTICES FROM BLESSED JEROME OF THE BOOK *TO A PHILOSOPHER-FRIEND, ON THE TROUBLES OF MARRIAGE*

[Col. 1121C] At the beginning of this little book I stated in advance that I would say either nothing at all or very little about the Troubles of Marriage, and I now repeat the same warning. If you wish to know how many troubles beset a virgin who is free and how many a wife who is bound, read Tertullian writing *To a Philosopher-Friend*, and the other little books *On Virginity*, and Blessed Cyprian’s excellent volume, and the works composed on this subject in verse and prose by Pope Damasus, and the little works which our Ambrose recently wrote to his sister (*Epistle 28, to Eustochium*).

[Col. 1122C] This is not the place to describe the troubles of marriage and to exult, as though amid rhetorical commonplaces, in rhetorical discourse. I believe that I treated the subject more fully against Helvidius and completed it in the book I wrote to Eustochium. Tertullian, certainly, played with this subject while he was still a young man; and my teacher Gregory of Nazianzus, discussing Virginity and Marriage, expounded it in Greek verse (*Against Jovinian*, book I).

#### [NOTE]

[Col. 1123A] Thus far Blessed Jerome, whose complete periods we have reproduced, both to avoid presenting anything in mutilated form and to enable the reader to understand which of the ancients wrote on Virginity, a subject so greatly disputed today. For this reason I greatly wish that this little work of the author still survived, whether composed in prose or—more probably, as the verb “played” appears to indicate—in verse.

Accordingly I find even less acceptable the judgment of a certain learned man who thought this was the same book as the one entitled *Exhortation to Chastity*. For apart from the fact that he is said to have written the former as a young man, whereas the latter is known to have been written near the end of his life, the latter does not deal expressly with the troubles of marriage.

Nor do I approve the opinion of those who maintain that two different books [Col. 1124A] of Tertullian appear to be meant in the passages cited, since Blessed Jerome indicates in express words that both were entitled *On the Troubles of Marriage*. Yet whether Blessed Jerome also attributes to Tertullian the other little books *On Virginity* that he mentions might perhaps have been doubtful according to the earlier editions of Jerome, if the reading were: “read Tertullian’s *To a Philosopher-Friend*, and other little books *On Virginity*.” Certainly the two books *To His Wife* and the aforementioned *Exhortation to Chastity* have almost the same subject, and the book *On the Veiling of Virgins* treats nothing else. The reader will find in our index many additional passages in Tertullian concerning virginity, continence, and the preservation of chastity.

### II. MENTION AND FRAGMENT OF THE BOOK *ON FATE*, FROM TERTULLIAN’S BOOK *ON THE SOUL* (chapter 20) AND FULGENTIUS PLACIADES, *ON ANCIENT EXPRESSIONS*

[Col. 1123]

[Col. 1123B] Powers, to be sure, exercise dominion. According to us, there are the Lord God and his rival, the devil; according to common opinion, there are also Providence, [Col. 1124B] Fate, necessity, fortune, and freedom of choice. For the philosophers distinguish these things, and we already know that they must be discussed according to faith under their own title.

#### [NOTE]

[Col. 1123B] Thus far the author. Since no work of his survives in which he treats those four subjects, he appears to me quite plainly to allude to his book entitled *On Fate*, since it is evident from the following citation by Fulgentius Placiades—long ago printed with the *Mythologies* of Hyginus—that Tertullian composed such a book:

> “For Tertullian too says as follows in the book he wrote *On Fate*: ‘Return this man, first enslaved to the problem of fate’”—under the word *problema*, in the book *On Ancient Expressions*. [Col. 1124B]

Blessed Jerome, moreover, in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, relates that a little book *On Fate*, or *Against the Astrologers*, once circulated under the name of Minucius Felix. Yet although that work too is by an eloquent man, its style does not appear to agree with that of his dialogue *Octavius*. Perhaps it is the very book that we here attribute to Tertullian, since Jerome elsewhere calls him not merely eloquent but most eloquent.

### III. CITATION OF QUESTIONS *ON CLEAN AND UNCLEAN ANIMALS*, FROM BLESSED JEROME’S EPISTLE 125 TO DAMASUS

[Col. 1123]

[Col. 1123B] Among other questions, Damasus asks, as question III:

> “If God made all things very good (Gen. I), why did he give Noah instructions concerning clean and unclean animals (Gen. VIII), since nothing unclean can be [Col. 1123C] good? And in the New Testament, after the vision shown to Peter, when he said, ‘Far be it from me, Lord, for nothing common or unclean has ever entered my mouth,’ a voice from heaven answered, ‘What God has cleansed, do not call common’ (Acts X).”

Blessed Jerome writes that he answered all but two short questions—namely, this one and the one discussed in the following fragment:

> “Not,” he says, “because I could not have answered those as well, but because they have been treated in the Latin language by most eloquent [Col. 1124C] men, our Tertullian and Novatian; and if we wished to introduce new material, a more extensive discussion would be required, etc. For Origen too discussed clean and unclean animals at considerable length in his work on Leviticus.”

#### [NOTE]

[Col. 1123C] Whether this refers to a particular book bearing that title—which nevertheless seems more probable—or to Tertullian’s discussion of the matter in a few lines in book II, chapter 18, *Against Marcion*, we leave to the reader’s judgment. Here we shall merely present the substance of his answer in these words:

> “And if the law detracts something from foods and pronounces unclean animals that were once blessed, understand this as a plan for exercising continence and as restraints imposed, etc.” [Col. 1124C]

Although this same point is presented in the book *On Jewish Foods*, but in very different words, it is evident from that fact—and from the remaining style of the entire book—that the work is not Tertullian’s but rather Novatian’s, since Blessed Jerome appears to allude to him in the words already quoted. The subject is treated there at greater length.

### IV. CITATION OF QUESTIONS *ON CIRCUMCISION*, FROM THE SAME PASSAGE OF BLESSED JEROME

[Col. 1123]

[Col. 1123D] Concerning Damasus’s penultimate question—

> “Why did Abraham receive circumcision as the sign of his faith?” (Gen. XVII)—

Blessed Jerome answers in the words already cited that he omitted this question together with the preceding one [Col. 1124D] because it had been treated in the Latin language by our Tertullian and Novatian. He adds that Origen also discussed Circumcision splendidly in the fourth *volume of his commentaries* (*ἐξηγήσεων τόμῳ*) on Romans.

#### [NOTE]

[Col. 1123D] It is not as certain that Tertullian wrote a distinct book *On Circumcision* as it is in Novatian’s case, to whom Blessed Jerome expressly assigns a book entitled *On Circumcision* in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*. Yet a very extensive treatment can also be seen in our author, volume I, *Against the Jews*, chapter 3.

There is indeed in volume IV—now volume IX—of the works of Blessed Jerome a book *On True Circumcision*, which Erasmus conjectures to be Tertullian’s. But apart from the fact that its style does not correspond, the contrary is proved both by the scriptural passages cited there according to Blessed Jerome’s version and by its mention of the Manichaeans. Consequently it cannot be attributed to Novatian either, but should rather—as a certain highly learned man judged—be attributed to Paulinus.

### VI. BRIEF NOTICE OF THE BOOK *ON THE TRINITY*

[Col. 1125]

[Col. 1125A] Blessed Jerome says in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*:

> Novatian wrote a large volume *On the Trinity*, as though an epitome (*ἐπιτομήν*) [Col. 1126A] of Tertullian’s work; many, not knowing this, consider it Cyprian’s.

#### [NOTE]

[Col. 1125A] These words of Blessed Jerome reasonably create doubt as to whether Tertullian wrote another work *On the Trinity* besides his book *Against Praxeas*. For an epitome (*ἐπιτομή*) is an abridgment of a larger work, whereas Novatian’s book *On the Trinity*, being a large volume, as Jerome himself testifies, is much longer than the book *Against Praxeas*. Thus, if Blessed Jerome’s manuscripts are not corrupt, it will be necessary to acknowledge that Tertullian composed another [Col. 1125B] book *On the Trinity*.

But if we read *ἐπίτασιν* (“amplification”)—which may perhaps have been Jerome’s reading—Novatian’s book will be regarded as an expansion of the argument treated by Tertullian in his book *Against Praxeas*. The same judgment must be made if we read *μίμησιν*, that is, “imitation,” since Novatian imitates Tertullian at times.

We, therefore, since we are reluctant to alter [Col. 1126A] the reading of ancient authors without manuscript evidence, have preferred to place a book *On the Trinity* among Tertullian’s lost works. This is especially warranted because, although Novatian treats the same argument more extensively concerning God the Father and God the Son, Tertullian in turn treats more extensively, in *Against Praxeas and the Monarchians*, the distinction of the Son from the Father, whom they made one and the same—as Sabellius did later, against whom Novatian argues more briefly. Furthermore, [Col. 1126B] the Holy Spirit is treated there more extensively and more correctly, since Novatian has only a single chapter on him. Thus, strictly speaking, Novatian’s book cannot be called an *epitome*, because it is a larger work; nor an *amplification*, because in certain matters it is more concise; nor an *imitation*, because it does not imitate Tertullian in every respect.

### VII. SUBJECT AND FRAGMENTS OF THE BOOK *ON THE ORIGIN OF THE SOUL, AGAINST HERMOGENES*, from the book *On the Soul*, chapters 1, 3, 22, and 24

[Col. 1125]

[Col. 1125B] Tertullian says:

> Having confronted Hermogenes solely concerning the origin of the soul, insofar as he presumed that it was constituted from the prompting of matter rather than from the breath of God, I now turn to the remaining questions, in which I shall appear chiefly to be contending with the philosophers.

[Col. 1125C]

> One encounter against Hermogenes has already been decided, as we stated at the outset, because we vindicate the soul as derived from the breath of God, not from matter; there too we were fortified by the unclouded rule of the divine determination: “And God,” it says, “breathed the breath of life into the face of man, and man became a living soul” (Gen. II; 1 Cor. XV)—a soul derived, surely, from the breath of God. Nothing further need be reconsidered concerning this matter; it has its own title and its own heretic.

> But against Hermogenes, who maintains that it—the soul, namely—is derived from matter and not from the breath of God, we defend the term “breath” (*flatus*) in its proper sense. For, contrary to the authority of Scripture itself, he changes “breath” into “Spirit,” so that—because it is incredible that the Spirit of God should fall into sin and soon afterward into judgment—the soul might be believed to derive from matter [Col. 1126B] rather than from the Spirit of God. For this reason we there defend it as “breath,” not “spirit,” according to Scripture and according to the distinction between spirits; but here, despite our opponents, we pronounce it “spirit,” according to the nature of breathing and blowing. There the question concerns substance, for breathing [Col. 1126C] is an act of a substance.

> Hermogenes has already heard from us about the soul’s other natural properties, together with their defense and proof, through which it is recognized as akin to God rather than to matter.

> We, however, who attach nothing to it, by this very fact weigh the soul as far inferior to God, because we acknowledge that it was born and therefore possesses a more diluted divinity and a feebler felicity: it is breath, not Spirit. Although immortal—insofar as this belongs to divinity—it is nevertheless capable of suffering, insofar as this belongs to birth; and thus it was capable of deviation from the beginning and, consequently, liable also to forgetfulness. Enough concerning this matter with Hermogenes.

[Col. 1125D] In these five passages the author first indicates the title and subject: *On the Origin of the Soul, against Hermogenes*. That *census* here means “origin” is evident not only from the first passage but also from the fact that the work refuted the heresy of Hermogenes, by which he maintained that the soul’s origin lay not in the breath of God but in matter. In precisely the same way he said that the world was made not from nothing but from matter coeternal with God—a heresy against which the author argued above in the book *Against Hermogenes*.

He also adduces two scriptural passages by which he indicates that he proved the soul to be God’s breath: Genesis II and 1 Corinthians XV. In these passages Hermogenes, to make them appear favorable to his heresy, rendered “spirit of life” where others rendered “breath,” contrary to the authority of Scripture, since the Greek reads *πνοήν* (*pnoēn*, “breath”). Most interpreters prefer—as Blessed Augustine also testifies in *The City of God*, book XIII, chapter 24—to translate this as “breath,” [Col. 1126D] “inspiration,” “breathing,” or “soul,” rather than “spirit.” In the third passage cited here, the author indicates that in the aforementioned book he undertook to defend the term “breath” rather than “spirit.”

Meanwhile, although Tertullian in another sense also acknowledges the soul to be “spirit,” from “breathing,” in the book *On the Soul*, he does not agree in every respect with the Catholic position. Catholics properly call the soul a spirit, though not the Holy Spirit, and therefore also translate *πνοήν* as “spirit” in the said passage of Genesis and elsewhere. But regarding this paradox of his, we have referred the reader to our Prolegomena. . . .

He next intimates that, closer to his intended subject, he treated the soul’s other natural properties against Hermogenes, in order to prove that the soul is akin to God rather than to matter. Finally, perhaps because someone might compare the soul with God, he writes that in the said book he defended the soul as far inferior to God: because it was born, [Col. 1127A] because it is capable of suffering and deviation, and consequently liable also to forgetfulness. Thus nearly the argument of the entire book, and what might be called certain fragments of it [Col. 1128A], may be seen here repeated in the author’s very own words.

### VIII. CITATION OF THE BOOK *AGAINST THE APELLETIANS*, FROM THE BOOK *ON THE FLESH OF CHRIST* (chapter 8)

[Col. 1127]

[Col. 1127A] But since these Apelletians in particular also plead the disgrace of the flesh—which, they maintain, was fastened by that fiery ruler of evil upon souls he had enticed, and was therefore unworthy of Christ, so that a substance drawn from the stars was appropriate for him—I must strike them down with their own equipment.

They name a certain illustrious angel who established this world and, after establishing it, admitted repentance. We have treated this matter in its proper place. For we have a little book against them, examining whether one who possessed the spirit, will, and power of Christ for those works had done anything deserving repentance, since they also interpret the angel [Col. 1127B] as the figure of the wandering sheep.

Therefore, on the testimony of its maker’s repentance, the world will be a sin, since all repentance is a confession of sin, because [Col. 1128A] it has no place except where there is sin. If the world is a sin because it is a body and its members are sin, then heaven too, and the heavenly things together with heaven, will likewise be sin. If the heavenly things are sin, then whatever has been conceived and brought forth from them must be the evil fruit of an evil tree.

Christ’s flesh, therefore, if constructed from heavenly things, consisted of the elements of sin; it was sinful, derived from a sinful origin, and will be equal in substance to that flesh—that is, ours—which Christ disdains to assume because it is sinful. Thus, if there is no difference in respect of disgrace, those who are displeased with our material must either devise for Christ some other material of a purer character, or [Col. 1128B] acknowledge this material of ours, than which even heavenly material could not have been better.
