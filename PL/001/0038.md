Thus latrines were leased out; so too the vegetable market and other such public properties, for the Serapeum and Capitol were advertised under the same law. In short, the Capitol and vegetable market were leased beneath the same spear to the contractor who sought them. But what passed to the contractor through this award? Evidently, when the vegetable market was leased for five years, he might collect fixed payments from the merchandise offered there throughout that period. Similarly, when the Capitol was leased, he might demand payments for the things Tertullian himself notes in book I *To the Nations*, ch. 10: for the temple ground, for access to the sanctuary, for offerings, and for sacrificial victims. Thus the Capitol, thus the vegetable market [Note: [Col. 0346A] **Thus the vegetable market.** So Rigaltius correctly reads, supported by the Leiden manuscript, the book *To the Nations*, and the Aldine edition; others read, “whether the Capitol, whether the vegetable market.” Of all the interpreters, who have labored greatly to extract a suitable sense, Nicolas Rigaltius alone correctly explained this entire passage; see him. Tertullian is not discussing the sale of gods or statues taken as spoil and booty from enemies, as La Cerda imagines, but their leasing or rental, from which the public treasury’s revenue increased. The learned men evidently failed to notice the remarkable information about antiquity disclosed to us here. Rome was everywhere so full of statues that they constituted, as it were, another population; hence the character in Petronius says with good reason: “Our district is so full of present divinities that you can more easily find a god than a man.” There were so many temples of gods that the little Greek here too could have exclaimed, “Hear me, temples!” The Capitol was especially adorned with them, and was leased by the tax farmers so that the temple attendants and custodians received enormous profit from access to the sanctuary, the temple ground, and similar things—hence Tertullian says that one could not become acquainted with the gods without payment. Thus, in the same way as the vegetable market where vegetables were sold was sought under lease, the Capitol and the sacred temples were also eagerly bid for by some for their private and the public profit.
