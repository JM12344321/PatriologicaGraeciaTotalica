Recent investigators of Gnostic matters have regarded it as altogether established and certain that, in this and the other chapters of his little book, Tertullian frequently alluded to the books of Valentinus himself, and that he manifestly had in his hands and before his eyes Valentinus’ principal volume, entitled *Sophia*. [Col. 0594D] Moreover, this other father of *Gnosis* (Γνῶσις) published a great many works, some fragments of which survive scattered throughout Clement of Alexandria’s *Stromata*: namely, certain fragments of Valentinus’ letters in book I, pp. 975 and 409, and III, p. 450; then a scrap of his homilies in *Stromata*, book IV, p. 641; and finally a detached fragment from his treatise *On Friends*, p. 509. In addition, the most distinguished Grabe collated and corrected a short dissertation *On the Origin of Evil* from two manuscripts of the Bodleian Library, nos. 2040 and 2584, in *Spicilegium Haereticum of the Second Century*, p. 55. All these texts are found republished in the appendix to the five books of Irenaeus *Against Heresies*, pp. 352ff., ed. D. Massuet, Paris, 1710.

Indeed, the celebrated volume *On Sophia* would not even now be entirely lost, if any credit is due to Woide, an English philologist of the last century. Among the manuscripts collected by Dr. Askew, he found one composed of parchment leaves, in quarto format, written in Greek uncial letters of extreme antiquity, but composed throughout in the Egyptian language of the Copts. It bore no title at its beginning, but at the end of its second part displayed these words: “The Second Volume of the Faithful Sophia.” Woide became persuaded that this was Valentinus’ book, to which Tertullian sometimes alludes, since he saw that the codex contained thirteen penitential psalms of Sophia, thirteen songs in praise of Christ as Sophia’s redeemer, and certain conversations of Christ with the disciples and the women of the Gospels.

Yet in these very annotations Woide supplied grounds for doubting whether these texts were rightly or wrongly attributed to Valentinus. For it is by no means probable that Valentinus composed his greatest work in the Egyptian language, and not a few of the passages that Woide extracted and translated have the character of a translation of this work. Nothing in fact clearly points either to Valentinus or to his following; indeed, certain features wholly foreign to Valentinus oppose Woide’s opinion—namely, Barbelo, Jaldabaoth, twelve saviors, and [Col. 0596A] other teachings concerning the dignity of the Apostles and the origin of souls. Furthermore, psalms or songs would wrongly be attributed to the book *On Sophia*; how much more, then, the discourses of Christ, which show more clearly that these materials were taken from the apocryphal *Gospel according to the Egyptians*. Nor is this conclusion weakened by the title *The Faithful Sophia*, from which one may infer only that the book treats a Gnostic subject.

To the same subject should likewise be referred another codex described by Woide. It was written in the same Egyptian language, in the Sahidic dialect, consisted of seventy-six quarto leaves, and was divided into two parts. The former is entitled *Book of Gnosis* (Γνώσεως liber); the latter, *Book of the Great Logos according to the Mystery* (liber Magni Λόγου κατὰ Μυστήριον). The very celebrated traveler Bruce brought it from the East to England. Any investigator of antiquity who would undertake the task of publishing this book would satisfy the desires of scholars to no small degree. Cf. Matter, *Hist. du Gnostic.*, vol. II, pp. 106–107. EDD.]

# [Col. 0595] THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIANUS AGAINST THE JEWS. (C, G, S)\*

## SUMMARY

[Col. 0595B]

—After a disputation was held between a Christian and a Jewish proselyte—Tertullian apparently speaking of himself in the third person—he takes up again in writing what could not be elucidated fully enough because of the clamor of the spectators shouting together. He argues against the Jews with the most solid reasoning and from the foundations of Sacred Scripture. This was written while the Church still enjoyed peace, before the British war, at the time when the Moors were being besieged by the Romans.

First, he vindicates the grace of God for the Gentiles also, proving that they can be admitted to God’s law. Chapter I.

For the primordial law, the matrix of all the commandments, was given to Adam and Eve in Paradise; and before the written law of Moses came the righteousness of natural law in Noah and Abraham, and even the priesthood in Melchizedek. Chapter II.

The circumcision of the flesh was indeed given to Abraham for a time, but as a sign by which Israel would be distinguished in the last age, when, according to its deserts, it would be forbidden to enter the holy city. Now, however, spiritual circumcision is for the salvation of the obedient people. Chapter III.

The observance of the Sabbath was likewise temporary, a figure of the eternal Sabbath. For it is established by the examples of Joshua the son of Nun and the Maccabees that the Sabbath was observed perpetually neither by the aforementioned patriarchs before the law of Moses nor by everyone after that law. Chapter IV.

What is more, it is evident from Malachi and the Psalms that the very sacrifices of earthly offerings made by Abel and Cain and prescribed in Leviticus were predictions of spiritual sacrifices. Chapter V.

And thus it may be concluded from the foregoing that a new law was to come. The question must therefore be asked whether the legislator of the new law is still awaited or has already come. Chapter VI.

That he has come is shown first from the prophecies concerning his kingdom over the Gentiles and the eternity of that kingdom. Chapter VII.

Next, from the times of the birth of Christ the Leader, of his Passion, [Col. 0596B] and of the destruction of Jerusalem, as foretold by Daniel. Chapter VIII.

Further, from the various predictions by which Christ’s birth was announced by the prophets. Chapter IX.

Likewise, from the things foretold concerning Christ’s Passion on the cross and from its figures. Chapter X.

Again, from the prediction of Jerusalem’s destruction, not only by Daniel but also by the prophet Ezekiel. Chapter XI.

In addition, from a repetition of certain passages of Sacred Scripture concerning the conversion of the Gentiles to Christ. Chapter XII.

[Col. 0596C] Finally, by adducing other scriptural passages from which it is proved that he has already come. Chapter XIII.

Lastly, the Jews are convicted from the very abundance of their error: from Christ’s second advent in sublimity after that first advent in humility. They were deceived concerning the first advent because he did not come in sublimity, since they did not understand that he was to come in humility. Chapter XIV.

Moreover, no one can doubt that both works are by the same author, not only because in the third book *Against Marcion* Tertullian repeats in almost the same words, though somewhat more expansively, what he treats more briefly here, but also because the blessed Cyprian, in the first two books of his *Testimonies to Quirinus against the Jews*, imitated this book so closely [Col. 0596D] that he excerpted many passages from it; Julianus Pomerius did the same in his three books *Against the Jews*. Certainly Eusebius, in his *Chronicle*, expressly recounts Tertullian’s opinion concerning Christ’s birth from this book; Pomerius transcribed it into his first book, and the blessed Jerome transcribed a considerable part of Chapter IX into his commentary on Daniel. Vincent of Lérins, Abbot Trithemius, and Politian likewise explicitly mentioned this work before Rhenanus committed it to the press.

Rhenanus also published a second, more carefully corrected edition from the Gorze codex, adding several scholia. Yet because the work was absent from Leland’s British codex and therefore was not collated by Gelenius, it had not yet recovered its original form. Accordingly, we too had to labor over its restoration. For this purpose we were aided by one Vatican manuscript of this book, two copies of the third book *Against Marcion*, the aforementioned commentary of the blessed Jerome, and several passages observed in both works by Latinius, as may be seen from our annotations. PAMELIUS.

# CHAPTER I

Recently [Note: [Col. 0597D] But where and when? Who was that Christian? SEML.] it happened that a disputation was held between a Christian [Note: [Col. 0597D] “And a Jewish proselyte.” This word occurs frequently in Scripture. Among the Jews it signifies a foreigner or newcomer who, though not himself a Jew, has come over from the Gentiles to their law. LE PR.] and a Jewish proselyte. Taking turns, as though pulling upon a contentious rope [Note: [Col. 0597D] “Upon a contentious rope.” He used the proverbial expression “taking turns upon a contentious rope”: each of them drew the day out into evening. So too in the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*: “Now,” he says, “to draw out upon the contentious rope whether destruction claims a human being in this direction or that.” RHEN.], they both drew out the day into evening. Since certain supporters [Note: [Col. 0597C] “Supporters” is absent from Pam., Oberth., and Seml.] of each side were also shouting them down [Note: [Col. 0597C] Lat. reads: “with certain spectators”—*spectantibus* for Rhenanus’ *spectatoribus*—“amid a kind of cloud of hissing and quarrels.”], the truth was obscured by a kind of cloud.

I therefore resolved [Note: [Col. 0597D] That is, “I resolved.” SEML.] to examine more carefully what could not be elucidated fully enough amid the concerted noise of the disputation [Col. 0597B], and to settle the questions thus reconsidered by means of a written treatment [Note: [Col. 0597C] “By written treatments,” Oberth. Seml.]. The occasion for defending the Gentiles’ claim [Note: [Col. 0597C] “Claim” is absent from Jun.] to divine grace also possessed this advantage: a man from the Gentiles, and not a Jew descended from the stock of the Israelites, had undertaken to claim God’s law for himself [Note: [Col. 0597D] “To claim for himself.” The Vatican codex has *vendicare* rather than *vindicare*; the meaning is the same. For *vindicare*, which derives from *vindiciae*, means to assert one’s claim to a disputed matter. PAM.]. This fact alone is enough [Note: [Col. 0597C] “Is,” Oberth. Seml.] to establish that the Gentiles can be admitted to God’s law, so that Israel may no longer boast that the Gentiles are reckoned “like a drop from a bucket” or “dust from the threshing floor” (Isa. 40:15).

Besides, we have God himself as a competent promisor and faithful guarantor. He promised Abraham that in his seed all the nations of the earth would be blessed (Gen. 22:18), and that from Rebecca’s womb two peoples and two nations would come forth (Gen. 25:23): namely, of course, the Jews—that is, Israel—and the Gentiles—that is, ourselves. Each, therefore, is called both [Note: [Col. 0597C] “Both” is absent from Pam. and Par.] a people and a nation [Col. 0597C], so that no one may dare to defend for himself a privilege of grace merely from the designation of a name. For God ordained that two peoples and two nations should come forth from the womb of one woman. He did not distinguish grace [Note: [Col. 0597D] “Nor did he distinguish grace.” The Fulda Codex reads, “Nor did grace distinguish,” which is more correct. RIG.] by their designation, but by the order of their birth: the one who would come forth from the womb first was to be subjected to the younger—that is, to the one who came afterward.

For thus God spoke to Rebecca, saying: “Two nations are in your womb, and two peoples shall be divided from your belly; one people shall overcome the other, and the elder shall serve the younger” (ibid.). Accordingly, although the people or nation of the Jews is earlier in time and greater through the grace of having first been deemed worthy under the law, our people is younger in the chronology of the ages [Note: [Col. 0597C] The words “in the chronology of the ages” are understood of Rebecca and the two peoples. Lat. and Rig. delete this parenthesis as though it arose from a gloss. Jun., however, thinks it has been transposed here from what follows; Seml. places it after “earlier and greater.” Obert.: “In the chronology of the ages it is understood, from Rebecca and the two peoples, that this is possible.” This passage is hyperbatonic or corrupt and could not be remedied from the manuscript. PAM.], since it obtained knowledge of divine mercy only in the final period of the age. Without doubt, therefore, according to the declaration [Note: [Col. 0598C] “Saying,” Rhen.] of the divine utterance, the earlier and [Note: [Col. 0598C] “And” is absent from Pam. and Obert.] greater people—that is, the Jewish people—must serve the younger; and the younger people—that is, the Christian people—must overcome the elder.

For according to the records of the divine Scriptures [Note: [Col. 0598C] “Of the divine Scriptures” is absent from Pam., Rhen., and Obert. Pam. uses “records” for “Sacred Scriptures.”], the greater [Note: [Col. 0598C] “Greater” is absent from Pam. and Rhen.] people of the Jews [Col. 0598B]—that is, the more ancient people—abandoned God, served idols, and, forsaking the divine, devoted itself to images. The people said to Aaron: “Make us gods who shall go before us” (Exod. 32:23). When gold from the women’s necklaces and the men’s rings [Note: [Col. 0597D] “And the men’s rings.” Similarly, the blessed Ambrose, Epistle 56 to Romulus: “Aaron, therefore, being compelled, asks for their rings and the women’s earrings.” Since this is read neither in the Greek text now current nor in the Latin edition of the blessed Jerome, the Septuagint’s edition must in certain places have differed from the one now extant. This is confirmed by the words that the Author cites presently. PAM.] had been melted in the fire [Note: [Col. 0598C] Jun. deletes “gold.”], and a calf’s head came forth for them, all Israel abandoned God and gave honor to this fabrication, saying: “These are the gods [Note: [Col. 0598C] “Gods,” Obert. Seml.] who brought us out of the land of Egypt.”

Thus also, in later times, when kings ruled over them, they worshiped Jeroboam’s golden calves and sacred groves [Note: [Col. 0598D] “And when they worshiped Jeroboam’s golden calves,” etc. He attacks Jewish superstition and idolatry, to which that nation was especially prone. The particular figure noted here is King Jeroboam I. After he had been made king, he feared that if the Israelites returned to Jerusalem for the sacred rites, they would become dissatisfied with the separation they had then effected. So, to provide them with something to worship among themselves, he set up golden calves, one in Dan and the other in Bethel; he added sacrificing priests and forbade the Israelites from thereafter going to Jerusalem. The account is found at 1 Kings 12 [Vulgate: 3 Kings 12]. LE PR.], and enslaved themselves to Baal. Hence the record of the divine Scriptures proves that they were always marked out as guilty of the crime of idolatry [Note: [Col. 0598C] Others read: “they were thought.”].

Our younger people, however—that is, the people who came later—abandoned the idols it had previously served and turned to the same God from whom Israel, as we recalled above [Col. 0598C], had departed. Thus the younger—that is, later—people overcame the elder people when it obtained the grace of divine favor, from which Israel was rejected.

# CHAPTER II

[Col. 0599A]

Let us therefore advance a step and define the substance of the question itself within precise boundaries [Note: [Col. 0599C] “Bring to an end,” Jun., from Pam.’s manuscript.]. Why should it be believed that God—the creator of the universe, governor of the entire world, fashioner of humanity, and planter of all nations—gave the law through Moses to one people, and be said not to have bestowed it upon all nations? For if he had not given it to all, he would by no means have permitted proselytes from the Gentiles to gain access to it.

Rather, as befits the goodness and equity of God, since he is the fashioner of the human race, he gave the same law to all nations. He commanded it to be observed at particular and appointed times—when he wished, through whom he wished, and in the manner he wished. For at the beginning of the world he gave Adam and Eve themselves a law: they were not to eat from the fruit of the tree planted in [Col. 0599B] the middle of Paradise; and if they acted contrary to it, they would surely die (Gen. 2:7). That law would have sufficed for them, had it been kept.

For in this law given to Adam we recognize all the commandments contained within it that afterward sprouted forth when given through Moses. These are: [Note: [Col. 0599D] “You shall love the Lord your God,” etc., “and your neighbor as yourself.” He imitates a Hellenism and the Greek expression πλησίον σου, “your neighbor,” as is customary with him when citing Scripture. LE PR.] “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul” (Deut. 6:5); “You shall love your neighbor [Note: [Col. 0599C] “Your neighbor,” manuscripts and Pam.] as yourself” (Lev. 19:18); “You shall not kill; you shall not commit adultery; you shall not steal; you shall not bear false witness. Honor your father and your mother” (Exod. 20:12–17); and “You shall not covet what belongs to another” (Deut. 5:16–21).

The primordial law given to Adam and Eve in Paradise is therefore, as it were, the matrix of all God’s commandments. Indeed, if they had loved the Lord their God, they would not have acted against his commandment. If they had loved their neighbor—that is, themselves [Col. 0599C]—they would not have believed the serpent’s persuasion; and thus they would not have committed murder against themselves by falling away from immortality through acting against God’s commandment. They would also have abstained from theft, had they not secretly tasted the fruit of the tree (Gen. 3:6), or attempted to hide from the sight of the Lord their God beneath a tree (ibid. 8). Nor would they have made themselves accomplices of the devil in his false assertion by believing him when he said that they would become like God [Col. 0600A] (ibid. 8). And thus they would not have offended God as their Father, who had fashioned them from the clay of the earth as though from a mother’s womb. If they had not coveted what belonged to another [Note: [Col. 0599C] “Did not covet,” Seml.], they would not have tasted the forbidden fruit.

Thus, in this general and primordial law of God, which God ordained to be observed in relation to the fruit of the tree, we recognize that all the commandments of the later law were individually contained. When issued at their appointed times, they germinated. For it belongs to the same one to teach the law more fully afterward who had earlier sent the commandment before it; just as it belongs to the one to instruct afterward who had earlier undertaken to form righteous people. What wonder is it if he who instituted the discipline also enlarges it [Note: [Col. 0599D] Irenaeus and others are accustomed to frame the matter in this way. SEML.]? If he who began it also perfected it [Note: [Col. 0599C] “Perfects,” Pam.; “advances,” Rhen. Obert.]?

Indeed, before the law of Moses was written [Note: [Col. 0599D] “Indeed, before the law of Moses was written,” etc. Thus the same author in the book *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 6: “Will you therefore seek the law of God, although you possess that law common to all in the public domain of the world, on the tablets of nature, to which the Apostle also customarily appeals?” Pertinent to this may be Cicero’s statement in the *For Milo*: “For this law,” he says, “was not written but born with us; we did not learn, receive, or read it, but seized, imbibed, and drew it from nature itself. We were not taught it but made for it, not instructed in it but imbued with it.” Robert Cedalia, in the *Shalshelet*, testifies that the precepts of the law were given to Adam and inscribed upon his breast: [text absent]. Origen does not differ from him in the *Philocalia*, in these words: “Besides these, the law is said to be that which, in accordance with the common conceptions, has been implanted in the soul and which, as Scripture terms it, is a word inscribed upon the heart, commanding what must be done and forbidding what must not be done” (παρὰ δὲ ταῦτα λέγεται νόμος ὁ κατὰ τὰς κοινὰς ἐννοίας ἐνεσπαρμένος τῇ ψυχῇ, καὶ ὡς ὀνομάζει ἡ γραφή, ἐγγεγραμμένος τῇ καρδίᾳ λόγος, προστακτικὸς μὲν τῶν ποιητέων, ἀπαγορευτικὸς δὲ τῶν οὐ ποιητέων). [Col. 0600D] Moreover, the natural law is said to be implanted within the soul and, as Scripture calls it, inscribed upon the hearts, commanding what must be done and forbidding what must not be done. LE PR.], I maintain that there was an unwritten law, understood naturally and kept by the fathers.

For how was Noah found righteous (Gen. 6:9; Sir. 44:17), if the righteousness of natural law did not precede him? How was Abraham accounted a friend of God (Isa. 41:8; Jas. 2:23), if it was not because of the equity and righteousness of natural law? How was Melchizedek called a priest of the Most High God (Gen. 14:18), if, before the priesthood of the Levitical law, there were not Levites who offered sacrifices to God? For it was only after the patriarchs named above that the law was given to Moses, when they had departed from Egypt, following an interval and span of many ages. Indeed, the law was given 430 years after Abraham [Note: [Col. 0600D] “The law was given 430 years after Abraham.” The meaning of this passage is not that this law was given to Abraham. For if anyone contends that the law of circumcision is under discussion here, chronology altogether opposes him, since only 391 years are counted from the Flood to the institution of circumcision. If he understands Abraham’s calling, he is even farther from the truth, since only 367 years elapsed between the Flood and that calling. Another law, therefore, is mentioned in this passage. Which law is meant is taught by Gen. 15:13; Exod. 12:40; Acts 7:6; and Gal. 3:17, where these 430 years are specified. They are counted from the first promise made to Abraham (Gen. 12:3). For in that same year the people of Israel were delivered from Egypt and received the law. Compare Estius, Cornelius a Lapide, Fromondus, *Complete Course of Sacred Scripture*, and, before them, Augustine, St. Thomas, etc. EDD.].

From this we understand that God’s law existed even before Moses: not only on Horeb or Sinai and in the wilderness, but more anciently [Note: [Col. 0600C] “More anciently; first,” Obert. Seml.—“First, but more anciently” is absent from Pam.]—first in Paradise, afterward among the patriarchs, and then reconstituted for the Jews at appointed times [Col. 0600C]. We should therefore no longer regard the law of Moses as though it were the primary law, but as a subsequent law, which God at a definite time also presented to the Gentiles, reconstituted for the better as promised through the prophets, and foretold would come [Note: [Col. 0600C] “Would come,” feminine, Rhen. Obert.]. Thus, just as the law was given through Moses at a definite time, it should be believed to have been observed and kept only for a time. Nor should we take away this authority of God, who, according to the circumstances of the times, reconstitutes the commandments of the law [Col. 0601A] for humanity’s salvation [Note: [Col. 0601B] “In salvation,” Seml.].

Indeed, whoever contends that the Sabbath must still be observed as though it were a remedy of salvation, and that circumcision on the eighth day must be practiced because of the threat of death, must show that in former times righteous men kept the Sabbath or were circumcised and thereby became friends of God. For if circumcision purifies a human being, why, when God made Adam uncircumcised, did he not circumcise him—either then or after Adam sinned—if circumcision purifies? Certainly, when he placed him uncircumcised in Paradise, he appointed him cultivator of Paradise.

Therefore, since God established Adam as neither circumcised nor Sabbath-observant, he consequently also praised Adam’s offspring Abel, who offered sacrifices to him while uncircumcised and not observing the Sabbath. God accepted what Abel offered in simplicity of heart, but rejected the sacrifice of his brother [Note: [Col. 0601B] “His” is absent from Seml. and Obert.; in Pam. it is placed after “Cain.”] Cain [Col. 0601B], who did not offer rightly and did not divide rightly (Gen. 4).

God likewise delivered Noah from the Flood although he was uncircumcised and did not observe the Sabbath (Gen. 6ff.). He also translated the most righteous Enoch from this world, although Enoch was neither circumcised nor Sabbath-observant (Gen. 5:22). Enoch, who has not yet tasted death, thereby showed us already, as a candidate for eternity [Col. 0602A], that we too can please God without bearing the burden of the law of Moses.

Melchizedek too, the priest of the Most High God (Gen. 14:18), was chosen for God’s priesthood though uncircumcised and not observing the Sabbath. Lot, Abraham’s brother, likewise proves [Note: [Col. 0601B] “And the approved Lot,” Pam. Rhen. Obert.] that, because of the merits of his righteousness [Note: [Col. 0601B] “His” is absent from Pam., Fran., Rig., and Venet.], he was delivered from the burning of the Sodomites without observance of the law.

# CHAPTER III

“But Abraham,” he says [Note: [Col. 0602B] “You say,” Obert. Rhen.], “was circumcised.” Yet he pleased God before he was circumcised, and nevertheless did not observe the Sabbath. For he had received circumcision, but a circumcision intended as a sign of that particular time, not as a privileged means of salvation.

Indeed, the patriarchs who followed were uncircumcised, such as Melchizedek, who [Note: [Col. 0601B] “Melchizedek offered bread and wine to Abraham himself as Abraham returned from battle.” The passage is Gen. 14, [Col. 0601C] and in the Latin Vulgate it is extremely intricate, since the offering of bread and wine is left ambiguous: was it made to God or to Abraham? Here, however, Tertullian states with complete clarity that Melchizedek offered bread and wine to Abraham as he returned from battle. It would follow that the verb “to offer” in this passage has no reference to sacrifice. For who could tolerate the idea that Melchizedek, priest of the Most High, was sacrificing to Abraham?

Our own Septimius, moreover, in Book V *Against Marcion*, shows that Melchizedek performed a priestly act when he blessed Abraham and received tithes from him. Yet that priest of the Most High, since he was also king of Salem, likewise performed an act most fitting to royal magnificence. Going out to meet Abraham as he led his army back from battle after brave and successful exploits, he received him and his victorious forces with bread and wine—that is, with everything necessary to restore bodies wearied by war.

And indeed the Greek translation of the seventy elders contradicts this interpretation: “And Melchizedek, king of Salem, brought out bread and wine; and he was priest of [Col. 0601D] God Most High; and he blessed,” etc. (Καὶ Μελχισεδὲκ βασιλεὺς Σάλημ ἐξήνεκε ἄρτον καὶ οἶνον· ἦν δὲ ἱερεὺς τοῦ Θεοῦ τοῦ ὑψίστου· καὶ εὐλόγησε, etc.). Josephus puts it excellently: “This Melchizedek provided gifts of hospitality for Abraham’s army and furnished a great abundance of provisions” (Ἐχορήγησε δὲ οὗτος ὁ Μελχισεδέκης τῷ Ἀβράμου στρατῷ ξένια, καὶ πολλὴν ἀφθονίαν τῶν ἐπιτηδείων παρέσχε).

Nevertheless, the most ancient and holy Fathers handed down a far different interpretation of this event: namely, that by the sacrifice of bread and wine Melchizedek expressed the mystery of the Lord’s body and blood, and prefigured the sacrament of the Lord that was to come much later. These are Jerome’s words in his *Epistle to Demetrias*; Chrysostom, Augustine, and others wrote in agreement with them. RIG.

—I do not know what Rigaltius finds so intricate here. Some assert that this offering was made to God, among them the blessed Jerome in Epistle 17 to Marcella, where he at least says that the king of Salem, as a type of Christ, offered bread and wine and inaugurated the Christian mystery in the Savior’s blood and body. Others say it was made to Abraham; among these too is the blessed Jerome, in Epistle 126 to Evagrius, where he recounts that entire history. In it, however, one must observe that Genesis states that the king offered Abraham tithes from everything [Col. 0602B], although the blessed Jerome, in the passage mentioned, asserts that he offered tithes only from the spoils of the enemy.
