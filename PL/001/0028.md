**SIGEBERT HAVERCAMP SENDS GREETINGS TO THE FAIR-MINDED READER.**

[Col. 0249D] Africa is always producing something new. That land, dreadful in its desolation and the nurse of wild beasts, enriched the schools of beasts and the spectacles once presented by the Romans with a thousand animals, diverse in the form of their terror and the magnitude of their strength. Before the delighted populace of Romulus, it sent Libyan monsters, themselves not unlike the spectators, against the most sacred bodies of the martyrs. It was this same Africa that produced not only foxes—a race [Col. 0250D] full of guile, a grievous evil to the grapes when the vines first began to burgeon in the Christian Church—but also supplied boars fearsome for their tusks, digging up the vineyards themselves, horribly roaring lions, and savage bears. Yet just as African hunters in particular were urgently demanded to moderate the delight of the Eternal City—for by that false name Rome flattered herself—men who, powerful in strength [Col. 0251A] and skilled at frustrating the bites of beasts, might evade their assaults and kill the beasts themselves, so too the truly eternal City of the true God saw a sufficiently great defense born for itself from this same Africa against Valentinians, Gnostics, Arians, and similar monstrosities of nature.

For why should I praise Augustine’s subtlety and learning? Why commend the honey-flowing eloquence of Lactantius? Why pursue the learned inquisitiveness of Arnobius? Why admire the piety that shines everywhere in Cyprian’s writings, which he himself sealed with his blood? Finally, why should I marvel at the rounded and gently flowing eloquence of Minucius, who earned much true glory even from a single little book? TERTULLIAN alone is the equivalent of them all: Augustine takes great delight in him; Lactantius recognizes his mastery of every kind of literature; Arnobius sets him before himself as a model to follow; Cyprian, [Col. 0251B] whenever he asks for his books, calls him “the Master”; and finally Minucius, in his own Dialogue—which is like a small curving bay enclosed from the vast sea of this *Apologeticum*—all but transcribes him.

Although it is not my purpose to reproduce here all that ancient and modern authors have thought about this TERTULLIAN, with no small accumulation of praise, I nevertheless cannot refrain from sampling a few judgments. Vincent of Lérins, a writer of the fifth century—not without eloquence—according to the date assigned to him by du Cange, condensed the highly honorable testimonies of the ancients into these words:

“He must readily be judged the chief of all our Latin writers. For what man was more learned than he? Who was more thoroughly versed in matters divine and human? Indeed, with a certain marvelous capacity of mind, he embraced all philosophy and all the schools of the philosophers, the founders and champions of those schools, all [Col. 0251C] their disciplines, and every variety of history and learning. And did he not excel in an intellect so weighty and forceful that he set himself almost nothing to conquer which he did not either penetrate by acuteness or crush by sheer weight? Furthermore, who could adequately recount the merits of his style? It is woven together with so great and, as it were, so dense a mass of arguments that those whom it could not persuade it compels to agree with it. Almost every word of his is a maxim; every thought, a victory.”

Among more recent writers, the illustriously noble Joseph Scaliger knew no one in the learned world more elevated or more accomplished in every kind of learning and science. See, then, how great it is to be praised by one who is himself praised. These are his words in the first [Col. 0251D] *Scaligerana*, p. 96:

“TERTULLIAN must always be kept at hand and read carefully, both because of the distinctive character of the Roman language and because of his knowledge of every discipline and branch of literature. For he was an all-knowing and acute teacher, who must not be handled with unwashed hands or read in passing, since everywhere he makes the reader pause. TERTULLIAN is certainly a most excellent author in every respect.”

There also survives the highly learned and exact letter of Lipsius, Book IV, Epistle IX, to Jacobus Pamelius, to this effect:

“I hear, most distinguished Pamelius, that you are engaged in correcting TERTULLIAN. Whether you have undertaken a greater task or a more useful one, I do not know. To correct a writer filled with ancient and recondite learning is arduous; for that same writer to be published in corrected form concerns not only you theologians but all of us who handle Greek [Col. 0252A] and Roman literature.”

With good reason, therefore, among Barthius—a noble scholar of wide-ranging learning—he bears the title **FATHER OF LEARNING**, *Adversaria*, Book XIII, chap. XVI. In the same author, Book XIX, chap. I, one may also read the following:

“Of the writers who have come down to us, no one is more learned, and there is no one from whom you can learn more, than TERTULLIAN. There is not a single stroke in him that was not placed with care, so that, just as others sometimes seem to err through poverty of learning, he sometimes appears to err through its abundance.”

To these should deservedly be added the testimony of Thomas Campanella, a man whose intellectual force and acuteness of judgment I have often admired. In his little work *On His Own Books and the Right Method of Study*, chap. IX, Crenius’s edition, p. 216, after enumerating the virtues and faults of certain writers who treated theological subjects, he concludes his discussion of our author as follows: [Col. 0252B]

“TERTULLIAN, finally, as the Master of Theologians, proves more in two words than the others do with a thousand arguments: weighty, difficult, keen, effective, brief, elegant; a cultivator of philosophical, though not civic, grammar, who by his African manner of speech attracts men as though to foreign wares.”

What wonder, then, if so great an author found not only such distinguished eulogists but also commentators from every nation? Consecrated to eternity together with the name of TERTULLIAN, the names of RHENANUS, PAMELIUS, JUNIUS, DE LA CERDA, HERALDUS, GOTHOFREDUS, SALMASIUS, and RIGALTIUS will remain, drawn from every condition and discipline. I shall now pass over ZEPHYRUS, BARRAEUS, GAGNAEUS, LATINIUS, GELENIUS, MERCERIUS, WOUWERIUS, and the quite numerous [Col. 0252C] company of other scholars who wished to leave posterity public monuments of their devotion to this author.

Most of these men gave us all the works of TERTULLIAN adorned with their explanations. First among them was RHENANUS—to whom a grateful posterity owes both this author and very many others brought into the light—in editions repeated two or three times; GELENIUS and BARRAEUS followed him, then PAMELIUS and JUNIUS, and LOUIS DE LA CERDA, a man of outstanding learning. After them, a man of recognized learning, NICOLAS RIGALTIUS, all but brought Tertullian back alive from the dead; I omit a certain GEORGE AMBINAS, who, despite the gaping bulk of the three volumes of his laborious *Omniloquium*, accomplished nothing worthy of note. Rigaltius’s editions were very frequently returned to the press at Paris under the auspices of PHILIPPE LE PRIEUR, but in a form much more corrupted than that of Rigaltius himself, as we have often demonstrated in this one little book.

The rest proceeded by parts, or left behind corrections and brief notes published separately from the author’s work. Among these the industry of DESIDERIUS HERALDUS stands out. He composed a commentary full of varied learning upon this *APOLOGETICUM*—the work to which we too first chose to apply our mind and thoughts—as well as some truly learned books of digressions. But long before him FRANCIS ZEPHYRUS had also, in some fashion, illustrated this work with a kind of paraphrase; and ALDUS, following the editorial care of Baptista Egnatius, had published it separately at the end of Lactantius in the years 1515 and 1535. Yet Joannes Tacuinus had anticipated him, having likewise appended it to Lactantius at Venice in 1509.

And yet, after so many men of such distinction, I have ventured to appear before the public, [Col. 0253A] not in order to advertise my zeal and diligence or to weave for myself a garland of praise from censuring and excerpting the flowers of others—a practice I detest and hate—but so that, as a theologian, I might render whatever service I could to the theologian TERTULLIAN and to our own theologians; so that I might contribute to the understanding of an author who, by universal acclaim, was supremely skilled in Greek and Roman antiquities and disciplines, should anything relevant to this work of his present itself to me in the writers of either language while I happened to be continually handling him. I do not deny that from my earliest years I have taken very great pleasure in, and been captivated by, reading and engaging with those writers. Finally, my purpose was to provide some treatment for this author who, if any man ever was, remains, after so many physicians, exceedingly corrupt and in the worst condition.

As for this last purpose, [Col. 0253B] since I was well aware how difficult it is—especially with this author—even from the following words of the great Scaliger in the *Scaligerana*, p. 47:

“Beza had done some work on Tertullian, but he did well not to publish it. It is difficult to publish anything upon that author without manuscripts,”

I began to look about for that kind of assistance which scholars certainly cannot do without and from which they ought not to be barred. Yet I did not think I needed to travel as far as Turin, where Mabillon, in his *Italian Journey*, p. 8, testifies that he encountered a manuscript of this *APOLOGETICUM* dating back six hundred years. Let those to whom it is granted to enjoy such good fortune consult those parchments.

For our part, an outstanding parchment manuscript presented itself to us, containing, besides this little book, many writings of TERTULLIAN. [Col. 0253C] It occupies the second place among the manuscripts of the very well-stocked Leiden Library and is catalogued there on p. 324. Although we do not venture to assert that it is extremely ancient, we affirm that comparison has clearly established for us that it was derived from a very good and very ancient exemplar, and that in many places it agrees with the Fulda manuscript and other excellent witnesses.

For I do not think that anywhere on earth there exist manuscripts of this *APOLOGETICUM* comparable in singularity and excellence with the one formerly kept in the Fulda Library. Francis Modius of Bruges, a most diligent and learned man, had written the variant readings of this manuscript into a copy of the edition published at Paris in 1580 by Michael Julianus under the editorship of RENATUS LAURENTIUS BARRAEUS—not by RENATUS LAURENTIUS himself, as JUNIUS writes, since he had not examined that edition, which has also been carefully reviewed by us on this basis. [Col. 0253D] When these readings subsequently came into the hands of Gaspar Scioppius, he transmitted them to JUNIUS. But since Junius’s entire edition had already been prepared, he relegated them to the end of his notes on TERTULLIAN.

What has always seemed especially remarkable to me is that neither DE LA CERDA nor HERALDUS himself cast his eyes upon this exceedingly distinguished collation. It remained hidden after those notes of JUNIUS, known to scarcely anyone except GOTHOFREDUS, until NICOLAS RIGALTIUS, a man of penetrating learning, used it in the more correct of his earlier editions—but in such a way that he took from it those readings that pleased him, without mentioning in his notes any of the others, though they were quite numerous. He therefore left us a sufficiently abundant gleaning from that very collation. Throughout our notes we have faithfully reproduced its readings just as Modius, a most trustworthy excerptor, recorded them. [Col. 0254A]

In addition, there came to us an exact collation of an Agobardine manuscript—not indeed of that manuscript from which the remaining writings of TERTULLIAN are now read, all but raised from the underworld, and in which alone are found the two hitherto exceedingly corrupt books *To the Nations*; but nevertheless a manuscript of quite good quality, which, like the Leiden manuscript just mentioned, had not hitherto been examined by any editor of TERTULLIAN.

Three arguments persuade me that these two Agobardine manuscripts are distinct. First, RIGALTIUS corrected a great many of Tertullian’s books from a very ancient Agobardine manuscript written more than eight hundred years ago; before each book he noted from which manuscript and in how many places he had corrected it, yet before the little book of the *APOLOGETICUM* he does not mention this manuscript. [Col. 0254B]

Second, in the extremely exact collation of that most ancient Agobardine manuscript which JOSIAS MERCERUS made in his own hand against the Pamelian edition—in 1625, as he himself recorded, for Agobard—not JOANNES MERCERIUS, as is printed in the library catalogue, p. 71, unless my memory altogether deceives me, though I have used that book very often—the margins of the *APOLOGETICUM* are not covered with any notes. That book may still be seen today in the Leiden Library, p. 71.

Third and especially, JACQUES GOTHOFREDUS, editor of the books *To the Nations* and himself the distinguished owner of this treasure before it was transferred into the library of the king of France, carefully drew up in his preface to those books a catalogue of the works contained in that manuscript, but did not number the *APOLOGETICUM* among them. [Col. 0254C]

The collation of the other Agobardine manuscript which we now publish likewise resulted from the diligence of JOSIAS MERCERUS. He must be distinguished from JOANNES MERCERIUS, who was a professor of law at Bourges, composed notes on Tertullian’s book *On the Pallium*, and lived much earlier. The hand of PAUL COLOMESIUS, who had attached the following words to the margin of the Rigaltian edition, testifies to this:

“In the margin of this volume you have the notes of JOSIAS MERCERUS and variant readings extracted by him from the Agobardine manuscript. PAUL COLOMESIUS copied them at Souldley, near Windsor, in the house of Vossius, in the year 1682.”

FRANCIS FABRICIUS, the most celebrated Doctor and Professor of Sacred Theology at the University of Leiden, had mentioned this book to me and said that he possessed it. When I then asked for it, he ordered it to be brought out and, with his characteristic kindness, made it available. But the work had already advanced as far as the end of Chapter XI; therefore it seemed necessary to me that the collation of the first eleven chapters should be inserted among the Addenda. [Col. 0254D]

The same procedure had to be followed with another parchment manuscript, catalogued on p. 376 of the Leiden Library and bound together with writings of Cyprian and others. At first this book escaped our diligence, but very recently we collated it with the greatest care and inserted its variant readings into the same Addenda. It was certainly an excellent manuscript. Although in the quality of its script it was less elegant than the first Leiden parchment, it surpassed it in age and accuracy.

I also used JUNIUS’s edition, in which JOSEPH SCALIGER had written certain conjectures. Throughout, these have been acknowledged with the honor due to the name of so great a man. I likewise used a copy of the same edition in which PETRUS SCRIVERIUS had added certain corrections from ancient manuscripts, a volume which I had long ago acquired from the Almeloveen library when it was dispersed. [Col. 0255A] These corrections should perhaps be referred to the manuscripts of Heribert Rosweyde, a learned Jesuit and friend of Scriverius. Certainly the following words, once prefixed by the most learned SCRIVERIUS to his book, concern him:

“Heribert Rosweyde, in a letter written to me on the sixth of May 1603: ‘I have lately obtained two manuscript copies of TERTULLIAN’S *APOLOGETICUM*. They agree wonderfully and defend the ancient readings against the acuteness of the critics. I was indeed astonished that the great Scaliger devised *Gomphosin* for us in TERTULLIAN—a plausible conjecture, certainly, but by no means necessary, etc.’”

Nothing further appears there.

Besides this accession of manuscripts, I diligently sought out and compared every edition hitherto published of the works of TERTULLIAN or of this *APOLOGETICUM*, and noted the points at which they differed, [Col. 0255B] so that no one may say that I approached so great an author and so distinguished a work of his—such as this *APOLOGETICUM* above all—unprepared. What I have accomplished will be for the fair-minded reader to judge. If I have slipped anywhere—something which very often happened even to the greatest men before me in this very work, and which cannot be avoided in this kind of writing—he will, I think, readily pardon it.

Certain matters still remain for me to discuss: matters pertaining to the life, character, and opinions of TERTULLIAN, not all of which agreed in every respect with the orthodox Church; the time at which he wrote this *APOLOGETICUM*; the party to which he then belonged, and whether he was devoted to Montanus; and why, following, as I have observed, the example of Justin Martyr, he made his two books *To the Nations* into a kind of advance guard for this *APOLOGETICUM*. [Col. 0255C] Some of these matters, however, have been treated more fully by others; some are touched upon in this commentary itself; and some will also be set forth by us in the Prolegomena to the remaining books which he composed under various titles against the Gentiles. If it pleases God, the arbiter of human affairs, I intend shortly to publish these books, illustrated by my own and others’ brief notes.

Indeed, that no moderate amount of diligence has been devoted by us to them is evident even from the fact [Col. 0256A] that in these two small books *To the Nations*, when the opportunity was not sought but presented itself spontaneously, we have removed more than twenty corruptions in this commentary of ours. Since no assistance from manuscripts remains in their case beyond the single surviving witness, it was necessary to allow some freedom to conjecture.

Yet I wish it to be known that TERTULLIAN not only applied great care to this *APOLOGETICUM*, but also issued this same work to the public twice, altering the words and style a little here and there. This will, we think, appear evident to the reader if he compares the passages cited by us on pp. 292b, 313a, 409b, and especially 439a–b. Nor should this seem surprising, even if no one before us has observed it, since it is easy to show that the same thing happened to both Lucretius and Lucan. [Col. 0256B] Thus Hugo Grotius not undeservedly renders the same judgment concerning Lucan at Book VI, verse 187; Conrad Rittershausen acknowledges that the phenomenon may be observed in many authors in his commentary on Oppian—his words are excerpted from it by Gaspar Scioppius, *On the Critical Art*, p. 87—and finally the great Salmasius demonstrated it by invincible arguments in his notes on Achilles Tatius.

So much for these matters. If they please you, kindly reader, we promise that our labor will continue to advance your benefit. For we have many works begun, half completed, and even finished, which we shall freely draw from our store so long as spirit and leisure remain, and so long as

> —within this vigorous age  
> Blood remains, and strength stands firm in its own power.

[Col. 0256C] For, as the sweet Muse of the Syracusan Theocritus soberly counsels, *Idyll* XIV, final verse:

> —ποιεῖν τι δεῖ ὅς γόνυ χλωρόν—“one must act while one’s knee is still vigorous”.

Written in my study in the village of Stad aan ’t Haringvliet, on the island of Overflakkee, on the very Kalends of June, in the year 1718.

# THE APOLOGETICUM AGAINST THE GENTILES, IN DEFENSE OF THE CHRISTIANS. (C)

## ARGUMENT OF THE ENTIRE WORK

[Col. 0257A]

While Septimius Severus was emperor and administered the Roman state together with his son Antoninus, Tertullian, burning with zeal for the true religion, whose sacred rites he had of course embraced, was distressed—as he ought to have been—that a religion which had begun in some measure to raise its head was being oppressed. This occurred at the beginning of the fifth persecution, during whose fury innocent Christians everywhere, without regard for sex or age, were tormented with punishments unheard of before that day.

With the supreme eloquence in which he then excelled, he undertook the defense of the Christians. Their condition was so deplorable that they were condemned without their case being heard and solely by a prejudgment founded upon their name. He judged it his duty to confront the common and public evil and, so far as lay within him, to restrain the storm that was daily breaking forth. For this reason he sent an apology to the Roman governors and for a time broke and weakened the unbridled assaults of his adversaries. For he pleaded the case with such vigor and learning that, if the dispute had been decided by reasons and truth, he would have driven [Col. 0257B] all the Gentiles into an impasse. He could have followed in the footsteps of Justin Martyr and Aristides; yet he imitated their eloquence in such a way that he appears to have surpassed them both in character of expression, weight of argument, and variety of learning.

# CHAPTER I

**ARGUMENT.—Since under Severus Christians had been denied permission to defend themselves in court, the author offered this work to the governors of the Roman Empire. In this chapter he demonstrates the injustice of that judicial procedure by which a defense is denied to the innocent and the truth is concealed. Meanwhile, the very many and very considerable persons who daily pass over to this sect make it worthy of some consideration; all of them, being free from any guilt of depravity, plainly show that they have assumed a disposition far different from that which is always discovered in criminals.**

When the supreme philosopher Anaxagoras, who had been highly neglectful of both public and private affairs, was addressed by someone in this manner, “Have you no concern at all for your homeland?” he replied, ἐμοὶ γὰρ καὶ σφόδρα, μέλει τῆς πατρίδος, δείξας τὸν οὐρανόν, that is, “Indeed I have concern for my homeland, and the greatest concern,” pointing his finger toward heaven. Diogenes relates this in his *Life* of Anaxagoras. Chrysostom, *Homily on Saints Bernice and Prosdoce*: “But the women enjoyed profound tranquility, since hope of future things fortified them on every side; because, even while living abroad, they were not living abroad. For they had faith as their true homeland, confession as their own city, and, since they were sustained by the best hope, they were affected by none of the things present, for they looked only toward those to come.” [Col. 0260D] Thus they could glory with Diogenes, the freest of all the Greeks: Ἔχειν πατρίδα ἀδοξίαν καὶ πενίαν ἀνάλωτον τῇ τύχῃ, that is, “that they possessed as their homeland contempt for glory and poverty, things which would never fall under the power of fortune.” HAVERCAMP.], her hope, favor, and dignity in heaven.
