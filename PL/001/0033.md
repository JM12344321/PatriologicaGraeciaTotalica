For, lest shameless pleasure grow cold even in winter, the Spartans were the first to devise for the games the hateful cloak [Note: [Col. 0302B] **The Spartans were the first to devise for the games the hateful cloak.** The Spartans, a hardy and warlike people, would have devised heavy cloaks, hateful because of their weight, for military campaigns undertaken even in winter rather than for games or theaters. Thus Tertullian says mockingly: “For, lest shameless pleasure grow cold even in winter, the Spartans were the first to devise for the games the hateful cloak.” “The hateful cloak,” as at the beginning of *On the Soldier’s Crown*: “He removed the extremely heavy cloak, taking his auspices from uncovering his head.” The following lines of Martial XIII.145, headed “A Woolly Cloak,” also imply that such cloaks were heavy:

> Such whiteness is in me, such charm in my nap,  
> That you would wish to put me on even at midsummer. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0302B] **The hateful cloak.** All these words must be pronounced ironically, for each carries its own emphasis. Rigaltius first restored this reading from the excellent Fulda manuscript. All the others, including the printed editions, have “the cloak”; some absurdly even have “the cloak for the naked,” as do manuscript L.B. and Rhenanus. He elegantly says “the hateful cloak” for “the odious cloak,” both because its fabric was thick, heavy, and weighty, and because it was new to Roman customs. Suetonius shows that it had not yet come into common use in the time of Augustus when, in *Claudius* 11, he says: “Because of this same ill health, while presiding over a gladiatorial show which he presented together with his brother in memory of their father, he wore a short cloak, contrary to custom.” On the use of the cloak, Martial XIV.137 writes:

> We are recommended for use in amphitheaters,  
> When our outer cloak covers your freezing toga.] [Note: [Col. 0301A] Thus Rigaltius and Havercamp from the Fulda manuscript; Heraldus, Semler, and others: “the Spartans devised the cloak,” etc.; Rhenanus: “the cloak for the naked,” etc.].

I see that no distinction in dress remains between matrons and prostitutes. In the case of women, too, those ancestral institutions have fallen which protected modesty and sobriety: when no woman knew gold [Note: [Col. 0302A] Rhenanus: “when no limbs knew gold except the single finger which,” etc.] [Note: [Col. 0302C] **No woman knew gold.** Yet that betrothal ring was made of iron, as Pliny teaches in XXX.1. Did Tertullian not know this? Or did he speak according to the custom of his own age, as Kippingius thinks, *Roman Antiquities* IV.2? Or rather, have they misunderstood Pliny, who was speaking not of a betrothal ring but of a symbol of the thrift and modesty by which a bride or newly married woman was recalled to those austere Sabine women of old? The editions of Rhenanus read: “when no limbs knew gold except the single finger which,” etc.], except on a single finger [Note: [Col. 0302A] “The single finger,” Paris manuscript.], which the bridegroom had pledged with the betrothal ring; when women were kept so completely from wine [Note: [Col. 0302A] “Abstained,” Paris manuscript.] [Note: [Col. 0302D] **Were kept from wine.** I have restored this reading from the Fulda manuscript. Manuscript L.B. agrees with it, having, like Heraldus, “were withheld from wine.” Rigaltius also reads thus, though not in all editions; for I remember finding “abstained from wine” in one of Prior’s editions, as the other editions commonly read. But the Fulda manuscript is excellent here. Thus the physician and imperial archiater Theodorus Priscianus, Book II, chapter 40: “They must be kept from all fleshy and highly nourishing foods, from pleasant scents or incense, and entirely from the sight or stimulation of beautiful forms, so that they may be given no opportunity either to touch or to see.” Tertullian loved this novel use of passive verbs, as is clear in *To the Nations* I.12: “It is transmigrated into marble, clay, bronze, or whatever material has pleased you, to become a god.” Likewise in this book, chapter IX: “But even now this sacred crime is persisted in secretly.” In the same way authors have also said that persons “were abstained.” Martial, Book IX, Epigram 87:
