## CHAPTER XI

If “God” has become a common designation through the fault of human error, insofar as many gods are said and believed to exist in the world, nevertheless “the blessed God and Father of our Lord [Col. 0497D] Jesus Christ” will be understood as none other than the Creator, who both blessed all things—you have Genesis [Col. 0498A] (Gen. I)—and is blessed by all things—you have Daniel (Dan. III).

Likewise, if “Father” can be said without qualification, it can be applied to no God more fittingly than to the Creator; nevertheless the same one will be “the Father of mercies,” who is called merciful, compassionate, and most abundant in mercy. You have this in Jonah (Jon. III–IV), together with the very example of the mercy that he bestowed upon the Ninevites when they entreated him; he was readily moved by Hezekiah’s tears (IV Kings XX), forgave the blood of Naboth when Ahab, Jezebel’s husband, entreated him (III Kings XXI), and immediately pardoned David when he acknowledged his offense (II Kings XII), evidently preferring the sinner’s repentance to his death, from the disposition of mercy, of course (Hos. VI). If Marcion’s God has produced or proclaimed anything of this kind, I shall acknowledge him as the Father of mercies.

But if Marcion ascribes this title to him from the time when he was revealed, as though he became Father of mercies from the time when he undertook to liberate the human race, we, on the contrary, deny him from the very time at which he is said to have been revealed. He cannot, therefore, ascribe anything to one whom he reveals only at the moment when he ascribes something else [Note: [Col. 0498D] “Something,” Pam.] to him. For if it had first been established that he existed, something could then be ascribed to him. What is ascribed is an accident; but accidents are preceded by the manifestation of the thing itself to which they happen. This is especially so when that which is ascribed already belongs to another: the one who has not previously been revealed will be denied to exist all the more strongly, since the very attribute by which his existence is affirmed belongs to one who has already been revealed.

Thus the New Testament also will belong to none other than the one who promised it beforehand; even if not its letter, at least its spirit—this will constitute its newness. Finally, the same one who had engraved the letter upon stone tablets had also proclaimed concerning the Spirit (Joel III.28): “I shall pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh.” And if “the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life,” both belong to him who said (Deut. He therefore says that we, now with unveiled face—the face of the heart, of course, which is veiled in the Jews—beholding Christ, are transformed into the same image from glory (that glory by which Moses too was transformed by the glory of the Lord) to glory. Thus, by setting forth Moses’ bodily illumination from his encounter with the Lord, and the bodily veil because of the people’s weakness, and by introducing in addition spiritual splendor in Christ, “as from the Lord,” he says, “of spirits” [Note: [Col. 0499D] “From the Lord, the Spirit,” another reading.], he testifies that the whole dispensation of Moses was a figure of Christ, unrecognized among the Jews but recognized among us.

But how can the treasure in our earthen vessels be held not to belong to the same one to whom the vessels themselves belong? The latter, because the very last moment of time is already at hand, and because of the merit of their sufferings under Antichrist, will receive an abridgment of death; but they will attain it after being changed [Note: [Col. 0502D] Lat. reads “of death; changed, they may attain”], being clothed over with what is from heaven rather than stripped of the body.

Thus, if [Col. 0501D] these will put that heavenly garment over the body, the dead too will certainly receive a body over which they themselves may put on incorruption from heaven, since of them also he says: “This corruptible thing must put on incorruption, and this mortal [Col. 0502A] thing immortality.” They put it on when they have received back the body; these others put it on over the body, because they have not lost the body.

And therefore he did not say without purpose, “Not wishing to be stripped of the body, but to be clothed over”—that is, not wishing to experience death, but to be overtaken by life—“so that this mortal thing may be swallowed up by life,” when it is snatched from death through being clothed over with transformation.

Therefore, because [Note: [Col. 0502D] “Therefore, he who,” another reading; “and therefore,” Ciaccon.] he shows this to be better, so that we may not be grieved if perhaps death comes first, he says that we have from God the pledge of the Spirit, as though pledged to the same hope of being clothed over; and that we are absent from the Lord so long as we are in the flesh, and for that reason ought to judge it better to be absent from the body and to be with the Lord, so that we may also welcome death willingly.

And indeed, he says that we must all be manifested before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each may receive what he committed through the body, [Col. 0502B] whether good or evil. If Satan disguises himself as an angel of light, this cannot be directed against the Creator. For the Creator is God, not an angel; Satan would have been said to disguise himself as the God of light, not as an angel, if he did not mean that Satan whom both we and Marcion know to be an angel.

In the matter of paradise his own pen is proof against every difficulty he suffers. Marcion’s god, therefore, also corrects in accordance with the Creator, who opposes the exalted, as one who casts rulers down from their throne. Or is he perhaps the very one who also gave Satan power over Job’s body, so that power might be proved in weakness?

Deny now, heretic, that your God is to be feared, when his Apostle was feared.
