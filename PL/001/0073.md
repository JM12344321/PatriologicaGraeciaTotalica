# The Book on Idolatry

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN ON IDOLATRY (C)

## SUMMARY

[Col. 0661D] After his treatise *On Spectacles*, Septimius composed another, *On Idolatry*, in which he proposes, discusses, and resolves various cases of conscience—if it is permissible to speak thus. For there were indeed some who labored under such great ignorance that they thought no one was polluted by the crime of idolatry except those who either burned incense to idols, offered them sacrifices, or were initiated into their mysteries or profane priesthood (chap. 2). He therefore defines idolatry as follows: “Every attendance and service concerning an idol; and it makes no difference,” he says, “whether a modeler shapes it, a sculptor carves it, or an embroiderer weaves it; nor does its material matter—whether an idol is formed of plaster, colors, stone, bronze, silver, or thread” (chap. 3). He adds that God forbids an idol both to be made and to be worshiped (chap. 4). From this he says that Christians may not [Col. 0663A] manufacture idols or sell them to those who will worship them, even when they have no other means of livelihood (chap. 5). He also forbids the construction of a temple, altar, shrine, or house in honor of idols (chap. 8). He even forbids trade in goods that pertain to idolatry (chap. 11); one must also abstain from feast days, even extraordinary ones (chap. 13); slaves may not serve as ministers of idolatry (chap. 18); nor is military service lawful when one must serve beneath the devil’s standard (chap. 19). He condemns and execrates every oath such as “By Hercules” and “By Fidius,” as well as the curse “May Jupiter be angry with you,” and the response that follows it, “No, with you.” Nevertheless, discrimination is needed in reading through this treatise: for nearly all the propositions Tertullian lays down here as general principles are true, but not everything [Col. 0663B] that he deduces from them is true. LUMP.
