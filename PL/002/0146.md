**SUAVILUDIUS**, one of the Christians who was still delighted by the attractions of spectacles, or by the charm of entertainments. II, 652 A: “I recently heard a new defense offered by a certain lover of entertainments (*suaviludius*).” II, 84 A: “But on this subject too, on account of our lovers of entertainments, we have also provided a satisfactory treatment in Greek.”

**SUB divo**, II, 1033 A: it should be read as a single word, *subdivo*. *Subdivum*, ὑπαίθριον (“in the open air”), clear weather.

**SUBARE**, to lust violently for sexual intercourse. I, 352 A: “Now foully lusting after his sister at the recollection of girlfriends long ago not so beloved”; read *non ita dilectarum* (“not so beloved”). Compare Homer, *Iliad* XIV, 314. I, 510 A: “I know also of the prostitute Phryne lusting after Diogenes, who was reclining above her, with passionate desire.” II, 951 A: [Col. 1371A] “Than one who is lusting.” II, 1028 B: “Who, by lusting, by rushing most disgracefully into pleasures, and by craving sexual intercourse.”

**SUBJACERE**, to be at hand or readily apparent. I, 363 A: “It lies ready to be understood.”

**SUBJICERE**, to circumvent. I, 313 A: “Those who are ignorant are deceived and imposed upon,” that is, they are circumvented on every side by the calumnies brought against them.

**SUBLIMANS**, used intransitively. II, 574 A: *Sublimantia*, ἀνωφερῆ (“upward-tending”), things seeking what is above.

**SUBLIMIS.** II, 1031 B: “The more exalted people,” the Roman people. II, 1044 B: “Or *sublimis*,” that is, with sidelong eyes (*oculis limis*), or eyes looking askance.

**SUBNERO**, one who reproduces Nero in his wicked manner of life, II, 1042 B. He means Domitian. Similarly Cicero, *To Atticus*, book X, letter 1: *Submarium*.

**SUBNIXUS.** I, 1255 A: “We are not speaking merely of men *subnixi* by slavery,” that is, either of those who, oppressed by slavery, exert themselves and take pains to earn their masters’ favor by obedience, or simply of those who are subject to slavery. I, 684 C: “Thus Daniel too, supported by Darius in other respects.”

[Col. 1371B] **SUBORNARI**, to be equipped or instructed. II, 19 A: “Heresies are furnished by philosophy.” I, 433 A: “Trained and equipped for every perversity of judgment.”

**SUBREMANERE.** II, 679 B: “From which he does not remain behind even at the end.”

**SUBSCRIBERE**, to concede. I, 380 A: “The Jews conceded it to Ptolemy.” II, 903 B: “That the Holy Spirit could more readily have conceded something of this kind to males, if he had conceded it to females.” II, 110 A: “He did not yield to their anxiety.” II, 719 B: “What conduct is this: that you will not even concede to him his own good precedents, yet fasten crimes upon him?” II, 983 A: “To grant no pardon for stains of this kind.”

**SUBSIGNARE**, to subscribe to, favor, or support. II, 278 C: “If you wish to attest your obedience to him.” I, 1251 A: “They attest only to him.”

**SUBSIGNATIO.** I, 1229 B: “He would give precedence through the formal attestation of repentance.” The words are drawn from legal usage. Legal writers call property *subsignatae facultates* (“formally subscribed assets”) because it has been bound [Col. 1371C] to the treasury by the signatures of lessees; likewise, estates entered upon the tax roll are formally sealed before the treasury and the censor. In this sense Tertullian calls repentance “formally attested”: by it a person has bound himself before God through a resolution to lead a holier life, with the sacrament of immersion or baptism applied as a seal. Instead of *anteponeret*, Rigault reads *ante componeret*. Through his sacraments God enters into settlement with the most ill-deserving of men and commands that they be numbered among his friends.

**SUBSTANTIA.** II, 1032 B: “From different substances and functions.” There are altogether four aspects which, as Aristotle teaches, natural philosophers must observe in every thing: οὐσία, δύναμις, ἔργα, πάθη—“substance, capacity, operations, and affections.” The first two of these pertain to the substance of a thing; the latter two are comprised under the designation “functions.” II, 187 C: “After Philip and the entire substance of this question.” II, 130 C: “To love from one’s whole substance.” II, 406 A: “Let the substances acknowledge their author.” II, 393 C: “The man will assuredly be dishonored on account of the substance that deserves honor.” II, 251 A: “Which of them excels in the substance and powers of the kingdom.” I, 1314 B: “The principal of so much interest [Col. 1371D],” that is, the original sum or capital; the Greeks call it οὐσία (“property”), κεφάλαιον (“capital”), and ἀρχαῖον (“original capital”). I, 1317 B: “The entire substance of the faith.” II, 951 B: “To rise again into the works of the flesh from the repose of continence is a matter of the substance of one’s loins”; earlier he had said, “To marry again is a matter of strength.” II, 808 B: “So that it is not the substance of the flesh, but its action, that is dishonored.” II, 817 A: “Possessing its own kind of substantial solidity,” that is, the kind belonging to a solid entity, which is opposed to an accident. I, 661 A: “So rich a substance of crimes,” concerning idolatry. II, 77 B: “I shall instead approach the more locally specific substance of the present case,” that is, the case in which an explicit passage of Scripture forbidding this is at issue. II, 82 C: “The substance has been handed down to you by God, its arrangement by the world,” that is, the thing itself—the flowers themselves—comes from God, but their arrangement, the manner in which the flowers are worn, comes from the world.

**SUBSTANTIALITER.** II, 550 B: οὐσιώδως (“substantially”), by reason of a common essence.

**SUBSTANTIVUM**, that which subsists. II, 214 A: “For they also maintain that [Col. 1372A] the very beginning in which God made heaven and earth was something, as it were, substantial and corporeal, which can be interpreted as matter.” II, 703 C: “Which brings about a diversity of substantial condition.”

**SUBSTILLUM.** Festus: the period before rain, when it is already almost damp, and after rain, when it is not yet completely dry—because it is already dripping or has not yet ceased to drip. II, 1033 A: “And any projectiles accompanied by showers; then drizzly weather, and afterward clear weather again.”

**SUBTEMEN.** II, 1030 C: “And indeed, in reputation for attention to the weft.” Varro, *On the Latin Language*, book IV: *subtemen*, because it passes beneath (*subit*) the warp (*stamini*). It is the inner part of the cloth, the covering, ἡ ἐνδὸν ἐπιπολή (“the inner surface”), which lies beneath the warp. Junius, however, together with Rhenanus, prefers *subtegmen*, that is, the inner cloth with which the garment was lined for convenience and ornament and for a display of studied elegance.

**SUBTER.** I, 1274 A: “Beneath which fire awaits her,” that is, in the lower regions and subterranean places.

**SUBVERBUSTI**, slaves burned with spits. II, 1044 A: “Spit-scorched slaves among the liberal arts.”

[Col. 1372B] **SUCCEDERE.** II, 543 A: “Because, when there are many things, it is possible for many to succeed them,” that is, because of their abundance it is easily brought about that some succeed others and that numerous things replace one another in turn. A Hellenism.

**SUCCIDERE**, said of runaway slaves who, when recaptured by their masters, tremblingly throw themselves at their knees. I, 454 A: “And yet, when seized, they are brought under control and fall down before their condition.” II, 557 B: “He fell down in supplication.” II, 1257 B: “And once he succumbed to impatience.” II, 709 A: “To have submitted even to human form.” Irenaeus: “To such an extent that he was even enclosed in a human body.” II, 84 A: “Hence the Apostle says that it unwillingly succumbed to vanity,” that is, yielded and was made subject to it. II, 983 A: “Lest they be compelled to yield to adultery and fornication.”

**SUCULAE** are stars improperly so called, as Cicero testifies in *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, chapter 85; they are otherwise called the Hyades, Ὑάδες, from ὕειν, “to rain.”

**SUDUM**, dry or clear weather. II, 1033 A: “Then drizzly weather, and afterward clear weather again.”

**SUFFECTURA.** II, 280 A: “The soul is, in a certain sense, [Col. 1372C] the support of the spirit,” that is, as though placed beneath the spirit to sustain it. He says *suffectura* just as, with the contrary sense, we say *praefectura* (“superintendence”). Thus he assigns *suffectura* to the soul so that we may understand a *praefectura* to have been given to the spirit. Similarly, in the book *On the Soul*, he said that the mind had been supplied as a platform (*suggestum*) to the soul.

**SUFFERENTIA**, patience. II, 392 A: “By which he might commend his patience above in the blessings”; immediately afterward he interprets the same quality as kindness. I, 1158 A: “By this saying we now admonish our very selves beforehand toward patience.”

**SUFFICERE.** II, 246 B: “Adequately transcribed in copies,” that is, transcribed in sufficient fullness.

**SUFFRAGIUM.** II, 76 B: “The votes follow from this,” that is, the matter is submitted for deliberation and decided by votes; a written record is made in the public proceedings, and the accused is handed over to the prefects to carry out the sentence.

**SUFFUNDERE**, to put to shame, to brand with disgrace. II, 891 B: “It belongs to so holy a man to be suffused,” with shame or modest blushes.

**SUFFUSUS.** II, 716 A: “From these years onward, however, the sex is both more deeply suffused [Col. 1372D] and more fully clothed”—more deeply suffused with modesty and consciousness of modesty.

**SUGGESTUS**, attire or equipment. II, 1045 A: “Priestly attire,” that is, the equipment of a priest. I, 368 A: “Platforms of images” are small shields or medallions hung in a circle among the standards, so that they formed many tiers and alternations of height and depth, such as paneled stages and galleries are seen on towers. Upon them were painted images of emperors who had been consecrated and enrolled among the gods, or who were still reigning, as is evident from Lipsius’s passage concerning the image of Vitellius. I, 379 A: “At the prompting of Demetrius of Phalerum,” that is, through his encouragement and effort. II, 323 B: “With his supporting influence going before, which lends authority”; *suggestus* is what had previously been called patronage. II, 211 C: “Matter plainly associated with it through the equipment of substance,” that is, through an ornament or addition of beauty. II, 225 B: “Heaven and earth assuredly possessing their own proper equipment.” II, 570 A: “And a fruit-bearing array,” that is, his ornament and retinue. II, 414 B: [Col. 1373A] “It was necessary for the new covenant to be sealed in that setting.” II, 810 A: “Until the night too revives with its own equipment”; the equipment of night is darkness, just as that of day is light. II, 871 C: “For it too will receive equipment and adornment.” I, 687 B: “Now, however, the matter of the mere equipment and apparatus of honor must be reconsidered,” that is, the garments.

**SUGGILLARE** is properly said of the face when, after being struck, it is disfigured by livid marks; hence it also means violently to injure and disfigure someone’s reputation. I, 284 A: “To expose the injustice of the public hatred directed against us,” that is, not merely to convict it, but to brand upon it its shamefulness, livid bruise, and weal, so that it may be publicly displayed. Junius prefers *sigillandam*, “to seal.” I, 473 A: “So that you stigmatize the prodigal.” II, 549 B: “If anywhere unworthiness has nevertheless deserved to be stigmatized.”

**SUGGILLATIO**, insult or contumely. I, 376 A: “Your justice is an insult to heaven.” II, 21 B: “The whole discourse of this denunciation.”

**SUI**, one’s relatives, agnates, and clansmen. I, 303 A: “How her relatives killed a matron by starvation because she had unsealed the compartments of the wine cellar.”

[Col. 1373B] **SUMMALE**, opposed to “partial”; that which comprehends the whole. II, 226 A; 964 B.

**SUPERARGUMENTARE.** II, 234 B: “Accordingly, he would have added a further argument.”

**SUPERCILIUM.** The eyebrows are at the bottom of the forehead, above the eyes; the word is then used for gloom and censorious severity, and likewise for haughtiness and pride. II, 221 C: “But you, with the brow of the head,” etc. II, 254 C: “They construct their astonishment with an elevated brow.”

**SUPERFICIES.** I, 364 B: “For that favor, to have consecrated the outward form of a similar beast,” concerning the head of an ass. I, 513 A: “Beneath an imposing appearance of gravity.”

**SUPERFERRE.** I, 1203 A: “Or from that which was being borne above”—the water above which the Spirit of God was borne.

**SUPERGRESSUS**, ὑπερβολή (“excess” or “surpassing measure”). II, 851 B: “Through surpassing measure into surpassing measure,” καθ’ ὑπερβολὴν εἰς ὑπερβολήν, “from excess unto excess.”

**SUPERNITAS.** II, 550 A: “The heights above heights, and elevations above elevations, however great.” Irenaeus: “In invisible [Col. 1373C] and ineffable heights.”

**SUPERSAPERE.** II, 677 B: “He therefore says that the person has surpassing wisdom who possesses the highest wisdom in his mind.”

**SUPERVECTARE.** I, 1263 A: “Who from the beginning was being borne above the waters.”

**SUPPARARE.** II, 564 A: “That it might be matched through desire for superior things.” Irenaeus: ὀρεχθῇ τῶν διαφερόντων, “that it might desire the things that were better.” II, 443 C: “So that seed might be provided for him from his own brother and from his own rib.” II, 938 A: “And it was being provided as though it were posthumous offspring.” II, 700 C: “And if not immediately, let it be provided.”

**SUPPARATURA**, ἀντικατάστασις (“replacement”), substitution. II, 884 A: “Nor will the substitution of sex be burdensome to the limbs.”

**SUPPARUM**, a linen covering for the lower body hanging down to the ankles. II, 1044 A.

**SUPPLAUDERE.** II, 1017 B: “John seems somehow to applaud the opposing side.”

**SUPPLICAMENTUM**, κόλασις (“punishment”), penalty, the punishment of sin. II, 112 A: “Because fear has punishment,” assuredly [Col. 1373D] the fire of the lake.

**SUPPLICIUM**, a criminal. I, 312 A: “And the divinities will have to be called criminals,” that is, those criminals who suffer punishment are to be called gods and are consecrated as gods precisely when they are punished.

**SUPPURARE.** II, 121 A: “The earth suppurates forth a great evil, the scorpion, from something slight,” that is, from a small and slender body. These words appear not to refer to scorpions drawing nourishment from the earth, but should be explained by the fact that scorpions are generated not only from one another but also arise from decay, from rotting wood, and even from earth. Hence the scholiast on Nicander’s *Theriaca*: γίνονται δὲ οἱ σκόρπιοι οὐ μόνον ἐξ ἀλλήλων ἀλλὰ καὶ ἀπὸ σεσημμένων ξύλων καὶ παγούρων, “Scorpions arise not only from one another, but also from decayed wood and crabs.”

**SUSPECTUS**, suspicious or suspecting, with active meaning. I, 401 A: “Lest they deceive those who were suspicious.” II, 576 A: “At least suspecting some maker,” that is, entertaining or harboring a suspicion. I, 1320 C: “Either one who is suspicious or one who does not desire to please.” I, 1270 A: “Suspecting pain arising from an insult.”

[Col. 1374A] **SUSPENDERE**, to restrain, check, or arrest. I, 487 A: “When they withhold both summer and winter seasons from rains.” Zephyrus appears to have explained it correctly: clear weather withholds rain for so long that summer conditions continue into winter. La Cerda explains it: “when it rains neither in summer nor in winter,” in which case a comma must be inserted.

**SUSPENSIO.** I, 1158 A; 1159 A: “And the manifestation of the Lord’s kingdom concerns our suspense,” that is, the delay affecting our hope or expectation and the Lord’s kingdom: we anxiously await what we know to be held in suspense.

**SUSPIRIUM.** II, 541 A, note (2); 542 A: “So many longings of the gates,” that is, the whole of what they had desired for so long.

**SUSTINERE.** II, 223 B: “Until then he kept waiting to be seen,” that is, he waited. II, 609 B: “If he has not yet come, he must be awaited.” II, 125 B: “Let the Gospels wait a little while, until I disclose their root, the Law.” II, 139 B: “For to endure to the end is nothing other than to undergo the end.” I, 639 C: “They maintain that the Tutelinae derive from the guardianship of crops,” that is, they assert and defend the claim that those [Col. 1374B] names were given to them from that origin. I, 645 B: “Why, then, should I conclude my speech about that dreadful place, which even perjuries cannot endure?” *Circum* is implied, as though perjured persons could not endure the dreadfulness of that place; that is, they are frightened by the horror of the place and therefore go to the Capitol to perjure themselves. For the solemn rites of oath-taking were performed on the Capitol, not in the Circus.

**SUTRINA**, a shoemaker’s workshop. II, 1046 B: “The Venetian shoemakers’ workshops,” in which Venetian hides were prepared for making shoes. For in the Venetian region there was a vast abundance of livestock, from whose hides shoes were made.

**SUUS.** II, 138 B: “Lest antiquity perhaps have had its own sacrament,” that is, one proper and peculiar to antiquity—namely, to the Jews—and not also to be shared with Christian newness.

**SYMBOLA**, a common contribution, namely whenever each person contributes something to a common fund. II, 562 A: “As sailors always rejoice at the common contribution.”

**SYMBOLUM.** I, 1238 B; 1239 A: “But if necessity [Col. 1374C] grants us the symbol of death.” Rigault interprets this as referring to sin; Latinius more correctly explains it as referring to baptism, through which we συναποθνήσκομεν καὶ συνθαπτόμεθα, “die and are buried together with Christ,” Romans VI.

**SYMPHON.** II, 588 A: “In the *symphon* of the Pleroma, by the angel”; others read, more correctly, “in the *nymphon*,” in this sense: let men undergo in the *nymphon*, or bridal chamber, what belongs to brides.

**SYMPLEGADES** are two crags or rocks in the sea near the Thracian Bosporus. They are called the Cyanean or Wandering Rocks and are dangerous to sailors. Strabo, book VII; Pomponius Mela, book II, chapter 7; Pliny, *Natural History*, book IV, chapter 12.

**SYNAGOGA.** II, 370 A: “Straight from heaven to the synagogue, as the saying goes,” etc. There are two interpretations of this passage. The first takes the proverb as referring to Christ, who came straight from heaven to the synagogue and considered nothing more urgent than doing the things to which he had been called. John IV. As the saying goes: “Attend to what we came for [Col. 1374D].” The second understands it as referring to Marcion; Latinius adopted this interpretation.

**Συζυγία**, “conjunction” or “pairing.” II, 68 A.

**Συμμισούμενον**, that is, “a fellow object of hatred.” II, 374 C.

**Συνταλαίπωρον**, that is, “a companion in misery.”

**SYNTHESIS**, a more elegant and sumptuous garment. II, 1042 B: “A many-threaded *synthesis*,” that is, a soft, delicate, and translucent garment of the kind women customarily wear. See Turnèbe, *Adversaria*, book XXIII, chapter 19.

**SYNTROPHUS**, σύντροφος, one brought up together with another. II, 554 B.

**SYRMA**, the loose and flowing garment of tragic actors. II, 1036 B: “Looser than every tragic robe.”

## T

**TABELLA.** II, 906 A: “These are the first tablets of natural betrothal and marriage, by which nature binds the girl to betrothal and marriage.” [Col. 1375A] Agreements of betrothal were generally entered upon tablets. These tablets were sealed with the signet rings of those who had been present.

**TABERNACULA**, shops. II, 976 C: “The baths and shops are kept closed until the ninth hour.”

**TABULAE** was the name given to folds of looser cloth because they were laid one over another like stories of a building. Ferrarius, *On Clothing*. Hence *tabulatus*; see that word. II, 1046 A: “Now that the folds are giving out.” II, 83 A: “You have this common law in the public realm of the world, on the tablets of nature.”

**TABULATUS.** II, 1031 B: “With a paneled assemblage,” that is, with the boss formed from several panels, or constructed by multiple layers of paneling.

**TALIA**, wrinkles and folds, as though they were incisions. II, 1046 A: “And from there draw it down into folds.” Others read *tabulas*; others, *tilias*.

**TAM.** II, 187 B: “But the doctrine of Hermogenes is equally recent,” namely, as recent as those other heresies against which we customarily plead their later date. Junius prefers *tantum*, “only.”

[Col. 1375B] **TANGERE.** I, 488 A: “We touch the god.” Gods were said to be “touched” when they were affected by vows and took petitions to heart.

**TANTUM quod** is the Greek πλὴν ὅσον or πλὴν ὅτι, “except that.” I, 281 A: “Gaius Seius is a good man, except that he is a Christian.” II, 426 B: “But no one would have knocked so late at the door of a new god, except one only just coming to light.”

**TANTUS**, with *numerus* (“number”) understood. I, 262 A: “And they are as many as we are marked down as being,” that is, their multitude is as great as you are well aware. I, 281 A: “The good is not valued as highly as the hatred of Christians,” that is, people do not rejoice as greatly that a formerly adulterous wife has become chaste, a formerly disobedient son submissive, and a formerly dishonest slave faithful, as they grieve, as though suffering a loss, that they have crossed over to this name; they would be unwilling to purchase such an achievement even at the price of their own loss. I, 480 A: “So many against one,” that is, so many Christians, etc. II, 255 A: “Whom I might name in so many bygone ages.”

**TARDABILIS.** II, 722 A: “With retarding stiffness.”

**TAUREA**, a whip or very stout thong made from [Col. 1375C] oxhide. I, 626 B: “Others have walked among the hunters’ oxhide lashes with shoulders most capable of endurance.” Juvenal, *Satires* VI: “The oxhide lash immediately punishes the crime and offense of curled hair.”

**TELINUM**, an ointment named after Telos, one of the Sporades islands, which Callimachus called Agathussa. Pliny, *Natural History*, book XIII, chapter 1. II, 1042 A: “Which, in Omphale’s case, I expect was done with balsam or Telian ointment.”

**TEMENUS.** II, 1035 B: One of the descendants of Hercules, who ruled the Messenian peoples of the Peloponnese. Others read “those descended from Temenus”; others, “those who were with Timenus.”

**TEMERARIUM**, made at random and without art, or from whatever material happened to be at hand. I, 430 A: “Makeshift altars of turf.”

**TEMPERARE.** I, 276 A, C: “Among you, torture is reserved for the interrogation alone,” that is, tortures are employed only for the purpose of interrogation, and cease immediately once a confession has been extracted.

**TEMPESTIVITAS**, from the Greek ὡραῖος τῷ κάλλει, “beautiful in the bloom of maturity.” II, 330 B; 621 A.

**TEMPORALITAS**, the season of the year. II, 1031 B: “Whatever further adornment condition, rank, or season provides.”

[Col. 1375D] **TENEBRAE.** “Let canine darkness fall,” that is, let the light be extinguished by a dog’s movement when the lamp falls.

**TENEBRICUS**, black or dark. II, 1045 A: “Because of the different character of his dark garment.”

**TENEBROSUS**, σκοτεινός, “obscure.” II, 318 A; 651 A. Heraclitus is so called because of the excessive obscurity he cultivated: “that obscure Heraclitus.”

**TENERE.** I, 648 B: “I hold evidence of blindness,” that is, I accuse them of blindness. II, 158 A: “We maintain the monarchy, they say,” that is, we affirm one God.

**TENUS**, a bond or noose. Τενίαι, δεσμοί, διαδήματα, “bands, bonds, diadems,” Hesychius. II, 1038 B: “In the manner of the former cord, which a band of linden-bark strips had joined.”

**TENUS.** I, 483 A: “As far as the eyes.” The sense is: if any trees perhaps remain there, they do indeed promise fruit to the eyes of those looking, but when that fruit is touched by the fingers of those gathering it, it dissolves into ashes.

[Col. 1376A] **TERMINUS.** II, 1005 A: “Against the Lord’s determination,” that is, against the Lord’s judgment and decision. For in Tertullian, *determinare* means to judge, pronounce, or define by judicial decision.

**TERRIGENA.** II, 299 A: “Among water-born and earth-born animals,” ὑδρογενῆ καὶ γηγενῆ τῶν ζώων.

**TESTITRAHUS**, one who seems to drag his testicles because of their size. Vossius, *Rhetoric*, book IV. Others read *textitrahum*, ἑλκεσίπεπλον, “trailing one’s robe,” II, 1031 B. Others read *vestitrahum* or *textitrahum*.

**TESTUDO.** I, 624 A: “By packing the tortoise formation closely.” This reading is undoubtedly to be preferred to all the others. For the words must be understood of the tortoise formation of shields, which was made by packing shields closely together. Livy, book XLIV: “Having formed a square column and packed their shields closely over their heads, with the first men standing, the second stooping lower, the third and fourth lower still, and the last even resting on their knees, they formed a sloping tortoise formation like the roofs of buildings.”

**TETRAO**, a kind of bird mentioned by Pliny, *Natural History*, book X, chapter 22; Athenaeus: τὰ τε τῶν φασιανῶν, οὓς τετράωνας, “and those of the Phasian birds which they call *tetraones*.” [Col. 1376B] II, 261 A: “A single little feather of the *tetrao*.”

**TETRICUS.** I, 1329 B: “For you, however, there is no reason for going forth that is not solemn,” that is, not weighty, serious, and sacred.

**THAMNUS**, θάμνος, any plant that grows like a shrub and produces abundant shoots. II, 702 B: “I have been a shrub and a fish.” Verses of Empedocles himself on this subject survive in Diogenes Laertius, but in a much better text in Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* VI:

> Ἤδη γάρ ποτ’ ἐγὼ γενόμην κοῦρός τε, κόρη τε,  
> θάμνος τ’ οἰωνός τε, καὶ εἰν ἁλὶ ἔλλοπος ἰχθύς.

“For once already I became a boy and a girl, a shrub and a bird, and in the sea a mute fish.”

**THENSA**, II, 95 B, or rather *Tensa*, from *tendere*, “to stretch,” as Asconius observes. According to Festus, it was a silver vehicle in which the attributes of the gods were conveyed during the Circus games into the Circus and to the cushioned banquet. I, 638 B.

**THISTRI.** I, 703 A: “As Cincius Severus, who was at Thistri.” Junius reads *Thustri* or *Thystri*, a free town in inland Africa: θυσδρός in Ptolemy, and perhaps θυσδρίς; hence *Thusdritanus* in Pliny, *Natural History*, book V, chapter 4, and book VII, chapter 4. Latinius restores *Thysdri* from Sallust [Col. 1376C], Oppius, and others. But in Pliny it is read without the aspirate. Cincius Severus is undoubtedly the same person whom Lampridius calls Cingius Severus at the end of his *Commodus*. And perhaps this was also the reason why Severus delivered him to death upon the accusation of his enemies, as Spartianus mentions in his *Severus*.

**THORI.** I, 452 B: “To bring couches out into public”; these are the ceremonial couches and cushions used at banquets for the gods.

**THURARIUS**, one who sells incense. I, 677 A: “Unless someone will also turn a blind eye to the incense-seller.”

**TIBERIS.** I, 455 A: “The Tiber too is a witness.” Godefroy refers this to the house of Tiberius, or the palace of the Caesars. But in ancient times the common people inhabited the riverbank, while the more powerful occupied the hills. Since he is speaking of the common people, he could not have indicated their place of residence more suitably.

**TIBICINARE.** II, 717 A: “Nor as though he himself had to be propped,” that is, supported by a buttress or prop; the metaphor comes from building construction.

**TITULARE.** II, 620 B: “He also designated the magicians by the appellation [Col. 1376D] ‘Samaritans,’” that is, marked and named them with that title. II, 936 D: “Even if you claim bigamy as your title.” II, 667 B: “Who will not ascribe the whole sum to the soul, when mention of the whole human being has been designated under its name?”

**TITULUS.** II, 1045 A: “Above all points and titles”; better, *tutulos*. *Tutuli* were priestly caps, whence priests are called *tutulati* in Varro. Turnèbe, *Adversaria* XXVI, 10: “The *tutulus* was a covering with which priests, when about to approach the sacred rites, covered and protected their heads.” I, 525 A: “Or learning from the inscription of the Pythia”; he refers to that famous inscription at Delphi above the doors of Apollo: γνῶθι σέαυτον, “Know yourself.” II, 965 B: “For abstinence from wine also has its own claims to distinction.” II, 310 C: “Will the title of duty give pleasure?” I, 1219 A: “As though adding something of his own to Paul’s title,” that is, as though he were adding something of his own to Paul’s titles and glory, something resembling a crowning addition. II, 987 B: “Nothing else for confirming such statutory headings.” He calls the provisions of the [Col. 1377A] divine law “titles,” and in the same sense above spoke of “titles of condemnation.” II, 939 B: “So that both titles of holiness might be entered in Christ’s register.” He uses a metaphor drawn from accountants and censors, who enter in their records the names and titles of those who make a declaration. See **DISPUNGERE**. II, 255 C: “Which title, out of the two, shall we inscribe for Marcion’s god?” *Incidere* means to record something upon monuments of deeds. Cicero: “To engrave an encomium upon bronze, marble,” etc. Pliny, speaking of Pompey the Great in book VII, chapter 26, mentions records of victories and triumphs. Tertullian similarly alludes to publicly honoring or disgracing someone by a surname and distinguishing him by a commemorative inscription.

**TONSURA.** II, 700 B: “Indeed, pestilences, famines, wars, and the engulfing of cities should be regarded as a remedy, like the pruning of an overgrown human race.” There is a similar passage in *On the Pallium*, chapter 2.

**TORMENTUM.** II, 1046 B: “We say nothing of the shoes, the toga’s peculiar torment.” Salmasius reads *termentum togae*, that is, “that at which the toga terminates”; the shoe [Col. 1377B] is the termination of the toga. He calls it peculiar to the toga because among the Romans neither the toga without shoes nor shoes without the toga were worn. II, 122 A: “Drawing back the barbed point at the extremity according to the design of the engine,” that is, as it appeared at that time in the scorpion, the military engine. I, 1303 A: “Alms without torture.” When the pagan husband of a Christian woman noticed something missing from his property—perhaps given by his wife to poor Christians—he would frequently rage to such an extent that he ordered his wife’s slaves to be tortured. But when both spouses were Christians, then there were alms without torture.

**TRADUCERE**, to expose to the light, reveal, or uncover. II, 45 C: “Thus they will more easily be exposed.” II, 156 A: “Then exposed,” that is, disclosed and refuted. II, 105 A: “Or let him show that they were his people,” that is, let him show and demonstrate that those who abandoned God and defected to the devil were the devil’s people. II, 115 B: “While I refuse to be exposed for what I am, I have confessed that I am what I refuse to be exposed as,” namely, a Christian. II, 671 A: “If even his prophet is proved by exposing the secrets of the heart.” II, 1028 A: [Col. 1377C] “So that he might expose their thoughts.” II, 647 C: “Which alone drives out that most destructive force,” that is, the Christian power which alone drives the demons away, compels them to depart, and expels them from human beings—by adjuration, of course. I, 446 A: “By reproof you shall reprove your neighbor.” The Greek of Leviticus XIX reads: Ἐλεγμῷ ἐλέγξεις τὸν πλησίον σου, “You shall surely reprove your neighbor.”

**TRADUCTIO**, illumination, demonstration, or exposure. II, 35 A: “Lest their exposures also be admitted,” that is, the certain proofs by which their lies are exposed and offered up to ridicule. II, 621 B: “Threatening the exposure of each person’s heart.” Rigault explains this as referring to the power of spiritual grace, which pierces each person’s conscience with the arrows of the divine commandments. II, 308 B: “When they perceive their own exposure,” that is, when they are accused and convicted of error.

**TRADUX.** II, 32 B: “From whom comes the transmission of the faith,” that is, the transfer of the faith from one church to another. The churches, as it were, handed apostolic doctrine from hand to hand among themselves. I, 1257 B: “She makes him [Col. 1377D] into the transmitter of that which she had drawn from Evil.” The sense is: Satan had approached Eve alone and had breathed upon her alone a spirit infected with impatience. But she, too impatient even to remain silent, poured Satan’s depraved suggestion into her Adam. Thus she herself was the transmitter of sin to Adam, and thereafter made him a fellow transmitter of that sin—that is, an agent and partner in transmitting sin to their common posterity. II, 661 A: “But since everything fine and translucent resembles air, this will be the soul, insofar as it is breath and an offshoot of spirit.”

**TRANSFIGURATIO.** II, 191 A: “Transfiguration is the destruction of the former state.”

**TRANSFRETARE.** I, 1227 A: “And so they sail across the whole course of life without the rudder of reason.”

**TRANSFUNCTORIUM**, also *perfunctorium*: something done with slight care and while attending to something else—when, namely, we take no care that something be done well, but only that it be done. II, 278 B: “And those very perfunctory commandments.”

[Col. 1378A] **TRANSITUS.** II, 888 B: “From the time when they have made the transition from their age,” that is, when they have passed beyond childhood.

**TRANSCRIBERE**, to transfer to another. II, 1036 A: “How many census registrations were transferred?” II, 619 B: “Before she had been transferred into a Syrophoenician woman.”

**TRIBUTUM.** II, 336 A: “Or by the tribute of blood,” concerning menstruation. I, 1252 A: “The tributes of the entire species,” that is, the conception and nourishment of the fetus in the womb, and the actual birth of all animals.

**TRICHEA.** II, 565 A: “Rough.” Junius reads *trechea* or *trachea*; Greek τραχεῖα or τρηχεῖα, “rough.”

**TRINITAS.** II, 19 A: “The trinity of man,” namely the hylic or choic human being, that is, the material and earthly person; the psychic human being, that is, the animate or natural person; and the spiritual human being. Such was Valentinus’s opinion. II, 684 A: “So that the Valentinian trinity may still be cut down.” II, 673 B: “For behold this entire trinity,” namely the parts or affections of the soul: the reasoning, irascible, and concupiscent. The soul’s threefold function is indicated in the Gospel.

**TRUCIDARE.** II, 986 C: “Whether chastity is slaughtered in bedrooms or in towers [Col. 1378B].”

**TRULLA**, a hollow vessel in which the burden of the belly is deposited beneath a commode-chair. I, 344 B: “Sometimes into a chamber pot concerning Minerva.”

**TUNICA.** II, 1030 C. The Carthaginian tunic differed from the Roman in that the Roman was sleeveless and shorter, while the Carthaginian had sleeves and was longer. Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book VII, chapter 12.

**TURBA.** II, 545 B: “From their crowd,” that is, from the crowd of pagan gods, and by its argument to persuade human beings of another multitude of aeons or ages, as the Valentinians do.

**TURPE.** I, 517 A: “Or is this more disgraceful?” A wicked person is a disgraceful sacrifice, because nothing ought to be sacrificed to God unless it is perfect in its kind. Below: “When you slaughter every worn-out, diseased, and scabby creature.”

**TURPILOQUIUM**, αἰσχρολογία, “obscene speech.” II, 1014 C.

## U

[Col. 1378C] **UBIQUE.** II, 1035 B: “Throughout the everywhere of the world,” εἰς τὰ πανταχοῦ τῆς γῆς, “into every part of the earth.” Salmasius denies that this is an Atticism.

**ULTIMARE**, to be in the final stage, to hasten toward the end. II, 1032 A: “Although the times of the fatherland were approaching their end.”

**UMBO** was the center of the toga and the artfully contrived knot of its converging folds; hence, the “navel” of the toga. II, 1046 A: “And entrust the entire construction, by the gathering of the boss, to the restraining clasps.”

**UMBRA.** II, 198 B: “The worst of painters colored this first shadow, plainly without light, with those arguments.” The expression is drawn from the art of painting, which Hermogenes had practiced. The beginnings of painting, Pliny says, consisted in tracing lines around a human shadow; this was called linear painting. The second stage used individual colors and was called monochrome. Thus the first stage had no color at all, so much so that it became customary to write beside the figures the names of those being depicted. Later they began to color the outlines with crushed pottery. The equivalent of this kind of painting in architecture is ἰχνογραφία (“ground-plan drawing”), which is a flat representation of the site and an outline description displaying [Col. 1378D] a model of the future work. The passage is explained as referring to the obscurity of the discourse, argument, or demonstrated subject.

**UNARE.** II, 190 B: “To divide them rather than unite them.” He uses the simple verb, from which we derive *adunare* and *coadunare*, “to unite.” Three Vatican manuscripts read *unire*, but that has a stronger flavor of African usage.

**UNDE**, from wherever. II, 217 B: “Although we might consider from wherever they were brought forth.” II, 221 C: “From wherever he has it.” II, 333 C: “From wherever he might produce the reality of the flesh.”

**UNGERE.** II, 122 A: “And to anoint the beast’s heel.” Rigault’s reading, *urgendi* (“to press”), is preferable. “To press the beast’s heel” is the same as “to press the beast with the heel.” Shortly afterward he himself says: “There too let the scorpion, in place of a sandal, strike it as an accursed thing.” II, 338 A: “First anointed, I suppose, for the sun.” He alludes to ἡλίωσις, or sunning, by which persons were anointed and warmed in order to dry and strengthen their limbs. Persius, *Satires* IV:

> “Or if, anointed, you linger and fasten the sun upon your skin.”

Junius prefers “for the bath-chair”; see that word.

[Col. 1379A] **UNGULA.** II, 970 B: “And against the horny claws.” Claws were among the instruments of torture. At first, as may be inferred from the word, they were actual claws cut from animals; later, iron claws were made in their form. With these they scraped—or, as some put it, “wrote upon” or “engraved”—the sides of the wretched. Septimius therefore says that skin dried and hardened by fasting would be more resistant to tortures of this kind.

**UNIFORMIS.** II, 661 B: “Uniform only in respect of what is called substance,” that is, endowed with one essential form and not several, as Plato teaches in the *Timaeus*. He afterward calls this substance “natural.”

**UNITAS.** II, 57 C; 58 A: “Schism is unity itself,” that is, they display the appearance of unity with the utmost care; or: there are indeed schisms among the heretics, but we notice them less because of their common and perfectly harmonious disagreement with us.

**UNIVERSITAS**, πλήρωμα, “fullness” or “totality.” II, 623 C: “For the totality of spiritual teachings does not belong to any human being.” “The world”: II, 258 B: “Finally, if the universe belongs to the Creator [Col. 1379B].”

**URBANITAS.** I, 404 A: He calls those “deceived by urban sophistication” whom, in chapter 17, he calls deceived by corrupt instruction: a belching soul whose wisdom has been formed in schools, trained in libraries, and acquired in academies and porticoes. *On the Testimony of the Soul*, chapter 1.

**USURPARE.** I, 1257 B: “It readily began to be appropriated by impatience for everything that had offended God.” Properly speaking, those to whom a right of possession belongs “appropriate” something. Thus impatience, now as though it possessed the human being, easily drew him into anything that would offend God.

**USURPATIO.** II, 750 B: “In a certain anticipation,” that is, in the advance infliction and anticipation of judicial punishments.

**USUS.** II, 564 B: “There is use of things,” in place of “it is customary” or “it happens in practice.” A Hellenism: χρεία ἐστί, “there is need” or “it is customary.”

**UT.** II, 185 A: “Even if we were thus to call them two divided beings,” that is, even if we were to say that there are two. II, 195 A: “For what purpose have you forsaken me?” The Greek ἵνα τί, “why?”—a formula very frequent in Optatus of Milevis.
