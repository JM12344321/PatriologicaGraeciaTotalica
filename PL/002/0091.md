You will recognize the Apostle as such everywhere: condemning the works of the flesh in such a way that he seems to condemn the flesh, yet taking care, by the indication supplied by other or connected passages, that no one should think so. For when he also says (Rom. VIII) that those who are in the flesh [Note: [Col. 0859D] “Are” (sint), Rhen. Seml.] cannot please God, he immediately recalls the statement from a perverse interpretation to its full meaning by adding: “But you are not in the flesh, but in the spirit.” For by denying that those who were manifestly in the flesh were in the flesh, he showed that they were not in the works of the flesh; and thus [Note: [Col. 0859D] “Thus” omitted by Rig. Ven.] that those, finally, who could not please God [Col. 0859D] were not those who were in the flesh, but those who lived carnally; [Col. 0860A] whereas those pleased God who, though situated in the flesh, walked according to the spirit.

And again he says: “The body indeed is dead, but [Note: [Col. 0859D] “Because of transgression.” This word is very often used by this author for sin; for where the Apostle says “sin,” he calls it “transgression.” LE PR.] because of transgression,” just as the spirit is life because of righteousness. But by opposing life to the death established in the flesh, he undoubtedly also promised life there by reason of righteousness, where he had determined death by reason of transgression. Otherwise he opposed life to death in vain, if life is not to be present where death itself is—the death to which he opposed it—so that death might, of course, be expelled from the body. Moreover, if life expels death from the body, it cannot accomplish this unless it penetrates there [Note: [Col. 0859D] “That” (illud), Pam. Rhen. Seml.; “those” (illae), cod. Wouw.] where that which it expels is present.

And why do I argue more intricately, when the Apostle speaks more plainly? “For if,” he says, “the Spirit of him who raised Jesus dwells in you, he who raised Jesus from the dead will also raise” [Note: [Col. 0860D] “Will give life to,” Pam. Fran.] “your mortal bodies, because of his Spirit dwelling in you.” [Col. 0860B] Thus, even if someone presumes the soul to be the mortal body, since he cannot deny the flesh as well, he is compelled to acknowledge the resurrection of the flesh also, in accordance with their participation in the same condition.

Learn still further from what follows that the works of the flesh are condemned, not the flesh itself: “Therefore, brethren,” he says, “we are debtors, not to the flesh, so that [Note: [Col. 0860D] “For living, if,” Rhen.; “for living according to the flesh. If,” Jun.] we should live according to the flesh. For if you live according to the flesh, you are going to die; but if by the spirit you put to death the actions of the flesh, you will live.”

Moreover, to answer each point individually: if salvation is promised to those who are situated in the flesh but nevertheless conduct themselves according to the spirit, then it is no longer the flesh that opposes salvation, but the operation of the flesh. Once the operation of the flesh—which is the cause of death—has been excluded, the flesh is thereby shown to be safe, because it lacks the cause of death. “For the law,” [Col. 0860C] he says, “of the Spirit of life [Note: [Col. 0859D] ‘In Christ Jesus has manumitted me.’ We were all slaves and delivered into Satan’s ownership, but the law of Christ shook the yoke [Col. 0860D] of servitude from our necks. Here he uses the term *manumission*, customary among jurists, who recognize three kinds of manumission: by census, by the vindicta, and by testament. By those three methods Christ manumitted us. LE PR.] in Christ Jesus has manumitted me from the law of transgression and death”—certainly the law which he had said before dwelt in our members [Note: [Col. 0860D] “Your,” Rhen. Seml.]. Therefore, our members will no longer be held by the law of death, because neither will they be held by the law of transgression, from which they have been manumitted.

“For what was powerless in the law, in that it was weakened through the flesh, God, by sending his Son in the likeness of the flesh of transgression, and through transgression” [Note: [Col. 0860D] “The flesh,” Rhen. Seml.] “condemned transgression in the flesh”—not the flesh in transgression; for a house will not be condemned together with its inhabitant. Indeed, he said that sin dwells in our body. Once transgression is condemned, however, the flesh is acquitted, just as, while transgression remains uncondemned, the flesh is bound by the law of death and transgression.

Thus too he called [Note: [Col. 0860D] “Even if,” Rhen. Seml.] the mind of the flesh death, and thereafter also hostility toward God, but not the flesh itself. “To what, then,” you will say, “is the mind of the flesh to be attributed, if not to the substance itself?” Certainly—if you prove [Col. 0860D] that the flesh understands anything of itself. But if there is no understanding without the soul, understand that the mind of the flesh must be referred [Col. 0861A] to the soul, although it is sometimes attributed [Note: [Col. 0861C] “Must be attributed,” Rhen. Jun.] to the flesh because [Note: [Col. 0861C] Rhen. Seml. add “on account of the flesh to it.”] it is exercised through the flesh. And for that reason he says that transgression dwells in the flesh, because the soul, by which [Note: [Col. 0861C] “Of the soul,” Rhen. Seml.; *abest a qua* (“by which” absent).] transgression is introduced, is the flesh’s tenant. The flesh is indeed mortified, but not in its own name; rather, in the name of transgression.

For elsewhere too he says: “Why, even now, as though living in the world, do you submit to decrees?” He was not writing to the dead, but to those who ought to cease living in a worldly manner.

## CHAPTER XLVIII

“But flesh and blood,” you say, “cannot possess the kingdom of God by inheritance.” We know that this too is written; but we have deliberately postponed it until now, so that what our adversaries [Col. 0863C] set up against us in the very first line of battle we might overthrow in the final engagement, after all the questions that serve, as it were, as its auxiliaries had first been cast down.

But even now let them expect [Note: [Col. 0863D] “Seek,” Rhen. Seml.] the preceding passages to be reviewed, so that this statement’s own origin may determine its meaning. [Note: [Col. 0863D] Rhen. Seml. insert “that.”] I take it that the Apostle, [Note: [Col. 0864D] ‘Having arranged for the Corinthians every distinction of ecclesiastical discipline.’ He did this in the preceding chapters of chapter XV, upon which Tertullian is now dwelling; for Paul had given many instructions concerning ecclesiastical discipline. LAC.] after arranging for the Corinthians every distinction of ecclesiastical discipline, had brought the sum both of his Gospel and of their faith to its conclusion in the committal of the Lord’s death and resurrection, [Note: [Col. 0864D] ‘So that he might derive the rule of our hope from that on which it was established.’ He derived it from Christ’s resurrection, since it was established from that. LAC.] so that he might derive the rule of our hope from that on which it was established.

Through these words, what matter does he appear to be preparing us to believe? “The resurrection,” you say, “of the dead, which was being denied.” Surely he wishes it to be believed on the basis of the Lord’s resurrection as an example? “Certainly,” you say. Now, is an example formed from dissimilarity or from likeness? [Col. 0864B] “From likeness, of course,” you say.

But how did Christ rise? In the flesh, did he not? Without doubt: if you hear that he died and was buried according to the Scriptures in no other respect than in the flesh, you likewise concede that he was raised in the flesh. For that very thing which fell in death [Note: [Col. 0864C] “Into death,” Pam. Fran.], which lay in burial, also rose—not so much Christ in the flesh as the flesh in Christ.

Therefore, if we shall rise according to Christ’s example, who rose in the flesh, we shall no longer rise according to Christ’s example if we ourselves do not also rise in the flesh. “For through a man,” he says, “came death, and through a man resurrection”: thus indeed distinguishing the authors—Adam as author of death, Christ of resurrection—while establishing the resurrection as belonging to the same substance as that to which death belonged, by comparing the respective authors under the name “man.” For “just as in Adam [Col. 0864C] all die, so also in Christ all will be given life”: they will be given life in the flesh in Christ, just as in Adam they die in the flesh [Note: [Col. 0864D] “All,” Fran.].

“But each in his own order”—clearly because each is also in his own body. For order is nothing other than the disposer of merits [Note: [Col. 0864D] The Pithoean codex reads: “For order will be arranged under the name of merits arranged”; Rig. thinks *dispositor horum* (“the arranger of these”) should be read for *dispositorum*.]. And since merits are ascribed to the body also, the order of bodies must likewise be arranged, so that there may be an order of merits.

But if some are also baptized [Note: [Col. 0864D] “Indeed,” Rhen.] for the dead, we shall see whether this is reasonable. Fran.] [Note: [Col. 0865D] ‘Vicarious baptism.’ Fabius likewise used the expression ‘vicarious death,’ *Declamation* 19. RIG.] would benefit even the flesh toward the hope of resurrection. Unless that resurrection were bodily, it would not otherwise [Note: [Col. 0865D] “Not unless otherwise,” Rhen. Seml.; “not otherwise,” thus Pam. Paris. Fran.; “not otherwise” in cod. Wouw.] be bound here by bodily baptism.

“Why are they themselves also baptized,” he says, “if the bodies that are baptized do not rise?” [Note: [Col. 0865D] “Who,” Rhen. Seml.] For the soul is sanctified not by washing, but by its response. “Why do we too,” he says, “stand in danger every hour?” Through the flesh, of course. “I die daily”—through the dangers of the flesh, of course, through which he also fought with beasts at Ephesus: namely, [Note: [Col. 0865D] ‘The beasts of the Asian affliction.’ Whether by these beasts he understands the uprising at Ephesus stirred up by Demetrius the silversmith, I could not readily say; yet, with the leave of more learned men, I would say that he certainly fought with beasts, but that battle occurred at Ephesus after this epistle, which he wrote at Ephesus, had been written. ## CHAPTER XLIX

We have now come to the flesh and blood that truly constitute the whole question: under what condition the Apostle disinherited those [Note: [Col. 0865D] “As though,” Pam. Fran.] substances from the kingdom of God. This too must be learned from the preceding statements.

“The first man is from the earth, earthy”—that is, made of mud, that is, Adam; “the second man is from heaven”—that is, the Word of God, that is, Christ. [Col. 0865C] Yet he is not otherwise a man, although from heaven, except because he too is flesh and soul, which is what man is, what Adam is. For above also he was called [Note: [Col. 0865D] “Even if,” Rhen. Seml.] the last Adam [Note: [Col. 0865D] “Will say,” Rhen. Seml.], deriving the shared name from participation in the same substance; for Adam’s flesh was no more from seed than Christ’s.

“Therefore, as the earthy one is, such also are the earthy; and as the heavenly one is, such also are the heavenly.” Are they such in substance? Or first in discipline, and afterward also in dignity, which discipline has secured? Yet the earthy and heavenly will in no way be distinguished in substance, since they have once been called men by the Apostle.

For although Christ alone is truly heavenly—indeed, above heaven—he is nevertheless man insofar as he is flesh and soul, and in no respect is he distinguished by this condition of his substances from the earthy quality. [Col. 0866A] Consequently, those who are heavenly according to him are understood to have been called heavenly not by reason of their present substance, but by reason of their future glory. For previously also, where this distinction flowed from a difference of dignity, it was shown that the glory of celestial bodies is one thing, that of terrestrial bodies another, that of the sun another, that of the moon another, and that of the stars another; for star differs from star in glory, but not in substance.

Finally, after first setting forth the difference of dignity—both that which must be pursued now in the same substance and that which must be attained then—he also adds an exhortation, so that here we may pursue Christ’s condition through discipline, and there attain his summit through glory: “Just as we have borne the image of the earthy, let us also bear the image of the above-heavenly.”

For we have borne the image of the earthy through fellowship in transgression, through partnership in death, and through exile from paradise. For [Col. 0866B] although [Note: [Col. 0865D] “Although also,” Pam. Par.] the image of Adam is borne here in the flesh [Note: [Col. 0866C] “Is thus thought,” Rhen. Seml.], we are nevertheless not admonished to cast off [Note: [Col. 0866D] “Lay down,” Pam. Par. Fran.] the flesh. If not the flesh, then it is our conduct, so that correspondingly we may also bear within ourselves the image of the heavenly one—not being gods [Note: [Col. 0866D] “Gods” (*dii*) is perhaps better. Editors.], nor yet established in heaven, but [Note: [Col. 0866D] ‘Walking according to the lineaments of Christ.’ Imitating the conduct which Christ maintained in the flesh. Bernard, sermon on Psalm XC. From this, of course, Christ is to be recognized above all others: by holiness, righteousness, and truth. Thus, in book II *Against Marcion*, the soul is said to possess the lines of God insofar as it is immortal, free and possessed of its own choice, often foreknowing, and capable of understanding and knowledge. RIG.] walking according to the lineaments of Christ in holiness, righteousness, and truth.

Indeed, he directs this whole matter so fully toward discipline that he says the image of Christ must be borne here, in this flesh and in this time of discipline. For in saying, in the imperative mode, “Let us bear,” he speaks to this present time, in which a human being has no other substance than flesh and soul. Or if [Note: [Col. 0866D] “Or even if,” Rhen. Seml.] this faith looks to some other, that is, heavenly substance, it has nevertheless been promised to this present substance, which is commanded to labor toward it.

Since, therefore, he places the image both of the earthy and of the heavenly in conduct—the former [Col. 0866C] to be renounced [Note: [Col. 0866D] “To be borne,” Rhen. Seml.], but the latter to be pursued—and then adds [Note: [Col. 0866D] “Adds,” Rhen. Seml.], “Now this I say,” that is, “because of those things which [Note: [Col. 0866D] “Which? Which,” Rhen.] I said above” (for the conjunction completes the sense by referring back to the preceding statements), “that flesh and blood cannot possess the kingdom of God by inheritance,” he commands that “flesh and blood” be understood as nothing other than the aforesaid image of the earthy.

If that image is reckoned as belonging to the old conduct, and the old conduct does not receive [Note: [Col. 0866D] “May not receive,” Rhen. Seml.] the kingdom of God, then flesh and blood, in not receiving the kingdom of God, are accordingly reduced to the old conduct [Note: [Col. 0866D] “Of truth,” Rhen. Seml.].

This holds, of course, if the Apostle never used substance for works, and does not do so here either. But if he denied that persons still situated in the flesh were [Col. 0867A] in the flesh, because he denied that they were in the works of the flesh, you must overthrow its mode of life, not its substance: not the substance, but those works of the substance which estrange it from the kingdom of God.

After making these works manifest to the Galatians also, he professes that he warns and has warned them (Gal. V) that those who do [Note: [Col. 0867C] “May do,” Rhen. Seml.] such things will not [Note: [Col. 0867C] “May not,” Rhen. Seml.] obtain the kingdom of God by inheritance. They do not, of course, bear the image of the heavenly one as they have borne [Note: [Col. 0867C] “Had borne,” Rhen. Seml.] that of the earthy; and therefore, on account of the old conduct, they are to be reckoned [Note: [Col. 0867C] “Considered,” others.] as nothing other than flesh and blood.

For even if the Apostle had suddenly broken out into this declaration, that flesh and blood must be driven from the kingdom of God, without any preparatory construction of the preceding sense, would we not accordingly interpret these two substances as the old man, devoted to flesh and blood—that is, to eating and drinking—whose part it is to say against faith in the resurrection: [Col. 0867B] “Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we shall die”? For by inserting this also, the Apostle censured flesh and blood on account of their fruits, eating and drinking.

## CHAPTER L

But even [Note: [Col. 0867C] “Even” omitted by Fran.] if we set aside interpretations of this sort, which censure the works of flesh and blood, [Note: [Col. 0867D] ‘The substances themselves also, understood as nothing other than what they are,’ etc. By another path he meets the heretics, in order to prove that nothing can be established from the passage which they prize for refuting the defenders of the resurrection of the flesh. For even if flesh and blood were taken strictly and rigidly by Paul, and not for the works of carnal people, faith in the bodily resurrection nevertheless cannot be broken. Irenaeus likewise meets the heretics with a twofold answer to the same passage. LAC.] it will still be permissible to vindicate for the resurrection the substances themselves, understood as nothing other than what they are. For it is not the resurrection that is directly denied [Note: [Col. 0867C] “Denied,” Pam. Fran.] to flesh and blood, but the kingdom of God, which follows upon resurrection; [Note: [Col. 0867D] ‘There is also a resurrection unto judgment.’ We have enclosed this clause in parentheses to make the sense clearer. Indeed, the general resurrection of the flesh is even confirmed when a particular resurrection is excepted. For when it is declared into which condition it does not rise, it is implied into which condition it does rise.

And thus, since the work of the substance, not its kind, [Col. 0867C] undergoes the distinction of resurrection according to merits, it is also apparent from this that flesh and blood are barred from the kingdom of God under the designation of guilt, not of substance. Yet under the designation of their form [Note: [Col. 0867D] ‘Yet under the designation of their form.’ He has just taken *form* in the same sense as *substance*.
