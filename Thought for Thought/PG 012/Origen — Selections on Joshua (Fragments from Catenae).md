## Source locus 1

“You and all this people.” What had been said beforehand was promised to those who keep the Law, not to Joshua son of Nun. The Israelites did not receive those things because they did not keep the Law, as the following passage shows.

<!-- source-locus: 2 -->
## Source locus 2

“Every place on which you set foot.” The men at Ai resisted because of Achar’s transgression. There is another point as well: although God gave the nations into Israel’s hands, Israel did not utterly destroy them, and they became thorns in Israel’s side.

<!-- source-locus: 3 -->
## Source locus 3

“They entered the house of a prostitute named Rahab.” The spies entrust their safety to a prostitute, and rightly so. Her speech shows that she alone is faithful and fears God; that is why her family too is saved through her.

<!-- source-locus: 4 -->
## Source locus 4

By the same author. Rahab’s name means “broadening.” She symbolizes the Church drawn from the nations, which says: “This place is too narrow for me; make room for me, so that I may live here.”

<!-- source-locus: 5 -->
## Source locus 5

By the same author. Rahab is not saved merely for receiving the spies: first comes her faith and her disposition toward God.

<!-- source-locus: 6 -->
## Source locus 6

“She hid them among the flax stalks.” Rahab hid them in the woody flax stalks lying on her roof, where she was preparing fine linen for them; they were worthy of a high priest’s garment. Flax stalks yield the fine linen from which priestly clothing is made.

<!-- source-locus: 7 -->
## Source locus 7

“She let them down through the window.” Her house, we should note, stood in the city wall. New things are kin to old ones: Paul’s case is similar, for he too was lowered in a basket through the wall.

<!-- source-locus: 8 -->
## Source locus 8

By the same author. As a second example, the Apostle escaped his enemies in the same way. As he says in his Second Letter to the Corinthians: “I was lowered through a window in a basket, down through the wall, and escaped.” Acts likewise says: “They watched the gates day and night so that they might kill him; but his disciples took him by night and lowered him down the wall in a basket.”

<!-- source-locus: 9 -->
## Source locus 9

“They stayed there for three days.” The journey itself did not take three days—the Jordan lies very near Jericho. They remained because the search for the spies was thorough: “The pursuers searched every road,” it says, “and did not find them.”

<!-- source-locus: 10 -->
## Source locus 10

“Forty thousand armed men.” Note that the number four—whether it denotes single units, hundreds, or a larger total—is associated with affliction.

## Source locus 11

“Make yourself stone knives from sheer rock.” We should ask why those born in the wilderness were not circumcised, while in Moses’ day a man gathering wood on the Sabbath was stoned at God’s command. A vast multitude remained uncircumcised in the wilderness without even being reproached. Yet God had declared that a person not circumcised on the eighth day must be cut off; he had not laid down the same penalty for Sabbath-breakers. This itself makes clear that circumcision and Sabbath are not good in and of themselves; they had their use for a particular time.

Circumcision was intended as an identifying mark. God wished them to be recognizable through it and to appear not to have withdrawn from the faith. Just as a branded sheep is easy to recover even if it wanders, so he wanted Jews who mingled with the nations to remain identifiable by circumcision. The evidence is this: they were circumcised in Egypt, but remained uncircumcised in the wilderness because they were alone, separated from Egyptians and from the other peoples of Palestine. Once they crossed the Jordan and were to live among the nations, they were commanded to be circumcised again. This is why “a second time” is said: they were not eight-day-old infants, but men already grown, some even approaching old age.

The stone knife signifies the Lord, who gave us the spiritual circumcision of mind and heart. Thus the Apostle speaks to us of Christ’s circumcision, “not in the stripping off of our flesh.”

*The source’s `ἀμροτόμου` is an evident dropped-kappa form of `ἀκροτόμου`, the reading in the Joshua 5:2 lemma and upstream PG text; it is rendered “sheer.” The sheep-and-branding sentence remains grammatically disrupted; the wording above retains its explicit reference to faith and its secure identifying-mark comparison.*

<!-- source-locus: 12 -->
## Source locus 12

“I am commander-in-chief of the Lord’s host; now I have come.” Recall that when Israel made the calf, God told Moses: “Lead this people up, for I will certainly not go up with you, because the people is stiff-necked. Look: I shall send my angel ahead of you. Take heed to yourself, then, and do not disobey.” Moses answered: “Unless you go up with us, do not lead me up from here.” By saying this he persuaded God, who remained with the people until Moses died.

After Moses’ death, God appears and speaks with Joshua son of Nun. Once leadership of the people has passed to Moses’ disciple, he entrusts him to Michael, the commander-in-chief. Michael was the people’s ruler, as another angel’s speech in Daniel shows.

<!-- source-locus: 13 -->
## Source locus 13

“As they went, they sounded the trumpets.” The trumpets themselves symbolize the events of the last day: “The Lord himself,” he says, “will descend from heaven with a command, with the voice of an archangel, and with God’s trumpet.” The priests’ circling the city for six days similarly signifies that, throughout the six days of this present age, all the saints sound the trumpet and proclaim forgiveness of sins to those who desire repentance.

<!-- source-locus: 14 -->
## Source locus 14

“Jesus said to the Israelites, ‘Cry out!’” This cry is presumably verbal glorification and a voice of praise directed to God. It fights the error of the people of Jericho and wins the astonishing, wonder-filled victory.

<!-- source-locus: 15 -->
## Source locus 15

“Thus Ozan did.” This shows that these words were written not by Joshua son of Nun, but by someone else many generations later. Properly understood, this does not conflict with what we said earlier. Joshua wrote what happened in his own day, or what was revealed to him, just as Moses wrote matters belonging to his own times.

Later, after wise Solomon had built the temple, the people fell into deep impiety and God abandoned the temple, so that Deuteronomy was scarcely discovered, lying carelessly discarded. For this very reason, after the people had undergone the Babylonian captivity, Ezra—exceptionally learned in the Law and able to recite the whole Old Testament from memory—wrote the Law and certain other events and revelations. Those things had been prophesied long before and came to pass later; the case just mentioned is of the same sort.

<!-- source-locus: 16 -->
## Source locus 16

“Even today I am strong, just as when Moses sent me.” The holy man is therefore strong in both bodies of teaching: he is strong in the old mysteries and in the new. That is what it means to be strong under Moses and under Joshua.
