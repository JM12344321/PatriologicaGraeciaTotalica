This twofold kind of restraint, by which the images of the gods were fixed fast as though bound with chains, is similarly reproached by Arnobius in Book VI *Against the Nations*: “If,” he says, “they are subject to the necessity of remaining in place, what could be more wretched or more unfortunate than they, if hooks and leaden fastenings thus hold them upon their bases?” [Col. 0370C] Both kinds are also joined together in Law II of the *Digest*, *On the Violation of a Tomb*: “Celsus asks whether, if a statue was neither secured with lead nor fastened down, it became part of the monument, or whether it remains among our possessions.”

But Prudentius, too, was made to experience Minucius’s fate and the critics’ heavy hand because the meaning of the same word was not sufficiently well observed. Hence in the very ancient manuscripts of the Vatican Library one may see *retinente solo* (“held by the ground”) substituted for *retinente veru* (“held by the iron bar”); Aldus and others who followed the authority of his text printed that reading. George Fabricius, however, finding *retinente veru* consistently written in his copies, but not having adequately understood the word’s proper usage among the ancients, resorted to emendation, that sacred anchor of critics. [Col. 0370D] Accordingly, since he knew that the Dioscuri were commonly armed with spears or javelins for a cavalry exercise, he sharpened this *veru*, made from rough and blunt iron, with the point of a javelin, and, by altering the wording, forged from his own abundant ingenuity the reading *impendent retinentque veru*—“they overhang and hold a spear.” From that time onward this reading established itself in most of the more recent editions. Yet I am so far from approving it that, on the contrary, I do not hesitate to defend the ancient and genuine reading tenaciously, relying both upon the evident nature of the thing itself and upon the authority of the old books. Among these I readily assign first place to the excellent manuscript of the Urbino Library, which recently, through the immortal benefaction of the most wise Pope Alexander VII, was added to the Vatican Library; in it I found the text written thus.

# DISSERTATION ON THE BOOK OF MARCUS MINUCIUS FELIX ENTITLED *OCTAVIUS*

By Dom Nicolas Le Nourry, Monk of the Order of Saint Benedict, of the Congregation of Saint Maur

## CHAPTER ONE

### ANALYSIS OF THIS BOOK; WHO ITS AUTHOR WAS; AND WHO WERE CAECILIUS AND OCTAVIUS, WHO ARE INTRODUCED IN IT AS DISPUTANTS

[Col. 0371B]

## ARTICLE I

### Analysis of This Book

This book contains nothing other than a dialogue in which Caecilius, devoted to the superstitious vanities of the pagans, endeavors by various arguments to prove that no one should abandon the ancestral worship of the gods handed down by his forefathers, and that everyone should persevere in it. Octavius, on the other hand, who, after rejecting that impious worship, had enrolled himself together with Minucius Felix in the Christian profession, so thoroughly overturns all [Col. 0371C] Caecilius’s arguments, and demonstrates the truth of the Christian religion with such weighty reasons, that Caecilius, fully convinced by them, embraced it with a joyful and grateful heart.

At the very beginning of the book Minucius relates how greatly he delighted in the pleasant recollection of the things that had happened to him with Octavius while the latter was staying with him in Rome, and especially in the memory of this disputation. For Octavius had unexpectedly come to him on certain business during the vintage holidays and the recess of the courts. Octavius’s arrival was as welcome to Minucius as it was unexpected. After they had satisfied for a day or two the eagerness born of their intense longing, they decided to go together to the seaside baths at Ostia, taking with them Caecilius, Minucius’s companion. Accordingly, at daybreak, as they proceeded toward the sea “walking along,” [Col. 0371D] as he says, upon the shore, Caecilius, “upon noticing” an image of Serapis, raised his hand to his mouth and pressed a kiss upon it with his lips. Octavius at once sharply reproached Minucius, saying that the disgrace of this impious act reflected no less upon him, as Caecilius’s host, than upon Caecilius himself. Nevertheless, after traversing [Col. 0372B] the remainder of their route, they walked along the seashore conversing about voyages.

There, moreover, while Minucius and Octavius watched with some pleasure the boys playing at throwing shells into the sea, Caecilius, by contrast, paid no attention to so pleasing a spectacle and displayed a rather gloomy frame of mind. Minucius therefore missed his customary cheerfulness. Caecilius frankly confessed that Octavius’s words had struck him deeply, and for that reason asked to be allowed to debate with Octavius concerning the truth of his religion. Octavius agreed, together with his companion; and, after a somewhat elevated spot had been selected, Minucius sat between Caecilius and Octavius.

Before Caecilius entered upon this disputation, however, he asked Minucius to hold the scales of a perfectly impartial judge, [Col. 0372C] just as if nothing whatever had yet become known to him about either religion. He then confidently asserted that it could very easily be proved that everything in human affairs is doubtful, unresolved, uncertain, and less true than plausible. It was therefore deplorable, he said, and deserving of everyone’s indignation, that certain uneducated men, ignorant of the sciences and arts, did not hesitate to make pronouncements about the supreme order of things and the divine majesty, concerning which the philosophers had not dared to establish anything certain down to that very day. Nor was this without reason, since they were persuaded that weak human beings were forbidden to investigate heavenly matters and ought to be satisfied if they came to know themselves more intimately. He then attempted by a brief induction to demonstrate that it was uncertain whether all things had been made by some God as their author. From the prosperous fortune [Col. 0372D] of wicked men such as Phalaris and Dionysius, the adverse fortune of good men such as Rutilius, Camillus, and Socrates, and the varied and unstable accidents of earthly affairs, he strove to prove that it had not yet been established for us whether the world is governed by God’s providence or whether fortune, unbound by laws, rules over all things.

[Col. 0373A] From this, therefore, he concluded that it was better to adhere to the religions handed down from antiquity and to fear and adore the gods than to pass any judgment about them. For from the very beginning of the world our ancestors had regarded these gods either as propitious beings or as kings. Every nation, and afterward the Romans, had so worshiped its own divinities that the Romans, through their pious worship, deserved to obtain supreme dominion over the whole world. He then did not hesitate to assert that this worship of the gods—which is holier in proportion as it is older—was sufficiently proved by certain miracles and had not been scorned and mocked with impunity by certain impious persons. After declaring that he had passed over many other things concerning the gods’ natural attributes and gifts, the songs of the poets, and the oracles that foretold the future, he confidently pronounced that the gods’ presence became manifest to anyone [Col. 0373B] from the temples they inhabited; from the seers who knew what was to come; from the benefits by which the gods themselves were accustomed to render good service to human beings; and, finally, from dreams, through which they were most clearly recognized. Although, therefore, the nature or origin of these gods might be uncertain, the consensus of all nations concerning them ought nevertheless to remain firm. Hence he said that the impious temerity of Theodorus, Diagoras, and Protagoras, who wished utterly to abolish or at least to weaken this religion, was by no means to be tolerated.

Yet far less, he said, should that unlawful and desperate faction of Christians be tolerated—their light-shunning nation, gathered from the lowest dregs of humanity and bound together by nocturnal assemblies, solemn fasts, and inhuman foods. They indeed regard temples [Col. 0373C] as tombs, reject the gods, mock sacred rites, set honors at naught, and finally despise tortures in such a way that, although they fear to die after death, they meanwhile do not fear to die. Nevertheless, though scattered throughout every region of the earth, they recognize one another by secret signs and love one another almost before becoming acquainted. They are said to adore the head of an ass, to worship the genitals of their priest, and to venerate a crucified man and the very instrument of his punishment. What more need be said? On a solemn day they gather for a feast, and there, after drinking the blood of an infant most cruelly slain, they ratify the covenant they have struck, and their novices are initiated. After this banquet of utterly monstrous savagery, when the lamps have been extinguished by a dog, they all mingle indiscriminately, as chance determines, amid shameless darkness; and thus they become incestuous either in deed or in conscience. Moreover, [Col. 0373D] whatever they worship, they strive with great care to conceal: they have no altars, no temples, no publicly recognizable images.

Their God, moreover, is said to be one, as is that of the Jews. Though they can neither see nor display him, they nevertheless suppose him to be thoroughly restless in mind, perversely inquisitive, present everywhere, and carefully investigating all human deeds and the innermost thoughts of the mind, even though he cannot attend to every individual. They also assert that the whole world will one day be consumed by fire and promise, after the resurrection of our bodies, a supremely blessed eternity of life to the just and an eternity of the greatest punishments to the unjust. For this reason they condemn funeral pyres and burial by fire, and ascribe everything to the divine will or to fate. Yet no dead person can be raised [Col. 0374A] from the underworld, and no one has ever, by the fortune of Protesilaus, been recalled to life even for a single hour. What will happen to these Christians after death they ought to infer from the fact that now, deprived of all divine assistance and subjected to the Romans, they are crushed by the greatest calamities and miseries. They abstain from respectable pleasures, spectacles, processions, public banquets, food offered to idols, flowers, and crowns, and reserve ointments for funerals.

Caecilius therefore urged his companions to stop probing the regions of heaven and the secrets of the universe and to follow this maxim of Socrates: “What is above us is no concern of ours.” And Socrates certainly deserved to be praised by the divine oracle for this reason: “Because he had learned that he knew nothing.” From this source flowed the prudent doubt of Arcesilaus, Carneades, and very many Academics regarding the highest questions, [Col. 0374B] as well as the hesitation of Simonides when questioned about the gods by the tyrant Hiero—a hesitation that all should imitate. Finally, Caecilius declared that he gladly subscribed to their opinion and, not without the disdain of an exalted spirit, challenged Octavius to answer his arguments; with this he ended his speech.

Minucius then answered Caecilius calmly that he should not exult in the elegance of his discourse, the subtle variety of his speech, or his considerable eloquence; rather, each of his arguments must be carefully weighed, so that whatever was true and valid in them might be selected, approved, and accepted. But Caecilius, with some indication that his feelings had been wounded, said that Minucius, by weakening the force of his argument, was departing from the office of a conscientious judge. For Octavius was entirely free [Col. 0374C] to refute, if he could, everything Caecilius had brought forward, and Caecilius was now fully prepared to listen to him in profound silence.

“I shall indeed speak,” Octavius replied, “and shall strive according to my powers to wash away the most bitter stain of abuse with a flood of truthful words.” Before all else, he declared that it was not right for him to conceal the doubtful, divided, wandering, and fluctuating character of Caecilius’s mind and opinion. Yet he acknowledged that this should be attributed neither to any malicious deceit on Caecilius’s part nor to a crafty and cunning disposition—something entirely foreign to his sincerity—but to ignorance of the truth.

Then, proceeding to weaken each of Caecilius’s arguments, he taught that no one should resent the fact that [Col. 0374D] Christians, however unlettered and poor, discuss heavenly matters. For such were not only the philosophers and other inventors of the arts, but also men of our own people who, although poor and needy, imparted wisdom and learning to others. And since ability is bestowed neither by fortune nor by wealth, but is implanted in us by nature, any human being of either sex and of any condition can investigate divine matters and form a judgment about them.

He indeed acknowledged that a person ought to know himself—who he is, whence and why he came, by whom and from what elements he was made—but he wholly and utterly denied that this knowledge could be acquired without first knowing the universe [Col. 0375A] and God himself. For no one, unless entirely bereft of reason, can observe the ordered beauty of the whole world without being immediately persuaded that it was not founded by a chance collision of atoms, but was created by God and is ruled and administered by him. Octavius confirms and establishes this from the motions and usefulness of the heavens, the sun, the moon, and the stars; from the cycle of the months; the alternation of darkness and light; the marvelous order of the seasons; the constant variation of crops; the bounds of the sea; the manifold armor naturally bestowed upon living creatures for their defense; the human form and figure; and the manner of generation and birth. For none of these things can come about without the providence of almighty God. God, moreover, takes thought not only for the whole world, but for each [Col. 0375B] of its parts. For who doubts that at his command Britain is made fruitful by the warmth of the sea, Egypt by the Nile, Mesopotamia by the Euphrates, and the East by the river Indus? Finally, just as we see that someone presides over a house arranged with splendid ornament, so we see that this Lord presides over the whole world.

Octavius further proved that God himself is one by the example of earthly empires—those of the Persians, Thebans, and Romans—where, he says, neither brother could ever tolerate his brother nor father-in-law his son-in-law as a partner in sovereignty. And what of the fact that, just as bees, flocks, and herds have only one king or leader, so there is one God, who, lacking beginning and end, governs all things?

Although he is infinite and immeasurable, and known in his full magnitude [Col. 0375C] to himself alone, and although he can neither be seen nor named by us, nevertheless, if you remove the earthly additions made to names, you will clearly recognize him. Indeed, Octavius argues that there is unanimous agreement among all concerning him from the fact that ordinary people invoke him while stretching their hands toward heaven. Moreover, the poets called him the father of gods and humans, and Virgil called him the creator, mind, and spirit of all things. Indeed, the more distinguished philosophers themselves—as Octavius shows individually—Thales, Anaximenes, Diogenes of Apollonia, Anaxagoras, Pythagoras, Xenophanes, Antisthenes, Speusippus, Democritus, Strato, Aristotle, Heraclides, Theophrastus, Zeno, Chrysippus, Cleanthes, Diogenes of Babylon, Xenophon, Aristo, and Plato, although [Col. 0375D] differing from us in words and expression, nevertheless adopted our view concerning that same one God, some more obscurely and others more plainly.

Having thus argued, Octavius clearly convicts of falsehood the ridiculous old wives’ tales and absurd traditions concerning Scylla, the Chimera, the Hydra, the Centaurs, and others, which our ancestors had retailed as miracles. No less clearly does he show that the worship of the gods was always foolish and impious. For the most ancient people first venerated as gods their kings, illustrious leaders, queens who were chaste and possessed of courage beyond that usual for their sex, and inventors of the arts, because of their splendid deeds or the benefits received from them. This is proved by Octavius [Col. 0376A] from the testimony of Euhemerus, Prodicus, Persaeus, and Alexander the Great, who recount the homelands, birthdays, tombs, or benefactions of the gods and the secrets of their priests. The pagans could not therefore deny these things, any more than they could deny that the sistrum of Isis, the tomb of Serapis or Osiris, and their sacred mysteries disclose nothing other than the sorrowful ends, deaths, and funerals of wretched gods. Who, moreover, does not see that the Eleusinian rites of Ceres and the other rites of Jupiter and Cybele are no less absurd than disgraceful?

What great absurdities and foul nonsense, besides, do the pagans retail about the form and appearance of their gods! They fashion Vulcan as lame; Apollo as beardless; his son Aesculapius as bearded; Neptune with sea-green eyes; Minerva with gray-blue eyes; Juno with bovine eyes; [Col. 0376B] Mercury with winged feet; Pan with hooves; Saturn in fetters; Janus with two faces; Diana with her robe girded high; the Ephesian Diana with many breasts; Trivia with three heads and many hands; Jupiter hideous with as many monstrous forms as the names by which he is called; Erigone hanging from a noose; the Castors living and dying on alternate days; Aesculapius struck by lightning; and Hercules burned in the fires of Mount Oeta.

These tales, first handed down by impious men, were afterward celebrated by others, and especially by the poets, who did no small harm to truth through their authority. Plato therefore not undeservedly expelled Homer from his city, for Homer sang in shameless mockery of Venus wounded, Mars driven into flight not without wounds, Jupiter freed by Briareus, Jupiter weeping over Sarpedon’s [Col. 0376C] fall in showers of blood, and Jupiter ensnared by the girdle of Venus; of Hercules carrying away dung; Neptune building Laomedon’s walls without any payment; Apollo pasturing Admetus’s flock; Vulcan forging the thunderbolts of Jupiter, who was born in Crete; the adultery of Mars and Venus; and Ganymede’s abduction by Jupiter himself. Yet by those ridiculous and obscene inventions the minds of children are corrupted, and finally they grow shamefully and wickedly old in them.

From Diodorus Siculus, Thallus, and Nepos, whom Octavius cites, everyone could nevertheless have learned very easily that Saturn was the foremost of these gods. Fleeing from Crete through fear of his son and received in Italy by Janus, he taught many things to uncivilized people; and, by imposing his own name upon various places, he proved [Col. 0376D] himself to have been a human being. Jupiter, moreover, who reigned in Crete after driving Saturn from it, died on that island, where his tomb is shown. Furthermore, Romulus after his death—through Proculus’s perjury—Juba at the wish of the Moors, and others as well, although unwilling and resistant, were proclaimed gods. Yet the true God can neither be born nor die.

Octavius next attacks the images and statues of the gods and makes it plain that, whether fashioned from cheap and filthy material or from precious matter, made in one way or another by the hands and skill of craftsmen, and very often befouled by birds and other animals, they are impiously worshiped by the pagans and wearied with prayers to no purpose and without effect. He also demonstrates by a brief survey how many ridiculous, obscene, and cruel rites were observed in celebrating [Col. 0377A] the mysteries of certain gods.

He then shows that Caecilius falsely boasted that the Romans gained dominion over the whole world by duly observing superstitions of this kind. For that kingdom began with fratricide and grew through crimes, impiety, and sacrilege. And what of the fact that their native gods—Romulus, Picus, Tiberinus, Consus, Pilumnus, Picumnus, Cloacina, Fear, Pallor, Fever, Disease, Acca Larentia, and Flora—could offer the Romans no assistance? Still less could the gods of other nations, whom the Romans did not worship until they had first defeated them in war and celebrated a triumph over them. Finally, no one is unaware that the Assyrians, Medes, Persians, Greeks, and Egyptians ruled before any of the Roman superstitions, [Col. 0377B] which are worse than all the rest.

The weapon lightly hurled by Caecilius from the auspices and auguries of birds is turned back against him by Octavius through the examples of Regulus, Mancinus, Paulus, and Caesar. By the similar examples of Amphiaraus, Tiresias, and the Pythian Apollo, Octavius also demonstrates that the argument drawn from oracles is no more valid. But because someone might press the objection that at least some oracles had been true, Octavius judged that the matter must be taken up at a deeper level and traced back to its first origin. He therefore says that there are certain impure spirits who, through lust and vice, lost the simplicity of their substance. Nor could the pagans deny this. For the poets call them *daemones* (“demons”), and they were known to the Magi and to Socrates, [Col. 0377C] who had one of them as guardian and counselor. Hostanes and Plato explain who these spirits are. Now the Magi and others, by the breath and inspiration of these ruined angels, who strive to ruin human beings together with themselves, perform various magical deceptions.

Those same demons lurk beneath statues and images, dwell in shrines, or creep into human bodies; they disturb the natural order of certain things, pour forth oracles wrapped in false responses, and bring about other extraordinary events—such as those Caecilius had recounted concerning Jupiter, the Castors, and Claudia—in such a way that they appear to the pagans to have performed true miracles. Yet they are then compelled to confess that these things were done not by God but by themselves, when they are adjured by Christians through the true God and driven by sacred exorcisms [Col. 0377D] from the bodies they possess. For this reason they flee Christians when they draw near and stir up such hatred against them among the pagans that the latter begin not only to hate them before knowing them, but also to impute to them unspeakable crimes that no one has yet been able to prove. Octavius indeed clearly testifies that he had learned this from his own experience: when he and his associates were still pagans, they tried by various tortures to compel Christians, not so much to confess those fictitious crimes as to renounce the faith of Christ.

There Octavius demonstrates with irresistible force that Christians were as falsely accused of sacrilege, incest, debauchery, and parricide as it was certain and true that precisely the same [Col. 0378A] crimes, or others entirely similar and even greater, were actually committed by the pagans themselves. First, he says, those who perversely clamored that Christians worshiped the head of an ass consecrated asses together with Epona and Isis, venerated the heads of oxen and rams, and dedicated gods represented either in goat or human form, or with the face of an ox or dog. Moreover, the Romans themselves worshiped the bull Apis, and others worshiped animals, serpents, birds, beasts, and fish, as did the Egyptians, who revered Isis and Serapis no more than the sharpness of onions and the breaking of wind from the belly.

As for Caecilius’s further objection that Christians worshiped the genitals of their priest, Octavius asserts that this was as false as it is certain that the pagans were defiled by utterly obscene acts of debauchery that we were never permitted either to name or to hear.

Nor was it any truer that we worship a man affixed to a cross for his crimes. For we rightly believe him not only innocent but God. The Egyptians, by contrast, slaughter victims and offer sacrifices to a man whom they themselves have chosen. Others invoke the divine powers of kings whom they have enrolled among the gods, supplicate their images, and implore their guardian spirits.

“We neither worship nor desire crosses,” Octavius continues. But perhaps the pagans venerate them as parts of their gods. For the shape of the cross is visible in the emblems, cantabrian standards, banners, trophies, and many other natural objects that they worship and that he enumerates.

[Col. 0378C] He also says that the accusation that Christians drink the blood of an infant killed by themselves is utterly shameless. The pagans, however, as he argues, both cruelly expose newborn children and, before they are born, kill them by the most cruel abortion. Saturn is said to have devoured his sons; infants are sacrificed by certain inhabitants of Africa, just as guests were sacrificed by the Taurians and the Egyptian Busiris, and human beings by the Gauls and Romans; and Jupiter himself is worshiped with this victim also—horrible to tell. What more? Catiline sealed his conspiracy with human blood; Bellona is propitiated with it; and epilepsy is cured by it. Finally, athletes devour beasts still fattened with human blood and limbs. Christians, however, are not permitted even to hear of or witness homicide, and are forbidden animal blood.

[Col. 0378D] The charge that Christians hold a banquet polluted by incest is as remote from all plausibility as it is certain that the pagans themselves are in fact guilty of incest. For the Persians have intercourse with their mothers, and the Egyptians and Athenians with their sisters. The poets, moreover, sing of the gods’ various execrable adulteries, of which very many pagans were themselves also aware. Christians, by contrast, bound by the bond of a single marriage, were always chaste outside it, or persevered in virginity, and held banquets marked by a chaste, sober, and dignified cheerfulness.

Although Christians reject purple robes and honors, they did not arise from the lowest dregs of the common people, nor are they factious or babblers in corners. The complaint repeatedly [Col. 0379A] made that their number was increasing daily was no uncertain testimony to their proven manner of life. They recognize one another, certainly, not by a mark upon the body but by the sign of innocence; and to the same degree that they love one another and rightly and deservedly call one another brothers, the pagans, though unknown to one another, rage more cruelly against their own selves in mutual hatred.

Nor can it be said that these Christians conceal what they worship because they have no shrines and no altars. For they were persuaded that God cannot be confined within any temple, that no image of him can be fashioned, and that no victim is more pleasing and acceptable to him than a person’s pure and sincere conscience.

Although we do not see this God with our eyes—just as we do not see the winds, the sun, or our own soul—we nevertheless clearly [Col. 0379B] recognize him from his works. Present everywhere, he sees all things, even the most secret thoughts of our mind, and we live near him and, as it were, in his bosom. But if it is said that God was of no benefit to the Jews, the writers of the Jewish annals were certainly most ample witnesses that they abandoned God before they were abandoned by him.

Moreover, since all things that have a beginning also have an end, it is no wonder if this world is ultimately to be consumed by fire. Octavius indeed shows that the Stoics, Epicureans, and Plato held this opinion concerning the conflagration of the world. But Plato, like Pythagoras, acknowledged only a partial restoration of a dead person to life. It is nevertheless certain that God can raise from death to life the human being whom he fashioned from nothing. [Col. 0379C] By certain examples Octavius proves that this resurrection of bodies will occur; Christians buried those bodies according to the ancient and better custom. In a manner not very different, he shows that after this life just and pious people are to be rewarded with eternal happiness, but the unjust with everlasting punishments. He then clearly demonstrates that the morals of Christians are far holier than those of the pagans. No less plainly does he teach that fate is nothing other than what God has spoken—*fatum* from *fatus est*—for God determines destinies in accordance with each person’s deserts. There he promises that he will at some time discuss fate more fully.

After this he makes it perfectly clear that the poverty reproached in Christians is not a disgrace but a glory; likewise, their adversities and the tortures inflicted upon them most unjustly, however horrifying, for their confession of Christ, and death itself, are not punishments but trials of virtue, triumphs, and spectacles worthy of God. [Col. 0379D] Octavius clearly shows this by comparing the deeds of certain most courageous pagans with those of our martyrs. But if any who do not know God abound in riches and honors or are raised to the highest pinnacle of rank and power, he asserts that these are victims fattened and crowned for punishment, who are lifted all the higher precisely so that they may fall with a heavier crash. For without the knowledge of God there can be no solid happiness.

Octavius further declares that Christians do not attend spectacles and processions because they had come to know with absolute certainty that these were no less impious than cruel. They likewise abstain from food offered to idols, [Col. 0380A] lest anyone think that they either yield to demons or are ashamed of their religion. Yet they do not spurn every color and fragrance of flowers. They are accustomed to use flowers scattered, loose, and soft, and to encircle their necks with garlands; but they consider it superfluous and useless to crown the head of a dead person. With the same tranquility with which they live, they also bury their dead, awaiting with the surest hope the crown of eternal happiness. Hence, however much Socrates, Arcesilaus, Carneades, Pyrrho, the Academics, and Simonides may assert that they know nothing for certain, Christians nevertheless rightly glory in having found the truth that those men sought in vain. Their true religion, therefore, once the superstitions of the pagans have been rejected, ought to be embraced by all.

When Octavius had concluded this speech and Minucius, [Col. 0380B] carried away in admiration of him, remained silent, Caecilius exclaimed that he had been defeated by Octavius, that he was now the victor over his own error, and that he already professed the Christian religion. Finally, a clearer explanation of our mysteries having been deferred until the following day, they all returned home joyful and cheerful.

## ARTICLE II

### Whether Minucius Felix Was the Author of This Book, and Who He Was

That Minucius Felix was the author of this book or dialogue is proved by such irresistible authority on the part of writers whose trustworthiness is beyond all question that no room for doubt on the matter can remain for anyone. For Firmianus Lactantius, who flourished under the emperor Diocletian, as Jerome notes (*Jerome, Catalogue of Writers*, § 80), and not long after Minucius, has these words concerning him (*Lactantius, Book I, On True Religion*, ch. 11): “Minucius Felix argued as follows in the book entitled *Octavius*: Saturn, when he had been driven away by his son and had come to Italy, etc.” These words we do indeed read in this book of Minucius. Afterward the same Lactantius says: “Among those known to me, Minucius Felix held no obscure place among advocates. His book, entitled *Octavius*, shows how capable a defender of the truth he might have been, had he devoted himself wholly to that study” (*Lactantius, Book V, On Justice*, ch. 1).

[Col. 0380D] Jerome, moreover, says in his *Catalogue of Illustrious Men*, or *Ecclesiastical Writers*: “Minucius Felix, a distinguished advocate at Rome, wrote a dialogue of a Christian and a pagan disputing”—the most ancient manuscript of Saint-Germain-des-Prés reads “of one disputing”—“which is entitled *Octavius* . . . Lactantius also mentions this Minucius in his books” (*Jerome, On Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 58), namely in those that, as you see, we have cited. To Magnus he further wrote the following concerning him (*Jerome, Letter to Magnus*): “Minucius Felix, an advocate of the Roman forum, in the book entitled *Octavius*, and in another *Against the Astrologers*—if, indeed, the title does not misrepresent the author—what portion of pagan literature did he leave untouched?” To Heliodorus also he writes the following concerning Nepotian [Col. 0381A] (*Jerome, Letter to Heliodorus*): “He used to say that saying of Tertullian . . . Thus Minucius Felix spoke, thus Victorinus, and in this manner Arnobius.” Finally, in the *Apology Against Jovinian* (*Jerome, Letter 30 to Pammachius*, or *Apology Against Jovinian*), he says: “I pass over the Latin writers Tertullian, Cyprian, Minucius, Victorinus, Lactantius, and Hilary.”

It would certainly be lengthy and useless to present all the testimonies of more recent writers concerning the author of this book. For it is sufficiently established that, with no one objecting, it is attributed by everyone to our Minucius and claimed for him with complete justice.

The additional name of the man who is called by some *Minutius*, with the letter *c* changed to *t*, was Marcus. For Octavius addressed him in these words: “It is not the part of a good [Col. 0381B] man, brother Marcus, to leave a man, etc.” Caecilius likewise said: “Although to you, brother Marcus, etc.”

How well known to all and how noble the consular family of the Minucii was among the Romans is plain; yet it has remained entirely unknown to everyone down to the present whether our Minucius derived his origin from that family or from some other. Nor has anyone more certainly ascertained what his native country was. Some nevertheless conjecture from the style of the work under discussion that he was born in Africa. But they neither prove nor can prove that any trace of the harsher African style is found in it. Could it not be said with better right that Caecilius and Octavius, who were bound to Minucius by so great a bond of affection, were actually Africans and his fellow countrymen? Yet, as we shall see shortly, this is neither certain nor can it be inferred without any doubt from even the closest intimacy of their friendship. [Col. 0381C]

We think, however, that no one will deny that Minucius lived for a long time at Rome, which was then the capital of all the other cities of the whole world, and that he discharged the office of an advocate there. Lactantius and Jerome, whom we have cited, openly and expressly assert this. Minucius himself writes at the beginning of this book that Octavius came to him at Rome and shortly afterward went with him and another companion to Ostia: “When,” he says, “the vintage holidays had relaxed the care of judicial business”—that is, when, during the vintage season, he had leisure from the forensic labor with which he was occupied throughout the year. Did not Octavius also say of himself and Minucius—or rather in the name of Minucius alone—“Nevertheless, we undertook to defend and protect some men accused of sacrilege and incest, and even parricides; but these men,” namely Christians, “we thought should not be heard at all”? From these words it is clear that, while he was still a pagan, he had discharged the office of an advocate.
