## CHAPTER XV

But true, complete, and pure virginity fears nothing more than itself. It does not wish to endure even the eyes of women; it has eyes of another kind itself. For there is something to be feared even among the pagans, [Note: [Col. 0910D] Which they call the evil eye. She who conceals the virgin even denies that she is a woman.

## CHAPTER XVI

In these considerations consists the defense of our opinion according to Scripture, according to nature, and according to discipline. Scripture establishes the law, nature bears corroborating witness, discipline demands it. Which of these does customary opinion support [Col. 0910C], or what plausible appearance does the contrary position possess? Scripture belongs to God, nature belongs to God, discipline belongs to God. [Col. 0911A] Whatever is contrary to these does not belong to God.

If Scripture is uncertain, nature is manifest, and in view of nature’s testimony Scripture cannot be uncertain. If nature is doubted, discipline shows what is more pleasing to God. Nothing is dearer to him than humility, nothing more acceptable than modesty, nothing more hateful [Note: [Col. 0911C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “more laborious.”] than glory and the desire to please human beings. Therefore, let that which you find pleasing to God be Scripture, nature, and discipline for you, since you are commanded to examine everything and pursue whatever is better (1 Thess. 5:21).

It remains for us also to turn to the women themselves, so that they may receive these teachings more willingly. I implore you, virgin—whether I should call you mother, sister, or daughter [Note: [Col. 0911C] According to the designations of age. He intimates that Christian women were once called sisters by the brethren, that is, by Christians who held no ecclesiastical rank, but daughters by the fathers, that is, by bishops and presbyters. Hence it is evident that the designations “fathers” and “brothers,” and likewise “daughters” and “sisters,” are not new in the Church. PAM.], according to the designations of age: veil your head. If you are a mother, veil it for the sake of your sons; if a sister, for the sake of your brothers; if a daughter, for the sake of your fathers. Every age is endangered in you.

Put on [Col. 0911B] [Note: [Col. 0911C] Rhenanus reads “lead around.”] the armor of modesty; lead around yourself the rampart of shame; build a wall for your sex, one that neither sends forth your eyes [Note: [Col. 0911C] “Sends forth the eyes.” The verb belongs to the terminology of the circus, and the metaphor is taken from it. Thus Statius, *Silvae*, book I:

> Scarcely had the day been sent forth, and already the favorable omens were at hand.

Bernartius went astonishingly astray here in understanding “the day sent forth” to mean evening, since it undoubtedly denotes the morning, as Casperius rightly observes against him. Thus Claudian, *Panegyric on Manlius*:

> Whatever the sun bathes when its rising has been sent forth.

Nor should the following verse of Virgil be understood otherwise:

> And Neptune perceived that winter had been sent forth.

LAC.] nor admits those of strangers. Fully assume the dress of a woman, so that you may preserve the state of a virgin. Make an outward representation of something that is inward, so that you may exhibit the truth to God alone—although you do not falsely represent yourself as married, [Note: [Col. 0911D] For you have married Christ. He praises above all the dress of the monastic life; from this it is plainly inferred that this manner of life flourished in the Church before this author’s time. It is Christ who commands even the betrothed and married women of others [Note: [Col. 0911C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “married women.”] to be veiled—and certainly his own all the more.

## CHAPTER XVII

But we also admonish you who have entered into marriage concerning the other form of chastity: do not [Note: [Col. 0911C] Semler reads “nor.”] fall away from the discipline of the veil [Col. 0911C], not even for a moment, so that, because you cannot cast it aside, you do not destroy it in another way, going about neither covered nor bare. [Col. 0912A]

Others, covered only as far as the crown of the head with small pieces of linen which, I suppose, are kept slight lest they weigh upon the head, and which do not descend as far as the ears—I pity them, if their hearing is so weak that they cannot hear through a covering.

Let them know that the whole head constitutes the woman [Note: [Col. 0912C] Junius reads “to a woman.”]. Its boundaries and limits extend as far as the place where the garment begins. As much space as her loosened hair can occupy is the territory of the veil, so that the neck too must be encompassed. For the necks themselves are what ought to be subjected, and it is on their account that a sign of authority must be borne upon the head (1 Cor. 11:10): the veil is their yoke.

The pagan women of Arabia [Col. 0912B] will judge you, for they cover not merely the head, but [Note: [Col. 0911D] The face also in such a way. Other women, [Note: [Col. 0913B] For whom, plainly, a palm is larger than every fringe and thread. ## ARGUMENT

[Col. 0913C]

—This treatise was written by Tertullian as a Montanist. In it he dissuades a certain widowed brother from a second marriage, which at one point he expressly permits and asserts was not forbidden by Saint Paul, while at another he condemns it and represents it as adultery. EDD.

## CHAPTER I

I do not doubt, brother, that after sending your wife ahead in peace [Note: [Col. 0913C] Rigault and Renet read “into peace.”], you have turned to composing your mind and are thinking [Col. 0914C] about the outcome of your solitary state [Note: [Col. 0914D] About the outcome of your solitary state—that is, about what will happen to you, now that you live alone and have survived your wife. RIG.], and therefore certainly need counsel [Note: [Col. 0913C] Rhenanus and Semler read “to digest counsel”; Franeker and Paris, “to direct counsel.”].

Although in a matter of this kind everyone ought to confer with his own faith and take account of its strength, nevertheless, because in this particular case the necessity of the flesh prompts [Note: [Col. 0913C] Junius reads “admonishes.”] reflection and, in nearly every instance [Note: [Col. 0913C] Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür, and Pamelius read “to seek.”], resists faith within the same conscience [Note: [Col. 0914C] Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür, and Pamelius add “which.”], faith needs counsel from outside, as though it were an advocate [Note: [Col. 0914C] Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, and Oberthür read “counsel as an advocate.”] against the necessities [Note: [Col. 0914C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “necessity.”] of the flesh.

That necessity can indeed very easily [Note: [Col. 0914C] Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, and Oberthür omit “very easily.”] be circumscribed, if God’s will rather than his indulgence is considered. [Col. 0915A] No one gains merit by making use of indulgence, but by obeying the will. God’s will is our sanctification. For he wills that we, his image, also become his likeness [Note: [Col. 0915C] Junius reads “in the likeness.”], so that we may be holy just as he himself is holy.

He distributes this good—I mean sanctification—into several forms [Note: [Col. 0915C] Rigault and the Venetian edition read “I distribute.”], so that we may be found in one of them. The first form is virginity from birth. The second is virginity from the second birth, that is [Note: [Col. 0915C] “From the second birth, that is” is absent from Rhenanus, Semler, Pamelius, and Oberthür.], from the washing of baptism. This either purifies within marriage by mutual agreement or, by personal choice, perseveres in widowhood. The third rank remaining is monogamy [Note: [Col. 0915C] Rhenanus reads “of monogamy.”], when, after one marriage has been ended, [Note: [Col. 0915] Thereafter the sex is renounced. “Sex is devoured by the virgin,” says Jerome to Principia, epistle 3. RIG.] the sexual life is thereafter renounced.

The happiness of the first virginity [Note: [Col. 0915C] Junius reads “of the first virginity.” The Wouwer codex reads: “The first happiness belongs to the first virginity—the second belongs to virginity’s virtue—the remaining form, namely, of not marrying thereafter—besides virtue, is also the praise of modesty,” etc.] is not to know at all [Note: [Col. 0915C] “At all” is absent from Rhenanus.] that from which you will later desire to be freed. The second [Note: [Col. 0915C] Rhenanus and Semler read “The second belongs to virginity.”] is the achievement of virtue: to despise that whose power you know very well. The remaining form—namely, to refrain from marriage after a marriage has been dissolved by death—is, besides praise for virtue [Note: [Col. 0915C] Rhenanus and Semler read “virtues.”], also [Note: [Col. 0915C] Rigault reads “For it is the praise of modesty.”] the praise of modesty. For it is modesty not to desire what has been taken away—and taken away [Col. 0915B] by the Lord God, without whose will neither does a leaf fall from a tree nor a sparrow worth a single penny fall to the ground.

## CHAPTER II

How modest, then, is that declaration: “The Lord gave; the Lord has taken away” (Job 1:21); “As it seemed good to the Lord, so it was done.” Accordingly, if we restore marriages that have been taken away, we are undoubtedly striving against God’s will, desiring to possess again what he did not wish us to possess. For if he had wished it, he would not have taken it away—unless we also interpret this as God’s will, as though he wished us again to possess what he had already ceased to wish [Note: [Col. 0915C] Others read “as though he wished it in us again”; the Wouwer codex, “in us, which God had already ceased to wish.”].

It does not belong to good and solid faith to refer everything to God’s will in this way, and for each person thus to flatter himself by saying that nothing happens without his nod [Note: [Col. 0915D] Others read “command.”], so that we fail to understand that something also lies within ourselves [Col. 0915C]. Otherwise every sin will be excused if we maintain [Note: [Col. 0915D] Rhenanus and Semler read “if we continue.”] that nothing occurs within us apart from God’s will; and this definition [Note: [Col. 0915D] Rhenanus and Semler read: “But there the definition destroys it”; the Wouwer codex has an uncertain reading, “but for you the definition.”] will proceed to the destruction of the whole system of discipline, and even of God himself, if he either brings forth from his own will those things which he does not will [Note: [Col. 0915D] Rigault and the Venetian edition read “may bring forth.”], or if there is nothing that God does not will.

But how [Note: [Col. 0915D] Rigault and the Venetian edition read “when.”] does he forbid certain things and [Col. 0916A] even threaten eternal punishment for them? Certainly [Note: [Col. 0916C] Semler and Leopold read “certainly also.”] he does not will the things he forbids, by which he is also offended; just as he commands and accepts the things he wills [Note: [Col. 0916C] Rigault, Paris, and Venice read: “Just as, conversely, he both commands and makes acceptable,” etc.], and settles the account with the reward of eternity.

Therefore, since from his precepts we have learned both what he wills and what he does not will, the will and choice [Note: [Col. 0916C] Pamelius omits “and choice.”] to choose one or the other nevertheless belong to us, as it is written: “Behold, I have set before you good and evil” (Ecclus. 15:18), for you have tasted from the tree of knowledge. Accordingly, we ought not to refer to God’s will what has been placed at the disposal of our own [Note: [Col. 0916C] Rhenanus and Semler read “of ourselves.”] choice [Note: [Col. 0916C] Rhenanus and Semler read “within God’s will.”], since he himself wishes us also to will what he wishes [Note: [Col. 0916C] Semler reads: “We, whom he himself wills, must also will.”]—he who does not will evil. Thus the will is ours when we will evil against the will of God, who wills the good.

Moreover, if you ask whence comes that will by which we will something contrary to God’s will, I shall say [Note: [Col. 0916C] Rhenanus and Semler read: “God’s will, so to speak.”]: from ourselves. And not without reason, for you must necessarily correspond to your own seed. Indeed, that founder both of the race and of sin, [Col. 0916B] Adam, willed the thing by which he sinned. For the devil did not impose upon him [Note: [Col. 0916C] Rhenanus and Semler read “his.”] the will to sin; he merely supplied material to his will. God’s will, on the other hand, had come to him in the form of a command to obey [Note: [Col. 0916C] The Wouwer codex and Leopold read “obedience.”].

Likewise, if you do not obey God, who established you with free power by setting a command before you, then by the freedom of your will you willingly incline toward that which God [Note: [Col. 0916C] Rhenanus and Semler read: “willingly allows you to incline toward what God”; Franeker, Paris, and Pamelius: “willingly you incline toward that which.”] does not will; and yet you suppose yourself to have been overthrown by the devil. Although he wills you to will something that God does not will, he does not thereby make you will it. For not even then did he compel those first-formed human beings [Note: [Col. 0916C] Semler reads “unwilling.”] to the will to sin. Indeed, they were neither unwilling nor ignorant of what God did not will; for clearly God did not wish it to happen, since he appointed death as the penalty once it was committed.

Thus the devil’s single work is to test [Note: [Col. 0916C] Rhenanus and Semler read “to moderate.”] what is within you—whether you are willing. But once you have willed, it follows that he subjects you to himself [Note: [Col. 0916C] Franeker, Venice, and Rigault read “that he also subordinates you to himself.”], not because he produced the will within you, but because he obtained an opportunity [Note: [Col. 0916D] Rhenanus and Semler read “possession.”] from your will.
