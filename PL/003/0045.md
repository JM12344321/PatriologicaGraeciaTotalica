When Octavius had finished speaking, for some time we remained stunned into silence, our faces fixed in attention; and, as for me, I fainted away from the greatness of my admiration, because he had embellished with arguments, examples, and the authority of his reading matters that are easier to feel than to express; because he had beaten back the malevolent with those very missiles of the philosophers with which they arm themselves; and because he had also shown that the truth was not merely easy, but attractive as well.

## CHAPTER XL

[Note: [Col. 0358D]

CHAP. XL.—“Caecilius burst out.” Without awaiting the arbiter’s judgment, he spoke unexpectedly; stirred by the disposition of his newly transformed mind, he could contain himself no longer. See chap. 16. CELLARIUS.—The Roman edition has “thus burst out.” This addition is rightly rejected; above also, in chap. 16, the simple phrase “burst out” is used. HEUMANN.

“Very greatly indeed” (*plurimum quantum*). After these words the following are added in the Roman edition [Col. 0359B]: “with the same tranquility with which we live.” They are not found in the manuscript, but were transferred here through a printer’s error from chap. 38, where these same words occur. Heraldus therefore already ordered them deleted. HEUMANN.—Heumann himself is mistaken, for we read these four words in the royal manuscript. EDITORS.

“And so, as I shamelessly appropriate the victory.” The common editions read: “and so, as a shameless man” (*et ita, ut improbus*). We, however, have retained the manuscript reading. Petrus Scriverius emended it: “We have won—hurrah!—as I shamelessly appropriate the victory.” Jacobus Gronovius ingeniously proposed: “We have won, and thus not shamelessly”; I would readily assent to that emendation, were it not that the word “thus” seems entirely superfluous. I prefer: “We have won, and I do not shamelessly appropriate the victory.” DAVIS.—Heumann, *Dissertationum Sylloge*, vol. I, part II, p. 483: “We have won. And so, not shamelessly, I appropriate the victory.” GALLAND.—“Not shamelessly.” In the sole manuscript it is evident that *ita ut* was written because the copyist overlooked the repetition of the first vowel, when he ought to have restored *ita aut*; or, as I think, in the exemplar he used there was written something which, when another person dictated and pronounced it too softly, he set down in place of *ita haut*. For I think it evident that Minucius wrote: “We have won, and thus not shamelessly do I appropriate the victory”; for he has in view the Greek expression κοινὸν ἑρμῆν, “a shared windfall,” and brings this gain into common ownership. GRONOVIUS.—It must certainly be read thus: “We have won. And so, not shamelessly, I appropriate the victory.” HEUMANN.

“Insofar as it may concern” (*quod pertineat*). I write “insofar as it concerns” (*quod pertinet*). HEUMANN.

“I yield to God.” Others want “I believe in God”; but we have retained the reading of the exemplar, which is correct. Our author above, chap. 38: “lest anyone suppose that we yield to the demons to whom a libation has been made”; there too Joannes Meursius wrongly emends “yield” to “believe.” Prudentius, *Peristephanon*, Hymn X, 960:

> Yield now to the miracles that have been proved.

Again, *Against Symmachus*, Book II, verse 3:

> We have spoken, and now Rome yields to our Christ.

Likewise Arnobius, Book VI, p. 197, said “to believe the truth that has been confessed.” DAVIS.

“Settle down together” (*consubsidunt*). The word is not Latin and was undeservedly inserted [Col. 0359D] into Faber’s lexicon. I reject the prefix *con-* and take the following *non* to have arisen from it; the copyist, who had written it prematurely, was unwilling to delete it. [Col. 0360B] “Settle down” (*subsidunt*), however, gives excellent sense. For in a vessel that part of a liquid which is turbid, and consequently somewhat heavier, is said to settle. “Some things settle down,” he says—that is, they still reside and remain at the bottom of his breast. Caecilius is therefore saying that certain questions for deliberation remain in his mind. They do not have such force as to compel him to doubt the truth of the Christian religion, but he nevertheless wishes them removed so that his knowledge of the truth may become more perfect. HEUMANN.

“Concerning the whole” (*ut de toto*). I delete *ut* and emend the text to “throughout the whole day” (*die toto*); and I now see with some pleasure that the anonymous Parisian also made this emendation. HEUMANN.—“Concerning the whole,” that is, concerning a complete subject supplying material for a proper disputation. *Ut* is not a conjunction but an adverb of comparison. LINDNER.

“More readily” (*promptius*). Delete this word as a variant reading admitted into the text from the margin by an ignorant copyist. HEUMANN.]

[Col. 0358B] ARGUMENT.—Caecilius, however, exclaimed that he had been defeated by Octavius, that he was now the conqueror of error, and that he henceforth professed the Christian religion; fuller instruction in the mysteries of the faith was meanwhile postponed until the following day.

While, therefore, I was silently turning these matters over within myself, Caecilius burst out: “I congratulate my Octavius very greatly indeed—with the same tranquility with which we live—but I congratulate myself as well, [Col. 0359A] and I do not await the judgment. We have won, and so I shamelessly appropriate the victory; for just as he is the conqueror of me, so I am the triumphant conqueror of error. 59 Accordingly, as regards the principal question, I both acknowledge providence, yield to God, and assent concerning the sincerity of the sect that is now ours. Nevertheless, some matters still settle together in my mind—not matters that contradict the truth, but matters necessary for complete instruction. Concerning these we shall inquire tomorrow, since the sun is now sinking toward its setting, when it will be more fitting and more convenient to deal with the whole subject.”

## CHAPTER XLI

[Note: [Col. 0360B]

CHAP. XLI.—“On behalf of all of us” (*omnium nostrum vice*). It should be emended to *vicem*. Curtius VII.2.5: “Sorrowful not for his own lot, but [Col. 0360C] for that of his brothers”; and in chap. 11.20 of the same book: “anxious for their lot.” The same author, X.5.21, concerning Queen Sisygambis: “She mourned her own lot, she the lot of her granddaughters.” Gronovius the elder, in his notes on Livy, Book I, chaps. 9 and 25, will also teach you that Livy often speaks thus, and that in Livy too ignorant copyists changed *vicem* to *in vice*. HEUMANN.

“And inspired.” The “and” may conveniently be absent. LINDNER.

“Octavius rejoiced because he had conquered.” In some books this is inserted in the following form: “Octavius rejoiced because he had conquered.” But even if “began” is understood, it will nevertheless repeat what was already expressed in “glad and cheerful.” CELLARIUS.—“Octavius rejoiced.” Either the final word must be deleted altogether with Fulvius Ursinus, or, by transposing the words, the text must be read: “Caecilius rejoiced because he had believed; Octavius because he had conquered.” Those more skilled in the refinements of Latin will readily admit this. DAVIS.

“And because this man.” Only the briefest attention is needed to see [Col. 0360D] that the text should very clearly be written: “Because this man too had believed” (*quod et hic crediderit*). HEUMANN.]

ARGUMENT.—At last they all depart glad and cheerful: Caecilius because he had believed; Octavius because [Col. 0360A] he had conquered; and Minucius because the former had believed and the latter had conquered.

“But I,” I said, “rejoice more fully on behalf of all of us, because Octavius has conquered for me as well, since the very great unpopularity involved in passing judgment has been removed from me. Yet I cannot repay his merit with praises expressed in words. The testimony of a man—and of one man alone—is weak. He possesses the extraordinary gift of God, by whom he was both inspired when he spoke and assisted when he prevailed.”

After this we departed glad and cheerful: Caecilius because he had believed; Octavius, rejoicing because he had conquered; and I because the one had believed and the other had conquered.

# INDEX OF THE CHAPTERS OF THE DIALOGUE ENTITLED *OCTAVIUS*, BY MARCUS MINUCIUS FELIX

[Col. 0359]

## ARGUMENT

In this dialogue Caecilius is introduced as a man devoted to pagan superstitions. By various arguments he attempts to prove that no one should abandon the ancestral worship of the gods inherited from his forefathers; that no one should anxiously inquire into religion, but that the ancient religion should be retained and the new rejected. Octavius, by contrast, had rejected that impious worship and, together with Minucius Felix, had enrolled himself in the Christian profession. He so completely overthrows all Caecilius’ arguments, and demonstrates the truth of the Christian religion with such weighty reasoning, that Caecilius, broken and completely convinced by the most serious disputation, embraces the true religion with a glad and grateful mind. The author first sets forth Caecilius’ speech in defense of the worship of the gods. He then introduces Octavius’ response to every part of Caecilius’ address.

[Col. 0359D] **CHAP. I.** At the very beginning of the dialogue Minucius relates how delightfully he was entertained by the recollection of the things that had happened to him with Octavius [Col. 0360D] while the latter was staying with him at Rome, and especially by the memory [Col. 0361A] of this disputation. col. 232

**CHAP. II.** Octavius’ arrival at Rome during the holidays of the courts is most welcome to Minucius. The two decide to go to the seaside baths at Ostia, accompanied by Caecilius, Minucius’ companion. As they proceed together toward the sea, Caecilius sees an image of Serapis, puts his hand to his mouth, and venerates it. 235

**CHAP. III.** Indignant at the superstitious man’s action, Octavius sharply reproaches Minucius, declaring that the disgrace of this impious deed redounds no less upon Minucius himself, as Caecilius’ host, than upon Caecilius. 237

**CHAP. IV.** Caecilius, somewhat saddened by this rebuke, in which Octavius censured Minucius [Col. 0361B] on his account, asks permission to debate with Octavius concerning the truth of his religion. Octavius agrees with his companion, and Minucius sits between Caecilius and Octavius. 240

**CHAP. V.** Caecilius begins his disputation. First, he asserts that everything in human affairs is doubtful and uncertain, and therefore that it is regrettable that Christians, generally uneducated and illiterate, dare to make any certain determination concerning the sum of things and the divine majesty. From this he argues that no providence governs the world and concludes that it is better to adhere to the ancient religions handed down by tradition. 242

[Col. 0361C] **CHAP. VI.** Every nation, and afterward the Romans, so worshiped its own divinities that through their worship they attained supreme dominion over the whole world. 250

**CHAP. VII.** When Roman auspices and auguries were omitted, the omission brought regret; when observed, they brought success. 253

**CHAP. VIII.** Intolerable is the impious audacity of Theodorus, Diagoras, and Protagoras, who wished either to abolish the religion of the gods entirely or at least to weaken it. Yet even less tolerable is the secretive race of Christians, which shuns the light and despises the gods; while they fear death after death, they meanwhile do not fear to die. 256

[Col. 0361D] **CHAP. IX.** The Christians’ religion is foolish: for they venerate a crucified man and the very instrument of his punishment. They are said to worship an ass’s head and adore the very nature of their father. They are initiated by the slaughter and blood of an infant, and in shameless darkness they all mingle indiscriminately as chance determines. 260

**CHAP. X.** Whatever they worship, they strive diligently to conceal. They have no altars, no temples, and no recognized images. Their God, like that of the Jews, is said to be one; although they can neither see nor display him, they nevertheless consider him troublesome, restless, and perversely inquisitive. 263

[Col. 0362A] **CHAP. XI.** They also assert that the whole world will one day be consumed by fire; and after the resurrection of our bodies they promise the righteous an eternity of most blessed life, but the unrighteous an eternity of the greatest punishments. 266

**CHAP. XII.** What will happen to the Christians themselves after death may be inferred from the fact that they are now bereft of every resource and oppressed by the greatest calamities and miseries. 271

**CHAP. XIII.** Caecilius finally concludes that the new religion must be rejected and that one must not pronounce rashly upon doubtful matters. 273

**CHAP. XIV.** With the disdain of a haughty spirit, Caecilius challenges Octavius to answer his arguments. [Col. 0362B] Minucius modestly replies that Caecilius should not exult in his own considerable eloquence or in the harmonious variety of his speech; rather, each of his arguments must be weighed carefully. 275

**CHAP. XV.** Caecilius responds to Minucius, not without some indication that his feelings have been wounded, saying that Minucius has departed from the duty of an impartial judge by weakening the force of his disputation. Octavius is entirely free to refute everything that he had brought forward for consideration. 278

**CHAP. XVI.** Octavius therefore begins his reply and expresses confidence that he will wash away the most bitter stain of their reproaches with a flood of truthful words. He then proceeds to weaken each of Caecilius’ arguments. [Col. 0362C] First, therefore, he establishes that no one should resent the fact that Christians, however unlearned, discuss heavenly matters; for it is not the authority of the disputant but the truth of the disputation itself that must be considered. 279

**CHAP. XVII.** He admits that a person ought to know himself, but utterly denies that this knowledge can be acquired unless he first recognizes the universe and God himself. From the structure and adornment of the world itself, everyone endowed with reason knows for certain that it was created by God and is ruled and administered by him. 284

[Col. 0362D] **CHAP. XVIII.** God exercises care not only for the whole world, but also for each of its parts. That all things are governed by the will of one God is proved by the example of earthly empires. Although he is infinite and immeasurable and, in the fullness of his being, known to himself alone, and although he can neither be seen nor named by us, his radiance is nevertheless perceived if supplementary additions to his name are removed. 287

**CHAP. XIX.** Moreover, the poets called him the father of gods and human beings, the creator of all things, mind, and spirit. Indeed, even the more eminent philosophers themselves held almost the same views as the Christians concerning the one God. 292

[Col. 0363A] **CHAP. XX.** But if the world is ruled by Providence and governed by the will of one God, ignorant antiquity must not carry us away into the error of mutual agreement—an antiquity which, delighted with its fables, introduced ridiculous traditions. It is demonstrated no less clearly that the worship of the gods was always foolish and impious, since the earliest mortals venerated their kings, renowned leaders, and inventors of the arts for their illustrious deeds just as if they were gods. 297

**CHAP. XXI.** Octavius confirms this by the testimony of Euhemerus, Prodicus, [Col. 0363B] Persaeus, and Alexander the Great, who enumerate the homelands, birthdays, and tombs of the gods. He further describes the gods’ miserable ends, deaths, and funerals. In addition, he ridicules the absurdities and foul nonsense that pagans retail concerning the form and appearance of their gods. 299

**CHAP. XXII.** These fables, first handed down by ignorant people, were afterward celebrated by others, especially by the poets, whose authority did considerable harm to the truth. Children’s minds are corrupted by such inventions and seductive falsehoods, and so the poor creatures grow old in them, although the truth is readily accessible—but only to those who seek it. 306

[Col. 0363C] **CHAP. XXIII.** Although pagans acknowledge their kings to be mortal, they nevertheless fashion them into gods, even against their will—not to establish belief in their divinity, but to honor their merits in posterity. Yet the true God has neither rising nor setting. Octavius then assails the images and statues of the gods. 309

**CHAP. XXIV.** By a brief survey he demonstrates how ridiculous, obscene, and cruel the rites observed in celebrating the mysteries of certain gods were. 312

**CHAP. XXV.** He then shows that Caecilius is wrong to boast that the Romans obtained dominion over the whole world [Col. 0363D] by duly observing superstitions of this sort. In fact, at their origin the Romans were assembled through crime and grew through the terror of their savagery. The Romans were therefore great not because they were religious, but because they committed sacrilege with impunity. 315

**CHAP. XXVI.** Octavius turns against Caecilius the weakly hurled missile drawn from the auspices and auguries of birds, using the examples of Regulus, Mancinus, Paulus, and Caesar. By other examples he demonstrates that the argument drawn from oracles is no more valid. 320

**CHAP. XXVII.** To establish this securely, he takes the matter up from further back, from its first origin. [Col. 0364A] This, indeed, is the source of the error: demons hide beneath statues and images, dwell in shrines, animate the fibers of entrails, govern the flight of birds, direct the lots, and pour forth oracles wrapped in false responses. Nevertheless, when adjured by Christians through the true God and driven from the bodies they possess, they are compelled to confess that these things were performed not by God, but by themselves. Hence they flee from Christians whenever they are near; and they stir up such hatred of Christians among the pagans that the latter begin to hate them before knowing them, lest, once they know them, they should either be able to imitate them or be unable to condemn them. 323

**CHAP. XXVIII.** They not only inflict hatred upon Christians, but also charge [Col. 0364B] them with unspeakable crimes, which no one has yet been able to prove. Such is the work of demons, for by them false rumor is both sown and fostered. Octavius demonstrates with the utmost force that Christians are as falsely accused of sacrilege, incest, fornication, and parricide as it is certain and true that precisely those crimes—or ones altogether similar and even greater—are actually committed by the pagans themselves. 327

**CHAP. XXIX.** Nor is it any more true that Christians worship a man affixed to a cross for his crimes. For they rightly believe him to be not merely innocent but also God. [Col. 0364C] Pagans, by contrast, invoke the divine powers of kings whom they themselves have enrolled among the gods, make supplication before their images, and implore their guardian spirits. 331

**CHAP. XXX.** Octavius also proves utterly shameless the slander that Christians drink the blood of an infant whom they have killed. Pagans, he says, both cruelly expose newborn children and kill them by cruel abortion before they are born. Christians are not permitted even to witness or hear of homicide. 333

**CHAP. XXXI.** The polluted incestuous banquet alleged against Christians is as remote from every probability as it is certain [Col. 0364D] that pagans are in fact guilty of incest. Christian banquets are not merely chaste, but sober as well. So far removed, indeed, are Christians from a desire for incest that for some even a chaste union is a cause for modest embarrassment. 335

**CHAP. XXXII.** Nor can it be said that Christians conceal what they worship merely because they have no temples or altars. For they are persuaded that God cannot be confined within any temple, nor can an image of him be fashioned. Present everywhere, he sees everything, even the more secret thoughts of our mind; we live near him [Col. 0365A] and in his bosom. 338

**CHAP. XXXIII.** But if it is nevertheless said that God was of no benefit to the Jews, the writers of Jewish annals are certainly most substantial witnesses that the Jews first abandoned God before they themselves were abandoned by him. 342

**CHAP. XXXIV.** There is nothing surprising if this world is ultimately to be consumed by fire, for all things that have a beginning also have an end. Nor do the ancient philosophers reject the doctrine of a conflagration of the world. It is nevertheless certain that God can raise from death to life the human being whom he fashioned from nothing. All nature, moreover, rehearses the resurrection that is to come. 344

[Col. 0365B] **CHAP. XXXV.** Octavius then shows that righteous and devout people are to be granted everlasting happiness, but the unrighteous are to be afflicted with eternal punishments. He then clearly demonstrates that the morals of Christians are far holier than those of pagans. 348

**CHAP. XXXVI.** No less clearly, he teaches that fate is nothing other than what God has spoken. The mind is free, and therefore a person’s conduct, not his birth, is judged. He next makes perfectly clear that the poverty for which Christians are reproached is not a disgrace but a glory, and that their suffering bodily evils is not punishment but military service. 350

[Col. 0365C] **CHAP. XXXVII.** Torments most unjustly inflicted for the confession of Christ are spectacles worthy of God. Octavius clearly demonstrates this by comparing certain exceptionally brave pagans with the holy martyrs. [Col. 0366A] He further declares that Christians do not attend public shows and processions because they know with absolute certainty that these are no less impious than cruel. 352

**CHAP. XXXVIII.** They likewise abstain from food sacrificed to idols, lest anyone suppose that they either yield to demons or are ashamed of their religion. Yet they do not reject every color and fragrance of flowers. They customarily use flowers scattered, loose, and soft, and encircle their necks with garlands; but they consider it superfluous and useless to crown the head of the dead. They bury their dead with the same tranquility with which they live, awaiting with absolutely certain hope the crown of eternal happiness. Their true religion, therefore, after the superstitions of the pagans have been rejected, must be adopted by everyone. 355

**CHAP. XXXIX.** After Octavius had thus concluded his speech, Minucius and Caecilius remained for some time stunned into silence, their faces fixed in attention. Minucius, indeed, carried away in admiration of Octavius, remained silent, wordlessly turning over within himself what he had heard. 357

**CHAP. XL.** Caecilius, however, exclaimed that he had been defeated by Octavius, that he was now the conqueror of error, and that he henceforth professed the Christian religion; fuller instruction in the mysteries of the faith was meanwhile postponed until the following day. 358

[Col. 0366C] **CHAP. XLI.** At last they all depart glad and cheerful: Caecilius because he had believed; Octavius because he had conquered; and Minucius because the former had believed and the latter had conquered. 359

# LETTER OF LUCAS HOLSTENIUS ON THE SPITS OF DIANA OF EPHESUS, IN CONNECTION WITH MINUCIUS XXI

[Note: [Col. 0365D] Above, chap. 21, col. 305.]

[Col. 0365D] Lucas Holstenius wishes prosperity to Francesco Cardinal Barberini, the finest patron of learning.

Since, by a singular act of generosity, you ordered the learned little work of Claude Menestrier—formerly a member of your household and a most skillful investigator of antiquarian matters—to be brought before the public, a work in which he lucidly explained the statues of Diana of Ephesus inscribed with recondite enigmas, [Col. 0366D] I thought it would not be unwelcome to you if I took the same opportunity to submit to you a thought that occurred to me long ago concerning ancient coins of that same Diana. I have done this all the more willingly and, I hope, with all the more justification because it embraces a subject left untouched by Menestrier and so far unnoticed by any antiquarian. I submit this writing to your judgment because no one understands more correctly than you the manifold [Col. 0367A] service that an exact knowledge of antiquity renders in adorning and enlarging every other branch of study. For since you rightly judged that nothing lies so deeply hidden or obscure in the writings of the ancients that it cannot borrow some light from ancient monuments, that was your reason for adding to the most celebrated library you assembled an abundant collection of ancient tablets, statues, coins, and inscriptions, so that you might place the whole memorial record of learned antiquity before the eyes of scholars. Now learn the matter itself.

Ancient coins stamped with the image of Diana of Ephesus, some of which are shown on the accompanying plate, differ from the statues produced through Menestrier’s industry in this respect: they support the goddess, burdened by the weight of her breasts, on either side with certain props or supports that one might take for knotted staffs. [Col. 0367B] All the examples I have seen—and I have seen very many—agree so consistently on this point that I think it must be affirmed beyond doubt that the Ephesian image was once supported in the same manner. Nor does a passage of Minucius Felix, a most learned writer, permit us to doubt the matter; there he mentions the image of Diana of Ephesus as built up with many breasts and iron spits (*verubus*). But the acumen of critics has thus far exercised itself wonderfully in corrupting that passage—so much so that, unless a defense is opportunely supplied from ancient books and monuments, it will hereafter be rendered inadmissible as testimony.

I shall explain the matter more clearly, so that it may be thoroughly perceived how unhappily Felix has been received and treated by his editors. The first Roman edition, prepared by Faustus Sabaeus [Col. 0367C] according to the Vatican manuscript, presents the passage I have mentioned as follows:

> Diana, meanwhile, as huntress, is girt up high; the Ephesian Diana is built up with many breasts and spits; and Trivia is rendered terrible with three heads and many hands.

Thus the learned writer accurately distinguished, by their peculiar marks and emblems, a threefold divinity of the single Diana, in accordance with the fabulous theology of the pagans. The Basel edition and the Heidelberg edition of Franciscus Balduinus scrupulously preserved the reading of the first edition, as did the later Roman edition, which appeared according to the recension of Fulvius Ursinus—except that this extremely precise scholar appended his own conjecture in the margin, advising that the text should read not “spits” (*verubus*) but “udders” (*uberibus*). The two editions of Desiderius Heraldus, an advocate of Paris, then reproduced the Roman reading; but one of them, changing a single letter, presents *veribus* in place of *verubus*, following, as he notes, the ancient manuscript itself [Col. 0367D] which, through the munificence of Leo X, passed from the Vatican Library into the royal library. That unique manuscript had been sent to King Francis together with a printed copy that the Roman editors had dedicated to him. This slight change induced Heraldus to judge that the reading should be either “udders” (*uberibus*), with Ursinus, or “swellings” (*tuberibus*), with Joseph Scaliger; he nevertheless inclined more toward the former conjecture.

These men, indeed, indulged in conjecture only so far as to keep their hands off Minucius’ text itself. But the others afterward were not equally scrupulous. For Joannes Wowerius, my fellow citizen, otherwise excellently furnished with learning and judgment, embraced Ursinus’ conjecture so completely that [Col. 0368A] he did not hesitate to admit it into Minucius’ text as unquestionably genuine. Rigaltius at Paris subsequently followed his example, as have, most recently, the Dutch editors in a repeated edition. The authority of Justus Lipsius seems to have lent no small support to this confidence, since they knew that he had approved this correction in his notes on Tacitus, *Annals*, Book III. All these men followed exactly in Ursinus’ footsteps. Geverhart Elmenhorst, however, a man certainly not lacking in diligence, took a different path. Not daring to change the Roman reading, he wretchedly dislocated the passage by transposing the words in the larger Hamburg edition, where Minucius’ words are read in this deformed state:

> Diana, meanwhile, as huntress, is girt up high and built up with spits; the Ephesian Diana has many breasts; and Trivia has three heads, etc.

Yet these things are comparatively slight beside what Petrus Faber, a celebrated authority on law, had already devised concerning [Col. 0368B] the same passage of Minucius in Book III, chap. 3, of his *Semestria*. Sharply assailing St. Jerome for distinguishing the many-breasted Diana of Ephesus from the huntress who holds a bow and is girt up high, he attempts, with great but fruitless effort, to prove that the ancients knew a many-breasted Ceres but were ignorant of a many-breasted Diana. Therefore, lest Minucius’ words support Jerome’s case, he distorts, corrects, and interpolates them as follows:

> Diana, meanwhile, is the Ephesian huntress girt up high, and Ceres is built up with many breasts.

But this is not, as he proclaims of himself, to correct a corrupted passage, but to corrupt one that was sound. So great a man would surely have refrained from this unfortunate controversy if he had, even through a lattice, [Col. 0368C] inspected an ancient image of Diana of Ephesus or her coins.

To the foregoing I shall add a man now celebrated in France, the king of this antiquarian court, Jean Tristan. In volume I he presents a coin of the ΚΑΔΟΗΝΩΝ, “of the Cadueni” (for I shall show that they are so named by Stephanus, Pliny, and himself elsewhere), stamped with the image of Diana of Ephesus; and, in illustrating it, he adduces Minucius’ much-abused passage, intending to make his own trial of ingenuity upon it. He therefore rejects “spits” (*verubus*), which he considers not a genuine word of Minucius but an invention of Wowerius—although Wowerius’ actual view was entirely different—and judges that the reading should indeed be “udders” (*uberibus*), but in a sense far removed from that of all the others. For he had very correctly perceived that grammarians establish either no distinction at all, or only a thoroughly foolish one, between breasts (*mammae*) and udders (*ubera*), and that this tautology [Col. 0368D] could therefore scarcely be attributed without fault to a writer of exact and concise diction. He accordingly proposed, with considerable ingenuity, that the many, milk-rich breasts of Diana of Ephesus should be explained as breasts swollen and distended with much milk. By this interpretation he plainly destroys the corruption introduced by Ursinus and all the critics who subsequently followed in his footsteps. Yet he did not attain Minucius’ meaning, because he rashly put his trust in the recent and interpolated edition that he appears to have used.

But all these men had their eyes and minds clouded as if by glaucoma when dealing with this perfectly clear passage of Minucius, because they had not investigated the precise meaning in which *verua* were properly named and understood by the ancient authorities on the Latin language. For since the roasting-spits—or “transfixing implements,” as Papias calls them—used in the kitchen, [Col. 0369A] and rightly ridiculed by Tristan in the French translation of Minucius, seemed to contribute nothing at all to the construction of Diana of Ephesus, they all agreed—astonishingly—that Minucius’ *verua* could only be understood and explained as the darts or javelins of Diana the huntress. Hence so many supreme and incomparable men conspired in rivalry to interpolate that word. Against this united host, however, I affirm that these *verua* are free from corruption, and I insist that they must be taken away neither from Diana of Ephesus nor from Minucius. For the *verua* with which the Ephesians constructed their Diana are not spears or javelins, but elongated iron supports which, placed beneath her arms, sustained the entire mass of her breast-laden chest.

[Col. 0369B] Since this extremely ancient image, in the manner of Egyptian statues, had its feet tightly pressed together, its very small base was unequal to bearing the weight of the body placed upon it and had to be reinforced with supports. If those were removed, the whole mass would necessarily weaken and collapse. Because those props or supports were extended at some length in iron, Minucius called them *verua*, using an exact and elegant word and following the usage not of the later age in which he lived, but of the Augustan age. For a Latin glossary of the highest quality, extant in three extremely ancient Vatican manuscripts, explains *verua* as iron rods and little rods.

Experience itself taught me that this explanation is true and genuine; and I think that even now you remember that experience, Most Eminent Cardinal, because you once witnessed it together with me. It was [Col. 0369C] fifteen years ago, unless I am mistaken, when the Society of Jesus at St. Andrew’s on the Quirinal, while laying the foundations of a new building, encountered several very large stones of Tiburtine limestone arranged in a square. Two of them were inscribed as follows, as I copied the inscription from the rough and poorly polished stone at your command and with your assistance:

> THIS AREA, ENCLOSED WITHIN THIS BOUNDARY OF TERMINAL STONES BY IRON RODS, TOGETHER WITH THE ALTAR BELOW, WAS DEDICATED BY THE EMPEROR CAESAR DOMITIAN AUGUSTUS GERMANICUS, IN FULFILLMENT OF A VOW THAT HAD BEEN UNDERTAKEN BUT HAD LONG BEEN NEGLECTED AND NOT DISCHARGED, FOR THE PURPOSE OF PREVENTING FIRES.

[Col. 0369D] Those boundary stones preserved two holes on either side and traces of the *verua*, or iron rods, surrounded with lead. By these rods they had formerly been joined to one another and had enclosed the inner area so that access was not generally open. I then perceived from the physical objects themselves how remarkably mistaken was N. Rigaltius, a man otherwise of the keenest discernment, when, in his notes on the writers *On the Demarcation of Boundaries*, he explained these *verua* as stakes sharpened to points like awls; I had learned with my own eyes and hands that they possessed no sharpness or point. In the same ancient sense, Marcellus, an early medical author, prescribes the following in chap. 10 for stopping a flow of blood from the nostrils:

> Continually quench a glowing iron *veru* in vinegar, and draw its smoke into the nostrils.

[Col. 0370A] Who does not see that what is meant here, simply and without qualification, is a mass of iron drawn out into a long rod—the form in which nearly all rough and unworked iron commonly comes?

Yet nothing more suitable for illuminating Minucius’ meaning and the matter at issue can be adduced than the verses of Prudentius from the first poem *Against Symmachus*. In them he describes the statues of the twin brothers Castor and Pollux in the bearing and posture in which they could then be seen in their temple beside the Sacred Way. These are the verses:

> The twin brothers too,  
> Bastard sons of a corrupted mother, offspring of Leda,  
> Riders of the night and two divinities of lofty Rome,  
> Loom overhead, held back by an iron rod, and fix in place  
> With poured lead the footprints that announce their mighty triumph.

He graphically depicts the Dioscuri represented in the posture of men running, [Col. 0370B] projecting beyond the perpendicular line and the base, so that they appeared to loom over those who saw them, not without horror and fear. For objects “loom overhead” when they hang above one’s head and seem on the very point of falling, as Valla rightly says. Fear and wonder, moreover, produced an impression of religious awe among the rude and superstitious populace. The Christian poet mocks that fear, since there was no danger that the statues would fall or run away: a *veru*—that is, an iron bar or hook fixed into their backs—held them in place, and their feet, lead-fastened to their bases, could not move.
