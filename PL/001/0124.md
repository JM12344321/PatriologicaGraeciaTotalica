Similarly, [Note: [Col. 1182A] “Similarly,” Semler.] [Note: [Col. 1181C] “Similarly also on station days.” It has been stated in *On Fasting*, ch. 10, n. 91, that a “station” was an assembly of Christians held on certain days for the purpose of prayer, and that Catholics prolonged these stations until the ninth hour, Montanists until nightfall. G. PANCIROLI.—“On station days.” What stations were has been very fully explained by others; I shall therefore be content with a few remarks. Hermas, *The Shepherd*, book III, Similitude 4: “‘What,’ said the Shepherd, ‘is a station?’ And I replied: ‘A fast.’” In fact, by the term “stations” ecclesiastical writers meant nothing other than a fast observed by Christians, so that stations of every kind were sometimes called fasts. For when the faithful fasted, they customarily gathered in the sacred buildings at daybreak and remained there until the ninth hour, devoting themselves to prayer and the contemplation of divine things; hence the name “stations” later became attached to fasts. Some scholars nevertheless debate whether stations differed from fasts. [Col. 1181D] For my part, I think there was a certain distinction only when the term “strict station” was used. For then only the fasts of Wednesday and Friday were meant, and accordingly the stations continued until the ninth hour, as we have said, whereas fasts were prolonged until evening. It is uncertain, moreover, whether Tertullian here means fasts of every kind, or only those observed on Wednesdays and Fridays. I would more readily adopt the latter view. MURATORI.] also on station days, most people think that they should not attend the prayers of the sacrifices, [Note: [Col. 1181D] “The prayers of the sacrifices.” Tell us, heretic, what is meant by these words. What else would you have “the prayers of the sacrifice” be than the Mass, which you impiously condemn? From these words it should be noted that all who attended the Mass—*missa* in Latin is “sacrifice”—were accustomed to receive the Holy Eucharist; and because they departed immediately after receiving it, they therefore did not wish even to attend Mass on that day, lest they break the station. Tertullian reproves this. PANCIROLI.—“The prayers of the sacrifices.” [Col. 1182A] He does not say simply “prayers,” nor “sacrifices of prayers,” but “prayers of the sacrifices,” because here he is treating of fasts and the Eucharist, which sacrifices are especially celebrated with prayers. The prayers themselves, however, are sacrifices, as we have already noted regarding the words in the short work *To Scapula*: “We sacrifice, as God has commanded, with pure prayer.” Likewise in book III *Against Marcion*, on the passage from Malachi, “And in every place a sacrifice is offered to my name, and a pure sacrifice,” he says: “‘Namely, the rendering of glory, blessing and praise, and hymns.’” And on p. 502, “a pure sacrifice,” namely, simple prayer from a pure conscience; and on p. 512, where he treats of the figures of the sacrifices of the Mosaic law: [Col. 1182B] “They signified that a man, once a sinner but presently cleansed by the word of God, ought to offer a gift to God at the temple—namely, prayer and thanksgiving in the Church, through Jesus Christ, the universal priest of the Father.” In very many passages, moreover, he commends the sacrifices of bodily afflictions and fasts; and in the book *On Patience* he calls them propitiatory offerings to the Lord. It should also be noted that in *On Fasting* a fast is called a carnal sacrifice. RIGALTIUS.] because the station would have to be broken by receiving the body of the Lord. [Col. 1182A] [Note: [Col. 1182B] “Because the station would be broken,” etc. The Christian people never withdrew from a station without having received the body of the Lord; once communion in it had been completed, the prayers were concluded and the devout assemblies brought to an end. But certain pampered persons abstained from the sacred communion because they imagined that, by receiving the body of the Lord, they were breaking their fast, the observance of which was certainly a matter of the greatest care and religious scruple among the ancients. Tertullian here endeavors to show how greatly they were mistaken, demonstrating that the food of the sacred table does not spoil Christians’ fasts, but completes and consecrates them. [Col. 1182C] MURATORI.] Does the Eucharist, then, dissolve an act of devotion pledged to God? Or does it not rather bind it more firmly to God? Will not your station be more solemn if you have also stood at the altar of God? [Note: [Col. 1182C] “Will not your station be more solemn if you have also stood at the altar of God?” Christians, then, had altars at that time! Whence, therefore, came the pagans’ hatred of them and the charges against them as impious, on the ground that they had neither temples nor altars and did not sacrifice? “Do you suppose,” says Minucius, “that we conceal what we worship if we have no temples and altars?” Just as Malachi, ch. I, called it “the table of the Lord” where in the same passage he had called it an altar, so here Septimius calls the table of Christ an altar. From it, namely, the body of Christ was received, in bread and wine consecrated by holy prayers in memory of the Lord’s Passion. Thus Paul, in 1 Corinthians 10, after saying that the bread which we break is a participation and communion in the body of Christ, which God willed to be handed over and sacrificed for us, adds that those who eat the victims become participants in the altar. He then warns the Corinthians that if, while they are dining among pagans, something is set before them [Col. 1182D] which is said to have been sacrificed to idols, they must take no portion of it; for they cannot be participants in the Lord’s table and in the table of demons. This precept, by applying the word “table” to pagan sacrifices, signifies that among the faithful the table of Christ is equivalent to an altar; and that the Jews who understand according to the flesh become participants through their sacrifices only in the altar, *thysiastērion* (θυσιαστήριον), the victim, namely, being consumed by flames, whereas Christians become participants in the *thysia* (θυσία), the sacrifice itself, namely, the body of Christ. And here our Septimius says that the morsel received by a Christian is participation in the sacrifice. Indeed, the Christian himself is reckoned as the Lord’s altar, to be steeped in blood—but Christ’s blood. This same author of ours, when restraining the faithful from spectacles and the circus, says: “Do you also desire some blood? You have Christ’s.” Finally, Chrysostom too, addressing his hearers as though in Christ’s own words, says: “If you desire blood, do not redden the altar of idols with the slaughter of irrational animals, but redden my altar with my own blood” (*Ei haimatos epithymeis*, φησί, *mē ton tōn eidōlōn bōmon tō tōn alogōn phonō, alla to thysiastērion to emon tō emō phoinisse haimati*; Εἰ αἵματος ἐπιθυμεῖς, φησί, μὴ τὸν τῶν εἰδώλων βωμὸν τῷ τῶν ἀλόγων φόνῳ, ἀλλὰ τὸ θυσιαστήριον τὸ ἐμὸν τῷ ἐμῷ φοίνισσε αἵματι). RIGALTIUS.—“The station will be more solemn.” [Col. 1183B] “Station” is sometimes used for “fast,” since there was no station without a fast; stations, or frequent gatherings at church, were held on Fridays and Wednesdays, on which prescribed fasts were observed. The doubt raised by the Magdeburg Centuriators, however, is frivolous: they say that this should be understood of the Montanists’ fast, but although these station fasts were prolonged until evening, that avails those heretics nothing, for the Fathers do not number this practice among Montanus’s errors. LE PRIEUR.—“If you have stood at the altar of God.” Alluding to stations, he uses a term proper to sacred rites. An animal dedicated as a victim is said to “stand at the altar.” Virgil, *Georgics* II:

> And led by the horn, the sacred he-goat will stand at the altar.

“The he-goat . . . .” And Martial:

> Guilty of harming the vine, the rose-crowned he-goat stood at the altars, about to die  
> (*Epigrams* 24, book VII).

Hence also the verse:

> Gnaw the vine, he-goat; yet when you stand here at the altar,  
> [Col. 1183C] there will be something that can be sprinkled upon your horns.

RIGALTIUS.]

[Col. 1183A] Once the body of the Lord has been received and reserved, [Note: [Col. 1183C] “Once the body of the Lord has been received and reserved.” It is thoroughly well known to scholars that the ancient adherents of the Christian religion were sometimes accustomed to carry away with them the body of Christ received from the priest, so that they might consume it at home and during private prayers; this custom was afterward abolished by councils. Leaving that aside, therefore, other matters deserve consideration instead. From this passage of Tertullian, namely, the opinion of certain recent heretics is refuted, who think it unlawful to preserve the body of the Lord after the sacred rites have been completed. Yet that what is now done in the Catholic Church was also done in ancient times is most plainly proved not only by these words of Tertullian, but also by Cyprian and other very ancient writers; it would be superfluous to repeat the testimonies which others have adduced. Secondly, since Tertullian here mentions only the reception of the body, and since we understand that the one body was customarily carried home and reserved, we may learn that it was once sufficient for Christians to partake of Christ’s body; and that this practice, preserved by the Catholic Church and confirmed by countless other ancient documents, is wrongly condemned by recent heretics. [Col. 1183D] MURATORI.] both are preserved intact: participation in the sacrifice and fulfillment of the duty. If “station” derives its name from the military model—for we too are God’s soldiery (2 Cor. 10:4; 1 Tim. 1:18)—then surely no joy or sorrow that befalls the camp cancels the soldiers’ stations. [Note: [Col. 1183D] “No joy or sorrow.” Whether something joyful or something sorrowful is announced, soldiers do not abandon the post at which they preside; nor shall we Christian soldiers abandon our stations, whether the day be joyful or one of fasting and affliction. Here it should also be noted that Christians’ stations are named after the stations of soldiers; for since we are soldiers of Christ, we borrow many things from soldiers. G. PANCIROLI.] For joy will carry out discipline more willingly, and sorrow more anxiously.

## CHAPTER XX

[Note: [Col. 1183D] Title in the Ambrosian codex: “On the Attire of Women.”] [Note: [Col. 1183B] Chapter XV in other editions.]

As regards only the attire of women, [Note: [Col. 1183D] The material that follows, down to the words “through the Churches which . . .,” was first added by Rigaltius from a very ancient manuscript; Muratori, however, added everything that is read down to the end of the book. EDITORS.] [Note: [Col. 1183D] “As regards attire,” etc. Tertullian thinks that he must also discuss the garments women [Col. 1184B] ought to put on when coming to prayer. He says that he was prompted to this because the practice concerning women’s garments varied according to place, and that he therefore wished to write on this subject particularly as a man of no place—that is, as one favoring no local custom and thus capable of treating the matter with perfect fairness. But it might seem impudent to treat this subject again after the apostle Paul had dealt with it plainly in 1 Timothy, ch. II. Yet he does not consider himself impudent, since he treats it after the Apostle indeed, but in accordance with the Apostle—that is, according to the Apostle’s intention. MURATORI.—“As regards only the attire of women.” These words occur in a very ancient manuscript and are not to be rejected. After the words “Through the Churches which,” however, the same manuscript continues, without any distinction: “Practical arrangement [Col. 1184C], so that both more numerous and more necessary tasks of their own,” etc., which occurs in book I of *On the Apparel of Women*. RIGALTIUS.] the diversity of observance has caused us—especially as men belonging to no particular place—to reconsider the matter, after the most holy Apostle, with apparent impudence; except that it is not impudent if we reconsider it in accordance with the Apostle. [Note: [Col. 1183B] “Causes,” Rigaltius, Paris, Venice, Semler, Leopold.] Concerning modesty in dress and adornment, [Col. 1184A] there is indeed a plain injunction from Peter as well: with the same voice, because also with the same Spirit as Paul, he restrains the ostentation of clothing, the pride of gold, and the elaborately seductive dressing of the hair. [Note: [Col. 1183B] “Or should it be *pandering elaborateness*?” says Semler.] [Note: [Col. 1184C] “The elaborately seductive dressing of the hair.” The Ambrosian codex reads “the morose seduction of crimes.” But the codex used by Rigaltius, which ends with these words, has “and the painstaking seduction of the hair.” I more readily approve this reading, so that it signifies and condemns women’s excessive zeal in arranging their hair and training it into curls, whereby men, as though solicited by procurers, are swept away into lust. The reading is consonant with both Apostles whom Tertullian cites. Peter writes thus of women in 1 Peter, ch. III: “Whose adornment should not be outward hair arrangement, or the wearing of gold, or the adornment of garments.” Paul, however, in 1 Timothy, ch. II, says: “Likewise also women, adorning themselves in seemly apparel, with modesty and sobriety, and not with braided hair, or gold, or pearls, or costly clothing.” Here you see mention made of the hair. MURATORI.]

## CHAPTER XXI

[Note: [Col. 1184D] Title in Muratori’s codex: “On Virgins.”] [Note: [Col. 1184B] Chapter XVI in other editions.]

But what is variously observed among the Churches must be reconsidered as uncertain: whether virgins ought to be veiled or not. [Note: [Col. 1184D] “But what is observed indiscriminately.” He had said that it is absolutely certain from the Apostle’s precept that women’s garments and ornaments ought to breathe modesty. He now says that the uncertain question—whether virgins ought to be veiled—must be treated. For some allowed virgins to be unveiled in the temple and at sacred assemblies; others, however, thought that a veil was necessary for them also, as for other women, for the sake of modesty and by authority of the apostolic precept. MURATORI.] [Note: [Col. 1184B] “But that,” Semler, Leopold.] For those who grant virgins exemption of the head [Note: [Col. 1184D] “Exemption of the head.” Those who grant virgins a bare head and exemption from the veil. MURATORI.] appear to rely upon the fact that the Apostle did not name virgins specifically, but declared that women were to be veiled; and did not designate the sex by saying “females,” but a condition within the sex by saying “women” (1 Cor. 11:5). [Note: [Col. 1184D] “The Apostle did not name virgins specifically,” etc. “But every woman praying or prophesying with her head uncovered dishonors her own head; for it is one and the same as if she were shaven” (*Pasa de gynē proseuchomenē ē prophēteuousa akatakalyptō tē kephalē kataischynei tēn kephalēn heautēs; hen gar estin kai to auto tē exyrēmenē*; Πᾶσα δὲ γυνὴ προσευχομένη ἢ προφητεύουσα ἀκατακαλύπτῳ τῇ κεφαλῇ καταισχύνει τὴν κεφαλὴν ἑαυτῆς· ἓν γὰρ ἐστιν καὶ τὸ αὐτὸ τῇ ἐξυρημένῃ). These are the Apostle’s words in 1 Corinthians, ch. XI, v. 5, which our Vulgate translator renders thus: “Every woman praying or prophesying with her head unveiled [Col. 1185B] disfigures her head, for it is the same as if she were made bald.” Those who contended that virgins had by no means been commanded [Col. 1185C] to pray veiled in the temple fashioned the following argument from the Apostle’s own words: If the Apostle had resolved to command this, he would not have used the word *gynē* (γυνή), which in Latin means “woman,” for *gynē* is commonly used only of married females. He would rather have said *thēleia* (θήλεια), the term by which Greeks designate all females, both married and unmarried; or he would also have added *parthenous* (παρθένους), that is, “virgins.” Therefore in that passage the Apostle signified not the whole sex, but a condition and part of the female sex, namely married women. Indeed, in the same Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. VI [sic; 1 Cor. 7:34], he himself distinguished virgins from women, saying: *memeristai de hē gynē kai hē parthenos* (μεμέρισται δὲ ἡ γυνὴ καὶ ἡ παρθένος), “the woman and the virgin are divided.” Tertullian addressed to himself a similar argument not only here, but also in the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, which he later wrote in the school of Montanus. There he says in ch. 4: “It is objected to us that the Apostle made no mention of virgins where he prescribed concerning the veil, but named only women; whereas, if he had wished virgins also to be covered, he would have made a declaration concerning virgins as well as women, [Col. 1185D] just as, he says, in the passage where he treats of marriage he declares what is to be observed concerning virgins also. Accordingly, they are not included in the law of veiling the head, since they are not named in this law; indeed, they are thereby shown to be unveiled, since those who are not even named are not commanded.” MURATORI.]

For if he had named the sex by saying “females,” he would have made an absolute [Col. 1185A] ruling concerning every woman. But when he names one condition of the sex, he separates the other by leaving it unmentioned. For, they say, he could either have named virgins specifically as well, or, more concisely, females in general.
