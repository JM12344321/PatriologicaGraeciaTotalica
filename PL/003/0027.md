Thus this most eminent man argues against that widespread opinion concerning Hermas, the author of the *Shepherd*. Yet that opinion can scarcely—indeed, cannot at all—be called an invention of modern writers; for Origen, in book X of his *Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans*, at the passage where Hermas is mentioned by the Apostle, determined that the book of the *Shepherd* should be ascribed to the Pauline Hermas; and other authors besides, as is evident from Eusebius and Jerome, handed down the same view. Moreover, some learned men in Germany still believe that this writer should be assigned to the first century; whether, however, they support their opinion with any new arguments, I have not yet had occasion to see.

Gallandi, however, in the *Prolegomena concerning Caius the Presbyter*, § IV, p. XXXI of vol. II of the *Bibliotheca Patrum*, wishes to distinguish the works of the Pauline Hermas and of Hermas, or Hermes, the brother of Pius; and he says that Justus Fontanini clearly [Col. 0190D] demonstrated the same thing in *Historia Litteraria Aquileiensis*, book II, chap. 1. Gallandi himself writes the following, which is well worth reading:

> “Just as the work of the apostolic man was called by the ancient Fathers the *Shepherd*, or *Book of the Shepherd*—a designation perhaps derived from the fact that throughout almost the entire work a Shepherd is introduced speaking to and instructing Hermas—so too it may have happened that the book of Hermes concerning Easter was called, at least by some, the *Shepherd*, because its author wrote about the things commanded him by an angel when the angel came to him in the garb of a shepherd, as the Second Henschenian Catalogue states. Hence the name *Shepherd* became attached not only to the writer but also to his book.”

Gallandi and Fontanini think that the book published by Hermes, the brother of Pius, was lost through the ravages of time; and Fontanini indeed denies that this Hermes ought to be called Hermas. Yet we see that the author of this fragment calls him Hermas, not Hermes, just as pseudo-Tertullian [Col. 0191A] has “Hermas.” Conversely, Usuard calls that Pauline Hermas “Hermes” in his *Martyrology* under May 9, fol. 70, Louvain edition.

Moreover, the author of the fragment may have had in mind the extant book called the *Shepherd* of Hermas; and even if you suppose that he mistakenly ascribed the work to Hermas, or Hermes, the brother of Pope Pius, nevertheless, as Freindaller rightly says—though in another place:

## AT THE BEGINNING OF THE THIRD CENTURY  
## MARCUS MINUCIUS FELIX, ADVOCATE AND APOLOGIST

[Col. 0193]

## PROLEGOMENA  
## ON THE LIFE, HISTORY, AND WRITINGS OF M. MINUCIUS FELIX THE APOLOGIST, BY DOM GOLHASR. LUMPER, O.S.B.

[Note: [Col. 0193C] *Critical Theological History of the Life, Writings, and Doctrine of the Holy Fathers*, part VII.]

### I

[Col. 0193C] *Minutius*, or *Minucius*, was Marcus’s family name; for Octavius addressed him in these words [Note: [Col. 0193C] Minucius, *Dialogue*, chaps. 2 and 5.]:

> “It is not the part of a good man, brother Marcus, [to …] a man,” etc.

Caecilius, likewise:

> “Although for you, brother Marcus,” etc.

Yet, just as the consular family of the Minucii was familiar to everyone and distinguished among the Romans, so it has remained unknown to everyone up to the present whether our Minucius derived his origin from it or from some other family. Nor is anyone better informed about what his native country was [Note: [Col. 0193C] Gallandi, vol. II, *Prolegomena*; Ceillier, [Col. 0193D] *Histoire des Auteurs*, vol. II, p. 222.]. Nevertheless, some suspect from the style of his book that he was born in Africa [Note: [Col. 0193D] Tillemont, *Mémoires*, vol. III, *Histoire ecclésiastique*, p. 71; Basnage, at the year 210, § 3. Dupin, following Rigaltius, reasons as follows: “Jerome, in his Catalogue, in which he observes chronological order, places him between Tertullian [Col. 0194C] and Cyprian.” It is evident that Minucius took not a few things from Tertullian, and that Cyprian copied Minucius’s words in more than one passage of his book *On the Vanity of Idols*. From this it is presumed that he was African; and his writing approaches the African style.]. But they neither prove nor can prove that anything characteristic of the harsher African manner of speech is found in him.

Might it not be said with better right that Caecilius and Octavius, who were bound to Minucius by so strong a tie of affection, were in fact Africans and his fellow-countrymen? Yet, as we shall see shortly, this is not so certain, nor can it be inferred beyond doubt from however close a bond of friendship they had with him.

### II

We believe, however, that no one will deny that Minucius lived for a long time at Rome, which was then the capital of all the other cities of the world, and there discharged the office of an advocate, as Lactantius [Note: [Col. 0194D] Lactantius, *Institutes*, book V, chap. 1.] and [Col. 0195A] Jerome [Note: [Col. 0195D] Jerome, *Catalogue*, chap. 58, and Letter 83 to Magnus.] plainly and explicitly assert.

Minucius himself writes at the beginning of his book [Note: [Col. 0195D] Minucius, *Dialogue*, chap. 2.] that Octavius came to him at Rome, and that shortly afterward he traveled to Ostia with him and another companion:

> “When,” he says, “the vintage holidays had relaxed my judicial responsibilities”—

that is, when during the vintage season he was free from the forensic labor that occupied him throughout the entire year.

Did not Octavius also say of himself and Minucius—or rather solely in Minucius’s name:

> “Nevertheless, we undertook to defend and protect certain sacrilegious and incestuous persons, even parricides; but these men,”

that is, the Christians,

> “we thought ought not to be heard at all” [Note: [Col. 0195D] Ibid., chap. 28.]?

From these words it is evident that, while he was still a pagan, Minucius performed the office of an advocate.

### III

In our own age, however, Heumann, a man of exceedingly acute intellect, thought that Minucius’s status should be questioned and that he ought to be removed from the number of advocates or jurisconsults [Note: [Col. 0195D] Heumann, *Parerga Gottingensia* X, pp. 208–212.].

Heumann says, namely, that Lactantius—who is believed to be the first of all the more ancient writers to report that Minucius exercised that profession—does indeed call him a “causidicus,” or advocate, but in that elegant sense in which he indicates an advocate not “of the forum,” but “of the Christian religion.” Once this is assumed, Heumann thinks that Jerome misunderstood Lactantius’s words. He goes so far as almost to persuade himself that Lactantius did not write, “Minucius Felix held a not undistinguished place among advocates,” but “held a not undistinguished place among the advocates of the Church.” That is, he says, from the abbreviated spelling *Ecce* for *Ecclesiae* (“of the Church”), *loci* (“of a place”) could easily have arisen.

But [Col. 0195C] the critic is found to have wasted his effort. I pass over the passage from Minucius just cited, which alone Heumann saw and twisted into another meaning. For even if, in his opinion, the words of that passage are judged insufficiently suited to settle such a dispute, elsewhere our “advocate,” and indeed an advocate “of no undistinguished standing,” argues his own case so clearly that there will be absolutely no one who, assuming the role of an impartial judge, will refuse to deliver judgment in accordance with his pleas. He speaks as follows [Note: [Col. 0195D] Minucius, *Dialogue*, chap. 28.]:

> “When we undertook to defend and protect certain sacrilegious and incestuous persons, even parricides, we thought that these men”—the Christians—“ought not to be heard at all.”

Here particular notice should be taken of the word *suscipiebamus*, “we undertook,” which is drawn from the forum, as Wowerius observed on this passage. Clients who had been taken under an advocate’s protection were called *suscepti*. Thus Servius, on the Virgilian line [Col. 0195D] [Note: [Col. 0195D] Virgil, *Aeneid* VI.609.]:

> And fraud fastened upon a client,

says:

> “He wishes us to understand corrupt advocates who are patrons of clients, whom we now call *suscepti*.”

The remainder of the context in Minucius which we have transcribed is moreover known to agree so closely with this meaning of the word that whoever examines the author’s complete statement must admit that, at least at one time—[Col. 0196A] namely, while he was still devoted to pagan superstition—he had performed the office of an advocate.

Nor could anyone rightly reply that Minucius introduces Octavius as speaking in this way, and that all that can at most be inferred from the passage is that Octavius, but not Minucius, acted as a forensic patron. For who, asks a learned man [Note: [Col. 0196D] Le Nourry, *Apparatus*, vol. II, p. 11.], does not clearly understand that Octavius said, either about himself and Minucius together or rather solely in Minucius’s name, “that he had once undertaken to defend and protect sacrilegious and incestuous persons, even parricides, but had thought that Christians ought not to be heard at all”?

### IV

But if it is evident that the matter stands thus, it will also follow that our Minucius continued to discharge the same office even after professing the Christian religion. For he writes [Note: [Col. 0196D] Minucius, *Dialogue*, chap. 2.] that Octavius came to him at Rome,

> “when the vintage holidays had relaxed his judicial responsibilities”—

that is, when, with the autumn holidays beginning, he was free from the forensic labor that occupied him during the remainder of the year.

Nor, finally, is there any reason for anyone to wonder, with Baudouin [Note: [Col. 0196D] Baudouin, *Dissertation on Minucius Felix*, § 5.], “how a devout advocate could have maintained his position in so profane and hostile a forum”; and consequently there is no reason for Heumann to infer from this [Note: [Col. 0196D] Heumann, passage cited, p. 212.] that Minucius was by no means an advocate in the Roman forum.

> “For if they consider,”

to use the words of the distinguished Mamachi [Note: [Col. 0196D] Mamachi, *Origins and Antiquities of Christianity*, vol. III, p. 299 ff.],

> “both that the emperors did not always take precautions to prevent any of our people from exercising public offices, and that not all emperors did so, they will certainly readily admit that in the third century—when our people had obtained Septimius Severus, who was at times more equitable toward them than his predecessors had customarily been [Col. 0196C] [Note: [Col. 0196D] Tertullian, *To Scapula*, chap. 4.], and Alexander [Note: [Col. 0196D] Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VI, chaps. 16 and 28.] and the two emperors named Philip, who judged that nothing should be decreed against us [Note: [Col. 0196D] Ibid., chap. 34.]—it was permissible for a Christian man to enter the forum and undertake the representation of those whom he judged to have suffered injury or to have been unjustly deprived of their property.”

Arnobius indeed testifies [Note: [Col. 0196D] Arnobius, *Against the Nations*, book II, p. 21, Paris edition, 1656.] that

> “orators endowed with great talents and men learned in the law sought Christian instruction, scorning the things in which they had trusted shortly before.”

And who can doubt that in these words Arnobius was referring to our Minucius—indeed, to his own Minucius—whom he knew perfectly well to have held the foremost place among such orators and jurisconsults?

Moreover, Tertullian said [Note: [Col. 0196D] Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 37, Migne ed., vol. I, col. 462.]:

> “We are but of yesterday, and we have filled everything that belongs to you: cities, islands, fortresses, towns, meeting places, the very camps, tribes, decuries, the senate, and the forum. We have left you only the temples.”

Heumann should therefore erase from his *Parerga* that observation in which he determined that Minucius must be struck from the roll [Col. 0197A] of jurisconsults; and let the authority of Firmianus and Jerome prevail, who assert that Minucius Felix held a position of no small distinction—indeed, an eminent position—among the advocates at Rome [Note: [Col. 0197D] See *An Apologetic Letter of M. Minucius Felix on Behalf of Himself and His Status, to F. O. M.*, that is, Friedrich Otto Mencke, in which, against Heumann’s observation, it is demonstrated that Minucius Felix rightly and deservedly retains his place among jurisconsults and advocates. This letter was reproduced in the *Nova Acta Eruditorum*, 1738, p. 210.].

### V

Minucius had been raised in paganism from infancy, but at a more advanced age he abandoned the superstition of the gentiles and embraced the Christian faith. He testifies to this about himself when he says the following concerning Octavius [Note: [Col. 0197D] Minucius, *Dialogue*, chap. 1.]:

> “When, after the mist had been dispersed, I emerged from the depth of darkness into the light of wisdom and truth, he did not reject me as a companion, but—what is more glorious—went before me”—

that is, Octavius preceded me in professing the Christian faith.

Caecilius too intimates this when, at the beginning of the *Dialogue*, he asks Minucius:

> “Although you have moved in both ways of life, have rejected the one [Col. 0197B] and approved the other”—

that is, although you have rejected the superstition of the gentiles, yet have approved the Christian faith—

> “nevertheless, for the present your mind must be so disposed that you hold the balance of a perfectly impartial judge and do not lean with partiality toward either side, lest the judgment appear not so much to have arisen from our arguments as to have been brought forward from your own sentiments” [Note: [Col. 0197D] Ibid., chap. 5.].

Eucherius, bishop of Lyons, teaches in the following words how devoutly and holily Minucius lived after his conversion [Note: [Col. 0197D] Eucherius of Lyons, *Letter to Valerian*, vol. V, *Bibliotheca Patrum*, p. 771.]:

> “And when shall I unfold from that catalogue of great numbers Firmianus, Minucius, Cyprian, Hilary, John, and Ambrose, men most illustrious for eloquence? They had said, I believe: ‘What is this? The unlearned arise and seize heaven, while here we, with all our learning, wallow in error and blood!’ They had said this, and therefore afterward they themselves took the kingdom by force.”

### VI

After Minucius gave his name to Christ and had been “instructed in the mysteries of this name,” to speak with Arnobius [Note: [Col. 0197D] Arnobius, passage cited.], he presented himself as a vindicator and champion of the truth [Note: [Col. 0197D] Le Nourry, *Apparatus to the Bibliotheca Maxima Patrum*, vol. IV, p. 19.].

He therefore composed a dialogue divided into two parts, one of which contains Caecilius’s speech on behalf of the pagans and the other Octavius’s speech on behalf of the Christians. He entitled it *OCTAVIUS*, as Lactantius and Jerome report, plainly in imitation of Plato, Cicero, and other ancient authors who prefixed to books of this kind, composed in dialogue form, the name of the person who plays the principal role in them.

The distinguished Blackwall says:

> “Minucius’s dialogue is arranged with acuteness, elegance, and clarity. You find in it almost as many weighty thoughts as periods, and these thoughts, flowing naturally from the subject, are skillfully woven into the discourse. He both fights and conquers by argument; and he possesses as much wit [Col. 0198A] as penetration. His natural genius was given to him not for invention, but for speaking the truth, which he does not know how to adulterate. He clears Christian doctrine of the charges brought against it by the pagans and turns his opponents’ calumnies back against their own religion so aptly and manifestly that he shows himself at once the strongest and most formidable assailant of a bad cause and the noblest advocate of a good one” [Note: [Col. 0198D] Blackwall, *On the Excellence of the Classical Authors*, chap. 10, § 5, p. 193.].

### VII

As for the other events of Minucius’s life, how long he lived, and how he died, there is profound silence among both ancient and modern writers. Nor is there agreement among writers concerning the age in which Minucius Felix lived.

Henry Dodwell [Note: [Col. 0198D] Dodwell, *Cyprianic Dissertations* III, § 16, p. 12.] persuaded himself that Minucius flourished near the end of the reign of Marcus Antoninus; and David Blondel [Note: [Col. 0198D] Blondel, *On the Eucharist*, p. 119.] made him a contemporary of Fronto, [Col. 0198B] who taught Marcus Antoninus around the year of Christ 160.

Most scholars, not without reason, number him among the ecclesiastical writers of the third century. It is nevertheless insufficiently certain and clear during which years of that age he flourished and, especially, during which emperor’s reign he lived at Rome.

William Cave [Note: [Col. 0198D] Cave, *Literary History of Ecclesiastical Writers*, vol. I, p. 101.] says:

> “Concerning the age in which he lived, I have nothing that I would venture to affirm as certain. Yet if conjecture is permissible in an obscure and doubtful matter, I would surmise that, as a contemporary of Tertullian and older than Cyprian, he was famous around the year 220.”

Johann Albert Fabricius [Note: [Col. 0198D] Fabricius, notes on Jerome, *On Illustrious Men*, chap. 58, p. 133, *Bibliotheca Ecclesiastica*.] observes that all agree that Minucius was not far removed from the reigns of Antoninus, Commodus, and Severus.

Ceillier [Note: [Col. 0198D] Rémi Ceillier, *Histoire des Auteurs ecclésiastiques*, vol. II, chap. 23, p. 222.] conjectures from Jerome that Minucius lived at Rome during the pontificate of Zephyrinus, since Jerome, in his catalogue [Col. 0198C] *On Illustrious Men*, listed Minucius among the writers who existed at that time. The very learned Baronius [Note: [Col. 0198D] Baronius, at the year 211, §§ 3–4.] and Tillemont [Note: [Col. 0198D] Tillemont, *Mémoires*, vol. III, “Note on Minucius,” p. 295.] held the same opinion.

In our own age, the distinguished Johann Daniel van Hoven, in a letter to Gerard Meerman, endeavored to show that Minucius was a contemporary of Athenagoras and to claim for him the leading place among Latin Christian writers [Note: [Col. 0198D] This letter occupies first place in the *V.D. Campenses*, or *Critical-Antiquarian Gleanings*. Kampen, 1766. It also appears in Lindner’s second edition of Minucius Felix, 1773.].

### VII

In addition to this dialogue, Jerome, in *On Illustrious Men*, chap. 58, describes another book by Minucius, *On Fate*, or *Against the Astrologers*, as follows:

> “Another work, *On Fate*, or *Against the Astrologers*, is also circulated under his name. Although it too is the work of an eloquent man, it does not appear to me to agree with the style of the preceding book.”

This book, which Jerome also mentions in his letter to Magnus [Note: [Col. 0198D] “In the book entitled *Octavius* [Col. 0199A] and in the other *Against the Astrologers*—if, however, its attribution does not falsely represent its author—what part of pagan literature did Minucius Felix leave untouched?” Jerome, Letter 83 to Magnus.], is no longer extant [Col. 0199A].

That Minucius was its author is not certain, although it is probable. For he himself indicates in the following words [Note: [Col. 0199A] Minucius, *Octavius*, chap. 36.] that he intended to write on fate:

> “And enough concerning fate—even if, in view of the occasion, it has been but little; we shall discuss it elsewhere more richly and fully.”

But, as the distinguished Dupin observes [Note: [Col. 0199A] Dupin, *Bibliothèque des Auteurs*, vol. I, p. 189.], it is uncertain whether he discussed fate more extensively in another book and whether the treatise extant in Jerome’s time was the one he had promised, or whether some author instead seized upon this passage as an opportunity to put forward a work falsely under Minucius’s name.

Since it is sufficiently evident that this monument of his genius has perished, it is truly surprising that Sébastien Le Nain de Tillemont [Note: [Col. 0200A] Tillemont, *Mémoires*, vol. III, p. 73.], while discussing Minucius and his *Dialogue* learnedly, should add that the work may be read in print in a certain edition of Arnobius.
