Servants of the living God, my fellow servants and sisters, by that right which I am reckoned to share with you, I—the very least [Col. 1316A]—indeed by my right of fellowship in service and brotherhood, venture to address you, [Note: [Col. 1315D] *Postremissimus* (“the very least”). A superlative used by Gellius. Rigault later reads *equidem meo jure* (“indeed by my own right”), that is, by the right that belonged to him to write to women, since he was their brother and fellow servant. LE PR.—Perhaps it would be better to read: *Et quidem meo jure* (“And indeed by my own right”). EDD.] [Note: [Col. 1315C] Semler: *postremissimus omnium, qui de meo jure—ad* [Col. 1316C] *nos verba ista facere, non utique affectationi* (Rigault: *affectionis*) *sed*, etc.: “the very least of all, who by my own right—to address these words to us, certainly not for ostentation” (Rigault: “of affection”), “but,” etc.] certainly not out of affectation, but acting out of affection in the cause of your salvation.

That salvation—not only women’s, but men’s also—is established chiefly in the manifestation of chastity. For since we are all a temple of God, because the Holy Spirit has been brought into us and consecrated there, chastity is the keeper and high priest of that temple, [Note: [Col. 1316C] Rigault: “the female keeper and high priestess.”] permitting nothing unclean or profane to be brought into it, lest the God who inhabits it, offended by the defiled dwelling, abandon it. [Note: [Col. 1315D] “Which permits nothing unclean or profane.” Cyprian the martyr transferred this into his own treatise: “And let us take care,” he says, “that nothing unclean and profane be brought into the temple of God, lest, being offended, he abandon the dwelling which he inhabits.” RHEN.]

But our present subject is not chastity itself, for the urgent divine commandments everywhere are sufficient to enjoin and exact it, [Note: [Col. 1315D] “For the enjoining and exacting of which,” etc. The passages of Scripture by which chastity is enjoined may be seen in the books *On Chastity*, both the Author’s and St Cyprian’s. Rhenanus, however, notes that the phrase “for me to rebuke” is expressed in the dative case, that is, “worthy of my rebuke.” I, however, from three Vatican manuscripts, read: “But indeed they persist,” for “let them persist”; and “of their beauty and luster,” with Latinius—that is, displaying the same beauty. PAM.] but the matters pertaining to chastity, namely, how you ought to conduct yourselves. For most women—may God indeed permit me to rebuke this [in all things deserving rebuke]—[Col. 1316B] either in simple ignorance or in bold dissimulation conduct themselves as though chastity consisted solely in the integrity of the flesh and avoidance of fornication, and as though nothing further were required inwardly concerning the regulation of dress and adornment. But they persist in their former pursuits of beauty and luster, [Note: [Col. 1316C] Wouwerius. Rigault deleted “But indeed they persist in their former.”] displaying the same appearance of themselves [Note: [Col. 1316C] Thus Wouwerius; Semler: “displaying the same presentation of themselves”; Rigault: “displaying the same outward surface.”] as the women of the nations, who lack consciousness of true chastity, because there is nothing true in those who do not know God, the guardian and teacher of truth. [Note: [Col. 1316C] “The women of the nations.” What am I to say of these? Are they chaste? The Angylae—a nation—compel their women during the first nights of marriage to submit to adulteries, and afterward bind them to chastity by laws, as Solinus relates in his *Polyhistor*. It would take too long to speak of the rest. LAC.]

For even if some chastity can be believed to exist among the Gentiles, it is manifestly so imperfect and unformed that, although inwardly it may to some extent act with self-restraint, it nevertheless dissolves itself in freedom of dress, [Col. 1317A] perversely desiring among the Gentiles that very thing whose outcome it avoids. [Note: [Col. 1316D] “Nevertheless dissolves itself in freedom of dress.” The most ancient copy reads: “It dissolves itself into freedoms of outward display.” This may be explained without difficulty if one understands *ambitus* here as what Tertullian himself called the “outward surface” above: namely, the body and the ornaments of the body, which surround and clothe the mind. He is discussing Christian chastity and shows that it ought to be more perfect than Gentile chastity. He therefore says that Gentile chastity is so perverse that, although the mind steadfastly perseveres in its resolution never to violate chastity, chastity itself nevertheless so dissolves and breaks itself amid liberties of cultivating and adorning the body that it seeks the thing whose actual result it avoids—that is, it seeks to please and delights in drawing the sighs of young men after it, although it is unwilling to yield to the lust of another which it has itself aroused. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1317B] Semler: “It dissolves itself because of the truth” (another reading: “because of the vanity”) “of the Gentiles, seeking consolation,” etc.]

How many women, after all, do not even desire to please strangers? How many do not wish to paint themselves for that very purpose, and then deny that they have been desired? [Note: [Col. 1317C] “How many women, after all, do not even desire to please strangers?” How many chaste Christian women are there today, he asks, who are so austere and seriously chaste that they do not even desire to please strangers? Who do not take care to have themselves painted and adorned, if only to please them? How many, finally, are there who would wish to deny—or who would not be ashamed to deny—that they had ever been desired? Gentile chastity, though, is such that women do not actually sin, yet present themselves in such attire as to seem to want what they do not want; they walk in such a way that by their very gait and dress they do not refuse what shameless women promise. [Col. 1317D] He then presses the point: “What wonder? Everything that does not belong to God is perverse.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1317D] “Who do not wish to paint themselves for that very purpose.” Here he denounces the cosmetics and rouge that women used to give themselves luster; and in doing so, as Clement of Alexandria says in *Paedagogus* III.2, they seem to censure the Maker of all things for not having bestowed sufficient beauty. LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 1317B] Rigault: “Who do not take care to have themselves painted for that very purpose—and deny” [Col. 1317C] “that they have been desired?”]

And yet this also is characteristic of Gentile chastity: not to sin, yet to wish to; or even not to wish to, yet not to refuse. What wonder is it? Everything that is not from God is perverse. Let those women, then, who do not possess the whole good, see how readily they mingle even what they do possess with evil. It is necessary for you to turn away from them in your manner of walking also, as in other matters, since you ought to be perfect, just as your Father who is in heaven is perfect.

## CHAPTER II

You who are perfect—that is, Christian and chaste—should know that the desire to be desired must not merely be avoided, but [Col. 1317B] even abhorred. [Note: [Col. 1317C] Rigault: “Since perfect—that is, Christian—chastity,” etc., omitting the remaining words.] First, because the pursuit of pleasing through beauty does not arise from an unblemished conscience, since we know that beauty is naturally an invitation to lust. Why, then, do you arouse that evil against yourself? Why do you invite that to which you profess yourself a stranger?

Then, because we ought not to open a path to temptations. Sometimes—may God keep this far from his own—they accomplish their purpose by persistence; at the very least, they disturb the spirit through scandal. [Note: [Col. 1317D] Rigault reads: “At the very least, they raise a scandal in the spirit.” They shake and disquiet the spirit. For the faithful, or saints, possess the Spirit of God. Accordingly, if they admit anything arising from evil, they disturb the Spirit of God; for the sake of a worldly thing, they shake the Spirit they received from the Lord. Broad and extensive is the operation of evil, hurling manifold provocations at the spirit. Thus Tertullian himself speaks in the books *On Patience* and *On the Shows*: “God,” he says, “commanded that the Holy Spirit, as being tender and delicate because of the goodness of his nature, be treated with tranquillity, gentleness, quiet, and peace.” For this reason it pleased the Fathers of the Council of Elvira that candles should not be lit in a cemetery during the day, canon XXXIII: “For the spirits of the saints must not be disturbed.” They are disturbed by a profane and Gentile rite. At night, indeed, I think this would have been burdensome or troublesome to no one, since it is plainly necessary both for the assistance of light and as a testimony to chastity. We shall make some observations about candles and lamps lit during the day at Book II, *To His Wife*, on the words: “And she will proceed from a doorway crowned with laurel and lit with lamps, as though from a new council chamber of public lusts.” RIG.]

We ought indeed to conduct ourselves with such holiness and with the whole substance of faith that, assured and secure concerning our conscience, we desire this state to continue in us—yet without presumption. [Col. 1318A] For one who presumes fears less, takes fewer precautions, and is exposed to greater danger. [Note: [Col. 1317C] Rigault: “For one who presumes now fears less; one who fears less takes fewer precautions; one who takes fewer precautions is exposed to greater danger.”] Fear is the foundation of salvation; presumption is an impediment to fear. [Note: [Col. 1318D] “Presumption is an impediment to fear.” Prosper of Aquitaine discusses this argument—how necessary the fear of God is for attaining salvation—in Book III, chapter 11, of *The Contemplative Life*. There he also speaks of fear in opposition to presumption, as Tertullian does here; for he says that people cease to fear God when enticed by the hope of presumed impunity. LAC.]

It is therefore more advantageous that we hope we cannot sin than that we presume we cannot. For by hoping we shall fear; by fearing we shall take care; by taking care we shall be safe. Conversely, if we presume, neither fearing nor taking care, we shall hardly be safe. One who acts as though secure is not vigilant and does not possess sure and firmly grounded security. But one who is vigilant will truly be able to be secure. Let the Lord, indeed, in his mercy, take care of his own servants, so that they may even be happily permitted to presume upon his goodness toward them.

But why should we be a danger to another? Why should we bring lust upon another—a lust which, if [Note: [Col. 1318D] “Which, if God, by enlarging the law, does not distinguish in punishment from the deed of fornication, I do not know whether she who has been the cause of someone’s perdition will go unpunished.” Christ enlarged the law when he said: “You have heard that it was said to those of old: You shall not commit adultery. But I say to you: Everyone who looks upon a woman to lust after her,” etc. RIG.] the Lord, by enlarging the law, does not distinguish in punishment from the deed of fornication, [Note: [Col. 1318D] “Not by enlarging the law.” Thus in the book *On Prayer*: “Thereafter the Lord openly, enlarging the law, ranks anger against a brother together with murder.” In the same sense he said in the book *On Monogamy*: “Those things which concern righteousness not only remain preserved, but are also enlarged.” RIG.] [Col. 1318B] [Note: [Col. 1318B] The Agobardine manuscript reads: “As though God, by not enlarging the law, does not distinguish lust in punishment from the deed of fornication.”] I do not know whether she who has been the cause of someone’s perdition will go unpunished. For he perishes at once in your beauty if he lusts after it; [Note: [Col. 1318B] Rigault: “as soon as he lusts after your beauty”; he also deleted [Col. 1318C] the words that follow and accepted the reading “he lusted after.”] he has already committed in his mind what he desired, and you have become a sword to him. [Note: [Col. 1318D] “You have become a sword to him.” Cyprian, in the book *On the Dress of Virgins*: “So that, even if you yourself do not perish, you nevertheless cause others to perish, presenting yourself as a sword and poison to those who see you.” RIG.]

Though you may be free of guilt, you will not be free of reproach. [Note: [Col. 1318C] Rigault: “a sword to him, so that, even if you are free of guilt, you will not be freed from reproach.”] It is as when a robbery has been committed on someone’s estate: the crime indeed does not touch its owner, [Note: [Col. 1318C] Rigault: “will not touch”; Semler: “does not bind him, yet the property,” etc.] but while his land is marked by the disgrace, he too is sprinkled with infamy. Are we to paint ourselves so that others may perish? Where, then, is: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself”; “Do not care only for your own concerns, but also for another’s”? [Note: [Col. 1318C] Semler: “Do not care for your own concerns.”] No declaration of the Holy [Col. 1319A] Spirit should be directed and applied only to the matter immediately at hand, and not to every occasion of usefulness. [Note: [Col. 1319C] Semler: “can be.”]

Since, therefore, both our own cause and that of others are involved in the pursuit of most dangerous beauty, you should now know that you must reject not only the apparatus of fabricated and elaborately produced beauty, [Note: [Col. 1319C] Semler: “lust”; François du Jon: “desire.”] [Note: [Col. 1319C] “The apparatus of elaborately produced beauty.” These words must be understood not only of the hair, but of the adornment of the entire body; by *suggestus* understand “apparatus.” LE PR.] but must even obscure natural attractiveness through concealment and neglect, making it correspondingly troublesome to the assaults of the eyes. For although beauty is not to be accused—as a bodily felicity, as an addition to God’s handiwork, as a kind of elegant garment of the soul— [Note: [Col. 1319D] “As an addition to God’s handiwork.” Cyprian the martyr, *On the Dress of Virgins*: “Because God’s work, his production and handiwork, must in no way be adulterated.” RHEN.] [Note: [Col. 1319C] Thus Gelenius and the Frankfurt edition; Rigault: “a good garment”; Semler: “good clothing.”] it must nevertheless be feared because of the injury and violence of spectators. [Note: [Col. 1319C] Rigault: “violent pursuit, which,” etc.] Even Abraham, the father of faith, feared this because of his wife’s beauty and, by falsely calling Sarah his sister, redeemed his safety through dishonor. [Note: [Col. 1319D] “And falsely called Sarah his sister.” The manuscript reads: “And Isaac falsely called Rebecca his sister.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1319C] The Agobardine manuscript: “And Isaac falsely called Rebecca his sister.”] [Note: [Col. 1319D] “And falsely called Sarah his sister.” This passage has exercised the ingenuity of many. Those who had undertaken a defense of lying truly misuse this passage and twist it to their own meaning. On this matter St Augustine should be consulted in the book *On Lying, to Consentius*, and Book XXII *Against Faustus*. LE PR.]

## CHAPTER III

[Col. 1319B]

Now let the dignity of beauty not be feared; let it be considered neither burdensome to its possessors, nor ruinous to those who desire it, nor dangerous to those associated with it—not exposed to temptations, not surrounded by scandals. It is enough that it is unnecessary to God’s angels. [Note: [Col. 1319D] “God’s angels”: the faithful, the saints. RIG.] For where chastity is present, beauty is idle, because the proper use and fruit of beauty is sensual indulgence—unless someone supposes there to be another harvest from bodily attractiveness.

Let those women display the beauty given to them and seek beauty not given to them, who suppose that, when they furnish it to another, they are also furnishing to themselves what is demanded from beauty. [Note: [Col. 1319D] “Let those women display the beauty given to them and seek beauty not given to them, who suppose that they are also furnishing to themselves what is demanded from it, because they furnish it to another.” These words are to be read with the intonation of a question, as if he said: “Should not Christian women be ashamed to increase the natural attractiveness of their beauty, or, if it is lacking, to supply it by art and falsehood—women who suppose that they are also furnishing to themselves what is demanded from that beauty, namely, the kindling of more violent lust and thereby the destruction of the soul, because they furnish it to another?” Certainly, if they are truly Christian, they will think about themselves in no other way. RIG.—As it seems to me, however, there is no question here, but what rhetoricians call a concession: Tertullian concedes that only those women may concern themselves with beauty who wish to furnish the works of the flesh no less to themselves than to another. EDD.]

Someone will say: “What, then? Once sensual indulgence has been excluded and chastity admitted, is it not permissible merely to enjoy the praise of beauty and glory in the body’s good?” [Note: [Col. 1319C] Rigault: “Once chastity has been admitted, is praise of the body, its glory, permissible?”] Let whoever finds it helpful see to glorying in the flesh. For us, however, there is first of all [Col. 1319C] no pursuit of glory, because glory has the character of self-exaltation. But self-exaltation is incompatible with those who profess humility according to God’s ordinances. [Col. 1320A] Next, if all glory is vain and stupefied, how much more the glory that exists only in our flesh! [Note: [Col. 1320D] “If all glory is vain and stupefied.” He uses *stuporata* in the sense of foolish and dull. In this sense Catullus said: “This fool of mine sees nothing, hears nothing.” Tertullian himself, in the preceding book, where he speaks of precious stones and pebbles, says: “They knew only how to build up this stupefaction of women.” RIG.]

Even if there is something in which we should glory, we ought to desire to please in the goods of the spirit, not in the flesh, because we are followers of spiritual things. Let us rejoice in the things in which we labor; let us gather glory from those things from which we hope for salvation. Certainly, the Christian will glory even in the flesh—but when it has endured being torn apart for Christ, so that the spirit also may be crowned in it, not so that it may draw after itself the eyes and sighs of young men.

Thus, since beauty is altogether useless in you, you rightly should disdain it if you do not have it, and neglect it if you do. If a holy woman is naturally beautiful, let her not thereby become an occasion of sin; if she does, she certainly ought not to ignore her beauty, but even to obstruct it. [Note: [Col. 1320C] Rigault: “If holy women are naturally beautiful, let there not thereby be an occasion of sin; but if there is, she certainly ought not to assist it, but even to obstruct it.”]

## CHAPTER IV

[Col. 1320B]

Speaking to you as though to Gentiles, by a Gentile precept common to everyone: you ought to please your husbands alone. [Note: [Col. 1320D] “You ought to please your husbands alone.” I do not recognize here the irony that Rhenanus supposes. I think the meaning is that Tertullian, forgetting his stricter discipline, permits women to go about somewhat more adorned, provided that they do so with no other intention than to please their husbands. For Clement of Alexandria likewise seems to make this concession, *Paedagogus* III, to those who have married less continent husbands. LE PR.] But you will please them precisely to the extent that you take no care to please others.

Be assured, blessed women: no wife is unattractive to her husband. She pleased him sufficiently when she was chosen, whether commended by her character or her beauty. Let none of you suppose that, if she moderates her personal adornment, she [Note: [Col. 1320C] We have thought that the pronoun *se* (“she”) should be inserted here. LEOP.] will incur her husband’s hatred and aversion. Every husband demands chastity. A believing husband, moreover, does not require beauty, because we are not captivated by the same goods that the Gentiles consider such; an unbelieving husband, on the other hand, even regards it with suspicion, if only because of that wicked opinion of the Gentiles concerning us. [Note: [Col. 1320C] Rigault: “from that wicked opinion concerning us.”]

For whom, then, are you nurturing your beauty? If your husband is a believer, he does not demand it; if [Col. 1320C] a Gentile, he does not trust it. [Note: [Col. 1320C] Rigault: “If for an unbeliever, he does not believe it to be innocent.”] Why are you eager to please either one who suspects you or one who does not desire it?

## CHAPTER V

[Col. 1321A]

These things are certainly not suggested to you in order to commend an altogether crude and savage appearance; [Note: [Col. 1321C] Rigault: “These things should not be suggested in favor of a crude,” etc.] nor are we recommending the virtue of filth and foulness, [Note: [Col. 1321C] Semler: “concerning the body’s good, filth—but concerning foulness we advise.”] but proper measure, balance, and rightness in caring for the body. [Note: [Col. 1321C] “Rightness in caring for the body.” Thus, in the book *On the Pallium*, he used “the rightness of the pallium” to mean a pallium that fitted well and sat well; and above, “an unjust color.” RIG.] One must not go beyond what simple and sufficient cleanliness desires, beyond what is pleasing to the Lord.

Those women sin against him who smear their skin with cosmetics, [Note: [Col. 1321C] Rigault: “press upon.”] stain their cheeks with redness, and daub their eyes with soot. [Note: [Col. 1321C] “Their eyes with soot”: with antimony, *platyophthalmon* (πλατυόφθαλμον, “eye-enlarging cosmetic”). Thus above: “that same black powder by which the corners of the eyes are extended.” RIG.—“They extend their eyes with soot.” Perhaps the reading is *perlinunt* (“smear thoroughly”). He means the *kalliblepharon* (καλλιβλέφαρον, “eyelid beautifier”), which they used for the beauty of the eyelids. In Petronius, a boy adorns his mistress’s head with a topknot and even draws out her eyebrows from a cosmetic box. Martial, Book IX, epigram 38: “And may you lie hidden, put away in a hundred cosmetic boxes.” Juvenal: “He extends the eyebrow, stained with damp soot, with a slanting needle.” Philostratus praises his wife in a letter because she did not dye her cheeks with rouge and was numbered not among painted women but among the truly beautiful. In Clement of Alexandria and the ancient writers, as found in Athenaeus, Book XV, *ophthalmous hypographesthai* (ὀφθαλμοὺς ὑπογράφεσθαι, “to outline the eyes”) is the practice under discussion here. LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 1321C] Thus the Frankfurt edition and others; Semler: “gather”; Rigault: “extend.”]

Plainly, God’s handiwork is displeasing to them. [Note: [Col. 1321D] “God’s handiwork.” Cyprian: “God’s work, his production and handiwork, must in no way be adulterated.” RIG.] In their own persons they refute and censure the Craftsman of all things. [Note: [Col. 1321C] Rigault: “In their own persons they plainly accuse themselves.”] [Note: [Col. 1321D] “They censure the Craftsman,” etc. The love of adornment (*philokosmia*, φιλοκοσμία) was so displeasing to Tertullian that he did not hesitate to assert that the devil was its craftsman. For since what is born is God’s, it follows that what is added artificially is the devil’s work. He also warns that injury is done to God when his work is adorned with alien rouge and borrowed luster. LE PR.] For they censure him when they correct his work, when they add to it—taking, of course, those additions from a rival craftsman, namely, the devil. [Note: [Col. 1321C] Rigault: “those additions. He is the devil.”]

For who would teach people to alter the body except the one who also transfigured the human [Col. 1321B] spirit by wickedness? He undoubtedly devised contrivances of this kind so that, in us, he might in a certain manner lay hands upon God. [Note: [Col. 1321C] Rigault: “so that in you it may appear that you are in a certain manner laying hands upon God.”] [Note: [Col. 1322C] “Might lay hands upon God.” Cyprian: “They lay hands upon God when they strive to remake and [Col. 1322D] transfigure what he formed.” RIG.]

What is born is God’s work; therefore, what is fabricated is the devil’s business. [Note: [Col. 1322C] Rigault: “what is imposed” (*emplattetai*, ἐμπλάττεται, “is molded upon”).] How criminal it is to superimpose Satan’s contrivances upon God’s work! Our servants borrow nothing from our enemies; soldiers desire nothing from the enemy of their commander. For to request something for your use from the adversary of him in whose power you are is transgression. Will a Christian receive the help of that evil one in any matter? I do not know whether the name “Christian” will continue to belong to such a person. For that person will belong to the one by whose teachings he desires to be instructed.

How alien these things are to your disciplines and professions, how unworthy of the Christian name: to bear a fabricated face when all simplicity is enjoined upon you; to lie in your appearance when you are not permitted to lie [Col. 1321C] with your tongue; to seek [Note: [Col. 1322D] “To seek what has not been given.” The old manuscript: “what is not yours.” RIG.] what has not been given, [Note: [Col. 1322C] The Agobardine manuscript: “what is not yours.”] when you are commanded to practice abstinence from what belongs to another; to commit adultery in your appearance, [Col. 1322A] when your pursuit is chastity! [Note: [Col. 1322C] Rigault: “when abstinence from another’s property is handed down to you? To commit adultery in your appearance, when your pursuit should be chastity?”] Believe this, blessed women: how will you preserve God’s commandments when you do not preserve his lineaments in yourselves?

## CHAPTER VI

I see some women turning their hair yellow with saffron. They are even ashamed of their own nation because they were not born German or Gaulish; thus they transfer their homeland to their hair. [Note: [Col. 1322C] Rigault: “thus they now transform their hair.”] They take a bad—indeed, the worst—omen for themselves from their flame-colored heads, [Note: [Col. 1322D] “They take the worst omen for themselves from their flame-colored heads.” Thus, Book I *To His Wife*: “They handle the image of that unquenchable fire, the omens of their own punishment.” Cyprian: “With an evil foretoken of things to come, you already take the fiery color of your hair as an omen for yourself.” Jerome to Laeta: “Do not dye your hair red, thereby taking for yourself some omen of the fires of Gehenna.” RIG.] and they consider beautiful what they defile.

Moreover, the force of the chemicals burns damage into the hair and reserves destruction for the brain; so too does the constant application of any unadulterated moisture, while the heat of the sun—desirable for giving life and dryness to the hair—does harm. [Note: [Col. 1322D] “The sun’s heat, giving life to the hair.” *Animando* means “coloring,” as in Lucretius’s “animated crops” and Arnobius’s “animated entrails.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1322C] Rigault: “the heat does harm.”] What beauty is there together with injury? What loveliness together with uncleanness?

Will a Christian woman heap saffron [Col. 1322B] upon her head as though upon an altar? For whatever is customarily burned as an offering to an unclean spirit may seem a sacrifice unless it is applied to the proper, necessary, and healthful uses for which God’s creation was provided. But the Lord says: “Which of you can make black hair white, or white hair black?” (Matt. 5:36). And so they refute God: “See,” they say, “instead of white or black, we make it yellow—more winning in charm”—although women who regret having lived to old age even attempt to make white hair black.

What audacity! The age once desired in their prayers causes them shame; [Note: [Col. 1322D] “The age once desired in their prayers causes them shame.” Quintus Serenus:

> Those who are ashamed of long life, whom sacred old age  
> Offends, desire to conceal their hastening gray hairs  
> And counterfeit black hair with deceitful dye, etc.

RIG.] the theft is confessed, [Note: [Col. 1322C] Rigault: “is accomplished.”] the youth in which we sin is sighed after, and the occasion for gravity is tampered with. Far be such folly from the daughters of wisdom. The more old age strives to be concealed, the more it will be revealed. [Note: [Col. 1322C] Rigault: “will be retained.”]

This is [Col. 1322C] our eternity, derived from the youthfulness of the head. [Note: [Col. 1322C] Rigault: “the true eternity derived from the youthfulness of the hair.”] This is the incorruptible eternity we are always to put on for the house of the Lord, [Col. 1323A] which *akakia* (ἀκακία, “innocence”) promises. [Note: [Col. 1323B] Rigault: “We have this incorruptibility to put on over ourselves for the new house promised by the Monarchy.”] [Note: [Col. 1323B] “We have this incorruptibility to put on over ourselves for the new man promised by the Monarchy.” Or, if it is preferred—and I certainly prefer it—“for the new house promised by the Monarchy.” We have emended the text in this way according to the traces of the ancient reading visible in Agobard’s manuscript, which are as follows: “We have this incorruptibility always to put on for the new while which the Monarchy promises.” He is indignant with Christian women who, being excessively occupied with bodily adornment, gave too little thought to the Apostle, who calls us to a far more excellent glory of our bodies before God. He alludes to Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 15 and 2 Corinthians 5, which command Christians to hope confidently in the resurrection, which will clothe mortal bodies with immortality. For then the souls of the blessed will return to their bodies as though to new dwellings—dwellings now eternal, clothed over with immortality as with a new garment, never to be worn away by any subsequent age. He therefore says that old age is concealed in vain: the more it is suppressed, the more it breaks forth; the more it is concealed, the more it is exposed. Then, at once indignantly and mockingly, he says: “This is true eternity, derived from the youthfulness of the hair! This is the incorruptibility we have to put on over ourselves for the new house promised by the Monarchy.” By “Monarchy” he means God, the Best and Greatest, the unity of the most holy Trinity, in the sense in which he very frequently used that word in his disputation *Against Praxeas*. This is the most certain text of this extremely corrupt passage. Nor am I now influenced by the reading of the Dijon or Pithou manuscript that has hitherto been received in all editions: “for the house of the Lord promised by the acacia.” Pliny and the medical writers do indeed describe the acacia as a kind of thorn in Egypt, Cappadocia, and Pontus, whose juice or gum possesses so great a power of thickening that it serves the tanners’ workshops in place of gall; it was also adopted for women’s cosmetics, for tightening loose skin and producing wrinkles, and even for dyeing the hair: *dynamin echei styptikēn, melainei de kai trichas* (δύναμιν ἔχει στυπτικὴν, μελαίνει δὲ καὶ τρίχας), “it has an astringent power and also blackens the hair,” says Dioscorides. Marcellus, too, mentions acacia among the preparations commonly prescribed in his time for blackening and curling the hair. But these things agree sufficiently with neither the Apostle’s meaning nor Tertullian’s. Still less am I influenced by what Rhenanus, Pamelius, and others attempted concerning innocence—which the Greeks call *akakia* (ἀκακία)—for they were deceived by the corruption of a somewhat ancient manuscript. RIG.]

You make excellent speed toward the Lord; you hasten admirably to depart from this most wicked age—you to whom it is ugly to draw near to your end. [Note: [Col. 1323B] Latinius: “toward the end.” Rigault: “to approach the end.”]

## CHAPTER VII

For what does so laborious an elaboration of the head contribute to salvation? [Note: [Col. 1323B] Rhenanus, from the Gorze manuscript: “adorning”; others: “arranging.”] Why is your hair not permitted to rest—now bound up, now loosened, now raised, now flattened? Some delight to constrain it into curls; [Note: [Col. 1323B] Another reading: “into ringlets.”] others, without virtuous simplicity, delight to let it escape loose and flying.

In addition, you fasten upon yourselves all manner of monstrous structures of stitched [Note: [Col. 1324B] “All manner of monstrous structures.” Understand this of the wig or small hairpiece that both men and women used because of the thinness of their hair, with other people’s hairs added to it. It was so skillfully glued to the head that the hair appeared natural rather than borrowed. On this, Juvenal, *Satire* 6:

> But concealing black hair beneath a yellow wig.

LE PR.] and woven hairpieces: now shaped like a cap, as though a sheath for the head and a covering for the crown; now built up backward upon the neck. [Note: [Col. 1323B] Semler: “You preserve it without virtuous simplicity. In addition—of fine,” [Col. 1324B] etc. The words “stitched and woven” are absent from Rigault’s edition.]

It is remarkable that this does not openly contend against the Lord’s commandments [Col. 1323B]. It has been declared that no one can add to her stature. You certainly add to your weight by building upon your necks certain round buns or shield-bosses. [Note: [Col. 1324C] “Certain round buns.” Other manuscripts have *collyridae*. A *collyris* is a kind of cake, round in shape, so that it appears to be the same as the boss at the center of a shield. Juvenal, *Satire* 6:

> But when she is plastered over and treated  
> With so many cosmetics, and her face receives poultices  
> Of cooked wheat flour and wet dough, will it be called a face or an ulcer?

The great Scaliger read “bosses of the eruptions,” that is, bubbles and blisters in the leather that was put on. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1324B] Rigault: “certain coils,” etc. Others: “certain round buns—and shield-bosses.” Scaliger: “the bosses of eruptions” (*pampholygas*, παμφόλυγας, “blisters”).]

If you are not ashamed of the monstrosity, be ashamed [Col. 1324A] of the defilement: do not fit the castoffs of another’s head—perhaps unclean, perhaps guilty and destined for Gehenna—to a holy Christian head. Indeed, drive away all this slavery to adornment from a free head.

You labor in vain to appear adorned; in vain you employ every highly skilled architect of hairdressing. [Note: [Col. 1324C] “Every highly skilled architect of hairdressing.” Such persons were called by the special name *cosmetae*. The ancient glosses: *kosmētēs* (κοσμήτης), “adornment-worker, adorner.” Juvenal: “The *cosmetae* lay down their tunics.” Girls were also employed for this purpose, for in the glosses we likewise read *kosmētrias* (κοσμητρίας), “female adorners.” Ulpian, in the law *Item legato*, *On Legacies* III, where he discusses the things said to be prepared for the wife’s benefit: “Likewise included in the legacy are slaves—for example, litter bearers who carried only the lady of the household; likewise other slaves, perhaps girls with whom women adorn their hair”—for so, it seems, the text should be read. RIG.—“Architects of hairdressing.” The word *structor*, used absolutely, is applied to various occupations: sometimes to an arranger of dishes, as Servius says, and in Petronius’s *Dinner of Trimalchio*: “The arranger had placed upon it food properly suited to the subject”; sometimes to a carver of dishes, as Juvenal, *Satire* 5:

> Meanwhile, lest any indignity be lacking, you watch the carver  
> Dancing and gesticulating with his flying  
> Knife until he completes everything his master prescribed;  
> And indeed it makes no small difference  
> With what gesture the hares, and with what gesture the hen, is cut.

LE PR.]

God commands you to be veiled, I believe, so that the heads of some of you may not be seen. And [would that I, most wretched man,] on that day of Christian exultation [might raise my head even among your heels!] [Note: [Col. 1324B] The words enclosed in brackets appear to have been improperly inserted by an overzealous copyist.] [Note: [Col. 1324D] “Among your heels.” Wondrous humility!] I shall see whether you rise again with white lead, purple rouge, and saffron, and with that whole circumference of the head; whether the angels lift you up, painted in that fashion, into the cloud to meet Christ.

If these things are good now and belong to God, they too will meet the bodies as they rise again and will recognize their proper places. But nothing can rise again except the flesh and the spirit [Col. 1324B] alone and pure. Therefore, the things that do not rise again in flesh and spirit stand condemned, because they do not belong to God. Abstain today from things condemned. Let God see you today as he will see you then.
