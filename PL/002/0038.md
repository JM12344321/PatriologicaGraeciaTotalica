The paralytic also is healed (Luke 5), and indeed in an assembly, with the people looking on. “For the people,” says Isaiah (Isa. 35:2), “shall see the majesty of the Lord and the glory of God.” What majesty and what [Col. 0377B] glory? “Be strengthened, you feeble hands and loosened knees.” This will be paralysis. “Be strengthened, and do not fear.” He does not repeat “Be strengthened” idly, nor add “Do not fear” without purpose, because together with the restoration of the limbs he was also promising the recovery of strength: “Arise, and take up your pallet”; and courage of spirit, so that they should not fear those who were going to say, “Who can forgive sins except God alone?” You therefore now have both the prophecy of this particular healing fulfilled and the prophecy of the events that followed the healing.

Likewise recognize in the same prophet Christ as the forgiver of offenses: “For” [Note: Semler reads “How.”], he says (Isa. 53), “he shall forgive the offenses of many, and he himself bears away our offenses.” And also in the earlier passage [Note: “Earlier” (priore); another reading: “first” (primore).] (Isa. 1:18), in the person of the Lord himself: “Though your offenses be like rose-colored cloth, [Col. 0377C] I shall make them white as snow; and though they be like scarlet, I shall make them white as wool.” In the rose color he indicates the blood of the prophets; in the scarlet, the Lord’s blood, as being brighter.

Micah too speaks of the forgiveness of offenses (Mic. 7:18–19): “What God is like you, removing iniquities and passing over injustices in the remnant [Note: Another reading: “the remaining injustices.”] of your inheritance [Note: Semler inserts “and.”]? He has not retained his wrath as a testimony [Note: “He has not retained it as a testimony.” Here, as elsewhere, Tertullian follows the version of the Seventy Translators. Since they read the Hebrew word as *lahed*, where it ought to have been read *lehad*, they wrote here εἰς μαρτύριον (*as a testimony*), where they might more correctly have written εἰς αἰῶνα (*forever*).—EDD.], because he desired mercy. [Note: “Desired”; Semler reads “and merciful,” which neither makes sense [Col. 0378D] nor agrees with the Septuagint.] He will turn again [Note: Junius reads “He will turn his attention”; “He will return,” according to the Septuagint.] and have mercy on us. He will sink our offenses and plunge our sins into the depths of the sea.”

But even if nothing of this kind had been proclaimed concerning Christ, I would still have examples of this kindness in the Creator, promising me the Father’s dispositions in the Son as well. I see that the Ninevites obtained forgiveness of their crimes from the Creator—not to say, even then, from Christ, since from [Col. 0378A] the beginning he acted in the Father’s name. I read also that the prophet Nathan said to David, when he acknowledged his offense concerning Uriah (2 Sam. 12:13): “And the Lord has put away your offense, and you shall not die.” Likewise King Ahab (1 Kings 21), Jezebel’s husband, guilty of idolatry and of Naboth’s blood, obtained forgiveness on account of repentance; and Jonathan, Saul’s son, erased by entreaty the guilt of having broken the fast (1 Sam. 14). [Note: “Jonathan, Saul’s son, the broken fast,” etc. He calls a fast that has been violated “unsealed” or “broken” (*resignatum*), for Jonathan had given occasion for violating the fast when he put honey to his mouth.—LE PR.]

Why should I rehearse the case of the people themselves, so often restored by indulgence toward their offenses? They were restored, of course, by that God who (Ezek. 33:11) prefers mercy to sacrifice and the sinner’s repentance to his death. Your first task, therefore, is to deny that the Creator ever forgave offenses; the consequence [Note: Semler and Oberthür add “is.”] must then be that you show that he proclaimed nothing of this kind concerning his Christ. In that way you will prove this new kindness [Col. 0378B] of a new Christ—if, that is, you prove that it is neither matched by the Creator nor proclaimed by the Creator.

But whether forgiving sins can belong to one who is denied to retain them; whether absolving can belong to one to whom condemning does not also belong; and whether it is fitting for one to pardon against whom no offense has been committed—we have already engaged with these questions elsewhere [Note: “Already engaged elsewhere.” The passage is Book I, chapter 26.]; and we prefer [Note: The Venice edition and Oberthür read “we preferred.”] to recall them rather than reconsider them.

Concerning the Son of Man our preliminary objection is twofold: Christ could not lie by declaring himself the Son of Man if he was not truly such; nor can one be constituted the son of man who was not born of a human being, whether father or mother. Accordingly, we must determine of which human being he should be understood as the son—of his father or of his mother. If he is from God the Father, then certainly he is not [Note: Semler and Oberthür insert “is.”] from a man. If he is not also from a man as father, it remains that he is from a human mother. If from a human being, it is already evident [Col. 0378C] that he is from a virgin. For where no human [Note: Semler and Oberthür omit “human.”] father is assigned, neither will a husband be assigned to his mother; and where no husband is assigned, she is a virgin.

Otherwise there will now be two fathers, God and man, if the mother is not a virgin. For she will have a husband, so that she is not a virgin; and by having a husband she will create two fathers, God and man, for one who would be the son both of God and of man. Fables perhaps attribute a birth of this sort to Castor or Hercules. [Note: “Castor or Hercules”: both were sons of Jupiter—Castor by Leda, the wife of Tyndareus; Hercules by Alcmena, the wife of Amphitryon.—RHEN.]

If these distinctions hold—that is, if he is the son of a human being through his mother because he is not so through his father, and if he is from a virgin mother because he is not from a human father—then this will be Isaiah’s Christ, whom he proclaims that a virgin will conceive. On what grounds, therefore, you admit the Son of Man, Marcion, I cannot see. If he is the son of a human father, you deny that he is [Col. 0379A] the Son of God; if he is also the Son of God, you turn Christ into the Hercules of a fable; if he is the son only of a human mother, you concede my position; if he is not even the son of a human mother [Note: The Venice edition, Semler, and Oberthür read “father.”], then he is the son of no human being, and he who said that he was what he was not must necessarily have committed a falsehood.

Only one thing can relieve you in your straits: if you dare either to call your god, Christ’s father, a human being as well—which Valentinus did with the Aeon [Note: Another reading: “which he did not do concerning him.”]—or to deny that a virgin is human, something not even [Note: Semler and Oberthür read “nor.”] Valentinus did. [Note: “Which Valentinus did with the Aeon.” Tertullian seems to offer relief to Marcion, reduced to the greatest straits, and to supply him with an evasion, as though he held the same opinion as Valentinus, who said that Jesus, whom he also called Christ, was composed from the Aeons. But afterward Tertullian sharply casts him down from that position.—LE PR.]

What now, if Christ is designated by the very title “Son of Man” in Daniel? Will that not suffice [Note: Semler and Oberthür read “does that not suffice?”] as proof of the prophetic Christ? For when he calls himself by that designation which was proclaimed concerning the Creator’s Christ, he undoubtedly causes himself to be understood as the very one concerning whom it was proclaimed. A mere sharing of names [Note: Junius reads “a double sharing.”] might perhaps seem possible; and yet we maintain that he ought to have been called neither Christ nor Jesus, since we uphold a distinction of status.

But the designation “Son of Man,” insofar as it arises from a circumstance, is correspondingly unlikely to coincide merely in addition to the sharing of a name. For it arises from a circumstance peculiar [Note: Junius reads “peculiarly.”] to its bearer, especially when the same cause is present through which the sharing occurs. Thus, if Marcion’s Christ too were said to have been born of a human being, then he too would be capable of sharing the designation, and there would be two Sons of Man, just as there are two Christs and two Jesuses.

Therefore, since the designation properly belongs to the one in whom it has its cause, if it is claimed [Note: Semler inserts “and.”] for another, in whom there is a sharing of the name but not also of the designation, then even the sharing of the name becomes suspect in the person for whom the sharing of the designation is claimed without cause. [Col. 0379C] It follows that the one and same person must be believed to be he who is found more qualified to possess both the name and the designation, while the other is excluded because, lacking the cause, he has no share in the designation. Nor will anyone be more qualified to possess both than he who first received both the name of Christ and the designation Son of Man—namely, the Creator’s Jesus.

He it was who was seen by the king of Babylon in the furnace with his martyrs, a fourth figure “like a son of man” (Dan. 3). The same person was revealed plainly [Note: Semler and Oberthür read “likewise he himself.”] to Daniel himself (Dan. 7) as the Son of Man, coming with the clouds of heaven as judge, just as Scripture also shows.

I have said that this could have sufficed concerning the prophetic designation of the Son of Man. But Scripture supplies me with more—namely, through the interpretation of the Lord himself. For when the Jews, looking upon him merely as a man and not yet certain that he was also God, as also being the Son of God, reasonably argued that a man could not forgive offenses, but God alone could, why did he not answer them in accordance with their point concerning a man (Luke 5:24), [Note: The Venice edition and Rigault read “would he answer.”] saying that he had authority to forgive offenses, since in naming the Son of Man he would also be naming a man? The reason must be that he wished, by the very designation “Son of Man,” drawn from the document of Daniel, to strike back at them and show that he who forgave offenses was both God [Note: Another reading: “him.”] and man—namely, that sole Son of Man who, in Daniel’s prophecy, had obtained the authority to judge and therefore surely also to forgive offenses; for he who judges also acquits. Thus, once this stumbling block had been removed by the recollection of Scripture, [Col. 0380B] they might more easily recognize from the very forgiveness of sins that he was the Son of Man.

[Note: “Finally, nowhere up to this point had he declared,” etc. The Author is speaking only of Luke’s Gospel, according to which Christ first declared himself the Son of Man in this passage. For if we speak of all the Gospels, St. Matthew records before this miracle Christ’s saying, [Col. 0380D] “The Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head,” which is first repeated in Luke 9. Whether those words of Christ occurred before this miracle, as those who composed Gospel harmonies contend, or afterward, as the Author seems to indicate, I leave for others to dispute.—PAM.]

Finally, nowhere up to this point had he declared himself the Son of Man; he did so first in this passage, in which he first forgave sins—that is, in which he first judged, by acquitting. Whatever the opposing side may argue in response to this, consider [Note: Semler and Oberthür read “inspect.”] what kind of argument it is. For it must tend toward such madness that it both maintains that he is the Son of Man without making him a liar, and yet denies that he was born of a human being, lest it concede that he is the virgin’s son.

But since divine authority, the nature of things, and common wisdom do not admit [Note: Semler and Oberthür insert “into.”] this heretical insanity, here too there is occasion to interpose the briefest possible remark about the substance of the body against Marcion’s phantasms. If he was born of a human being, as the Son of Man, he is a body from a body. [Col. 0380C] Indeed, you might more easily find a human being born without a heart or a brain—such as Marcion himself—than without a body, as Marcion’s Christ is supposed to have been. Just so: inspect the heart or brain of that man from Pontus.

## CHAPTER XI

He brings forward the publican whom the Lord chose as an argument, as though he had been chosen by an opponent of the Law, one foreign to the Law and profane in relation to Judaism. [Note: “Foreign to the Law and profane in relation to Judaism,” etc. Above too he has more than once joined *extraneus* (“foreign”) with the genitive. Here he also said “profane in relation to Judaism,” meaning one rejected by the Jews as execrable.—RHEN.] He has forgotten even Peter, a man of the Law, who nevertheless was not merely chosen but also obtained testimony that his recognition had been granted by the Father (Matt. 16:17).

He had nowhere read that Christ was proclaimed as the light, hope, and expectation of the nations. Rather, Christ confirmed the Jews’ position by saying (Luke 5:31) that a physician is not necessary for the healthy but for those who are ill. For if he wished the Gentiles and publicans whom he was choosing to be understood as the sick, he was affirming that the Jews were healthy, since he denied that a physician was necessary for them. If this is so, he did wrong to descend for the destruction of the Law, as though to remedy an illness, when those who lived under it were healthy and did not need a physician.

Moreover, what sense does it make for him to have proposed the analogy of a physician without fulfilling it? For just as no one brings a physician to the healthy, so neither does one bring his own physician to persons so utterly [Note: Junius reads “insofar as.”] foreign to him as man is to Marcion’s god—man who has his own author and protector and ought rather to have Christ as physician from that author. This analogy establishes in advance that the physician is more properly supplied by the one to whom the sick belong.

And from where did [Col. 0381B] John suddenly appear in their midst? Suddenly Christ, and suddenly John! Everything that possesses its own complete order in the Creator’s dispensation appears in this way in Marcion’s. But more about John elsewhere; for [Note: Rigault and the Frankfurt edition omit “for.”] we must answer each of the points presently before us. For now I shall maintain this: I shall demonstrate that John accords with Christ, and Christ with John—John being, of course, a prophet of the Creator, and Christ being the Creator’s Christ. Thus the heretic may be ashamed that he has vainly frustrated John’s proper sequence.

For if John had performed no ministry whatsoever in accordance with Isaiah (Isa. 40:3), as the crier in the wilderness and preparer of the Lord’s ways through the proclamation and commendation of repentance; if he had not also baptized Christ himself among the others—then no one would have contrasted Christ’s disciples, who ate and drank, with the pattern of John’s disciples, [Col. 0381C] who fasted and prayed continually. For if any divergence existed between Christ and John and between the flock of each, there would be no demand for comparison, and the object of the challenge would be pointless. No one would be surprised or distressed if rival proclamations belonging to different deities also failed to agree with each other concerning their disciplines, since they had first failed to agree concerning the authorities from which those disciplines came.

So entirely did Christ belong to John, and John to Christ: both belonged to the Creator, and both were preachers and teachers arising from the Law and the Prophets. Otherwise Christ would also have rejected John’s discipline as belonging to another god, and would have defended his disciples as rightly walking in a different manner, since they had been initiated into a different and contrary divinity.

But as things stand, by humbly giving as his reason that the bridegroom’s sons could not fast so long as the bridegroom was with them, while promising that they would fast afterward, when the bridegroom had been taken away from them, he did not defend his disciples but rather excused them, as though they had not been reproached without reason. [Col. 0382A] Nor did he reject John’s discipline, but rather conceded it, assigning it to John’s time and reserving [Note: Junius reads “and.”] it for [Note: Rhenanus reads “of the time.”] his own time. Otherwise he would have rejected it and defended its rivals [Note: Semler and Oberthür read “reserving it, if not,” omitting the remaining words.], if the discipline that already existed had not been his own.

I hold fast to [Note: The Paris and Frankfurt editions read “And now I hold fast to.”] my Christ even under the name of bridegroom, concerning whom the Psalm says (Ps. 19:5–6): “He is like a bridegroom coming forth from his chamber; his departure is from the summit of heaven, and his turning aside reaches even to its summit.” [Note: “And his turning aside,” etc. We have also read *devertere* and *deversari* above. A little later we have written: “This bride Christ also calls to himself through Solomon.”—RHEN.]

He also rejoices before the Father through Isaiah: “Let my soul exult,” he says (Isa. 61:10), “in the Lord, for he has clothed me with the garment of salvation and the tunic of gladness, like a bridegroom; he has placed a headdress upon me like a bride.” [Note: The Frankfurt edition, following the Septuagint, reads: “And like a bride he adorned me with adornment.”] For in himself [Note: Semler and Oberthür omit “for” (*enim*).] he also reckons the Church, concerning which the same Spirit says to him (Isa. 49:18): “And you shall put all of them around yourself like a bride’s adornment.” [Note: The Frankfurt edition reads “a bride.”]

[Col. 0382B] This bride Christ also [Note: Semler and Oberthür omit “also.”] summons to himself through Solomon by the calling of the Gentiles. For you have read (Song 4:8): “Come, bride, from Lebanon.” The mention of Mount Lebanon is elegantly introduced, for among the Greeks “Lebanon” is a term for frankincense; [Note: “Which is a term for frankincense among the Greeks.” In Theophrastus and Dioscorides it denotes frankincense and the frankincense-producing tree. In Dioscorides, Book I, chapter 38, φλοιὸς λιβάνου means “the bark of the frankincense tree.” Among the Hebrews too, frankincense is named from its whiteness, and Mount Lebanon is distinguished by its loftiest cedars.—LE PR.] for he will betroth the Church to himself out of idolatry.

Now deny that you are utterly insane, Marcion. Look: you attack even the law of your own god. He does not join marriages; he does not accept marriages already joined; he baptizes [Note: Semler and Oberthür read *tingit* for *tinguit*.] no one unless celibate or a eunuch; he reserves baptism until death or repudiation. Why, then, do you make his Christ a bridegroom? That title belongs to him who joined male and female, not to him who separated them.

You have also erred in that pronouncement of the Lord in which he seems to distinguish new things from old. You have been puffed up with old wineskins and had your brain knocked out by new wine; and thus you have sewn the patch of heretical novelty onto the old—that is, the earlier—Gospel. In what respect, [Note: The Venice edition reads *Inquo*.] I should like to learn, is the Creator different [Note: Some read “otherwise.”], when through Jeremiah (Jer. 4:3) he commands: “Renew for yourselves a new renewal,” does he not turn them away from old things? When he declares through Isaiah (Isa. 43:18–19), “The old things have passed away; behold the new things that I am making,” does he not turn them toward new things?

We established long ago that this appointed disposition of the former things, promised rather by the Creator, is manifested by Christ under the authority of one and the same God, to whom both the old and the new belong. For neither does he put new wine into old wineskins unless he has also possessed the old wineskins; nor does anyone put a new patch on an old garment unless he has also had the old garment. A person does not refrain from doing something, if it ought not to be done, unless he possesses the means from which he could do it, if it ought to be done.

Accordingly, [Note: Semler and Oberthür insert “and.”] if he directed the analogy to this purpose, to show that he was separating the newness of the Gospel from the oldness of the Law, he was showing that the Law from which he separated it was his own. The separation was not to be characterized as a separation of one’s own things from things belonging to others, because no one joins his own things to another’s in order afterward to be able to separate them from those other things. Separation presupposes the conjunction from which it is made. Thus the things [Note: Junius reads “And so also the things.”] that he separated he also showed to have been united in one, just as they would have remained had he not separated them.

Yet we concede this separation as one effected through reform, enlargement, and advancement, just as the fruit is separated from the seed although it is fruit arising from the seed. In the same way the Gospel is separated from the Law as it develops out of the Law: it is something other than the Law, but not foreign to it; different, but not contrary.

Nor is the form of Christ’s speech new. When he presents analogies and resolves questions, he comes from the seventy-seventh Psalm: “I shall open,” he says, “my mouth in a parable”—that is, in an analogy—“I shall utter problems”—that is, I shall expound questions. [Note: “I shall open my mouth in a parable.” That is: I shall shape my words to the capacity of human weakness. Concerning the parables of the ancient writers, Seneca says in Letter 59: “Those who spoke simply and for the sake of demonstrating a matter are full of parables, which I consider necessary—not for the same reason as poets do, but so that they may be supports for our weakness and may bring the learner and hearer into the immediate presence of the matter.” Cicero calls a parable a comparison. Quintilian, Book VIII: “In every parable, either the analogy comes first and the subject follows, or the subject comes first and the analogy follows.”—RIG.]

If you had wished to prove that a man belonged to another nation, you would surely have proved it from the distinctive character of his speech.

## CHAPTER XII

Concerning the Sabbath too, I first submit that this question could not have arisen [Note: Rigault and the Venice edition read “not even.”] unless Christ carried about with him the title “Lord [Note: Semler and Oberthür read “God.”] of the Sabbath.” [Note: “Unless Christ carried about the title Lord of the Sabbath,” etc. Here Tertullian alludes to Christ’s saying, “The Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath,” about which Epiphanius says: “At once,” he says, “the Savior confesses himself to be two things together, both Son of Man and Lord of the Sabbath, teaching that the Sabbath should not be regarded as foreign to his own creation.”—PAM.]

For there would have been no dispute over why he was destroying the Sabbath if he ought to destroy it. Moreover, he ought to destroy it if he belonged to another god, and no one would have been surprised that he did what was appropriate to him. They were therefore surprised because it was not appropriate for him to profess the Creator as God and yet attack his Sabbath.

And so that we may settle each of the initial points and not have to raise the same arguments everywhere against every argument of our opponent that relies on some new institution of Christ, the following definition will now stand: the reason why the newness of each institution was disputed was that up to this point nothing had been disclosed concerning a new divinity, just as nothing had been disputed concerning one. Nor can it be retorted that the very newness of each institution sufficiently demonstrated that Christ belonged to another divinity, since it has been established that the newness itself was proclaimed by the Creator and therefore should not be regarded as surprising in Christ.

Surely another god ought first to have been disclosed and his discipline introduced afterward, because God confers authority upon a discipline, not a discipline upon God—unless, of course, Marcion, so thoroughly perverse, learned his letters not through a teacher but his teacher through his letters.

[Col. 0384A] I direct the remainder of my argument concerning the Sabbath as follows. If Christ interfered with the Sabbath, he acted according to the Creator’s precedent. For during the siege of the city of Jericho, when the Ark of the Covenant was carried around its walls for seven [Note: Semler and Oberthür read “eight.”] days, including the Sabbath, the Creator, by his own command, destroyed the Sabbath through work—or so those suppose who hold the same opinion concerning Christ, unaware that neither Christ nor the Creator destroyed the Sabbath, as we shall soon explain.

And yet even then the Sabbath was shaken through Jesus, so that this too might be announced in advance concerning Christ—even if he persecuted the Jews’ most solemn day with hatred, as a Christ who did not belong to the Jews. By professing hatred of the Sabbath, too, he followed the Creator, as the Creator’s Christ, who exclaims through the mouth of Isaiah (Isa. 1:14): “My soul hates your new moons and Sabbaths.” But in whatever sense these words may have been spoken, we know [Col. 0384B] that in this case too a blunt challenge must nevertheless be applied.

I shall now debate the very subject matter in which Christ’s discipline seemed to destroy the Sabbath. The disciples had become hungry; on that day they had plucked ears of grain and rubbed them in their hands. [Note: Rigault, Semler, and the Venice and Oberthür editions read *efflixerant*; the Paris edition reads *effixerant*.] By preparing food they had broken the holiday. Christ excuses them and is charged with violating the Sabbath. The Pharisees accuse him; Marcion seizes upon the issue of the controversy (that I may frame some issue by means of the truth of my Lord): the written word and the intention [Note: *Scripti et voluntatis*: γραμμάτων καὶ διανοίας, “the letter and the intention.”—RIG.].

The letter is drawn from the Creator’s Scripture, while the ostensible defense is drawn from Christ’s intention, as though from the example of David entering the temple on the Sabbath and preparing food by boldly breaking the loaves of presentation, that is, the showbread. For David too remembered the privilege granted to the Sabbath from the beginning, when the day itself was first made known: I mean exemption from fasting. For although the Creator had forbidden manna to be gathered for two days, he permitted it only on the day of preparation, so that the previous day’s provision of food might free the holiday of the following Sabbath from fasting.

It is well, therefore, that the Lord also followed the same rationale in “destroying” the Sabbath, if they wish it to be called a destruction of the Sabbath [Note: Another reading: “of the day.”]. It is well that he also expressed the Creator’s disposition by honoring the Sabbath as a day not for fasting. Indeed, he would then truly have destroyed the Sabbath and the Creator himself if he had commanded the disciples to fast on the Sabbath, contrary to the established letter and intention of the Creator.

But because [Note: “He did not defend the disciples resolutely.” That is, not forcefully, in the sense in which Fabius Quintilian said in *Institutes*, Book V: “We should by now not even say that one bleats or neighs forcefully, unless we were supported by the judgment of antiquity.”—RIG.] he did not defend the disciples unconditionally but excuses them; because [Note: “He sets human necessity against it, as though pleading for them.” The excuse Christ presented to the people did not destroy the Sabbath. The necessity for food was too immediate for the time of gathering it to be postponed to another occasion. He therefore permitted it, yet did not abolish the Sabbath.—LE PR.] he sets human necessity against the charge as their advocate; [Col. 0385A] because he preserves as the higher honor of the Sabbath the duty not to cause sorrow rather than the duty to remain idle; because he places David and his companions on equal terms with his own disciples in both guilt and pardon; because he approves what the Creator allowed; and because, following the Creator’s example, [Note: Semler inserts “and.”] he himself is so good—for all these reasons, he is supposedly foreign to the Creator!

After this (Luke 6:7), the Pharisees watch to see whether he will perform healings on the Sabbath, so that they may accuse him—certainly as a destroyer of the Sabbath, not as one who professed a new god. Perhaps I shall press this one point everywhere: no other Christ had ever been proclaimed.

The Pharisees, however, were entirely mistaken [Note: Semler reads “will be mistaken.”] concerning the law of the Sabbath because they did not notice that it conditionally prescribed rest from works, limiting it to a particular class of works. For when it says concerning the Sabbath day (Exod. 20:10), “You shall not do any work of yours upon it,” [Note: “You shall not do any work of yours upon it.” Since we have been unable to find this anywhere in the Law of Moses, which he cites here, I suspect that it was once read at Exodus 31, where today the text reads: “Everyone who performs work upon it, that soul shall perish from the midst of his people”; or rather at Leviticus 23, where the following is found very frequently: “You shall perform no servile work upon it,” and, “Everyone who performs work upon it, that soul shall perish from the midst of his people”—especially since he cites this same passage above as well, in the book *Against the Jews*, chapter 4.—PAM. Perhaps Tertullian read *tuum* (“yours”) in place of *tu* (“you”), and the Greek σοῦ in place of σύ, at Exodus 20:10, in this form: “You shall not perform upon it any work OF YOURS”; οὐ ποιήσεις ἐν αὐτῇ πᾶν [Col. 0386D] ἔργον ΣΟΥ (“you shall not do upon it any work of yours”; σοῦ in place of σύ, as in the Septuagint).—EDD.] by saying “yours” it defined human work, which each person carries out through his own craft or business, not divine work.

The work of salvation and preservation, however, is not man’s but God’s peculiar work. Thus the Law says again (Exod. 12:16): “You shall not perform any work upon it except what shall be done for every soul”—that is, for the sake of delivering a soul. [Note: “Except what shall be done for every soul.” The Septuagint indeed reads πλὴν ὅσα ποιηθήσεται πάσῃ ψυχῇ, but it might be translated very well as “except those things, whatever they may be, that are done by every soul.” The Hebrew reading expressly signifies only those things that are eaten by every living creature, almost as our Vulgate translates it.—EDD.] For God’s work may also be done through a man for the salvation [Note: The Paris and Frankfurt editions read “in the salvation.”] of a soul, though it is nevertheless done by God. This is what Christ the man was about to do, because he was also God.

Therefore, wishing to lead them into this understanding of the Law through the restoration of the withered hand, he asks: “Is it lawful on the Sabbaths to do good or not—to deliver a soul or to destroy it?” By promising [Note: The Codex Wouwerianus reads “permitting.”] the work he was about to perform for a soul, he reminded them which works the Sabbath law prohibited—namely, human works—and which it commanded—namely, divine works, those performed for every soul. He was called Lord of the Sabbath because he was protecting the Sabbath as something belonging to himself.

Even if he had destroyed it, he would have done so rightly, as being all the more its lord because he had instituted it. But as its lord he did not destroy it at all. From this it can now be evident that not even when the Ark was carried around Jericho was the Sabbath destroyed by the Creator. For that too was God’s work, which he himself had commanded and which he had arranged for the sake of the souls of his own people, who were placed in the peril of war.

And if somewhere he professed hatred of Sabbaths by saying “your Sabbaths” (Isa. 1:13), he attributed to human beings [Note: The Venice edition reads “a human being.”] those Sabbaths—not to himself—which a people full of offenses celebrated without the fear of God, loving God with their lips [Col. 0386A] but not with their heart. He assigned a different status to his own Sabbaths—that is, to all that was conducted according to his discipline—which he afterward, through the same prophet (Isa. 58:13), declares to be true, delightful, and not to be profaned.

Thus Christ too did not abolish the Sabbath at all. He preserved its law both above in the case of the disciples, when he acted for the sake of the soul by granting hungry men the comfort of food, and now when he heals the withered hand. Everywhere by his deeds he presses the declaration (Matt. 5:17): “I did not come to dissolve the Law, but to fulfill it”; nor [Note: Semler reads “if.”] did Marcion silence him by removing these words.

For here too he fulfilled the Law: by interpreting its qualification, by illuminating the distinction between works, by doing those things that the Law excepts from the Sabbath rest, and by making still holier through his beneficence the Sabbath day itself, which had been holy from the beginning through the Father’s blessing. On that day, namely, [Col. 0386B] he supplied divine assistance—something an adversary [Note: The Frankfurt edition reads “for adversaries.”] would have furnished on other days, lest he adorn the Creator’s Sabbath or render to the Sabbath the works owed to it.

If on that day the prophet Elisha also restored the Shunammite woman’s dead son to life (2 Kings 4:23), you see, Pharisee—and you too, Marcion—that from long ago it belonged to the Creator to do good on the Sabbaths, to deliver a soul and not destroy it. Christ introduced nothing new that did not conform to the pattern, gentleness, mercy, and [Note: The Frankfurt edition reads “and.”] proclamation of the Creator.

For here too he embodies a prophecy of a particular healing. The loosened hands grow strong, just as the loosened knees did in the paralytic.
