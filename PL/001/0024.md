Into this Montanist heresy Tertullian gradually fell. First, from a love of chastity, he wrote to his wife a book on single marriage, in which he indeed discourages second marriages, but does not condemn them as fornication. Indeed, since in Book II *To His Wife* he argues at length that, if women wish to marry, they ought at least to marry “in the Lord,” that is, a Christian, he does not appear at that time to have condemned second marriages altogether. Nevertheless, these two books are not entirely free from error. Most notably, in Book I, chapter 1, no. 11, he calls second [Col. 0202C] marriages “confusion”; in chapter 4, no. 23, “an affront”; in chapter 5, no. 34, “detestable”; and elsewhere also “filthy” and “a voluptuous disgrace.” Such too are those passages already cited from the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*. That he was speaking in these passages of second marriages, not first marriages, is established by these words of St. Augustine, Book I *On the Good of Widowhood*, which are also offered here as an antidote:

> Because the Apostle called the good of the unmarried honorable, we ought not therefore to suppose that the marriage bond is disgraceful; otherwise we shall condemn even first marriages, which neither the Cataphrygians nor the Novatians, nor Tertullian, their most eloquent supporter, dared to call disgraceful.

Thereafter, in the book *Against Hermogenes*, he discloses his error more openly in these words:

> He defends the law of God in lust; and, wholly an adulterer both in preaching and in flesh, [Col. 0202D] he reeks of contact with those who marry.

Finally, in the books *On the Exhortation to Chastity* and *On Monogamy*, following Montanus, he not only calls second marriages fornication, adultery, and illicit intercourse, but also attempts to prove this by various arguments—though in vain, as will be evident to the reader from the antidote supplied by our annotations on both works. To these, lest we become too lengthy here, we refer the reader. There, in support of the catholic position that second marriages are not forbidden, we have cited at intervals Blessed Jerome’s letter *On Monogamy* to Ageruchia, commonly called Gerontia; his writings *On Widowhood* to Salvina and to Pammachius; St. Augustine’s book *On the Good of Widowhood* and Book II *Against the Adversary of the Law and the Prophets*; St. Epiphanius *Against Montanus*, Heresy 59, and *Against the Cathari*; and the ancient commentaries on Paul’s Epistles, Romans 7, 1 Corinthians 7, and 1 Corinthians 5. To these [Col. 0203A] may be added that statement of Blessed Jerome in Letter 54 to Marcella—although elsewhere he sometimes appears rather unfavorable toward them—in defense of second marriages:

> We do not so much seek second marriages as permit them, since Paul commands young widows to marry; they, however—namely, the followers of Montanus—regard repeated marriages as so criminal that anyone who has done this is considered an adulterer.

Likewise there is the statement in his commentary *On the Epistle to Titus*, also cited by us elsewhere:

> Tertullian too wrote a heretical book *On Monogamy*; no one who has read the Apostle will fail to know that it contradicts the Apostle.

Likewise St. Augustine, in *On Heresies*, heresy 126:

> Tertullian therefore became a heretic because, passing over to the Cataphrygians whom he had previously destroyed, he also began, contrary to apostolic teaching, to condemn second marriages as fornication.

[Col. 0203B] **26. On Montanus as the Paraclete.** Vol. II, *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 1, no. 6. Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chapter 51, no. 613, and chapter 58, no. 652. *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 11, no. 98, and the final chapter, final number. *Against Marcion*, Book I, chapter 29, no. 203, and Book V, chapter 15, no. 247; and *Against Praxeas*, chapter 1, no. 11; chapter 2, no. 22; chapter 8, no. 59; chapter 13, no. 78. Vol. V, *On Monogamy*, the whole of chapter 2, and more than once in the following books: *On Flight in Persecution*, *On Fasts*, and *On Modesty*.

Against this exceedingly absurd heresy, Tertullian’s own words from before his heresy may suffice. In *On Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 52, heresy 21, no. 327, he calls it a blasphemy of those heretics who were [Col. 0203C] called Cataphrygians that they said that the Holy Spirit had indeed been in the Apostles, but that the Paraclete had not; and that they said the Paraclete had spoken more through Montanus than Christ had set forth in the Gospel. And yet the wretched man—perhaps led astray by them because they detested second marriages—in so many passages already cited calls Montanus the Paraclete and repeatedly cites his words under the title of “the Paraclete.” Indeed, he twists to support this heresy of his the passage in John 16:

> I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now. When that Spirit of truth has come, he will teach you all truth and will announce to you the things that are to come.

Yet he himself interprets this passage in a catholic sense in the aforementioned book *On Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 8, no. 62, speaking of the Apostles:

> They were soon to receive the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete, who was to lead them [Col. 0203D] into all truth.

And again:

> The Apostles, destined for the nations, were themselves also to receive the Paraclete as their teacher.

There he treats the Holy Spirit and the Paraclete as the same, whereas they wrongly distinguished the Holy Spirit from the Paraclete, as has been stated from Tertullian himself. The very context of the Evangelist establishes this; and thus all the ancients interpret that passage, while St. Augustine expressly argues against this heresy in three tractates on the Gospel of John. For the sake of brevity, we refer the reader to them.

**27. On the denial of penance to adulterers.** Vol. V, the entire book *On Modesty*.

As we noted in the Life of the Author, Montanus and his followers, the Cataphrygians, entirely abolished [Col. 0204A] all penance—as is attested by Blessed Jerome in his often-cited letter to Marcella and by St. Pacian in his treatise *Against the Novatians*. Tertullian differs from them in this respect: he believed it ought to be denied only to adulterers. Perhaps at first he was led astray by certain bishops of his own age who, as St. Cyprian attests in the book *On the Lapsed*, did not think that penance should be granted to adulterers. Yet, apart from the fact that Cyprian there proves the contrary position from Scripture, namely, the Church’s power to grant pardon for every kind of sin, the Author, while still catholic, in Vol. II, in the book *On Penance*—which Cyprian there imitates—expressly demonstrates that penance is necessary for all offenses.

And this is what he says in *On Modesty*, chapter 1, no. 9:

> In opposition even to the former fellowship of [Col. 0204B] my own opinion with theirs.

St. Pacian speaks in much the same way when addressing the Novatians, in words also cited more than once elsewhere:

> Tertullian after his heresy belongs to you, for you have taken many things from him. You will hear him confessing in his own letter, and in one which he published while a catholic, that the Church can forgive sins.

Likewise St. Jerome says that Tertullian, in the book *On Modesty* which he wrote against penance, overturned his former judgment by a new opinion, in this following his own insane and blasphemous women.

To these the reader may add all those writings of the ancients on penance of which we have made mention in Vol. II, in the aforementioned book *On Penance*, chapter 1, and what we have said both on that book and on the other, *On [Col. 0204C] Modesty*. There, in response to each of the Author’s arguments to the contrary, we have generally answered from Pacian, adding for this purpose an exposition of several passages of Scripture, often from Tertullian himself.

At chapter 9, nos. 72 and 73, we also cited St. Jerome’s letter to Damasus *On the Two Sons*, where he expressly argues against the Author and, among other things, defends the position that the publicans were not Gentiles, as Tertullian wished, but Jews. He proves this by the examples of Matthew, an Apostle from the circumcision; of the publicans baptized by John; and of the publican praying in the temple, Luke 5, 7, and 18. If they had been Gentiles, Christ would not have dined with them, lest he break the law, nor would this publican have been admitted into the temple. Indeed, whenever it was noted as a fault in Christ that he associated with publicans, sinners [Col. 0204D] and publicans are almost always joined together; and thus he was not reproached because he dined with Gentiles, but because he dined with sinners.

To this pertains the Pharisee’s murmuring in Luke 7 concerning the sinful woman, in whom Tertullian himself recognizes both penance and pardon in Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 18, no. 265. Yet the Author nowhere dares to call her a Gentile, because it is established that her village was near Jerusalem, John 11.

Nor does the statement, “There shall be no one paying tribute among the sons of Israel,” do anything for the Author’s case. For even if they were not obliged to pay tax before, by God’s disposition, they were subjected to the Romans—and although it is sufficiently established that the occupation of the publicans was hateful and infamous—it nevertheless appears from the words of John the Baptist to them, Luke 3, that it could also be practiced without sin:

> Do nothing beyond [Col. 0205A] what has been appointed for you,

or, as it is translated from the Greek:

> Exact nothing beyond what has been appointed for you.

For the rest, let the diligent reader consult the aforementioned annotations of ours.

**28. On “Psychics,” the name with which he slandered Catholics.** Vol. III, *Against Marcion*, Book IV, chapter 22, no. 325; *Against Praxeas*, chapter 1, no. 17. Vol. V, *On Monogamy*, chapter 1, nos. 4 and 5; chapter 11, nos. 86, 87, and 88; and chapter 16, no. 120; the entire book *On Fasting Against the Psychics*; and *On Modesty*, chapter 1, no. 8; chapter 6, no. 47; chapter 10, no. 83; and chapter 21, no. 190.

We have sufficiently demonstrated, even from the Apostle, 1 Corinthians 2, at the beginning of *On Fasting Against the Psychics*, no. 2, that “Psychics” means “animal” or “natural men.” After he had fallen into heresy, [Col. 0205B] Tertullian fastened this name upon Catholics, while on the contrary calling his own Montanists “spiritual,” because they followed Montanus as the spirit and Paraclete. He thereby slandered Catholics as if they were wholly animal men, following *passivitas*, that is, license in life, because they entered into second marriages, avoided martyrdom by flight, and rejected the Montanist fasts.

But the wretched man failed to notice that Montanus had led an animal life, since Priscilla and Maximilla, who abandoned their husbands and followed Montanus, were harlots; that their followers escaped martyrdom by denying Christ; that, if they suffered anything, they endured it because of robberies previously committed; and finally that, although they observed three Lents—on which more presently—they nevertheless indulged their appetites during the rest of the year.

Nor, however, was it new for heretics to call Catholics [Col. 0205C] “Psychics” and themselves “spiritual”; for the Author himself, following St. Irenaeus, ridicules precisely this practice among the Valentinians in *Against the Valentinians*, chapters 29 and 30. After them Blessed Epiphanius also wrote of it in his book *On Heresies*.

**29. On the two Lents.** Vol. V, *On Fasting Against the Psychics*, written in their defense, especially chapter 15, no. 90.

Since the Montanists annually observed three full Lents of seven weeks each, Tertullian appears to have differed from them in this respect after he began to hold separate assemblies apart from them: he observed only two. For thus he writes:

> How slight is our prohibition of foods? We offer to God two weeks of xerophagy in the year, [Col. 0205D] and not even the whole of those, since Sabbaths and Sundays are of course excepted.

Yet they are not on that account to be judged heretics. For Amalarius, Book I *On the Divine Offices*, chapter 36, and Book IV, chapter 37, describes the fasts of certain devout persons who observed three Lents: one before Easter, according to apostolic tradition; another from devotion before St. John’s feast—or rather after Pentecost; and a third before the Nativity of the Lord. Similarly, our *Micrologus*, in the book *On Ecclesiastical Observances*, chapter 35, records a second Lent for penitents, which even today is customary among certain monks.

To this also pertains the statement of St. Jerome in his letter to Lucinius of Baetica, cited by Amalarius:

> Would that we could fast at all times, as we read in the Acts of the Apostles that the Apostle Paul and the believers with him did on the days of Pentecost and [Col. 0206A] on the Lord’s Day.

Likewise relevant are those extremely rigorous fasts of the early monks, described at length by Jerome as well as by Theodoret and Nicephorus.

But what was condemned as heresy among the Montanists was their insistence upon the necessity of observing these Lents, whether three or two, to such a degree that for this very reason they slandered Catholics with the name “Psychics”—that is, as has already been said, “animal men”—because Catholics were unwilling to observe them together with the Montanists. And this is what St. Jerome urges in Letter 54 to Marcella against Montanus:

> We fast for one Lent, according to the tradition of the Apostles, at the season appropriate to us; they keep three Lents in the year, as though three Saviors had suffered. It is not that [Col. 0206B] one may not fast throughout the whole year, except during Pentecost, but that it is one thing to offer a gift under necessity and another to offer it voluntarily.

Hence Eusebius—according to the more accurate translation from the Greek, previously corrupted by certain persons so that they might condemn ecclesiastical fasts generally as Montanist—in Book V of the *Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 17; Theodoret, *Heretical Fables*, Book III, on the Montanists; and Nicephorus, Book IV of the *Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 22, write that Montanus introduced and sanctioned new laws of fasting contrary to the custom of the Church.

**30. On divorce as lawful for Christians on account of adultery, so that they may marry another.** Vol. II, Book II *To His Wife*, chapter 1, no. 4. Vol. III, Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 34, no. 537.

[Col. 0206C] At first sight Tertullian indeed appears to hold some such position when he compares divorce to the death of a husband, in order to prove more strongly that after the latter it is not lawful to marry another, just as it is not lawful after divorce except on account of adultery. But he explains himself in Vol. V, *On Monogamy*, chapter 9, no. 70, and chapter 11, no. 89. In the former passage he speaks as follows of a divorced wife:

> So that the admission of another man, whether through marriage or promiscuously, may be pronounced by God to be altogether adultery.

And soon afterward, more clearly:

> The dissolution was effected, of course, by death, not by divorce, because the Apostle would not permit divorced persons to marry contrary to the former commandment.

Away, then, with Erasmus and his followers, who wrongly cite the Author in their own support. Certainly the contrary has now been defined by the Council of Trent, session 34, canon 9.

[Col. 0206D] **31. Concerning the Trinity.** Vol. I, Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 21, no. 316; *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 3, no. 15; *Against Praxeas*, chapter 3, nos. 29 and 34; chapter 4, no. 39; chapter 8, nos. 55, 59, and 60; chapter 9, nos. 62 and 63. Likewise Vol. V, Novatian, *On the Trinity*, chapter 11, no. 56; chapter 22, no. 98; chapter 24, no. 106; and chapter 31, no. 141.

That Tertullian did not doubt the faith of the Trinity—that is, of three Persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, but of one essence—is clearer from very many passages of his than to require proof, especially from his entire book on that subject and, according to our edition, bearing that very title, *Against Praxeas*. There, against Praxeas and his Montanist followers, who were called Aeschinians, he proves not only that the same person [Col. 0207A] cannot be called both Father and Son, but also expressly asserts:

> The rule of faith which has run down from the beginning of the Gospel orders unity into a Trinity: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Novatian speaks no differently in his book *On the Trinity*. In chapters 1, 3, and 29, he sets forth the rule of faith by which we believe in God the Father and almighty Lord, the supremely perfect Creator of all things; in the Son of God, Jesus Christ, our Lord and God; and also in the Holy Spirit.

But when we ascribe to them paradoxes concerning the Trinity, we mean only that they sometimes speak obscurely on this subject, since they lived before every heresy concerning the Trinity. And lest anyone should perhaps take from such passages an occasion for his own error or heresy, we add how they may be explained according to a sound understanding.

We now omit, first, [Col. 0207B] Rhenanus’s slanders against the scholastic theologians, contained in his admonition to the reader concerning certain doctrines of Tertullian—especially since he slanders Tertullian himself as well, both regarding certain corrupted or misunderstood passages, on which more presently, and because he falsely ascribes to him the heresy of those who think that in the Eucharist the body of Christ is present only figuratively. We vindicated Tertullian from this charge in our annotations on Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 40, no. 662. He also places among Tertullian’s doctrines or paradoxes those things which Tertullian most admirably develops at length, not only concerning public penance but also, as Rhenanus himself admits, concerning secret confession, in the book *On Penance*. Rhenanus did not hesitate even to doubt whether that book was Tertullian’s, although St. Pacian copied many things from it.

For this reason I am all the more astonished that, in the latest Paris edition of the year 1580, not only was [Col. 0207C] that entire admonition retained, but all of Rhenanus’s errors—many of them savoring of heresy—were also retained without any antidote being added. Thus the catholic reader must be warned that he should avoid those annotations no less—even when Rhenanus’s name is omitted—than those which were prohibited by the Roman pontiffs Pius IV and Pius V in earlier editions until they should be expurgated.

But let us come to the matter itself. First, where Tertullian speaks as follows in the *Apology* and *Against Praxeas*:

> They are three, not in condition but in degree; not in substance but in form; not in power but in kind; yet of one substance, one condition, and one power, because there is one God, from whom are [Col. 0207D] those degrees and forms and kinds.

By “condition” he means what the scholastics—and he himself also—call “substance”; by “degree,” what they call “notion”; by “form,” what they call “properties”; and by “kind,” what they call “mode of possessing.”

Next, the statement in *Against Hermogenes*—

> There was a time when neither sin nor the Son existed—

was spoken more forcefully out of zeal according to knowledge, to show that matter is not eternal. By it he intended to signify only what St. Hilary says in Book XII *On the Trinity*: that the Father alone is eternal, but that the Son is coeternal with him. I omit that in the same place Hilary answers similar words proposed to himself in the person of an Arian:

> This exceeds human sense and the understanding of the world; the reasoning of human understanding does not grasp it, but it is the profession of faithful wisdom.

The Author explains himself in the same sense [Col. 0208A] in *Against Praxeas*, chapter 5, no. 43 and following, and throughout chapters 6 and 7.

Thereafter, in these words from the same book *Against Praxeas*—

> assigning the second and third places of rank to the Son and the Holy Spirit, who share the substance of the Father—

he means what the scholastics say: that the Son and the Holy Spirit are produced by two emanations from the unproduced Father; or that the Son is the second Person and the Holy Spirit the third, as he afterward says more than once.

But Rhenanus wrongly charges him there with calling the Son and the Holy Spirit members, chief agents, and instruments, since those words refer rather to the angels, who are alien to the Father’s substance yet do not overthrow the monarchy of God. Similarly, when Tertullian calls the Holy Spirit the third degree, by “degree” he means origin.

Again, what in *Against Praxeas* [non-Latin text omitted] he attributes not only [Col. 0208B] to the Holy Spirit but also to the Son from the Father, Saints Hilary and Augustine, in their books *On the Trinity*, and other later Fathers prefer to distinguish in such a way as to attribute generation to the Son and procession to the Holy Spirit.

When in the same place he interprets this as the bringing forth of one thing from another, he understands the Persons as really, but not essentially, distinct; for he continues:

> Because we say that the Son was brought forth from the Father, but not separated.

Those propositions must also be interpreted suitably:

> Every origin is a parent, and everything brought forth from an origin is offspring;

and likewise:

> The Trinity, descending from the Father through connected and interwoven degrees.

Now, where he says:

> The Father is the whole substance, while the Son is a derivation and portion of the whole,

he takes “whole” to mean “full” or “fullness,” no differently from the scholastic doctors when they say: The Father possesses the fullness [Col. 0208C] of deity in the first moment of origin, because he has it from himself; the Son in the second moment of origin, because it is communicated to him by the Father through generation from one; and the Holy Spirit in the third moment, because from two through spiration he receives the same numerically identical essence.

And this is what the Author afterward expressed in other words:

> From the one Father are all things which are in God through unity of substance, and in the unity is a Trinity of one substance.

Likewise:

> Unity, deriving the Trinity from itself, is not destroyed by it but administered,

so that “the whole substance” is the same as “one undivided substance in three.”

Finally, the Son appears to be called a “portion” by the Author because he is neither the Father nor the Holy Spirit, although the three Persons are one God, and the Son is not the one who is the Father, nor the Father the one who is [Col. 0208D] the Holy Spirit.

When, however, he says in the same place that the Father is greater than the Son, this must be understood not with respect to the deity, as though it were lesser in the Son, but insofar as—as the following words indicate—the Son, begotten by the Father, is begotten.

And although the following statement is expressed rather obscurely—

> The very fact that they are called Father and Son: is not the one something other than the other?

—he afterward explains himself admirably, so that he agrees with the scholastics that the Father is called one person, the Son another, and the Holy Spirit another, but not one thing and another thing, because the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are not the same Person, yet are of the same substance.

If there are any other statements in the aforementioned book *Against Praxeas* that are expressed too harshly or obscurely, let the reader remember that this entire book must be read cautiously and understood favorably.

Let us return to Novatian’s book *On the Trinity*. First, his statement [Col. 0209A] in chapter 11—

> with the divinity of the Word mingled in the very union—

even if something similar is found in the aforementioned book *Against Praxeas*, must be understood of a mingling by union, not of a mingling of the divine and human natures. We noted this there at no. 56, confirming it from several other passages of that book. The right faith, according to the Athanasian Creed, is that we believe and confess that Christ is one, not by conversion of the divinity into flesh, but by assumption of humanity into God; altogether one, not by confusion of substance, but by unity of person.

Next, the statement in the same place—

> Before whom there was nothing except the Father—

must be understood in the same sense as what we cited above from Tertullian’s *Against Hermogenes*, in accordance with what [Col. 0209B] we noted on that same book, chapter 31, no. 141.

[Col. 0210A] Nor is his statement there that the Son is lesser than the Father to be understood otherwise than we have already explained: he is lesser insofar as he is the Son. The passage which appears to interpret the unity of the Father and Son as no more than a concord of fellowship must also be read cautiously, since for this position he wrongly adduces 1 Corinthians 3; for catholic faith and Scripture also recognize the reality of essence in the Father and the Son.

Finally, if he does not appear to agree with the catholic tradition of the Fathers from Scripture concerning the Holy Spirit, we think this occurred not because he denied that the Holy Spirit is equal to the Father and the Son, but because, before the matter had been defined, the ancient Fathers customarily spoke more obscurely on account of the subject’s supreme obscurity—yet in such a way that their words admit some suitable sense.

## Proverbial Expressions Contained Throughout This Tertullianic Work, by Blessed Rhenanus and Andrew Hoy of Bruges

[Col. 0209]

# PROVERBIAL EXPRESSIONS CONTAINED THROUGHOUT THIS TERTULLIANIC WORK, ILLUSTRATED WITH BRIEF SCHOLIA, BY BLESSED RHENANUS AND ANDREW HOY OF BRUGES

[Col. 0209B] **1. He has gone away now, and must return.** Vol. I, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, chapter 4, no. 22.

Tertullian himself explains at great length the use of this proverb, which is also customary among Germans and Gauls: for a man “to have gone away” is for the soul to have departed from the body, [Col. 0209C] and thus for him to have ceased to be among the living. Thus the Greeks customarily use these words, [non-Latin text omitted], that is, “to die”; all these expressions signify departure and withdrawal.

The entire force of the proverb appears to reside in the little word “now,” which suggests a long interval of time. Such was the force of the truth concerning the resurrection and the immortality of the soul that, handed down as though from hand to hand by their ancestors, it could not be torn from human minds even amid such profound darkness of vain opinions. Instead, it was preserved in a familiar and commonly used proverb concerning those who had long since died, as though people presumed—that is, anticipated and grasped beforehand in their minds—the resurrection of bodies, or the return to their bodies of the souls that had departed: [non-Latin text omitted], that is, “to return,” [non-Latin text omitted].

[Col. 0209D] **2. To leap across a very wide chasm.** Vol. III, Book V *Against Marcion*, chapter 14, no. 231.

“To leap,” in the sense of “to leap over” or “cross by a jump.” The full meaning is to omit something altogether, or at least to pass over the intervening words. It is taken from those who prefer to shorten a journey by a leap rather than incur loss by taking the easier walk. Thus Scaliger to Cardan:

> You fly across the broadest field with the lightest leap.

Tertullian soon explains himself:

> You have removed so much from the Scriptures,

that is, you have cut out, rejected, and broken off so much that very large gaps remain in the continuous text of the books. In Book V against the same opponent:

> What great pits he has made in the Epistle by removing what he pleased.

Virgil, Book IV:

> With these words she breaks off her speech midway.

[Col. 0210B] **3. To transfix with one’s gaze.** Vol. I, *On the Pallium*, chapter 4, no. 84.

It means to look sharply, with one’s eyes intent upon and, as it were, fastened upon the object: [non-Latin text omitted]. Virgil:

> . . . . . . His eyes fixed upon the dreadful maiden.

Thus those who are clothed in garments alien to nature’s moderation—garments which drive men [Col. 0210C] obliquely from an upright condition—offer themselves openly to be looked at, or rather laughed at, and seem to wish to turn everyone’s eyes upon themselves. Yet this kind of inspection or spectacle, which they court, is joined with the disgrace and insult of those who wear such garments, so that it counts not as praise but as blame.

To this are appended: “To point out with the finger” and “To betray by a nod.” On these below.

**4. The drawn edge of the sword.** Vol. V, *On Modesty*, chapter 14, no. 115.

This is said when the reins, as it were, of full right, authority, and power are let loose in open rebuke. In Virgil:

> He went with his sword enclosed.

The opposite is Cicero’s statement in the First Oration *Against Catiline*:

> But we have now allowed the edge of this authority to grow [Col. 0210D] dull for twenty days. For we possess a decree of the Senate, but it is enclosed in the records, like a sword hidden in its sheath.

Thus in Apuleius:

> Teeth bared, sharpened and thrusting forth to inflict wounds.

**5. Nothing to do with Andromache.** Ibid., chapter 8, no. 65.

This appears to allude to a play by some ancient tragedian entitled *Andromache*. From Tertullian it may be gathered that this proverb means nothing other than the expressions “Nothing to the verse,” “Nothing to Bacchus,” and [non-Latin text omitted], that is, something foreign to the subject.

For he compares heretics to stage performers. The performers, adapting allegorical gestures to songs, represent things entirely foreign to the present play and scene—as though he had in view the Greek proverbs [non-Latin text omitted], or [non-Latin text omitted], [Col. 0211A] “to dance outside the chorus” and “to dance away from it.” The heretics, for their part, assign the same parables wherever they please and do not set them forth where they fit most aptly.

**6. To bring forth to the wrestling ground.** Vol. II, *To the Martyrs*, chapter 3, no. 44.

**RHENANUS.** The place of contest in which athletes meet is called [non-Latin text omitted] by the Greeks, because it was customarily surrounded by a trench, [non-Latin text omitted]. Hence Blessed Jerome proverbially says “to leave the wrestling ground and the place of contest,” meaning “to depart from the subject proposed” or “to leap beyond the lines.” For thus his text should be read where he writes to Pammachius against the errors of John of Jerusalem:

> Since these are the questions at issue, why do you abandon the matters over which the contest is being waged and, leaving the wrestling ground and place of contest, [Col. 0211B] linger over foreign and utterly irrelevant disputes?

Moreover, he explains himself when he adds, “and the place of contest.” For *and* here has the same force as “that is.”

It must not be overlooked that Tertullian speaks very pointedly. Athletes and gladiators are said to be “brought forth” in public spectacles by the very sponsors who present the games. Thus shortly afterward:

> So that, well trained by every hardship, we may be brought forth to the stadium of the tribunal.

According to Tertullian’s meaning, it is to lead someone into the theater, the arena, or the public place of contest, for the sake of displaying and testing his skill and strength and of carrying away the prize, or [non-Latin text omitted]; it is to descend into the arena.

Paulus Leopardus, Book I of the *Miscellanies*, chapter 22, explains the meaning of the word and rejects the interpretations of Erasmus and Caelius concerning *scamma*. Blessed Ambrose, Book I *On Duties*:

> The athletes are still on the wrestling ground,

also speaks proverbially, as though to say: [Col. 0211C] The prize is still being contested, and the palm remains uncertain.

**7. Attend to that for which we have come.** Vol. III, Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 7, no. 65.

During sacred rites silence was customarily commanded by the words:

> Keep silence,

and, in Greek, [non-Latin text omitted]. For the same reason it was customary to cry:

> Attend to this.

Plutarch appears to say this in his *Numa*: [non-Latin text omitted]. Muretus, *Various Readings*, Book 15.

It will therefore be a proverb by which we command someone to pay attention to the matter undertaken, so that he applies his whole mind to that alone, neither speaking of anything else nor considering anything else within his mind.

[Col. 0211D] **8. From contributed money.** Vol. III, *Against the Valentinians*, chapter 12, no. 136.

**RHENANUS.** Although mention is made here of a common contribution—for Tertullian says, “They contribute to a common fund,” and shortly afterward, “All contributed one good thing”; Irenaeus likewise says, “Each one contributed that which he possessed within himself as best and most flourishing, and they made a contribution”; and again shortly afterward, “The construction of the second Christ, whom they call the Savior, composed from a contribution”—nevertheless, since the manuscripts consistently have “from contributed money,” I see that one might read not so much *collatitio*, “contributed,” which is obvious to everyone, as *collocatitio*, “money invested,” in the sense of loaned and foreign money. Good men, contributing such money into a common fund and placing it with a friend engaged in business, [Col. 0212A] would thereby both enrich and assist him, first obtaining security from him.

Hence perhaps arose a proverb commonly used among Africans at that time: something was said to be done “with invested money,” or someone boasted of being rich, although he possessed no resources of his own, but only borrowed and foreign resources. Indeed, in Irenaeus elsewhere, concerning this same Savior, we read:

> They deposited upon him, as it were, their own flower, as something invested.

Here perhaps *deposititium*, “something deposited,” should be read. The meaning will be the same if you read *collatitio*, “contributed,” or at least not greatly different.

Certainly among the ancients many things were made with contributed money; upon such works were inscribed the letters:

> AERE COL.

Perhaps Tertullian had this custom in mind here. There is a charitable contribution disbursed for the use of the poor; and a contribution or collection, also called an assessment, which is a kind of monetary taxation; and [Col. 0212B] [non-Latin text omitted], a contribution toward the furnishing of a dinner, either in money or provisions. Hence a “contribution dinner” is a banquet of companions or messmates, at which each person brings his own food.

[Non-Latin text omitted]: the proverb will apply to those who are of no worth by themselves but depend entirely upon the resources of others, so that, when these are withdrawn, they collapse and lose both credit and fortune. They are rich in borrowed money, as Horace said:

> In my own money I am poor.

**10. The ass from Aesop’s well.** Vol. III, Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 23, no. 349.

The words “you have only just come” or “you have only just appeared” are to be understood. It is said of someone who has arrived from elsewhere unknown, distinguished by no renown of name, and who, as though emerging only now from some corner, has hitherto lain hidden—or, to use Horace’s word, escaped notice—and then suddenly dares to boast of something great and illustrious, indeed to claim authority, favor, and reputation for himself.

[Col. 0213A] **11. The middle balance-point, or to be borne at the equilibrium of the balance.** Vol. III, *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 41, no. 142; and Vol. V, *On Modesty*, chapter 9, no. 73.

In *On Modesty*:

> For an equation of examples will not be admitted, even though it agrees most perfectly at the balance-point, if it is most harmful to salvation.

If, as Rhenanus says from Festus, the *agina* is that opening into which the beam of a balance is inserted—that is, in which the balance moves or turns—and if the balance is said to rest “in the middle *agina*” whenever the weights are equal, so that neither side inclines downward, then it will be the same in proverbial use as the *ansa*, which is [non-Latin text omitted], the point upon which the tongue of the balance turns, or in the middle of which the pointer moves to and fro until, when the scales are equal, it corresponds to the highest point; [Col. 0213B] that is, it inclines to neither side but divides the distinction with equal weight.

The proverb may be applied to those who belong to neither party and therefore offend neither side; to those who embrace the virtues, which are certain means situated between extreme vices; to impartial judges; and to similar cases. See the annotations of Pamelius.
