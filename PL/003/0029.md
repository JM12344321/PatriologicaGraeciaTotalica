Moreover, Caecilius, like another Lucian in the *Peregrinus*, ridicules the Christians because they worship a crucified man. Octavius briefly denies that the one whom they worship is merely a man, and retorts that their adversaries are rather “worshippers of men” (*anthrōpolatrai*, ἀνθρωπολάτραι), since they worship kings as gods and even swear by their genius; indeed, they punish someone who perjures himself in this way more severely than if anyone knowingly [Col. 0210C] deceived his own Jupiter by swearing an oath. I suspect that a certain constitution of Alexander Severus is being noted here; it has, however, been incorporated into the books of law, Law II, Code, *On Things Lent*. Tertullian, in the *Apologeticum*, certainly bears witness that Christians of old were unwilling to use such a formula of oath. But I am all the more surprised that it is commended in the laws of the later Christian Caesars.

Caecilius insultingly charges the Christians with “worshipping the cross itself.” Octavius denies it. Their adversary curses them as “gallows-bearers.” Our author—for he is not ashamed of the Christian cross—does not refuse to be called a cross-bearer in his own fashion, and, concerning the religious sign of his cross—which was certainly familiar to our ancestors of old—he replies with what Justin and Tertullian had written before him, and Lactantius afterward in Book IV, chapter 27. [Col. 0210D] For it is well known how highly Constantine valued it; certainly, in order that the matter might be less disgraceful in common estimation, he decreed that criminals should no longer be fastened to a cross. Julian the Apostate nevertheless did not cease, like Caecilius, to reproach the Christians with the worship of the cross in order to disgrace them, as may be understood from Cyril.

The shamelessness of the slander is astonishing. It eventually reached such a point that their adversaries charged the Christians with I know not what monstrous rites, “such portents as mad [Note: [Col. 0210D] Juvenal, *Satire* 15, at the beginning.] Egypt worships,” as a certain poet said. Caecilius even dares to pretend that they “worship the head of an ass,” although he was not the first to invent this. Octavius repels so foul a slander by denying it, and proves by recrimination that it could more truly be said of their adversaries, following his Tertullian (*Apologeticum*, ch. XVI), who relates both that Christians were commonly called ass-worshippers and that Christ himself had been portrayed by an impious painter with ass’s ears, like another Midas. [Col. 0211A] He indicates, however, that it was a fabricated tale, since Cornelius Tacitus had mendaciously related something of the kind about the Jews. Yet many years before Tacitus, Apion of Alexandria, in the time of Caligula, had accused the Jews of a certain “ass-worship” (*onolatreia*, ὀνολατρεία). Moreover, since Josephus had already refuted such a lie, Tacitus, who persists in it, is less deserving of pardon—and least of all are those who, with still greater shamelessness, bespatter the Christians with this reproach.

More disgraceful still is the fact that Caecilius, with the same lack of shame, cries out [Col. 0211B] that they “worship certain shameful parts, I know not what.” To what depths does his filthy abuse rush? Octavius considers it consistent with his own modesty not to refute this at greater length. Meanwhile, he is compelled to hear other charges no less abominable, as Caecilius declaims that the Christians’ meetings before dawn are criminal assemblies, polluted by incest and parricide, almost as the Bacchanalia once were at Rome. Octavius not only repels such charges from his own people but also turns them back upon their adversaries. How incestuous the rites of the Romans were is not unknown. For even their poet says, [Note: [Col. 0211D] Juvenal, *Satire* 6, verse 313.] “The secrets of the Good Goddess are well known.” They also sacrificed human victims for a long time, although among other nations they condemned this ferocity, which I will no longer call sacred but bloodthirsty and criminal. It is memorable, moreover, that Plutarch relates in his *Problems* that they advanced this as a pretext for [Col. 0211C] excusing their own conduct and condemning that of others. Yet it is astonishing that such rites were imputed to the Christians. This slander, however, was ancient and long-lived. The suspicion of promiscuous lust was readily increased by the credulity of certain people, who judge others according to their own character; by the nocturnal gathering of a mixed multitude; by the Christians’ calling one another brothers and sisters; and also by their greeting one another with a kiss. What am I to say of the fact that some shamelessly abused the kiss, as Clement of Alexandria both admits and laments in the *Paedagogus*? The Christians opposed to these charges many sufficient testimonies to their own most chaste modesty. But shameless malevolence listens to nothing: just as its tongue is unrestrained, so it has neither brow nor ears. Josephus indeed relates that Apion had long before fabricated something similar concerning the assemblies of the Jews. [Col. 0211D] Thus nothing remained for our people, oppressed by such infamy, except what Aeschylus aptly and elegantly says: “to commit the truth to time” (*tēn alētheian anatithenai tō chronō*, τὴν ἀλήθειαν ἀνατιθέναι τῷ χρόνῳ). They were not impostors who deceived people with words, nor clamorous and violent men who wished in turn to overwhelm their adversaries with taunts, curses, and defamatory pamphlets. They preferred to refute falsehood by the facts themselves and at last to conquer in this way, by sitting still and remaining silent; meanwhile, they sustained and consoled themselves with a good conscience. Thus they conducted a good cause well.

It is again astonishing that Caecilius regarded that “sharing of the name of brother” among them as so extraordinary, [Col. 0212A] since Ulpian himself wrote that one who is loved with brotherly affection, although he is not a brother, may nevertheless rightly be called simply “brother”; and this too has been incorporated into the books of law, Law 58, *On the Institution of Heirs*. Indeed, I have learned from a certain fragment of Philo, preserved in Eusebius’s *On Preparation* (*Peri proparaskeuēs*, περὶ προπαρασκευῆς), Book VIII, chapter 12, that even the Essenes of old, before the age of the Christians, addressed one another in this way.

More horrible is the other charge brought against them, “the crime of infanticide.” This slander began shortly after the age of Trajan and endured until the time of Constantine, together with the tragic suspicion of some Thyestean banquet. But whence came this suspicion? Was it because Christians were accustomed to say that they fed upon the body and blood of Christ? Augustine, by contrast, relates that some once believed that Ceres and Bacchus were worshipped by the Christians, because they heard that bread and wine were held in such importance in their mysteries and otherwise did not understand the matter. [Col. 0212B] What am I to say of Epiphanius’s account [Note: [Col. 0212D] Epiphanius, Heresy XLVIII, section XIV, p. 416.] that certain heretics once called Gnostics, Cataphrygians, and Pepuzians, who falsely claimed that they were Christians, committed the very thing that Caecilius here imputes to all Christians? But was it just that good men should therefore be accused of what was utterly foreign to them? In place of proof, their adversaries advanced reputation and rumor. Yet the civil laws answered on behalf of our people that this too was done without any right, and Quintilian himself [Note: [Col. 0212D] Quintilian, Book V of the *Institutio Oratoria*, chapter 3.] said that “it can befall anyone, even the most innocent, through the deceit of enemies spreading falsehoods, that he suffers under adverse rumor.” Octavius sufficiently shows how far removed the Christians were [Col. 0212C] from every kind of homicide when he relates that their religion did not permit them even to watch killings in gladiatorial games. Theophilus had previously said the same to Autolycus; Athenagoras said it at the same time in his *Apology*; Tertullian discussed it more fully in his book *On the Spectacles*; and Lactantius did so afterward in Book VI, chapter 20. Minucius also affirms, as does Tertullian, that our people did not taste the blood of a slaughtered animal. For through many centuries our people diligently observed the law of Noah, which the Apostles had reiterated and enacted anew, no less than the apostolic decree concerning fornication (*peri porneias*, περὶ πορνείας) from the same council. Certainly, if Caecilius had preferred to tell the truth frankly rather than to lie wickedly, he could have learned how honorable, religious, modest, and chaste was the Christians’ solemn meal, which they called the *agapē* (ἀγάπη), not only from our Tertullian’s *Apology* but also from a certain letter of his own Pliny to Trajan. [Col. 0212D] But what could restrain the insolence of abandoned abuse? I have said more about that meal in my annotations on that letter, and there is no need to repeat it now. Yet however innocent the Christians’ nocturnal assemblies were, the Law of the Twelve Tables, confirmed by many decrees, nevertheless stood in their way, and the judgments against unlawful associations were severe. Our people replied that the republic faced no danger from their gatherings, because they were utterly foreign to all ambition and to every thought of holding office; there was nothing in the republic to which they aspired, [Col. 0213A] but they were content with their own private condition, as Tertullian replies in the *Apologeticum*.

But to pass over now the remaining heads either of Caecilius’s accusation or of Octavius’s defense, it is helpful to observe this: although this ingenious and bitter slanderer either invents or exaggerates everything and adds certain lesser crimes against majesty, he nevertheless passed over the crime of high treason, which others had previously charged against the Christians and which seemed especially suited to crushing the accused at once. Did he conceal it because there was not even the slightest suspicion of it? This, at least, was the perpetual glory of our ancestors: no injuries could so provoke them that they became hostile in spirit toward princes or tyrants, although they were treated most outrageously by them; nor did they consent to any conspiratorial plots against them, though such plots were otherwise both plausible and frequent at that time; nor did they forget what was worthy of their patience, endurance, moderation, and self-restraint. [Col. 0213B] So far were they from being turbulent or seditious, or from being swept away by a monstrous desire for vengeance. For although they were surrounded by such multitudes that they could easily have accomplished whatever they undertook in this regard, they nevertheless considered it unlawful. Indeed, otherwise they would have abolished the very principles of their religion and overturned its foundations. More than that, they would have dissolved all the bonds of human society. It was well known what many philosophers ostentatiously proclaimed about tyrannicides. But our people did not allow themselves to be inflamed by such bellows—so far were they from being incited in the name of religion [Col. 0213C] to public parricide. Nothing was omitted that could have inflamed them, and patience repeatedly wounded is said to become madness. But religion restrained them and armed them only for invincible endurance. On this matter, which I hear is being debated even in the present age—and am astonished that it is—we shall elsewhere confirm our case from the continuous history of several centuries. For now I shall touch only briefly upon what Minucius could have said and what happened shortly before his lifetime.

Tertullian says to Scapula (ch. 2): “We are defamed in matters concerning the majesty of the emperor; yet Christians could never be found among the Albinians, the Nigrians, or the Cassians.” It is useful to append another passage from the same Tertullian, the ancient recollection of which it is of the greatest importance to revive in our own times. [Col. 0213D] “Whence,” he says in the *Apologeticum* (ch. 35), “came the Cassii, the Nigri, and the Albini? Whence those who lie in wait for Caesar between the two laurels? Whence those who practice wrestling so as to throttle him? Whence those who burst armed into the palace, more audacious than all the Stephani and Parthenii? From the Romans, unless I am mistaken—that is, from non-Christians.” I shall not be reluctant to illustrate even this passage from history. Avidius Cassius, governor of Syria, had risen against the emperor Marcus Antoninus, under the pretext that he wished to restore and preserve the republic, which Marcus’s lax indulgence was ruining. Yet none of the Christians, who were so numerous in Marcus’s army against the Marcomanni, belonged to the Cassian faction. Romans who were especially intimate with Commodus arranged for that impure tyrant, and most hostile enemy of our people, to be strangled at home. [Col. 0214A] Was any Christian present among them? Pescennius Niger in Syria, and afterward Clodius Albinus in Britain and Gaul, opposed Septimius Severus, who was striving toward imperial power with the spirit of a gladiator, since each believed himself armed with as good a right as Severus. For Tertullian himself, in his book *On the Pallium* (ch. 2), once acknowledged and called these men “Augusti” no less than Severus. Yet no Christians were found either in their army or in their faction. Plautianus, who stood nearest to the prince and possessed the greatest authority after him, sent his own prefect into the council chamber in order that Severus and Caracalla, by whom many cruel deeds were being committed, might be slain; and shortly afterward he himself burst into it. [Col. 0214B] Did the Christians follow Plautianus, though it seemed to be in their interest that those tyrants be removed? On the contrary, they treated and healed Severus, when he was already confined to bed, with their own oil, as Tertullian bears witness in *To Scapula*, where he also commemorates the Church’s solemn prayers for rulers. He then adds: “That we act according to the rule of divine patience is sufficiently evident, since, although we are so great a multitude of people—almost the larger part of every city—we conduct ourselves in silence and moderation. Far be it from us to resent suffering those things which we desire, or to contrive any vengeance for ourselves when we await it from God.” The things he repeats in the *Apologeticum* are not dissimilar, and they deserve to be impressed upon people in these times as well. He certainly says that the Christians could easily [Note: [Col. 0214D] Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chapter XXXVII: “Even a single night, with a few small torches, could amply effect vengeance, if it were permitted among us to repay evil with evil.” Baldwin expressed Tertullian’s thought rather than his words; but in what immediately follows, the words themselves are taken from the same passage of the *Apologeticum*. (LINDNER.)] have avenged themselves “in a single night with a few small torches,” [Col. 0214C] if they had either been, or had wished to be, incendiaries—as they had nevertheless been called ever since the time of Nero. “But far be it,” he says, “that a divine sect should be vindicated by human fire, or should grieve to suffer that by which it is proved.” He says that, if they had wished to act openly and repel force with force, they certainly did not lack “the strength of numbers and troops.” But, he says, “under this discipline it is more lawful to be killed than to kill.” He says that they could even have conquered without arms by withdrawing and leaving the Roman world empty, had they wished to migrate elsewhere. For then there would have been no one left for the Roman emperor to rule. So few remained who were not Christians. But he indicates that our people wished both to remain citizens and not to pass over to the enemies of the Romans, while nevertheless [Col. 0214D] being called by the Romans not merely enemies, but enemies of the rulers and of the human race. Yet this injury was so manifestly unjust that even the enemies of the Christians were at last ashamed to act with such ingratitude and to speak so shamelessly.

Thus Caecilius, however shameless he was, blushed to fabricate against them any charge of high treason. He preferred to object to, carp at, lie about, and slander them in every other way. [Col. 0215A] Nor does he “ascribe to the Christians the cause of public calamities,” although at that time other adversaries were especially accustomed to do so maliciously. In Minucius’s most sorrowful age, the condition of the Roman Empire was indeed dreadful, and its dismemberment and confusion were horrifying. What need is there to say that every kind of evil then flooded in, and that the world was shaken and thrown into confusion by wars, fires, floods, earthquakes, the ruin of cities, and the destruction of peoples and kingdoms, and was finally exhausted by famine and a public plague? Yet Caecilius does not go so far as to slanderously declare—as the common people cried out at that time, and as was vaunted for many centuries both before and afterward—that this whole chaos ought to be charged to the Christians’ account and expiated with their blood. Before the age of Minucius, Tertullian said: “If the Tiber rises into the fields, if the sky [Col. 0215B] stands still, if the earth moves, if there is famine, if there is pestilence, immediately the cry goes up: ‘The Christians to the lion!’” [Note: [Col. 0215D] Certain words are missing from Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, chapter 40, and should be supplied as follows: “If the Tiber rises to the walls, if the Nile does not rise over the fields, if the sky stands still,” and so forth. (LINDNER.)] The cry was no less hateful and monstrous in the time of Cyprian, as is sufficiently evident from his letter *To Demetrianus*. It was the same in the time of Arnobius. For in his books *Against the Nations* he too labors to free our people from this flame of public hatred and odium. What of Symmachus himself afterward? Does he not dare to sing the same refrain again? He certainly deserved to hear the cry once raised at Rome after the death of Commodus: [Note: [Col. 0215D] Lampridius, *Life of Commodus*, chapter 18, has transmitted both this and similar acclamations. (LINDNER.)] “The informers to the lion!” But our people were foreign to bloodshed and the desire for vengeance. Ambrose did not permit him to exult any longer over so false a charge. Yet when the city itself was captured and plundered not long afterward, the impious slanderers were not ashamed finally to ascribe even such a calamity to the Christian religion; and Augustine was once more compelled to confront so foul an accusation. For he admits that this was one reason why he wrote his books *On the City of God*. Caecilius therefore acts less maliciously: unlike the others, he does not hurl such a torch against our people, nor does he so outrageously burden their innocence. Rather, as though exalted by great victories and crowned with supreme prosperity, he arrogantly insults them as wretched, conquered, and enslaved; on this account he contemptuously depresses their religion as much as he gloriously exalts his own superstition. [Col. 0215C] For profane men depend in this way upon fortune and the success of events. Thus Cicero himself, in his speech *For Flaccus*, insultingly attacks both the nation and the rites of the Jews. From Josephus we also learn that Apion confidently reproached the Jews with their servitude and miseries [Col. 0215D] as testimonies of a false religion. It is astonishing, however, that even after the age of Constantine the emperor Julian attacked our people on the same grounds, as we learn from Cyril’s replies. How much more readily could Caecilius do this, who had seen Christians only as afflicted, wretched, poor, and insignificant? Thus, with great arrogance, haughtiness, and contempt, he spits upon them and tramples them down as “refuse and offscourings” (*katharmata kai peripsēmata*, καθάρματα καὶ περιψήματα)—to use the Apostle’s [Col. 0216A] words. But Octavius looks down upon his boastful spirit from a loftier elevation and opposes to it a certain holy pride, by which our people were rich even in poverty, free even in servitude, and, finally, victorious in the midst of death. Yet although Caecilius began by foaming at the mouth, intemperately pouring forth abuse, breathing threats, casting out bombastic horrors, and finally insulting Christ, he nevertheless yields soon afterward, conquered by the truth. Nor does he await the judgment of Minucius as arbiter and judge, but anticipates it of his own accord. This conversion is truly admirable, especially because it was so sudden. But it shows that many at Rome were capable of being healed, although they otherwise seemed abandoned and beyond hope, and that error readily yields to truth. Thus Caecilius could, as though by a formal surrender in law, be adjudged to Octavius when the latter claimed him. [Col. 0216B] He certainly does not refuse to be conquered and to surrender; struck, as it were, by a thunderbolt from Octavius’s speech, like another Paul he suddenly embraces, once admonished, the religion that he had attacked in ignorance.

I wish, however, that Minucius had also set forth for us the whole history of this *metanoia* (μετάνοια), or change of mind, so that we might understand by what rule, on what condition, with what sponsors, by what rite, and with what ceremony Caecilius, after returning to Rome, was enrolled in the association and communion of the Church. For the memory of that ancient custom is also worth seeking. But let us suppose that what Jerome, in his additions to Eusebius’s *Chronicle*, and Augustine, in Book VIII of the *Confessions* (ch. 2), relate concerning two other men—Arnobius and Victorinus—of the same condition, rank, nation, and ability, also happened to Caecilius. Indeed, [Col. 0216C] this Caecilius admits that, after he had understood what true religion is not, he desired an understanding, knowledge, and instruction in true religion; Minucius writes that this catechesis was postponed until the following day. But for that very reason I grieve all the more that this second lesson—which would have been the best and largest part of Minucius’s book—was not published, or at least is no longer extant. For it is not enough to refute falsehood; one must also teach the truth, so that both what God or religion is not and what God or religion is may be explained. But all is well: what we miss here can be supplied by Augustine’s book *On Catechizing the Uninstructed*, which elegantly describes both the rules and the form of ancient catechesis and also advises how one must deal with learned and literary people. I believe that catechesis in Minucius’s age was not dissimilar, and I wonder that it is not observed in our own times as well. [Col. 0216D] It is pleasing, moreover, now that we have heard Caecilius as accuser and adversary, to behold him, with circumstances reversed, as a catechumen. Catechumens were then also called “hearers” or “those hearing,” as is evident from many passages of Tertullian; they were especially teachable and by no means obstinate, as Lucian himself indicates in the *Philopatris*. Consequently, there were no contentious disputations with them; religion was taught by calm admonitions. After catechesis, those who had now given their names to the Church so that they might be initiated through Baptism were called “competentes,” or candidates; and it is pleasing to behold our Caecilius now in their order as well. After Baptism, the candidate finally became a participant in the “mysteries” (*tōn mystēriōn*, τῶν [Col. 0217A] μυστηρίων), from which he had previously been commanded to remain far away. Our ancestors certainly were not accustomed either to debate those mysteries indiscriminately or to discuss them in the presence of profane persons. Theodoret is a witness [Note: [Col. 0217D] Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, in the *Dialogues* to which he gives the title *Eranistes*, Dialogue 2, p. 159, Leipzig edition, introduces the Orthodox speaker replying as follows to Eranistes when questioned about the Holy Eucharist: οὐ χρὴ σαφῶς εἰπεῖν· εἰκὸς γάρ τινας ἀμυήτους παρεῖναι. “It is not proper to speak openly, for perhaps some are present who have not yet been initiated into the mysteries.” Eranistes replies: αἰνιγματώδως ἡ ἀπόκρισις ἔστω. “Then let the answer be set forth in the form of an enigma.”] in the *Eranistes*. For they believed that such matters should be treated with greater reverence and even with greater caution, and they foresaw far in advance what the matter might otherwise come to. Thus, although, when their “assemblies” (*synaxeis*, συνάξεις) were accused, there seemed to be a strong reason why they should explain everything done in them, they nevertheless said nothing about the mysteries in court, nor did they dispute or litigate about them with their adversaries.

These, then, are the matters that I thought fit to mention briefly by way of preface, in order to prepare, as it were, a road toward reading Minucius’s *Octavius*—a reading that both commends to us the memory of Christian antiquities and rouses us to renew it, since we otherwise pursue Roman antiquities with such zeal. Would that the learned men at Rome, who leave no stone unturned and overlook no letter even upon the stones themselves in order to unearth something belonging to the ancient republic, would also collect the monuments of the ancient Church, in which that same city abounds, and raise up more Minuciuses who both lived and were buried there. [Col. 0217B] Enough—enough now—of new little books about religion. Let us labor instead to restore to the light the ancient remains of this kind; let us at last be students of learned antiquity rather than empty novelty; and let us both contemplate the portraits of our ancestors and display them to others, lest perhaps we seem to be new men. Yet although their religion and spirit were the same, their outward appearance and condition were not always the same as well. For they did not always lie harassed and cast down, nor were they either so ungrateful or so foolish as to refuse to enjoy the best legal status and condition when this was permitted them through Christian princes; nor were they so witless as not to use this benefit; nor so unjust as to regard their own emperors as equivalent to pagan tyrants—as we say—if they found something wanting in them; nor so peevish as to concede absolutely nothing to the public peace. I speak of those who were not degenerate. But Minucius did not see the splendor of a free Church; he saw only the squalor of an enslaved one. It is our duty, however, rightly to consider and distinguish both conditions and the circumstances of both periods, and prudently to observe this difference in their external form [Col. 0217D] and historical setting, so to speak, so that we may rightly use both the memory of antiquity and the examples of our ancestors. [Col. 0217C]

For here, I admit, [Col. 0218A] great prudence and the utmost caution are necessary. When a new law is proposed, the vote “As you propose” is often cast, but “I reject it” still more often; it is necessary, and the situation requires, that these formulas occur to us and meet with our approval. It is said that these words are carved upon an ancient marble at Rome:

> HAPPY ANTIQUITY, MORE NOBLE IN ITS WHITE PURITY THAN YELLOW GOLD.

But we would misuse its precedents disastrously unless we applied free and impartial judgment in distinguishing and choosing among them, lest either we confuse everything indiscriminately, or maliciously pass over what we ought to imitate, or intemperately insist upon things that do not suit the present age. When wills were once under discussion, a certain civil law replied that “the custom of most trustworthy antiquity must be retained.” Law XVIII, Code, *On Wills*. And when the boundaries and order of ecclesiastical government were under consideration, that declaration was heard at the Council of Nicaea: ΤΑ ΑΡΧΑΙΑ ΕΘΗ ΚΡΑΤΕΙΤΩ—“Let the ancient customs prevail.” [Col. 0218B] There was nevertheless no superstition that intemperately and violently insisted upon what the welfare of the republic rejected. And just as wise jurists, when a question arose concerning the right of magistrates, solemnly replied that one must consider not only what had been done at Rome but also what ought to be done—see Law XII, *On the Office of the Governor*—so too we must consider not merely what our ancestors did, but also at what time, under what circumstances, and for what purpose. Nor must we forget the warning of a certain law, that judgment should be made not so much according to examples as according to laws. I therefore act with the greatest willingness when I either cultivate jurisprudence and history in conjunction or at least frequently profess that they ought to be cultivated together—and indeed both of them in each domain: I mean human and divine affairs, or civil and ecclesiastical affairs. For their understanding and recollection require such a conjunction of these studies. Thus, if the emperor Tiberius rightly judged that military discipline—as [Note: [Col. 0218D] Suetonius, *Tiberius*, chapter 19: “He enforced discipline most rigorously, reviving from antiquity various forms of punishment and disgrace.”] Suetonius says—should be recovered from antiquity, we should much more strongly believe, and more correctly say, that civil and ecclesiastical discipline ought to be recovered from it in good faith and with sound judgment. [Col. 0218C] Meanwhile, because I perceive that one can err here in many dangerous ways, I shall again and again entreat all good and prudent men that, turning away from factions and remaining foreign to partisan zeal, they should first assign to antiquity only as much authority as is owed to it; and then calmly consider and observe not only what the ancient Christians in the time of Minucius did when they could do nothing else, but also what they desired, what they determined should be done when they had the power, and, indeed, what they finally did when they became free, in order rightly to establish both the republic and the Church. [Col. 0218D]

# JOHN AUGUSTINE ERNESTI’S PREFACE TO LINDNER’S FIRST EDITION

[Col. 0219A] Greetings to the learned and Christian reader.

In the institutions of those through whom God restored to the human race the understanding and knowledge of all good literature, and especially of sacred literature, I find this principle: that they themselves joined sacred learning with humane learning, were no less zealous in reading the writers of the ancient Church, both Greek and Latin, than in preserving what they had received from the more ancient Greeks and Romans, and were the authors and advocates of the same conjunction for those who committed themselves to their discipline and authority. Not to seek examples from afar and among foreign peoples: Erasmus, whom everyone numbers among the foremost restorers of learned humanism, took no less care to be thoroughly versed in the writings of the ancient Doctors of the Church than in the profane writings of the Greeks and Latins. Greek and Roman antiquity owes him no more than ecclesiastical antiquity does, since he reviewed, corrected, and elucidated according to the evidence of manuscripts considerably more volumes by the Doctors of the ancient Church than by classical authors. [Col. 0219B]

In our own lands, however, the first man who emerged as a founder of humane learning here in our own Leipzig, Peter Mosellanus, interpreted Greek and Latin writers in such a way that, by interpreting the Holy Scriptures and lecturing upon ecclesiastical poets, he also led the young men who emulated his studies—that is, the flower of youth—to the understanding and reading of the Fathers. Caspar Borner retained this form of instruction received from him and handed it down to his own pupils. He too was a man, in the judgment of Joachim Camerarius (*Life of Philip Melanchthon*, p. 71), born to establish branches of learning, and Luther and Melanchthon accorded him as much authority in this Academy as they did to anyone. We have a suitable witness to this in Georg Fabricius, a pupil of Borner’s school, who received from him the same form of instruction. For in the letter in which he commends a corpus of Christian poets to Alexander, administrator of the bishopric of Merseburg, he says:

> At Leipzig he had heard Caspar Borner interpreting the hymns of Prudentius with the greatest learning; and Borner had for many years faithfully preserved for public use that course of reading, which had been handed down to him, as it were, from hand to hand by the most illustrious Peter Mosellanus. [Col. 0219D]

Nor did Joachim Camerarius depart from this method. He was the third among those who established literature among us; both his training under Mosellanus and the *Notes on the Figures of Speech of the Lord Jesus Christ and the Apostles*, together with other little works concerning ecclesiastical matters, show clearly that he treated ecclesiastical writers studiously and diligently. [Col. 0220A]

What am I to say of Philip, that teacher of Germany? Who does not know that, although he was preeminent in Greek and Latin literature insofar as it is learned from non-Christian poets, orators, historians, and philosophers, he was likewise the foremost scholar of ecclesiastical literature in those times, especially in these lands, and so thoroughly versed in every class of writers of the ancient Church that he might have seemed to have studied nothing but them? And, what is more astonishing, while still almost a young man he had advanced so far in that field that, at the disputation on religion held in this city in the year 1519 under the authority of Duke George, when the theologians Carlstadt and Luther disputed with Eck, he even supplied passages from the Fathers during the debate for them to use against Eck. [Col. 0220B] From this it may be understood that the form of instruction then prevailing in those parts of Germany that attached the greatest importance to humane learning was to join both kinds of literature almost from earliest childhood.

Our native land thereafter possessed in Georg Fabricius a diligent guardian, advocate, and promoter of this course of instruction, as the very letter mentioned above declares. In it he gravely complains that those who wish to be, and to appear, devoted to Latin elegance generally neglect the writings of the ancient Doctors of the Church, although reading them supplies excellent learning useful to the Church and nourishes piety, for which one ought to have considerably greater concern than for elegance.

What of Barthius, Reinesius, [Col. 0220C] Rivinus, Rittershusius, and no small number of others? Who can possess any adequate acquaintance with them and yet not know that, in addition to their other learning—especially their knowledge of Latin literature—they treated, cultivated, and adorned this class of writings with the greatest diligence? How many others among more recent literary scholars could I mention who devoted very great attention to reading this class of writers? Among them was Johann Friedrich Gronovius, the most elegant interpreter of Roman authors of them all, who elucidated and corrected the ancient ecclesiastical writers with good observations no less than he did the Roman authors. [Col. 0220D]
