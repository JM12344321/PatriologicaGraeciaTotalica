## § II. The time at which our TERTULLIAN was converted to the faith

**3.** As for the other controversy, concerning the time when our TERTULLIAN was converted to the faith, a far greater difficulty [Col. 0043C] presents itself. Some who think that he flourished around the year of Christ 160 were deceived by the author's words in *On Monogamy*: “about 160 years having since elapsed” (*On Monogamy* 3). How those years are to be counted—whether from Christ's Passion or rather from the period when the apostle Paul wrote his First Epistle to the Corinthians—will be discussed more fully below under the year of the Lord 214. Thus Eusebius, the Venerable Bede, Freculphus of Lisieux, Martin of Poland, Conrad of Lichtenau, abbot of Ursperg, Vincent of Beauvais, Hermannus Contractus, and Trithemius write much more correctly that TERTULLIAN flourished around the year of Christ 200. Yet all are silent about the year in which he gave his name to Christ, except TERTULLIAN himself, and he does so in exceedingly obscure words.

It is indeed clear [Col. 0043D] that immediately after his baptism he composed the book *On the Pallium*, on this occasion: when, in Christian fashion, he laid aside the toga and put on the pallium, some friend objected, “So, from the toga to the pallium?” Among other things he replies: “But I now also bring to it fellowship with the divine sect and discipline. Rejoice, pallium, and exult: a better philosophy has now deemed you worthy, since you began to clothe a Christian” (*On the Pallium* 5, 6, and 3).

It is not equally easy, however, to understand what these words of the same book mean: “But antiquity is now vain, when our own courses lie before us. How greatly has this age transformed the world! How many cities has the threefold power of the present Empire either brought forth, enlarged, or restored, God favoring so many Augusti in union! How many census lists have been transcribed! How many peoples driven back! How many orders ennobled! How many barbarians shut out! Truly the world is the most cultivated estate of this Empire: every aconite of hostility, every cactus, and every thornbush of treacherous intimacy has been uprooted; it is planted and delightful beyond the orchard of Alcinous and the rose garden of Midas.” Rhenanus, the Magdeburg Centuriators, and others following them interpret “the threefold power of the Empire” as Severus Augustus and the Caesars Pescennius Niger and Clodius Albinus—Severus and Niger having been proclaimed by their armies in Pannonia and the East, and Albinus in Gaul. But Rhenanus contradicts himself when he admits that Niger's proclamation occurred against Severus's will and that Severus judged both men enemies and afterward killed them. Hence, amid such hostility among three Caesars, the words “God favoring so many Augusti in union” cannot apply, still less “every aconite of hostility having been uprooted.” Moreover, because the choice of the three Caesars in different provinces occurred at the very beginning of Severus's reign, it can hardly agree [Col. 0044C] with the following words: “How many census lists have been transcribed!” and so forth, “how many barbarians shut out!” This last raises the greater difficulty, since it establishes that the book was composed only after the barbarians had been shut out—that is, after the Arabs, Parthians, and Adiabenians had been defeated.

We therefore later discovered that what, in our summary and notes, we assigned to about Severus's fifth year should rather be applied to the third, since Albinus began to defect from Severus in the fourth year and was killed in the fifth. At that time, therefore, Severus Augustus, his son Antoninus, and the said Albinus ruled together, and the Empire had already been pacified, since both Julianus, who had seized it after Pertinax was killed, and Pescennius Niger had already been defeated and slain.

**4.** All indeed agree [Col. 0044D] that Albinus was a partner in the Empire. According to the consular fasti, immediately after Severus received all the imperial titles, “D. CLODIUS ALBINUS WAS NAMED CAESAR BY IMPERATOR CAESAR L. SEPTIMIUS SEVERUS AUGUSTUS”; and in the next year Severus entered the consulship with Albinus, who from then on was also called SEPTIMIUS, apparently in honor of Severus. Severus furthermore honored him by ordering, as Spartianus attests, coins to be marked with his image. These still [Note: [Col. 0044D] That is, around 1579, when this Life of Tertullian by Pamelius was first published.] survive with both Greek and Latin inscriptions in the collections of the noble lords Marcus and Guido Laurinus, lords of Watervliet, along with many others that will be mentioned below. Supporting our view are these, issued after Niger had already been killed: “IMP. CAES. D. CLODIUS SEPT. ALB. COS. II. / MONETA AUG.,” “CS. VICT. AUG.,” “MINERVA THE PEACE-BEARER / A FERTILE AGE,” and finally “HAPPINESS.” All seem to relate to the phrase “every aconite of hostility having been uprooted.”

**5.** As for Antoninus, Severus's son, two things confirm our conjecture that he too had already been distinguished with the title Caesar: first, Code 3, *On Undutiful Wills*, law 1, which Severus and Antoninus rescripted on 1 July in the consulship of Falco and Clarus, a consulship falling in Severus's first year; and second, this coin legend: “THE UNCONQUERED PIOUS EMPERORS AUGUSTI” (with the effigy of Severus and Antoninus still a beardless boy), “THE GREATEST PARTHIAN VICTORY.” [Col. 0045B] This may indeed refer to the words “how many barbarians shut out!”, as may this: “L. SEPT. SEV. PERT. AUG. IMP. II. ARAB. PAR. ADIAB. COS. II. P. P. S. C.” Spartianus agrees especially in these words about the period after Niger's death: “He then conducted many operations around Arabia, reducing the Parthians too to submission, as well as the Adiabenians, all of whom had sided with Pescennius. On his return he was therefore called Arabicus, Parthicus, and Adiabenicus; but he refused a triumph lest he appear to triumph over a civil victory.” Eusebius agrees too—or rather St Jerome, who inserted much relating to the Latins into Eusebius's *Chronicle*—placing the Jewish, Samaritan, and Parthian war before the killing of Albinus, as do Orosius, John Zonaras, and Hugh of Fleury.

It is no objection that Herodian [Col. 0045C] writes that the Parthian war was still postponed: greater trust should be placed in a Latin than a Greek writer who observed no historical order; and Spartianus later writes that Severus again set out against the Arabs, Parthians, and Adiabenians and, after finishing the war against them, celebrated a triumph—the same triumph discussed below under Severus's tenth year. Especially pertinent are surviving inscriptions on marble and coins in which he is twice styled Parthicus. One, from his eleventh year, is on the Arch of Septimius at Rome: “TO IMP. CAES. L. SEPT. SEV. AUG. IMP. XII. PARTH. ARAB. PARTH. ADIAB. AA. X. / DEPARTURE OF THE AUGUSTUS,” apparently for Britain.

The former also in part explains “how many peoples driven back!” Likewise “how many orders ennobled!” refers to military orders or legions, as shown by these inscriptions of Severus's second, third, and fourth years: “IMP. CAES. L. SEV. ... TRIB. POT. II. IMP. IV. COS. II. PROCOS. P. P. / THE ORDER OF CLUSIUM”; “TO THE VIRGIN DIANA, SACRED, FOR THE SAFETY OF IMP. L. SEPT. SEV. ... TRIB. POT. III. IMP. V. / THE ORDER OF CLUSIUM”; and “IMP. CAES. ... IMP. VIII. TRIB. POT. IV. / LEGION VII OF CLUSIUM.” Nevertheless [Col. 0046A] we shall begin our history with Severus's first year, and with TERTULLIAN already a Christian from Severus's third year, once we have prefaced certain remarks on his designations Q. SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIANUS OF CARTHAGE, and consequently on his people, family, stock, parents, homeland, education, studies and youthful writings, state of life, and age when Severus began his reign.

## § III. His praenomen, nomen, cognomen, and agnomen

**6.** All antiquarians know that Q.—which Vincent of Beauvais, Trithemius, Poliziano, and a manuscript codex long ago attributed to our author—is a praenomen, perhaps because he was born fifth, since Roman praenomina were generally taken either from an event or from grandfathers, great-grandfathers, or more remote ancestors [Col. 0046B] (in whose place today each person's given names have succeeded). Such too is QUINTA SEPTIMIA, which Onuphrius lists among female praenomina.

SEPTIMIUS is the designation of a clan that among the Romans was first royal, then plebeian, and finally consular and patrician. First there is found SEPTIMIUS MESIUS, KING OF THE AEQUI; then, in various marble inscriptions, partly among the epigrams of the ancient City and partly in Aldus Manutius's *Orthography*, the female names SEPTIMIA ANNIA, FL. HORATIA SEPTIMIA OCTAVILLA, SEPT. FELICISSIMA EURESIA, SEPT. SECUNDA, and SEPT. EXORTA; and, among men of the military order—which is more relevant to our purpose—T. SEPTIMIUS FELICISSIMUS OF THE SECOND COHORT; L. FLAVIUS L. F. SEPTIMIUS AFER OCTAVIANUS, C. V., OF THE SECOND CAVALRY SQUADRON, DECEMVIR FOR JUDGING LEGAL DISPUTES; L. SEPTIMIUS ANTIGONUS, SOLDIER OF THE SIXTH PRAETORIAN COHORT; T. SEPTIMIUS, SON OF GAIUS, SERGIAN TRIBE, MILITARY TRIBUNE; and finally L. SEPTIMIUS SEVERIANUS and SEPT. CARITO, V. P.

Among men of consular rank there were, under Julius Caesar Augustus, P. SEPTIMIUS; under Caesar Trajan, SEPTIMIUS AGNIDINUS, V. C., ACTING THROUGH THE SPANISH PROVINCES, V. C., P. T., HEARING CASES AS IMPERIAL DEPUTY; under Marcus, SEPTIMIUS SEVERUS; under the emperor Verus, L. SEPTIMIUS SEVERUS, SON OF M. SEPTIMIUS GETA, later emperor; under that very Severus, D. CLODIUS SEPTIMIUS ALBINUS, proclaimed Caesar with him as stated above; P. SEPTIMIUS, SON OF MARCUS, GETA, Severus's brother; Q. SEPTIMIUS PLAUTIANUS, father-in-law of his son Antoninus; and—strangely, since his elder son Antoninus omitted the name SEPTIMIUS—P. SEPTIMIUS GETA, SON OF SEVERUS AUGUSTUS, IMPERATOR CAESAR AUGUSTUS.

Whether our African author himself belonged to their clan is not so clear. Meanwhile he gives himself that name in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, last chapter, no. 140: “May peace and grace overflow from our Lord Jesus, together with SEPTIMIUS TERTULLIAN, whose little work this is.” Hence Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 5.1, and St Jerome, letter 188 to Fabiola, call him SEPTIMIUS TERTULLIAN; Trithemius, Poliziano, and all manuscript and printed codices to date agree with them.

The cognomen FLORENS signifies a particular family of the Septimian clan, though one unknown among the Romans; yet it is attributed to the author not only in printed editions but also in a manuscript codex and long ago by Trithemius and Poliziano. Besides all other writers who mention him, he calls himself by the name TERTULLIAN in the words already cited and also in these: “I ask only that, when you pray, you remember TERTULLIAN the sinner as well,” and “Remember in your prayers TERTULLIAN who exhorts you to these things.” The name seems derived from Tertullus, just as Octavianus from Octavius and Septimius from Septimius, and to signify a branch of the Florens family. Perhaps of that branch is the man named in this inscription surviving at Mainz near St Maurice and at Milan near St Victor by the Vercelli gate: “C. SARTORIUS, SON OF LUCIUS, OF THE OUFENTINE TRIBE, TERTULLIANUS, VETERAN OF LEGION XI, [Col. 0047B] CURATOR OF ROMAN CITIZENS AT MOGUNTIACUM,” or MUNGOTIARIUM. At least this much is established from Eusebius's *Chronicle* and St Jerome's *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*: our TERTULLIAN was an African, the son of a proconsular centurion. St Isidore, the Venerable Bede, Nicephorus, Freculphus of Lisieux, Marianus Scotus, Ado, and Vincent of Beauvais also affirm it.

## § IV. What sort of education did he receive, and what was his knowledge of letters and the sciences?

**7.** The agnomen OF CARTHAGE indicates Carthage as his homeland. These authors agree here too when they say “of the province of Africa, of the city of Carthage,” as does St Optatus of Milevis in book I *Against Parmenian*, and Nicephorus expressly when he writes that Tertullian was born at Carthage in Africa. What is more, [Col. 0047C] the author himself indicates it abundantly clearly.

[Note: [Col. 0047D] Eusebius seems to have written that Tertullian was Roman and noble, *Ecclesiastical History* II.2: [non-Latin text omitted]. His report that Tertullian was skilled in Roman laws led some to believe that the author was the jurist, and there is indeed a jurist of the same name. It is established, however, that he is a different man from our Tertullian; Eusebius does not report that he was a jurist, but that he was skilled in Roman laws. What Eusebius adds about his country and nobility cannot be defended unless it is understood of Roman writers, the sense being that Tertullian was one of the most illustrious Latin authors. Rufinus understood this passage of Eusebius in this sense, translating it “very illustrious among our writers.”]

At the beginning of *On the Pallium* he says: “First always in Africa, Carthaginian men, noble through antiquity, happy through novelty.... Yet once your dress too was different.... But after the benefit conferred by the injury upon you..., after the obscene omens of Gracchus..., after Pompey's three altars and Caesar's long delays—when Statilius Taurus set up the walls and Sentius Saturninus explained the rites—when concord assists, the toga was offered.... Yet you denounce the pallium generally, even though unmindful that it was yours. I indeed do not wonder, in view of the earlier lesson. For that Carthage too is called the Ram, fiercest in its pursuit of war, the first of all to take up arms. Yet when the final times of the homeland arrived” (behold, he calls Carthage his homeland) “and the now-Roman Ram dared assail its former walls, the Carthaginians were immediately astonished as at a new and foreign disposition. So powerfully can remote antiquity change with time. Thus, finally, even the pallium is not recognized.” We have treated this at somewhat greater length because of the opinion of Vadianus, who on his map of Palestine assigns Tertullian as his birthplace a certain town near the Altars of the Philaeni brothers.

**8.** How fortunate TERTULLIAN'S education was is attested by his books against the Gentiles, which, as St Jerome testifies in his letter to Magnus, contain the whole learning of the secular world. From these books alone it is evident that he had read every sort of author—historians, poets, and grammarians. As for the jurists, the matter is too well known to require proof; and the same may be inferred of physicians and philosophers from the book *On the Soul*. The reader will find a catalogue of all these, Greek as well as Latin and divided into their classes, among our indexes. Hence Lactantius calls him skilled in every kind of literature; Eusebius, illustrious for his knowledge of laws and Roman affairs; St Jerome, a man of sharp and vehement intellect, than whom none was more learned or acute; St Augustine, most eloquent, swelling his speech with sounding cheeks; and Nicephorus, exceptionally powerful in eloquence, very sharp and ingenious.

Vincent of Lérins develops all this much more fully: “Among the Latins,” he says, “this man must readily be judged the chief of all our writers. For who was more learned than he? Who more practiced in divine and human affairs? With a certain marvelous capacity of mind he embraced all philosophy and all the sects of the philosophers, the authors and champions of those sects, all their branches of learning, and every variety of history and study. Did he not also excel in an intellect so weighty and vehement that there was scarcely anything he proposed to overthrow which he did not either penetrate with acuteness or crush by force? And who could adequately celebrate the praises of his style? It is woven with such a density of arguments that those whom it could not persuade it compels to agreement; nearly as many words as judgments, as many senses as victories.”

Nevertheless, compelled by experience itself, we must admit what Lactantius writes of him: that he is not very accessible, less polished, and extremely obscure; and, according to St Jerome, dense in thoughts but difficult in expression. It is therefore unsurprising that Nepotian could easily distinguish his style, and that of other Africans too—as the same St Jerome testifies in Nepotian's Life—the styles, I mean, of Minucius Felix, St Cyprian, Arnobius, Lactantius, and Victorinus. Rhenanus somewhere noted the chief cause of his obscurity: through constant reading of Greek authors he absorbed Greek modes of expression so thoroughly that, even when writing Latin, he could not forget them.

St Jerome himself is an ample witness that Tertullian wrote certain things in his youth, even before accepting the faith of Christ. “This,” he says in *Against Jovinian* I, “is the place to describe the hardships of marriage and, as though in a rhetorical commonplace, to exult in speech. TERTULLIAN too, certainly, when he was still a young man, made sport of this subject.” In letter 22 to Eustochium he gives the book's argument and title: “If it pleases you to know how many troubles a free virgin escapes and to how many a wife is bound, read TERTULLIAN TO A PHILOSOPHER FRIEND.” Hence we judge that even from that time he observed the chastity he held in such esteem throughout his whole life. We therefore understand these words of the author as spoken not so much in his own person as in that of others, by way of example: “I know that I neither committed adultery with one flesh nor now strive for continence with another” (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 59).

## § V. His conduct in paganism; his marriage

**9.** That TERTULLIAN nevertheless entered into marriage is clear from his two books *To His Wife*. Perhaps he did so because the Papian, Poppaean, and Julian laws had not yet been abolished: these, as he himself admits, compelled people to beget children and forbade the unmarried and childless to receive a whole inheritance under a will. More will be said below about their repeal by the emperor Severus. The legislation fixed a definite age for marriage—thirty-five according to Plato—and thus confirms our conjecture that he married before accepting the faith, although no mention of any children of his occurs anywhere.

Trithemius writes that he gloriously taught rhetoric at Carthage for some years; we shall presently establish that he was afterward also an orator, or advocate in legal causes. That he was also learned in law is evident from the various legal phrases and juristic terms he employs. Perhaps at Rome he had Cervidius Scaevola as his teacher and the emperor Severus and Aemilius Papinian as fellow students. Yet he was younger than Severus, who was born in AD 146, so that Tertullian was forty at the beginning of Severus's reign. From then until the end of the reigns of Severus and his son Antoninus Caracalla, under whom the author flourished, there were about twenty-four years, making TERTULLIAN about sixty-three. From that year—which physicians call climacteric—the decrepit old age to which he lived is customarily reckoned to begin. Supporting this is the statement of St Jerome and Nicephorus that after middle age he fell into the heresy of Montanus: in our view, around his fifty-third year, in Severus's fifteenth year, a full decade before his death. But enough of these matters. Let us proceed to the history of those twenty-four years.

## The Year of Jesus Christ 194

**10.** In the year of the Lord 194, when C. Sosius Falco and C. Julius Erucius Clarus were consuls; in the eighth year of St Victor, son of Felix the African and Roman pontiff; and in the first year of Imperator Caesar L. Septimius, son of Marcus, the African, Pius Pertinax Augustus, TERTULLIAN, still a pagan, practiced as an advocate at Carthage. This is established by his own words, written about himself in the person of the pallium after he had become a Christian: “I owe nothing to the forum, nothing to the parade ground, nothing to the senate-house; I keep watch for no office, seize no rostra beforehand, haunt no praetoria, worship no railings, wear down no benches, disturb no laws, and trumpet no causes. More harm has been done to the state by togas than by breastplates” (*On the Pallium* 5).

He certainly admits that he had been a pagan. Speaking of the last judgment, eternal life and fire, and the raising of the dead, he says: “We too once laughed at these things; we were of your number. Christians are made, [Col. 0050A] not born.”

[Note: [Col. 0050D] Tertullian himself expressly attests in his own words that, together with the superstitions of the Gentiles, he served lust, in *On the Resurrection* 59: “I know that I neither committed adultery with one flesh nor now strive for continence with another.” At that time he also delighted in spectacles, of which he says, “I prefer not to supply the full measure rather than to remember” (Tertullian, *On Spectacles* 19), that is, to say little of them rather than remember what he saw. In the book *On Repentance* he admits that he was the greatest of all sinners and, urging repentance upon the fallen, says (*On Repentance* 4 and 12): “You sinner like me—indeed, less than I, for I acknowledge my own preeminence in offenses—seize upon repentance and embrace it as a shipwrecked man trusts some plank.” In chapter 12 he says of a life spent in vice: “What more can I say about these things”—that is, repentance—“since I am a sinner of every mark and born for nothing but repentance?”]

In the author certain matters pertaining to this year may be found. First, concerning the murder of the emperor P. Helvius Pertinax, son of Publius, killed on 28 March, there is this passage of the *Apology*: “Whence come those who train in the wrestling-school to squeeze throats? Whence those who burst armed into the palace, bolder than all the Sigeriuses and Partheniuses? From among the Romans, unless I am mistaken” (*Apology* 35). For he was killed at the palace gates by armed Praetorian soldiers through the conspiracy of Laetus, his own praetorian prefect; at the latter's command the emperor Commodus had shortly before been strangled by the athlete Narcissus, with whom he was accustomed to practice wrestling.

Then, concerning Imperator Caesar Pescennius Niger, who, after Imperator Caesar M. Didius, son of Marcus, Commodus Severus Julianus Augustus had been killed on the senate's orders, seized imperial power on 1 May, the same passage says: “To be sure, the other orders, religious according to their authority, in good faith breathe nothing hostile against Caesar—from the senate itself, the equestrian order, the camps, and the palaces themselves. Whence came the Cassiuses and the Nigers?” The first refers to Avidius Cassius, of the family of those Cassii who killed G. Julius Caesar, who attempted to wrest the principate from the emperors Antoninus Pius, Marcus, and Verus; the second to Niger's conspiracy against the emperor Severus.

In the same place he describes the solemnities of the Caesars—such as were celebrated on 1 June at Severus's new inauguration, after he had already been acclaimed emperor by the army on 15 May—in these words: “Why on a joyful day do we not shade our doorposts with laurels or break into daylight with lamps?” And shortly afterward: “I address the Quirites themselves, the native rabble of the seven hills: does that Roman tongue spare any Caesar of its own? The Tiber is witness, and the training-schools of the beasts. If nature had stretched some transparent material over our breasts so they could be seen through, whose inmost heart would not appear engraved with the scene of Caesar after Caesar presiding over the distribution of a largess—even at the very hour when they shout: ‘May Jupiter add years to you from ours’?” Herodian indicates this in book II, writing among other things that the Praetorian soldiers laid aside their weapons and advanced crowned with laurels in ceremonial dress. Spartianus and the consular fasti likewise record that on 2 July, thirty days after coming to the city, Severus departed for the East against the usurper Niger, after first giving a largess to the people and a donative to the soldiers. Finally there is this coin inscription: “IMP. CAES. L. SEPT. SEV. PERT. AUG. COS. / THE GENEROSITY OF THE AUGUSTUS.” The omitted continuation belongs here too: “To bring hearths and couches out into public, to banquet street by street, to turn the city into a tavern, and to make mud with wine.”

We further judge that this passage from the book *To Scapula* belongs to this year: “Severus himself, the father of Antoninus, was mindful of Christians. For he sought out the Christian Proculus, surnamed Torpacion, steward of Euodia, who had once cured him with oil, and kept him in his palace until Proculus's death. Antoninus too knew him very well, having been reared on Christian milk” (*To Scapula* 4). That a grave illness befell Severus at the beginning of his reign is evident from Aelius Lampridius, if only because he wrote that, since Severus's sons were not yet of an age to rule, Severus intended that, should some human misfortune happen to him, Pescennius Niger and Clodius Albinus would succeed him. Niger was killed in the following year. [Col. 0051B] And because, as will be explained more fully below, Antoninus was named Caesar Augustus in Severus's fourth year, when he was thirteen, the author's statement about his upbringing must be referred to this year. Similar is Spartianus's account of him: “Whenever he saw condemned men exposed to beasts, he wept or turned away his eyes. When he was seven years old and heard that a boy who was his playmate had been severely beaten because of the Jewish religion, for a long time he would not look at his own father, the boy's father, or the man responsible for the beating.” That he was reared on Christian milk is further confirmed by his birth at Lyon, where the Christian faith flourished.
