AMENTIA (MADNESS). II, 684 B, “When God sent madness upon him”; thus he calls Adam’s sleep. See ECSTASY.

Ἀμήτωρ (*amētōr*), one who is without a mother. II, 73 A.

AMOR (LOVE). II, 247 A–B, “The loves of the Colchians”: love-philters, poisonous love-potions, and all other poisons. Compare Horace, *Odes* II.13.

AMPLIARE (TO ADJOURN; TO ENLARGE). I, 1318 A, “As though the Lord, by enlarging the law, does not distinguish it from the act of fornication.” II, 937 B, “That they may not only remain preserved, but also enlarged.” II, 76 B, “And the case was adjourned.” A case is said to be *ampliari* when its hearing is deferred or remitted to another time or another tribunal. Examples of adjourned cases occur in Livy, XLIII, where he says of M. Fitinnius: “After being twice adjourned, the defendant was acquitted at the third hearing.” And shortly afterward, concerning P. Furius Philus and M. Matienus: “Both were accused of the gravest crimes, and their cases were adjourned.”

AN, perhaps. I, 416 A, “Perhaps Christians,” that is, perhaps after becoming [Col. 1258A] Christians. The sense of the whole passage is: Believe them when, adjured by us, they speak the truth; for if they were permitted to lie, they would certainly do so whenever a Christian sought to establish the truth, lest they lose you, who are so profitable to them—or rather, lest at some future time they be driven from some possession of their own by you yourselves, if perhaps you became Christians.

ANABIBAZON, ἀναβιβάζων, “ascending”: among astrologers, the sign or natal planet (*planēta genethlios*, γενέθλιος) under which a person is conceived or born. II, 266 A, “For perhaps his Anabibazon stood in his way.”

ANDROMACHA (ANDROMACHE). II, 996 B, “For it has nothing to do with Andromache,” that is, nothing to do with Christ. Others regard this as proverbial, supposing that he is alluding to a play by some ancient tragedian entitled *Andromache*, and that the expression means nothing more than the phrases “Nothing to do with the verse,” “Nothing to do with Bacchus,” οὐδὲν πρὸς ἔπος, ἀπροσδιόνυσα—that is, irrelevant to the matter. For he compares the heretics with actors. Actors, adapting allegorical gestures to songs, represent things wholly remote from both the present play and the scene; likewise the heretics assign the same parables wherever they please, not [Col. 1258B] where they belong, and most effectively exclude them from their proper place. See PROVERBIAL FORMULAS, I, 210 D.

ANGELIFICARE (TO ANGELIFY). II, 832 B, “Re-formed and angelified in the kingdom of God.”

ANGINA (ANGINA). II, 732 B, “Or through compression of the liver there may be an angina of the mind,” that is, anguish of spirit.

ANGUSTIA (NARROW CIRCUMSTANCES). II, 905 A, “From narrow circumstances,” that is, from slender means or fortune.

ANIMA (SOUL). II, 228 B, “Swimming and flying souls.” II, 905 A, “Both in soul through expectation and in flesh through transformation,” that is: that virgin of yours is already married—already in soul through expectation of a husband, and married in flesh through the transformation of puberty. She is married in flesh and soul, just as in the phrase, “In face and shoulders like a god.” The meaning of the words that follow, “for whom you are preparing a second husband,” is this: your virgin, whom you consider a virgin, has ceased to be one because of her age—not, indeed, through being defiled by a man, but through age; accordingly, the husband to whom you give her will be her second.

ANIMALIS (ANIMAL; NATURAL), ψυχικός (*psychikos*). II, 953 C, “Recognize, then, the animal [Col. 1258C] faith, consisting entirely in zeal for the flesh.”

ANIMARE (TO ANIMATE; COLOR; GIVE LIFE OR SPIRIT). I, 1322 A, “Then the desirable heat of the sun, simultaneously animating and drying the hair.” *Animando* means “coloring,” as Lucretius speaks of “animated crops,” and Arnobius of “animated entrails.” I, 1202 B, “If waters know how to animate,” that is, to restore to a person the eternal life from which he had fallen through sinning. I, 1208 A, “To animate so great a brilliance with another spirit.” A machine is said to be animated when, through the modulations of a musician’s fingers, it releases in a brilliantly clear sound—distinguished by manifold variation—the breath compressed into water by pressure and embodied in it. Septimius, however, calls such breath “another spirit,” namely, a trained and articulated one. II, 994 C, “And you would believe that their mutual comparisons had animated them.” Colors are animated when they are so aptly placed together that each is enlivened by the vigor of the other.

ANIMATIO (ANIMATION). II, 556 B, “And the whole enthymesis, that is, animation together with passion,” etc. Irenaeus: “She laid aside her former intention, together with the passion that had befallen her.”

ANIMATRIX (ANIMATOR). II, 147 A, He calls perfect love the animator of confession, that is, that which incites, impels, and gives [Col. 1258D] courage to confess Christ.

ANNOTATIO (ASSIGNMENT), the charge that someone be lodged with care. I, 345 A, “Under the same assignment of the quaestor.”

ANNUS (YEAR). I, 487 A, “The year is in anxiety.” Zephyrus applies this to people’s anxiety concerning that year; but Tertullian seems to attribute anxiety and, as it were, travail not to people but to the year and season. II, 976 C, “When heaven stands amazed and the year is parched.”

ANNULUS (RING). I, 302 A, “The betrothal ring.” Tertullian says in this passage that it was golden. Pliny, however, *Natural History* XXX.1, teaches that this betrothal ring was made of iron. Kipping, *Roman Antiquities*, thinks Tertullian spoke according to the custom of his own age. Or did he rather misunderstand Pliny, who was speaking not of a betrothal ring but of a symbol of the frugality and modesty by which a bride or newly married woman was recalled to those ancient and uncouth Sabine women?

ANNUUS (ANNUAL). II, 79 B, “We make offerings for the dead and for birthdays on their annual day.”

[Col. 1259A] ANTECESSOR (FORERUNNER). II, 890 B, “He alone is the forerunner, because he alone comes after Christ”; concerning the Paraclete. II, 364 A, “Bereft of the protection of their predecessors.”

ANTECESSIVUM (ANTECEDENT). II, 894 G, “Both the subjective to the antecedent and the partial to the universal.”

ANTECURSOR (FORERUNNER). II, 825 A, “And thereafter the flower, the forerunner of the fruit.”

ANTELIUS. I, 684 A, “Among the Greeks also we read of Apollo θυραῖος (*thyraios*, ‘of the doorway’) and of the Antelii, demons who guard doorways.” Hesychius: Ἀντήλιοι θεοὶ, οἱ πρὸ τῶν θυρῶν ἱδρυμένοι. Εὐριπίδης, Μελεάγρῳ—“Antelian gods: those established before the doors. Euripides, in *Meleager*.” Then: ἀντήλιος, ὁ ἴσος τῷ ἡλίῳ φαινόμενος· καταχρηστικῶς δὲ ὁ ἀντικρὺ ἡλίου ἱδρυμένος βωμὸς ἢ θεός—“*Antēlios*: one appearing equal to the sun; by an extended use, an altar or god established directly opposite the sun.”

ANTELUCANUS (BEFORE DAWN). II, 79 B, “Even at assemblies before dawn.” I, 273 A, “Assemblies before dawn for singing to Christ.”

ANTENNA (YARDARM). II, 346 B, “For on the yardarm also, which forms part of the cross, the extremities are called horns.” Thus in Virgil: “We turn the horns of the sail-clad yardarms.”

ANTIAE are the front locks of hair hanging down from the forehead. II, 1042 A, “The molars shadowed among the forelocks.”

ANTICIPARE (TO ANTICIPATE). II, 858 B, “For every consummation and [Col. 1259B] perfection, although last in order, is anticipated in desire.”

ANTIQUITAS (ANTIQUITY). I, 515 A, “For the antiquity already established for the divine literature continues to benefit my argument.”

ANTIQUIUS (MORE ANCIENT; PRIOR). II, 213 B, “Something will have been more ancient than the Father,” that is, prior by nature, not in time, as the following words teach.

ANTISTARE (TO SURPASS; EXCEL; BE SUPERIOR). I, 442 A, “Who certainly live and surpass the dead.” II, 279 B, “For not even the continence of virginity excels.” With Rigault, read *virginitati*, “excels virginity.” II, 845 C, “But we surpass many sparrows.”

ANTISTATUS (ATTENDANT BODY). II, 563 B, “The commensurable attendant body of angels,” that is, the angels who are Soter’s attendants, standing before him or appearing to him in order to perform their ministries. He calls them “commensurable” because their kind could be compared with Soter’s substance. Junius interprets *antistatum* as προστασίαν (*prostasian*), “attendance” or “presidency.”

ANTISTES (PRESIDENT; CHIEF PRIEST). I, 259 B, “The chief priests of the Roman Empire.” Zephyrus understands this of the pontiffs themselves, whose authority administered sacred affairs, ceremonies, and, in short, all religion, [Col. 1259C] matters in the interpretation of which the highest concerns of the commonwealth were involved. Hence Cicero calls them “chiefs of public counsel” and “priests of the immortal gods.” Ancient inscriptions establish that the emperors themselves were also among them, since in those inscriptions they are called chief pontiffs.

ANTITHESIS (ANTITHESIS). II, 267 C, “For these are Marcion’s antitheses, that is, contrary oppositions.”

AORI, ἄωροι (*aōroi*), those who perish by an untimely death. II, 745 B.

Ἀπάτωρ (*apatōr*), one who is without a father. II, 73 A.

APEX, the insignia of priests: it was a sewn cap with which the flamens covered their heads. II, 1015 A; I, 363 A. II, 164 A, “An *apex* made of a ray”: by an analogy drawn from the caps of the flamens, he calls *apex* that threadlike thing which we see flashing amid darting rays when the sun shines, and in a manner hanging before our eyes. Lucretius calls them “weapons of daylight”; P. Varro, “darts”; Prudentius called them “points of the sun” or “of the rays.” II, 988 A, “Between the two most prominent summits of crimes.” II, 629 B, “Concerning, namely, the tips of Christ’s cross.” By “tips” he means what he calls [Col. 1259D] “horns” above.

APOCARTERESIS, ἀποκαρτέρησις (*apokarterēsis*), is the resolve and choice to die through abstinence from food and starvation. I, 512 A, “Lycurgus chose death by starvation as a hypocrite.” II, 262 B, “If through death by starvation you prove yourself a Marcionite,” that is: prove by the deed itself—by voluntary starvation and self-killing—that you are a hypocrite who rejects the Creator.

APORIATIO (PERPLEXITY), hesitation, fear, confusion. I, 69 A, “Conceived and uttered out of perplexity.”

APOSTOLICUS (APOSTOLIC). Hermogenes is called “apostolic” (II, 198 A), because, although the Apostle did not brand him in his mind, he nevertheless marked him by sharing his name. II, 472 B, “He confirms the Scripture of the Apostolic Acts.” It was customary among the ancient Fathers to call τὰς τῶν ἀποστόλων Πράξεις (“the Acts of the Apostles”) simply *Apostolica*, Ἀποστόλικα (“the Apostolic [books]”).

APOSTOLI (APOSTLES), whom this designation interprets as “those sent.” II, 52 A.

[Col. 1260A] APPARENTIA (APPEARANCE), ἐπιφάνεια (*epiphaneia*, “manifestation”). II, 268 A, “From the appearance of Christ down to Marcion’s audacity.”

APPARERE (TO ATTEND), to provide for, tend, or minister. I, 347 A, “For the soothsayer also attends upon the dead.” I, 527 A, “Another who attends upon God’s judgment,” that is, ministers to it. II, 814 C, “Rather to serve as a vessel for the soul.” I, 650 A, “Since ears and eyes attend upon the spirit.” Thus Cicero also calls the senses the mind’s attendants. I, 687 A, “Who attended upon idolatrous kings to the very end of their idolatry.”

APPARITIO (SERVICE). II, 59 A, “Devoted service.”

APPARITOR (ATTENDANT). I, 650 A, “Nor can the world be furnished, since its attendants are defiled”; he is speaking of the senses, the eyes and ears. See APPARERE.

APPENDIX (APPENDAGE). II, 744 A, “In paradise, to which even then the patriarchs and prophets [went as] appendages of the Lord’s resurrection.” II, 806 B, “And filth, the appendage of this duty”—fasting. II, 977 B, “Wantonness and luxury are, of course, the appendages of gluttony.”

APPLICARE (TO APPROACH; APPLY; ATTACH). II, 123 A, “They approach by means of the passions,” that is, they come near gently and softly. II, 502 D, “He attached an angel of Satan [Col. 1260B] to his apostle to buffet him.” I, 341 A, “The beasts whom you attach to Liber.”

APULIA. I, 652 B, “Because God has eyes even beyond the Apulias.” This word appears to signify the awnings used in theaters and amphitheaters for decoration and to ward off the scorching blows of the sun. These were sometimes purple, a color with which the finest wool was probably dyed, such as came from Apulia. Martial: “Apulian fleeces of the first quality.” Hence the awnings also acquired the name “Apulias.”

AQUA (WATER). I, 1202 A, “Water, a worthy vehicle for God.” I, 1224 A, “After the water,” that is, after baptism.

AQUARIOLUS (PROCURER). I, 495 A. Festus: “*Aquarioli* was the name given to the sordid attendants of unchaste women.” They also performed the role of pimps and arranged engagements for prostitutes. Turnebus, *Adversaria*, book XIV, chapter 12.

AQUATILIS (AQUATIC). I, 1259 A, “After the aquatic train that followed the rock, they despair of the Lord.”

AQUATIO (DRAWING OF WATER). II, 962 A, “Israel had sinned while drawing water [Col. 1260C] at Mizpah.”

AQUIGENUS (WATER-BORN). II, 299 B, “Among water-born and earth-born animals,” ὑδρογενῆ καὶ γηγενῆ τῶν ζώων, “the aquatic and terrestrial kinds of animals.”

AQUILEGUS (WATER-DRAWING). II, 707 A, “How greatly they will congratulate themselves upon their former and familiar water-drawing marks,” that is: beasts of burden—mules and asses—employed in turning these machines would congratulate themselves upon their condition if they ever happened to be changed into human beings, since they would then not sweat so greatly in turning these wheels. Vitruvius describes them. They were devices that drew up and poured out water by means of buckets.

AQUILEX (WATER ENGINEER) was the name given to the craftsman charged with finding, conducting, and managing water. II, 326 C, “God did not immediately promise that he would be a water engineer and farmer.”

AQUILICIA, sacred rites instituted for drawing forth and obtaining water. I, 487 A, “You sacrifice water-drawing rites to Jupiter.” This was a Roman custom, for among the Carthaginians Juno more properly presided over this concern. Compare chapter 33.

ARA (ALTAR). II, 1031 A, “After Pompey’s three altars,” namely, his triumphal altars—that is, his triumphs. I, 1182 A, “Will not your station be more solemn [Col. 1260D] if you have also stood at God’s altar?” He alludes to stations and employs a technical term of sacrificial ritual. An animal dedicated as a victim and awaiting sacrifice is said to “stand at the altar.” Virgil, *Georgics* II: “And the sacred he-goat, led by the horn, will stand at the altar.” I, 1286 A, “For God’s altar ought to be presented as clean.” Elsewhere he calls the Christian person God’s altar, from whose heart prayers are borne to God. Here, however, in a certain exceptional sense, he calls the heads of the ecclesiastical order God’s altar, because they convey the Church’s vows to God and through them the Christian Church customarily brings the sacrifices of its prayers before God. I, 677 B, “He will spit upon smoking altars and blow upon them.” II, 1048 A, “I am accustomed to prescribe remedies for morals from any ledge or altar.” Here, of course, it is the philosopher in the cloak who speaks, not the Christian. For a Christian could not be brought to leap upon an altar for the sake of making a speech, since Christians abhorred altars and sacrifices, and Christians passing by at that time would blow upon them. II, 136 C, “And the altar, a funeral pyre.”

[Col. 1261A] ARBITER (WITNESS; OBSERVER). II, 960 A, “He was going to lament those same leaders and arbiters of his,” that is, spectators and witnesses. II, 317 B–C, “With the Father as arbiter,” that is, witness. II, 414 A, “He takes three of the disciples as arbiters of the future vision and voice.”

ARBITRATRIX (ARBITRESS). II, 299 B, “Which was found in the Lord as arbitress of his works.”

ARBUSTUS (TREE-GROWN; ROBUST). II, 320 B, “He who made his affairs more tree-grown at their beginnings,” that is, one who managed his affairs in a more robust form—one, namely, who did not plant slips, but already-grown trees, and indeed larger trees, which produce fruit much earlier than slips.

ARCANUM (SECRET). I, 1297 A, “Whose secrets they preserve for the danger which they believe,” etc. By “the wives’ secrets” he means that pagan husbands do not betray their wives when they participate in Christian sacred rites. Those pagan husbands therefore preserve their wives’ secrets for the danger which they believe—that is, for the danger they believe threatens the Christian name, when persecution, as they hope, will blaze forth, so that they may then betray them if perhaps, through some hurt they have suffered, they desire their wives’ destruction. This is the meaning of “if perhaps they are hurt”: if perhaps [Col. 1261B] some injury to their feelings occurs, if there is some quarrel. See OBJECTION.

ARCHETYPUS (ARCHETYPAL). II, 548 A, “Archetypal teachers” are the principal and more ancient teachers.

ARCHIGALLUS, the chief of the Galli, the priests of Cybele, mother of the gods. I, 425 A. See EMPIRE. II, 815 A, “Either of the female sculptor or of the archigallus.”

ARCHIVA (ARCHIVES), journals, annals. I, 401 B, note 4, “You have it recorded in your archives.”

ARCHON, ἄρχων (*archōn*), prince or chief magistrate. II, 88 B; II, 215 A, ἄρχοντες (*archontes*), “rulers.”

Ἀρχή (*archē*, BEGINNING; PRINCIPALITY). II, 215 A, “ἀρχή denotes primacy not only in the ordering sense but also in the sense of power.” II, 551 A, Προαρχὴν and τὴν ἀρχὴν, “the pre-beginning” and “the beginning.” II, 579 A, “The earthy one, flesh by the substance of the ἀρχή, matter.” The Greek text of Irenaeus requires: “the earthy body by substance, flesh from matter.”

ARCUATUS (ARCHED). II, 122 A, “Rising with an arched rush”; concerning the scorpion.

ARCTARE (TO CONSTRAIN). II, 710 B, “Lest, after being committed to custody, you be constrained until the whole debt is discharged.”

[Col. 1261C] ARDOR (HEAT; BURNING). II, 106 B, “For when we are scorched by the heat of persecution.” II, 123 A, “And they are equipped at a fixed time, and at no other than that of the Gnostics’ heat.” I, 510 A, “I know also Phryne the prostitute, rutting beneath the heat of Diogenes as he lay upon her.”

AREA (OPEN GROUND; THRESHING FLOOR; BURIAL GROUND). I, 701 A, “Let there be no burial grounds; there were no threshing floors of their own.” He plays upon the word. For when that man cried, “Let there be no *areae*,” he meant burial grounds; when Tertullian replies, “There were no *areae* of their own,” he means threshing floors. *Area* is used for a cemetery. Pontius the Deacon, in the *Acts of Cyprian*: “He was buried in the *area* of the procurator Candidus.”

ARENA. I, 321 A, “A spectacle in the arena,” that is, a gladiatorial show. I, 467 A, “With the savagery of the arena.”

ARENARIUS NUMERORUM (ARITHMETICIAN), one who shows pupils various arithmetical combinations of numbers upon an abacus sprinkled with dust. II, 1050 A.

ARGUMENTARI (TO ARGUE; DEVISE), to proceed by subtleties or cleverly devise or expound something. I, 635 B, “Lest anyone think that we are arguing sophistically.” I, 639 B, “From it they devise the name of the Circus.”

[Col. 1261D] ARIDUS (DRY). II, 1040 A, “Dry fattening” is ξηροφαγία (*xērophagia*, “dry eating”), which was characteristic of athletes. II, 954 A, “Either late or dry foods.”

ARIES (BATTERING RAM). II, 800 B, “And everywhere this first ram is poised against us, by which the condition of the flesh is battered.” A ram is a beam suspended by ropes, its head sheathed in iron so that it appears to possess, like a ram, a very hard forehead; it is also drawn backward in the manner of a ram so that it may strike with greater force and bring down walls. Hence the verb *temperari* (“to be poised”), because skill and a certain balance were required in preparing and positioning it for its intended blows. The expression may be proverbial for: “A powerful argument is brought against us, one difficult to resolve; we are assaulted by open force,” just as rams and mines are contrasted with one another in arguments. II, 1031 B, “For the ram also,” etc.

ARITHMI, the Book of Numbers. II, 431 C.

[Col. 1262A] ARMARIUM (CABINET; ARCHIVE). I, 1307 B, “Because it is not admitted into the Jewish cabinet”—that is, into that canon of Scriptures which was preserved in the temple of the Hebrew people through the diligence of successive priests. Augustine so interprets it in *The City of God* XV.23. The same author, in book XII *Against Faustus*, says of the Jewish people: “What else is that nation but, as it were, the document chests of Christians, carrying the Law and the Prophets?” I, 670 B, “How much more quickly he constructs the cabinet.”

ARMARE (TO ARM). II, 687 B, “Which alone I could have armed as testimony to the fullness of the divinity.” II, 259 B, “He armed the knowledge of himself with so many works.”

ARTIFEX (ARTIST; PERFORMER). I, 655 B, “That artist of fists,” that is, a boxer. II, 762 B, “But the maker of the spectacle.” I, 636 A, “Hence the Romans borrow performers whom they have summoned,” that is, actors.

ARTUS (NARROW). II, 1030 C, “Nor narrow for the hands,” that is, more confined or constricted. See Cicero, *Against Catiline*. I, 1243 B, “How greatly the matter is in straits.”

ASINARIUS (ASS-WORSHIPER), a worshiper of an ass. I, 365 A; 577 C, “Because [Col. 1262B] among the worshipers of every kind of cattle and beast, we alone are ass-worshipers.”

ASINUS (ASS). II, 416 B, “The ass from Aesop’s well”: an inept fellow suddenly arriving and filling everything with his untimely braying.

ASPIS (ASP). II, 331 C, “The asp—as they say—borrows its poison from the viper.” Pliny says of the asp, *Natural History* VIII.23: “The necks of asps swell”; and “there is no remedy for their stroke, unless the affected parts are amputated immediately.” Its poison is, of course, so swift-acting. Of the viper, in the same book, chapter 39: “Among serpents, the viper alone is said to hide in the earth, while the others hide in hollows of trees or rocks”—but Aristotle, *History of Animals* V, gives the contrary account, saying that the viper hides beneath rocks while the other serpents conceal themselves in the earth. “All of them sleep without venom during their season of retreat.” In book VII, chapter 2, Pliny says that “the inhabitants of Mount Athos feed upon vipers’ flesh.” This is certainly evidence of a less effective poison. The proverb will therefore mean that the most pestilential creature [Col. 1262C] is instructed in wickedness by one less evil.

ASSECTATUS (PURSUED). II, 948 B, “And a widow pursued for some time,” that is, desired. Rhenanus reads *affectatae*, “desired.”

ASSENTATOR (ABettor; APPROVER). II, 206 A, “God is found to be an abettor of evil, one who assents to evil’s being done.” That is, although God recognizes matter as evil, he nevertheless uses it—as Hermogenes’ doctrine maintained—and either reinforces its depravity or at least ignores it. Junius conjectures *assectator*, “follower,” one who would have been obliged συνακολουθεῖν (*synakolouthein*), “to follow along with,” and endure the nature of matter.

ASSERTOR (CHAMPION; CLAIMANT), anyone who claims another into either free or servile status, a defender of another’s liberty. II, 273 C, “Such a claimant would also be condemned in the secular court.” I, 1287 A, “Whose champion disputed with the Lord,” that is, an advocate who undertakes to defend a person.

ASSIGNARE (TO ASSIGN; MARK; BIND). I, 1269 C, “And he assigns the virgin,” that is, he keeps her marked and bound by the chastity she shares with widows. I, 1272 A, “He assigns the public confession,” namely, to the repentance mentioned immediately before. II, 18 B, “But they faithfully assigned to the nations the discipline received from Christ.” [Col. 1262D] II, 1046 A, “And let him entrust the entire construction of the gathered fold to the restraining clasps.” III, 137 B, “Assigning the enduring stains of his blood to the flints.” II, 1023 B, “Discipline governs a person; power assigns him,” that is, points him out, displays him as distinguished, χαρακτηρίζει (*charaktērizei*, “characterizes” or “marks”).

ASSUMERE (TO ASSUME). I, 1236 B, “And they assume repentance.” A person is said to “assume repentance” when he professes his name among the hearers.

ATHENAE (ATHENS). II, 20 B, “What, then, has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” that is, what have philosophers to do with Christians?

ATTENTARE (TO ATTACK), to assail. II, 55 B: for the impious, proceeding from their own opinions, assail the understanding of the pious as though with the hand, and strive for this one thing: to transform truth into falsehood.

ATTONITUS (INTENSELY ALERT; ENTRANCED). II, 58 B, “An intensely alert diligence,” which is always afraid lest it commit some error. I, 656 B, “Entranced by mimes,” that is, attending to the mimes with an entranced mind.

[Col. 1263A] AVENA (WILD OAT). II, 156 A, “The Praxean wild oats.” He compares false doctrine to wild oats, for, as Pliny writes, the wild oat is a corruption of grain. In the same passage: “Those wild oats, indeed, had then shaken out their seed everywhere.”

AVOCATOR (ONE WHO CALLS AWAY). II, 762 B, “Not a raiser of the dead, but one who calls away the living,” that is, one who calls the living away from life to death, and from right faith to heresy.

AVOCATRIX (SEDUCTRESS). II, 647 C, “The seductress of all truth.”

AUCTOR (OWNER; PRINCIPAL), the lawful owner. II, 51 B, “And from the owners themselves,” that is, κυρίοις (*kyriois*, “lords”), the transferors of ownership. II, 113 B, “But since the principals themselves, who preside by authority, are those in whose hands lies the authority for maintaining the Church.” In the Glosses of Philoxenus, *auctor* is rendered ἀρχηγός (“leader”) and αὐθέντης (“master”); and in the books of the land surveyors, *auctores* is used absolutely for teachers and masters.

AUCTORAMENTUM (CONTRACTED SERVICE). I, 476 A, “Under contract to the belly.”

AUCTORARE (TO HIRE ONESELF OUT), to seek wages. I, 697 D, “Let us enter these contests as hired combatants.” *Auctorati* were those who, [Col. 1263B] for an offered wage or prize, had bound themselves and pledged their service as gladiators. I, 626 B, “Some have now hired themselves out even to the flames,” that is, bound themselves. II, 77 C, “And sufficiently hired under the protection of consent,” that is, confirmed and bound by its authority.

AUCTORITAS (AUTHORITY), supreme power in administering an empire. I, 258. II, 49 A, “Whence authority is also available to us,” that is: from that Church its institutions likewise furnish authority—κῦρος καὶ βεβαίωσις (*kyros kai bebaiōsis*, “validity and confirmation”)—to us Christians, just as do the other aforementioned and similar things; and that chair is available, etc. II, 977 A, “And they sigh at the authority of the star that delays,” that is, they grieve that the rising of the stars is delayed so long, since before it is unlawful to break the fast.

AUDIRE (TO BE HEARD OF; BE CELEBRATED). II, 1034 A, “It alone used to be heard of,” that is, formerly it was on everyone’s lips because of its fertility, and scarcely anything else was discussed. II, 796 A, “We shall know what the Sadducees hear from Christ,” that is, what name they are called and in what ways they are refuted. I, 1239 A, “Another among the hearers,” [Col. 1263C] that is, the catechumens.

AUDITUS (HEARING). II, 404 A, “Who raised up for hearing,” that is, for the hearing or for the purpose of hearing, by a Greek idiom: τῇ ἀκοῇ ἤγειρον, “they raised up for hearing.”

AURIGA (CHARIOTEER). II, 930 C, “Among others’ eunuchs and your own charioteers.” Rigault reads *perorigae tuae*. In Varro, *On Agriculture* II, where the mating of mares is discussed, *peroriga* means anyone who brings them to the stallion. Accordingly, he calls the Psychics *perorigae*, because, as he himself says, they pressed marriage upon people and, as it were, provoked sexual desire. Le Prieur prefers *urigae*, that is, those who, so to speak, aroused sexual desire. I, 641 B, “Deservedly they clothed idolatry even in charioteering colors,” that is, in their idolatrous games they assigned to their charioteers colors corresponding to the idols; for he calls these colors “charioteers,” just as Virgil, *Aeneid* XII, uses “charioteer sister” adjectivally. I, 661 A, “The charioteer wholly red upon the fiery wheel”; he alludes to the Red faction of the racers.

AURUGINARE (TO BECOME JAUNDICED). II, 675 C, “Those who become jaundiced from an excess of bile,” that is, suffer from jaundice. *Aurugo* means jaundice, from the color of gold. [Col. 1263D] An old glossary: ἴκτερος, “jaundice”; ἰκτερικός, *auruginosus*, “jaundiced,” one who suffers from that disease.

AUSPEX (AUSPICE; COMMANDER). II, 140 C, “As formerly, not under an auspice,” that is, under the person by whose command and leadership the affair was conducted. Rigault, however, dislikes this word and thinks nothing is missing except the name of a place.

AUT (OR). II, 164 E, “Thus he is either Father or Son,” that is: thus he is either the Father, who is designated by the word “Father” and not “Son”; or he is the Son, who is designated by the word “Son” and not “Father.” When the discourse concerns this or that person, the designation corresponds to the truth of the person. I, 1175 A, “Or how many arteries are needed”: *aut* means “otherwise,” in the Greek manner, for ἤ sometimes has this meaning.

AUTEM (BUT). The word itself is placed like a clasp—the connective particle attached to link the narrative together. II, 221 B.

Αὐτεξούσιον (*AUTEXOUSION*, SELF-DETERMINATION). II, 685 A, “Having within us the free power of choice subject to him, which is called αὐτεξούσιον.”

AUTHENTICUS (AUTHENTIC; ORIGINAL). II, 49 A, “Among whom their authentic letters [Col. 1264A] are read aloud,” that is, sounding forth the voice of each one in the same language in which they were written by the apostles. II, 546 B, “He broke away from the Church of the authentic rule.” II, 946 A, “Let us know plainly that it is not so in the authentic Greek text.”

AXIS. Festus: a round post around which a wheel turns. I, 531 A, “Bound to the post of half an axle.”

## B

BACCHANS (BACCHANTIC). II, 1043 B, “Worthily indeed, so that it might rustle something beneath the bacchantically raging garments.” “Bacchantic garments” are the purple robes proper to women celebrating the Bacchic rites.

BAJULI (BEARERS). I, 1281 B, “Nor German bearers.” German bearers, attendants of eminent men or more delicate women, carried them either seated in covered carriages or reclining in litters; the bearers were selected especially for their height.

BALTEUS or BALTEUM (BELT; ENCLOSING BARRIER). I, 634 B, “For they call the paths the pivots of the belts running around the circumference.” There were two kinds of path: one transverse, for those proceeding around the circumference; the other [Col. 1264B] straight, as though down the slope—as the author says—for those making their way around the seating area. The circuit nearest or surrounding the seating area belonged to the more distinguished spectators; the sloping section belonged to the common people surrounding that circuit. Tertullian relates that the circuit in which the senators’ orchestra stood was divided by belts and pivots.

It is established that the terms *baltei* or, more frequently, *baltea* were applied metaphorically to structures that served, as it were, as belts and barriers for other things, binding and securing them in their proper place. At public spectacles, likewise, *baltei* or *baltea* was the name given to the barriers between sections of seating, by which the στίχοι (*stichoi*), or rows, of senators, equestrians, and magistrates were divided so that they might watch comfortably—say, ten, fifteen, twenty, thirty, and so forth enclosed within each belt, according to their greater proximity to or distance from the circus or seating area, the length of the belts diminishing accordingly.

Each belt had two pivots of its own at either path. Through these the spectators entered from right and left; having entered, they closed them so that no one else could enter the belt. [Col. 1264C] By synecdoche they were called “pivots” because the doors turned and were mounted upon hinges, so that spectators—as the author also calls them in chapter 20—might have free entrance and exit when passing from the path into the belt or from the belt into the path. A similar arrangement existed among the common people, except that, in place of the more honorable belts, the people had plain seats, without barriers and handrails, and certain slight divisions between the paths in place of the pivots reserved for the more distinguished.

These, therefore, are the paths of which the author speaks: between those pivots and divisions, one could pass out of the spectacle—or in the opposite direction—from the stage and fore-stage, which were separated by a curtain, following the pivots of the belts around the circuit where the more distinguished sat, and following the divisions of the common people down the slope.

BAPTISMUS (BAPTISM). II, 1209 A, “After the baptism of the world,” that is, after the Flood. II, 1213 A, “When Paul alone of them had put on Christ’s baptism.” II, 1217 A, “The baptism of blood.”

BARBARIA (BARBAROUS LAND). II, 890 B, “Through Greece and certain [Col. 1264D] barbarous lands belonging to it.” By the “barbarous lands of Greece” he means certain regions outside or adjoining Greece with which the Greeks had dealings or wars. Horace: “Greece, colliding with barbarism in a protracted war.”

BARDUS (DULL). II, 230 B, “The painter is not duller than you.”

BASILIA, βασιλεία (*basileia*, KINGDOM). II, 350 B, “In the first division of the Kingdoms.” They divided the *Basiliai*, or “Kingdoms”—that is, the Books of Kingdoms—into two parts. The first comprised the two books entitled Samuel; the second, the two Books of Kings. Those who read “in the First Book of Kingdoms” are mistaken, for the passage occurs in the first division of the Kingdoms, in the second of the books entitled Samuel, chapter 7. II, 389 A, “So also previously in the Kingdoms.”

BAXA, a kind of gilded women’s shoe. II, 670 B; II, 1043 B, “The slipper and the *baxa* are gilded every day.”

BEKKOS. I, 570 B, “The first sound reported was *Bekkos*”; its interpretation is that it is the Phrygian word for bread.

BENEDICTUS (BLESSED). II, 42 A, “Under the episcopate of the blessed Eleutherus.” [Col. 1265A] Among the earliest Christians, “Blessed” was an eminent title. II, 1003 C, “The good shepherd and blessed Pope.”

BENEFICIARII was the name given to soldiers selected through the favor of the emperor for specified offices or duties; thus they attended upon the emperor in carrying out his commands. II, 118 C.

BENEFICIUM (BENEFIT). II, 1031 A, “The benefit of an injury.” The injury was inflicted upon Carthage in its destruction; the benefit consisted in permission to rebuild it.

BERYLLUS, βήρυλλος (*bēryllos*, BERYL), is one of the bright green gems found among the Indians. Pliny, *Natural History* XXXVII.5, gives one of its varieties as chrysoberyl, pale indeed, but shading into a golden color. Agricola, *On Fossils* VI, writes that chrysoberyl is also called “Choaspites.” Beryl is somewhat dimmer, with a radiance inclining toward gold. II, 660 B, “Nor therefore is there watery matter in beryls because they shimmer with a strained brilliance.”

BESTIA (BEAST). II, 261 B, “Those very beasts of your bed and mat”—bedbugs and fleas. II, 913 A, “Like a certain beast,” etc.: a circumlocution for the ostrich or “sparrow-camel,” [Col. 1265B] which Aristotle describes very well in *Parts of Animals* IV, and Pliny at the beginning of *Natural History* X.

BESTIARII, those who fought with beasts in the arena, whether condemned criminals or, when criminals were lacking, others matched against savage wild animals. I, 317 A. When they ate their last meal, a generous table was set before them. I, 492 A, “I do not recline at the public Liberalia, as is customary for beast-fighters eating their final supper.”

BIAEOTHANATI, βιαιοθάνατοι (*biaiothanatoi*), those whose souls are violently torn away through the savagery of punishments. II, 747 B.

BIBERE (TO DRINK). I, 471 A, “Who have drunk one spirit of holiness.” He contrasts them with those who, in the *Tablet of Cebes*, were said to drink error and ignorance. Shortly afterward: “To feast upon discipline.”

BISCULUM. I, 1246 B, “Some little piece of iron or bronze for digging around the nails.”

BLATTIRE (TO BABBLE), to speak incoherently and inanely. II, 1032 B, “As Silenus babbles into the ears of Midas.”

BOTRONATUS (CLUSTERED) describes a mass of hair arranged in clusters. Hesychius: [Col. 1265C] Βοτρυδὸν, ἑτέρα τῆς ἑτέρας ἐχομένη, ὡς οἱ βότρυες τὰς ῥάγας ἔχουσι πυκνὰς καὶ κατὰ συστροφήν, ἢ συνηγμένοι ὁμοῦ—“In clusters: one thing adjoining another, as bunches of grapes have their berries closely packed and twisted together, or gathered together in one mass.” I, 1329 A.

BOTULUS (SAUSAGE). II, 954 B, “The outer and inner sausages of the Psychics.” This is a bitter transferred epithet directed against the orthodox, as though he had said: “Those coarse, fat, and obese Psychics, resembling sausages, as tightly stuffed as sausages.” He was therefore justified in first asking pardon of their ears. For a sausage’s interior is crammed with a stuffing of meat, like the belly of some glutton, while its exterior is shamelessly distended in the manner of a penis, like that god of Horace made from a fig-tree trunk: “And the red stake projecting obscenely from his groin.”

BRABIUM, βραβεῖον (*brabeion*), a prize. I, 624 B, “The prize of angelic substance.”

BREVIARIUM (EPITOME). I, 1153 B, “So that the epitome of the whole Gospel is truly comprehended in the prayer,” that is, a brief review and summary. Pliny, *Natural History* VII.26: “This is his epitome from the East.”

BUCCELLA (MORSEL). II, 943 B, “And they will plainly distribute husbands [Col. 1265D] and wives in that way, like morsels,” that is, to everyone who asks. For he has in view the saying: “Give to everyone who asks you.”

BUCCINAE. These are smaller shells produced in the sea. II, 1033 B.

BYSSINUM (FINE LINEN). I, 1334 A, “Clothe yourselves in the silk of integrity, the fine linen of holiness.” Byssus is commonly said to be a kind of Indian flax drawn like wool from a tree very similar to the Pontic tree, although some maintain that it is the bark of an Indian plant.

BYTHION (DEPTH). II, 551 A.

BYTHOS, βυθός (*bythos*), abyss, depth. II, 68 A; 551 A. Valentinus’s Only-Begotten, born of Father Βυθός (“Depth”) and Mother Σιγή (“Silence”).

## C

CACABULUM, a vessel used to receive the discharge of the bowels and bladder. I, 344 B.

CACABUS (COOKING POT). II, 977 B, “Among you the love-feast boils in cooking pots.”

CACTUS, a kind of thorn. Hesychius: Κάκτος, ἄκανθα [Col. 1266A] ὑφ’ ἧς ἐὰν πληγῇ νεβρὸς, ἀχρεῖα ἴσχει τὰ ὄντα εἰς αὐλούς—“Cactus: a thorn by which, if a fawn is wounded, it renders useless those parts suitable for making pipes.” II, 1036 A. Some read *exsecto* (“cut out”), others *exacto* (“driven out”).

CADAVER (CORPSE). II, 819 B, “So that from falling (*cadendo*) it is thereafter called a corpse (*cadaver*).” II, 878 C, “When cold, when pallid, when hardened, when a corpse.”

CAECUS (BLIND; HIDDEN). II, 903 A, “With its own hidden good,” that is, latent, not set before the eyes, as though submerged in darkness and obscurity, unknown; in what follows, “manifest” is opposed to it. II, 662 B, “But gnats also make a sound, not blind even within the darkness of the ears.” II, 733 C, “To pass the night in blind sleep,” that is, without visions.

CAEDERE (TO CUT). II, 281 C, “For which it is better even to exist by cutting.” The sense is: for which holiness it would be better—that is, more useful and desirable—to exist “by cutting,” namely, to be equal to marriage by cutting and excising it. “We would be better treated,” he says, “if our holiness were of such a kind as to lead us to abolish marriage and make us without any share in marriage.” II, 876 A, “Although the questions have already been cut down from another quarter,” that is, overthrown. II, 347 A, “And with hands striking the breast in prayer.”

[Col. 1266B] CAESARIATUS (HORSE-CRESTED). II, 1040 A, “Wearing horsehair crests,” that is, whose helmets bore a mane of horsehair.

CAESIO (BEATING), πληκτισμός (*plēktismos*). I, 477 A, “Then we depart, not into bands for beatings.” Suetonius mentions this insolence in *Nero*, chapter XXVI: “For he had become accustomed to beat people returning from dinner, to wound those who resisted, and to plunge them into the sewers.”
