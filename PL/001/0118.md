To understand the force of this argument, it must be recalled that some think Cybele was surnamed from Mount Cybelus in Phrygia. No one doubts that her celebrated image was located at Pessinus, a city of Phrygia, and was afterward installed at Rome with the greatest pomp, as we have said elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 19, art. [Col. 1122C] 1; *On Arnobius*, ch. 20, art. 2; and *On Lactantius*, ch. 31, art. 3).

Tertullian then argues from this as follows: Did Cybele love the Romans on account of Aeneas, who came from Troy, a city of Lesser Phrygia, and was therefore her fellow countryman, because after he had been rescued from the burning of Troy and the arms of the Achaeans, they traced their origin back to him? Did she, moreover, despise her own native land and prefer the Romans to it because she foresaw that her image would be transferred to Rome and that Greece—the conqueror of her native Phrygia—would be conquered by the Romans? Why, then, did she allow Phrygia, where she was worshipped with the highest honor, to be taken by the Greeks? Yet grant, if you wish, that she allowed this. But how could she have foreknown that she herself—or, what amounts to the same thing, her image—would one day be carried from Pessinus to Rome? For she foresaw this no more than she foresaw the day on which the emperor Marcus Aurelius died at Sirmium. Indeed, Tertullian says, either she did not know it, or she sent very tardy messengers to her chief priest, the archigallus, who, on the sixth day after that emperor’s death, made an offering for his welfare of impure blood drawn from his own arms. Christians therefore rightly and deservedly ridiculed such a goddess, either ignorant of the future or cruel, who permitted not only the blood of her archigallus to be wrongly shed, but also her own native country to be utterly destroyed. She could therefore have rendered the Romans no assistance in extending the boundaries of their empire without displaying an unjust and ungrateful disposition.

[Col. 1123A] Nor should Jupiter have embraced them with any greater zeal and favor (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25). Could he have forgotten that cave on Mount Ida in the island of Crete, where the pagans proclaimed that he had been born and buried? Had his love of country been transformed into hatred, so that he allowed his own Crete, as Tertullian puts it (*ibid.*), to be shaken by the Roman fasces and arms? Had the brazen instruments of the Corybantes also slipped from his memory, whose clash and din frustrated the most savage cruelty of his father Saturn, who wished to devour him? Finally, had he willfully buried in oblivion the exceedingly pleasant odor of his nurse there (*ibid.*)?

Many, however, are uncertain who that nurse was and what her most pleasant odor was. Some suspect that in these words Tertullian alludes to [Col. 1123B] the horn of Amalthea, from which nectar and ambrosia flowed. But this suspicion is undoubtedly groundless. For the subject is Jupiter’s nurse, whom some believed to be the she-goat Amalthea, others the Nymphs, or daughters of Melissus, king of Crete. Accordingly, if our Author followed the latter opinion, the odor of the Nymphs, who were said to have nourished Jupiter with honey and milk, could rightly be called exceedingly pleasant. But if, as is more probable, he had the former in view, he is ironically assailing the foolish divinity of Jupiter, who had sucked the milk of a foul-smelling goat and could not forget that most repulsive odor. Yet if he always remembered her and his native Crete, he ought to have preferred Crete to the Capitol, and to have given dominion over the whole world to the Cretans rather than to the Romans. We have discussed elsewhere his birth, nurses, upbringing, and tomb [Col. 1123C] (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 15, art. 2).

What, then, of Juno, this Jupiter’s sister and wife? Would she, Tertullian asks, have wished the Punic city of Carthage to be destroyed by the race of the sons of Aeneas (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 427; and bk. II *To the Nations*, ch. 17), that is, by the Romans? Did she not prefer that beloved city of hers to Samos, where she had been brought up or married to Jupiter, as is evident from these verses of Virgil, quoted by our Author only in part and without naming their author?

> One city more than all lands Juno is said  
> To have cherished, even setting Samos second. Here were her arms,  
> Here was her chariot; that this should be the ruling power  
> Over the nations, if somehow the Fates permit, the goddess even then both strove and cherished.

[Col. 1123D] (Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. I, v. 19 ff.)

Yet that wretched wife and sister of Jupiter, Tertullian continues, however greatly she loved Carthage, could not prevent its being overthrown by the Romans. For she had no power against that city’s fatal destiny. Nor is this at all surprising, because, our Author continues, “Jupiter himself plainly stands subject to Fate” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 428). Is not that declaration confirmed quite openly by the Delphic Apollo’s response to the messengers of Croesus, which Herodotus reported in these very words: τὴν πεπρωμένην ἀδύνατά ἐστιν ἀποφυγεῖν καὶ θεῷ “Even for a god it is impossible to escape the lot appointed by Fate” (Herodotus, bk. I, § 91).

Concerning Jupiter himself, Cicero (Cicero, bk. II *On Divination*, p. 281, line 44) and Homer, [Col. 1124A] whom he cites (Homer, *Iliad* 16, v. 434; and *Odyssey* 3, v. 137 ff.), pronounce as follows: “Nothing occurs outside Fate.... Homer holds this view when he represents Jupiter lamenting that he could not rescue his son Sarpedon from death.” The same is signified by that Greek verse expressing the thought:

> What has been prepared to happen surpasses even supreme Jupiter.

Seneca likewise says: “Whatever it is that has commanded us to live thus and die thus binds the gods by the same necessity. An irrevocable course carries human and divine things alike. That very founder and ruler of all things did indeed write the Fates, but follows them; he always obeys, though he commanded only once” (Seneca, *On Providence*, ch. 5, p. 501). Hence that Greek proverb, Ανάγχῃ οὐδὲ θεοὶ μαχόνται: “Not even the gods resist necessity.” See Erasmus’s observations on this proverb [Col. 1124B] and Lipsius’s on the words of Seneca just cited. See also Vossius (Vossius, bk. II *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, ch. 44, p. 276 ff.), and, if you wish, what we have remarked in our work on Minucius Felix concerning Fate, and in the following discussion concerning Jupiter and Juno (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 6, art. 3; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 31, art. 4).

If, therefore, Juno and Jupiter himself and all the gods are subject to Fate, as the pagans admit, they undoubtedly could never have granted any increase to the Roman Empire. For if Fate is more powerful than Jupiter and all the gods, the pagans, says Tertullian (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 428), ought surely to have bestowed greater honors upon the Fates, which surrendered Carthage to them, than upon Juno and that utterly prostituted she-wolf Larentina, [Col. 1124C] whom we discussed a little earlier. It is therefore false that by their great devotion toward Juno, Jupiter, and their other gods, whether native or imported, the Romans merited dominion over the whole world.

## ARTICLE II

### The Empire Was Not Given to the Romans Because of Their Devotion to the Gods, Since It Had Been Founded Before Numa Instituted the Various Rites—Here Explained—for Worshipping Those Gods, Before Images of the Gods Had Been Made, and Before There Was Any God at Rome

By still other weighty arguments Tertullian demonstrates (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 429) that the Romans could not have received their empire, or any extension of its boundaries, from any one of their gods or from all the [Col. 1124D] others together. For it is certain that several gods of the Romans—Saturn, Jupiter, and others—attained supreme royal power. Tell me, then, if you please, from what other gods whom they worshipped did they receive it? Was it given to Saturn by that thoroughly contemptible Sterculus whom we mentioned a little earlier? But he lived after Saturn. No pagan could ever say which other god gave Saturn his kingdom. Yet there is no better reason for saying that it was granted to the Romans by some god.

Moreover, their empire had been founded before the worship of the gods was established at Rome. For with Numa, says Tertullian (*ibid.*), began [Col. 1125A] “superstitious curiosity,” that is, religion and superstition—the depraved and perverse solicitude expended upon false gods. But for what reason do you suppose it was instituted by him? Consult Livy (Livy, bk. I, § 19 ff.), Eutropius (Eutropius, bk. I, *Breviary*, p. 64), and other writers of Roman history, and they will answer you: in order that he might restrain and refine the fierce and warlike dispositions of the Romans through fear of religion and the gods.

But what, pray, were those first rites of Roman religion? “At that time,” says our Author, “divine worship among the Romans did not yet consist either of images or of temples,” as we have explained elsewhere, where we investigated the origin of temples and images (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 430 ff.; and bk. I *To the Nations*, ch. 17, p. 583; vol. I of the *Apparatus*, bk. III, [Col. 1125B] dissertation 1, chs. 7 and 8, p. 717 ff.).

At that time, moreover, there were no “Capitols contending with heaven,” as he continues (*ibid.*, p. 28); that is, the Capitoline temple had not yet been constructed, so magnificent that it seemed to raise its summit as far as the heavens. Nor should you suppose this an exaggeration born of a mind excessively hostile to the pagans. For Martial, himself a pagan, sang the same thing in these verses:

> Nor the innermost shrine of the Capitoline Thunderer,  
> Nor the temples that gleam nearest to their own heaven.

(Martial, bk. II, Epigram 51.)

Silius Italicus likewise:

> She too, where the ancient royal house we faithfully revere now stands,  
> Will set golden Capitols upon the Tarpeian rock,  
> And the pinnacles of the temples will touch our heaven.

(Silius, bk. III, v. 620.) [Col. 1125C]

If you wish, you will readily find more material related in the books of Alexander Donatus and others on the city of Rome (Donatus, bk. II *On the City of Rome*, ch. 4 ff.).

Where, then, someone will ask, was divine worship conducted at that time? On “makeshift altars of turf,” says Tertullian (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 430; and bk. II *To the Nations*, ch. 17), that is, altars erected hastily, suddenly, and as occasion happened, as one may learn from Cicero (Cicero, bk. II *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 228, line 12) and Apuleius (Apuleius, *On the God of Socrates*, beginning). The vessels with which the service was performed, our Author says, were “squalid and still Samian”; that is, they were then made of clay and soft earth. We have discussed these elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 30, art. 2).

Tertullian added (Tertullian, passage cited): [Col. 1125D] “And the smell from them,” as we read in both of his works, the *Apology* and the second book *To the Nations*. In our manuscripts, however, one of these two words has, as frequently happens, been written as a single word: *exillis*. From this perhaps Rigaltius wished to emend it to *exilis* (“meager”), by a conjecture not altogether infelicitous, though he ought to have offered at least some reason for it. It can be supported by the fact that in the same place Tertullian asserts that the rites of Roman religion were then poor. Yet the two words *ex illis* (“from them”) can also refer to the Samian vessels to which they are appended. We therefore dare determine nothing concerning this variant reading.

Moreover, because in Numa’s time there were no images of the gods among the Romans—indeed, [Col. 1126A] because he had forbidden them to be made, as we have shown elsewhere (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, pp. 733 and 1227 ff.)—Tertullian inferred from this: “And God himself was nowhere.” For he seems to have been wholly persuaded, as we shall see in the following chapter, that the opinion had spread throughout the minds of the pagans that there were no gods other than their images, or that the divinity of the gods resided in them.

You therefore see how rightly he concludes from what has been said: “Thus the Romans were not religious before they were great, and consequently they were not great because they were religious” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 430). For their empire had been founded and established before either they had gods or, at least, worshipped and adored them with so many superstitious rites and such an empty display of ceremonies. Yet surely they needed an equal measure of the gods’ aid and support both for extending that empire and for laying [Col. 1126B] its foundations—aid which it is nevertheless manifest they never possessed. But this is demonstrated by Tertullian even more clearly through other arguments, which we must examine.

## ARTICLE III

### The Empire of the Romans Was No More Given or Extended by Any Gods of the Pagans Than Were the Empires of the Babylonians, Medes, Egyptians, Assyrians, Amazons, and Jews, Who Reigned Before Those Same Romans; That the Romans Extended the Boundaries of Their Empire by Impiety and Sacrilege; and That the Existence of Any of These or the Other Pagan Gods Cannot Be Proved by Any Miracles, Divinations, or Oracles Whatever

[Col. 1126C] It is indeed certain, and the Romans themselves openly admitted it, that earthly kingdoms are instituted, granted, and dispensed by someone. But that power can belong only to him alone who created all things. Accordingly, none of the Roman gods ever gave any kingdom to their worshippers or to others. And surely, before their Pontiffs, Quindecimvirs, Salii, Luperci, and Vestals—whom we have discussed elsewhere—and before all the ministers of the Roman gods, indeed before any gods were worshipped at Rome, the kingdoms of the Babylonians, Medes, Egyptians, Assyrians, and Amazons had been established and, as everyone knows, flourished with great power and glory (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 26, p. 432; and bk. II *To the Nations*, ch. 27, p. 583).

It is therefore evident that these nations received the power to reign neither from the Roman gods nor from those whom they themselves worshipped: [Col. 1126D] not from their own gods, who could not defend those by whom they were held in the highest honor; nor from the Roman gods, who had not yet been fashioned and by whom the Romans believed the empires of those nations to have been overthrown. We have already seen (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 18, arts. 4 and 5) how Minucius Felix employed this argument against the pagans.

But to make the point clearer by certain examples, Tertullian presses them: “Why did Minerva not defend Athens from Xerxes, or why did Apollo not rescue Delphi from the hand of Pyrrhus?” (Tertullian, bk. II *To the Nations*, passage cited). For no pagan god either at Rome or anywhere else was adorned with such great divine honor as Minerva [Col. 1127A] at Athens, where her renowned image, known to everyone, stood. Nor was any god honored with greater worship than Apollo at Delphi, where he was believed to issue oracles celebrated throughout the whole world. Nevertheless, neither Apollo rendered Delphi safe from the arms of its enemies, nor Minerva Athens. How, then, could gods, whether foreign or native, extend the boundaries of the Roman Empire? Were they more powerful than the other gods? But the true God must be omnipotent and therefore can only be one: he who grants and dispenses kingdoms.

Come now, and let us follow Tertullian as he presses his case (same author, *Apology*, ch. 26, p. 432). The Jews, he says, had their own kings, whom they would possess even now, had they not bloodied their hands with the dreadful parricide against Christ. [Col. 1127B] Yet these same Jews utterly despised the gods of the Romans. They did not, therefore, merit their kingdom through devotion to those gods. The Romans, moreover, having made a treaty with these enemies of their gods, honored their God with slaughtered victims and endowed his temple with gifts and offerings. With what eyes, I ask you, could those gods look upon the treaties, sacrifices, and gifts made by the Romans to the true God and to their own adversary? Ought these things not to have inflamed their wrath rather than their inclination to enlarge the boundaries of the Roman Empire?

Furthermore, it is absolutely certain that kingdoms are seized and invaded by men through arms and victories (*ibid.*, ch. 25, p. 431). Yet these things cannot generally occur without cities being overthrown and, consequently, temples and walls alike being destroyed, priests and citizens alike being slain, [Col. 1127C] and sacred and profane property alike being carried off and plundered. Thus, says Tertullian (*ibid.*), the Romans committed as many sacrileges as they erected trophies carried to Rome from the sacred and profane spoils of captured cities; they celebrated as many triumphs over gods who could not protect their own countries and the cities in which they were worshipped as over nations. They had as much booty as there still remain at Rome images of captive gods.

The term *manubiae* (“spoils”), which we have discussed elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 16, art. 2; and *On Lactantius*, ch. 31, art. 4), appears there to be used by Tertullian for spoils which the Romans carried back with them to Rome from captured cities. Chief among them were images of the gods, which they customarily installed in temples and thereafter adored. The Roman Empire therefore increased not through any devotion or religious observance toward the gods, [Col. 1127D] but through impiety, crimes, and sacrileges.

By what reasoning, then, our Author continues, do those gods permit themselves to be worshipped by their enemies, the Romans, and give an empire without end to men whose injuries, rather than their acts of worship, they ought to have requited? In our manuscripts and various editions, especially the older ones, the reading is *adolationes*, or *adulationes* (“flatteries”), as it occurs differently written by others. Some, however, have preferred *adorationes* (“acts of worship”), so that this noun may correspond to the preceding verb *adorari* (“to be worshipped”). But what if Tertullian meant that the gods could not patiently endure flatteries—that is, the empty blandishments of their worship—offered by those who flattered them at the very time they were devastating everything through impiety and sacrilege? Whichever reading you decide upon, [Col. 1128A] it must be admitted that nothing more absurd can be imagined than that the Romans enlarged the boundaries of their empire through devotion to the gods, although they had extended those boundaries through wars and victories, and consequently not without injury and insult to those very gods.

By most of the same arguments of Tertullian against the Roman gods that we have expounded thus far, Minucius Felix utterly demolished the fictitious divinity of those same powers, as we showed in our first dissertation (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 16, arts. 2 and 3).

Yet the Romans and all other pagans insisted that their gods were true gods, since by astonishing signs, miracles, predictions, oracles, divinations, and certain deeds which seemed to surpass all the powers of created things, [Col. 1128B] they had time and again proved their divinity and omnipotence. And certainly these things, which they believed to have been performed outside the customary order of nature, had so bewitched their eyes and minds that they were altogether persuaded they must be attributed to the power of the gods alone. But Tertullian shows that this argument is advanced and pressed only by those who were ignorant of the true cause of those effects.

Once empty prejudices had been set aside, they could have recognized that all these things had been done through the extraordinary agility of demons, their evil arts, deceits, frauds, and impostures, by which these lying and impure spirits wholly deluded unwary men. To this is added the fact that the pagans could not deny that many deeds resembling the extraordinary acts of their gods, as they supposed them to be, had been performed by men devoted to magic, [Col. 1128C] through the deceits and frauds of the same demons. Who, then, unless he were mad, would say that these men were therefore true gods? We have attempted above to elucidate and disclose the nature and weight of the arguments by which Tertullian proved this point (above, ch. 8, art. 3 ff.). In earlier dissertations we also explained these and many others besides (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 16, art. 1 ff.; *Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 18, art. 1 ff.; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 32, art. 4), by which other defenders and champions of our religion uprooted this pagan opinion and argument.

# CHAPTER XXIX

## On the Images, Statues, and Representations of the Gods

## ARTICLE I

### By What Arguments Tertullian Demonstrates That Images of the Gods Should Not Be Worshipped: There Were None at Rome Before Numa Pompilius, Nor for a Long Time Thereafter; They Were Made from the Same Material as Cheap Vessels; Through a Ridiculous Consecration They Changed Their Destiny; Fashioned by Artisans, They Were Subjected to Various Insults by Beasts and Men; and Beasts Were Most Absurdly Assigned to Liber, Cybele, and Caelestis

[Col. 1128D]

Since it has been demonstrated more than sufficiently that the gods of the pagans are false and nonexistent, [Col. 1129A] it necessarily follows that their images, statues, and representations are empty things, deserving no honor whatever. For what can the image of a false god be except false? It is therefore never worshipped and adored without great impiety. Tertullian, however, refutes and assails this error of the pagans with certain particular arguments, which must now be explained by us.

We have already heard him say that no images or representations of the gods were made at Rome during the reign of Numa Pompilius or before his age (preceding chapter, art. 1). For “at that time,” he says, “the talents of the Greeks and Tuscans had not yet flooded the City with the fashioning of images” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 430). Here he seems to allude to the opinion of Tatian, as we observed elsewhere (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 543), and of others who believed that the Tuscans were the inventors of pottery and [Col. 1129B] of fashioning images from clay and earthenware. Yet these images of the gods were not brought to Rome immediately after Numa Pompilius died. For, as Varro—cited by Augustine—and Clement of Alexandria testify, the Romans worshipped their gods without images for one hundred and seventy years or more. But we have discussed these matters in the already cited volume of our *Apparatus* (*ibid.*), and have shown there in addition that Numa forbade any image or representation to be made.

Throughout that entire period, therefore, in which the Romans lacked them, “God himself,” says Tertullian, “was nowhere” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 430). Why, except because the pagans had persuaded themselves that the images of the gods were in actual fact their gods? Indeed, Clement of Alexandria, whom we have also cited elsewhere (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, [Col. 1129C] p. 734), confidently asserts that in those later times the pagans fashioned the gods whom they worshipped. Tertullian had therefore said elsewhere that the gods were valued by those same pagans according to the temples in which their images had been placed (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 10, p. 330; and ch. 25, p. 427). Elsewhere he calls those same images of the gods, which the Romans, as we said before, carried to Rome in triumphal procession, “captive gods.” But it is superfluous to prove at greater length what he everywhere assumes as certain and what will appear more clearly from what is about to be said.

With what great blindness, however, the true God, he says, is despised by the pagans, while the statues of those same gods are worshipped (same author, *Apology*, ch. 41, p. 488)! [Col. 1129D] For what was there to be discerned in them except that their materials were “sisters of common vessels and utensils,” or that they were made from those same vessels and utensils, “as though changing their destiny by consecration, while the license of art transformed them” (*ibid.*, ch. 12, p. 339)? He thus openly declares that the materials of the images—that is, the woods and metals of which they were made—were the same as those of the vessels and utensils destined for common, and sometimes exceedingly base, human use. Thus they had changed their destiny and lot, first at the will and whim of the craftsmen by whom they were fashioned, and then by the ridiculous consecration upon receiving which at last they were worshipped by the pagans as true gods. But we have pursued these matters at greater length elsewhere (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 19, art. 4), [Col. 1130A] and made plain how insane and absurd this pagan opinion is.

For what greater disgrace can there be than to pursue with divine honor gods who, Tertullian says, “trace their rank from metals” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 12, p. 339)—where we must undoubtedly understand “originated and were made”? For Juvenal used the verb *censeri* in this same sense when he sang:

> What do pedigrees accomplish? What profit is there, Ponticus,  
> In tracing one’s rank through a long lineage? . . .

(Juvenal, *Satire* 8, v. 1.)

Moreover, whether these images of the gods were to be made from metals depended upon the decision of the emperor, who owned those metals (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 29, p. 439). According to his nod and will, therefore, those gods were made [Col. 1130B] or rejected. How many insults, besides, do they undergo in being fashioned and manufactured by artisans? First, says Tertullian (*ibid.*, ch. 12, p. 339), “they are built upon a cross and a stake”; next they are shaped with axes and cut with large saws, while with rasps—that is, files—they are polished smooth. What of the fact that before the lead, he adds, the glue, and the *gomphoi*, in Greek γόμφους, or nails by which they are joined together, the gods are headless? (Varro, bk. VI *On the Latin Language*, p. 54.) Does he not signify by these words that the head of the image was made separately, afterward set upon its body, and fastened to it with nails?

But these “distinguished gods,” as he calls them (Tertullian, passage cited, p. 339), or images animated by no breath of life, do not feel these indignities. For statues and cold images, most like dead men, [Col. 1130C] are no more affected by the dishonor of their manufacture than by the worship and homage of those who adore them. To this is added the fact that “kites, mice, and spiders,” he says, “understand this insensibility of the images” (*ibid.*), since they nest in those statues with impunity and defile them with their droppings. Thieves and other impious men understood it even more readily, for these images could never be safe from their thefts and insults without guards of imperial soldiers. But we have elucidated these matters in our earlier dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 21, art. 1; *On Arnobius*, ch. 22, art. 3; and *On Lactantius*, ch. 33, art. 5).

Against this the pagans clamored that these were impious expressions and sacrilegious insults. “But gnash your teeth,” our Author says to them, “and foam as much as you wish; assuredly these outcries will do you no good. For you have at some time heard [Col. 1130D] a certain Seneca, whom you cannot contradict, arguing against your superstition in more numerous and more bitter words” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 12, p. 339). We have indicated what these words of Seneca were in another of our dissertations, where we also cited this passage of Tertullian (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 33, art. 3). Yet even if Seneca had remained silent, our Author’s argument is no less valid: the pagans could indeed foam against it in rage, but could not break its force.

Who, then, will not deplore the blind impiety of those who, so that greater veneration might be shown to their gods, or images, “assigned beasts,” says Tertullian, “to Liber, Cybele, and Caelestis” (Tertullian, *Apology*, [Col. 1131A] ch. 12, p. 339), that is, to the chariots in which they were represented as riding? Liber’s chariot was drawn by tigers, as may be learned from Ovid (Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, final fable; and *The Art of Love*, v. 560) and many others. It will therefore suffice to cite these verses of Virgil:

> And Liber, who, victorious, turns the yokes with vine-leaf reins,  
> Driving tigers down from Nysa’s lofty summit.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VI, v. 779.)

Cybele’s chariot, however, was carried by lions, as Lucretius sings:

> Her the learned poets of the Greeks in ancient times sang  
> As driving paired lions from her seat upon a chariot,  
> Teaching that the great earth hangs within the expanse of air  
> And that earth cannot rest upon the earth.

(Lucretius, bk. II, v. 600 ff.)

Diodorus Siculus writes that lions and leopards [Col. 1131B] were placed beside this goddess’s image because she was said to have been nourished by them (Diodorus, bk. III of the *Library*, p. 135).

The third goddess to whose chariot beasts were yoked is called *Caelestis* by Tertullian, as it is written in our manuscripts, though some others read *Cereri* (“to Ceres”). Now Ceres was indeed called Caelestis, and her chariot was carried by serpents, as Ovid confirms in these verses:

> . . . The fertile goddess yoked twin serpents  
> To her chariot and curbed their mouths with bridles,  
> And was carried through the air midway between heaven and earth.

(Ovid, bk. V of the *Metamorphoses*, final fable.)

Yet not Ceres alone, but Venus also was surnamed Caelestis, and they babbled that her chariot was drawn by swans and doves. If you ask for a witness, Ovid is again at hand:

[Col. 1131C]

> Carried in her light chariot through the midst of the air,  
> Cytherea had not yet reached Cyprus on swans’ wings.

(*Ibid.*, bk. X, final section.)

And later:

> And through the light air she rode, borne by yoked doves.

Finally, the chariot of Juno, who was also called Caelestis, was drawn by horses, as Homer and Virgil attest, and by peacocks, as others testify. For Ovid again sang:

> . . . Saturn’s daughter, in her well-fitted chariot,  
> Enters the clear upper air with painted peacocks.

(Ovid, bk. II of the *Metamorphoses*, shortly before the end.)

It is therefore as difficult to say what Tertullian signifies by the name *Caelestis* as it is easy to prove that beasts were, as Tertullian says, assigned by the pagans to their gods or their images. Yet no person of sound mind [Col. 1131D] will ever deny that nothing is more absurd than seriously venerating and worshipping such images as true gods or as endowed with divine power.

## ARTICLE II

### How Absurdly the Pagans Said That Images Were Their Gods, Although Very Many Worshipped Certain Gods Unknown to Others and Adopted by Themselves, or Pawned and Sold Their Household Gods and Exchanged Them for the Cheapest Vessels, or Offered Public Gods for Sale at Auction, and Considered Those Gods Holier Who Were More Heavily Taxed and Sold at a Higher Price

Tertullian could not patiently endure the pagans [Col. 1132A] who, willfully closing their eyes to the light of right reason, which shone more brightly than the noonday sun, contended with extraordinary obstinacy that the images, statues, and representations of false gods must be retained and worshipped. For in place of a reason, he says, there was only that foolish outcry: “These are gods to us” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 13, p. 344). But these were mere words by which they were wholly condemned on their own judgment.

For they could not deny that no one could be named and honored as a god without the Senate’s approval, as we saw above (ch. 18, art. 9). Nevertheless, it lay within the power of any pagan to choose or reject whatever god he wished, or whatever image of a god pleased him. Thus, a god accepted by one person was rejected by another at his pleasure. Yet this could not be done without injury to the rejected god, [Col. 1132B] or, as Tertullian himself says in addressing them: “Since some of you worship some gods and others worship others, you certainly offend those whom you do not worship. Preference for one cannot proceed without insult to another” (Tertullian, passage cited).

Some think that Augustine had these words in view where he censures and refutes this entire argument as follows: “I do not say what Tertullian says, perhaps more wittily than truly: ‘If gods are selected like onions, then surely the rest are judged rejected.’ I do not say this. For I see that even from among selected persons some are selected for some greater and more distinguished purpose; as in the army, when recruits have been chosen, from among these too some are chosen for some greater military task.... There is no need to go through many examples when the matter is obvious. Therefore, it is not because certain gods have been selected from among many that either the writer who recorded this, their worshippers, [Col. 1132C] or the gods themselves ought to be censured; rather, one must consider who these are and for what purpose they appear to have been selected” (Augustine, bk. VII *City of God*, ch. 1, p. 162).

It may perhaps be answered, however, that Augustine is there speaking of the pagans’ select gods, while Tertullian, in the cited passage, is discussing images of gods of every kind. But others will object that the pagans, as we have said, believed those very images to be their gods. Moreover, although they did not always speak of both in the same way, Augustine’s argument against Tertullian nevertheless seems no less forceful. Yet it cannot be denied that its weight is far greater if Augustine had in view what our Author argued at greater length in the books *To the Nations* against Varro’s opinion concerning the select gods themselves (Tertullian, bk. II *To the Nations*, ch. 9), and especially these words of his: “If the pagans had certain gods, [Col. 1132D] they ought to have been content with them and not to desire select gods. In this too they are detected as irreligious. For if gods, as you wish, are selected, those who are not selected are declared rejected” (*ibid.*). Some were so persuaded that Augustine was referring to this passage that, following him, they boldly restored the noun *bulbi* (“onions”) in this text in place of the verb *vultis* (“you wish”).

Someone will nevertheless insist in reply that Tertullian, even though he discusses images of the gods in the *Apology*, drew from those things which the pagans believed to be gods the same conclusion as he drew in the book *To the Nations* concerning the select gods themselves: “The status of each god depended upon the Senate’s estimation. No one was a god unless a man, [Col. 1133A] when consulted, had wished him to be one” (same author, *Apology*, ch. 13, p. 344). But he must observe that these words do not denote select gods, but the images of gods of every kind, which the pagans, as Tertullian added in that same place, could pawn, sell, exchange, and break at pleasure. Yet this could not occur without their disgrace and dishonor, especially when one was rejected and another chosen. Can it not also be said that the force of the argument Tertullian drew from this rests in the fact that whatever gods are imagined must either be omnipotent or be no gods at all? But from among several omnipotent beings one cannot be chosen as stronger than another, although this may certainly be done among recruits and soldiers.
