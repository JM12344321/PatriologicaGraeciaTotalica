When in a certain place (Luke 11) he had prayed to that higher Father—lifting sufficiently shameless and reckless eyes toward the Creator’s heaven, by whom, harsh and cruel as he was, he might have been struck down by hail and lightning, just as he too might have been crucified by him at Jerusalem [Note: [Col. 0425D] “Crucified” (*suffigi*), Paris.]—one of his disciples approached him and said: “Lord, teach us to pray, just as John also taught his disciples”; [Note: [Col. 0425D] “Also” is absent from Paris.] evidently because he supposed that a different God must be prayed to in a different way. Whoever has presumed [Note: [Col. 0425D] *Praesumpserit* in Wouwer’s codex.] this must first prove that another God had been revealed by Christ. For no one would have wished to learn how to pray before he had learned whom he was to pray to. Therefore, if [Col. 0425B] he had learned this, prove it. If thus far you prove it nowhere, know that he requested a form of prayer addressed to the Creator, to whom John’s disciples also prayed.

But because John had introduced a certain new order of prayer, Christ’s disciple quite reasonably presumed that he should request the same [Note: [Col. 0426D] *Expostulandum*, Semler.] from Christ, so that they too, according to the particular institution of their own master, might pray not to another God but to God in another way. Likewise, Christ would not have imparted knowledge of prayer to the disciple before imparting knowledge of God himself. Thus he too taught prayer directed to the one whom the disciple had known up to that point. Finally, examine which God the meanings of the prayer indicate.

[Note: [Col. 0425D] “Whom shall I call Father?” The Author rightly gives this alone, because although the Greek is now read, πατὴρ ἡμῶν ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς, “Our Father who is in the heavens,” and similarly in Syriac, nevertheless in the Latin codices at Luke 11 nothing more than “Father” is read. I suspect that the Greek phrase was added from the Gospel of Matthew 6, according to which it is read in *On Prayer*, chapter 2: “Our Father who art in heaven.” PAM.]

Whom shall I call Father? Him who did not make me at all, from whom I derive no origin? Or him who begot me by making and forming me? From whom shall I ask for the Holy Spirit? [Note: [Col. 0425D] “The Holy Spirit.” Gregory of Nyssa has this in place of “Hallowed be thy name.” SEMLER.] From him by whom not even the spirit of the world [Col. 0425C] is supplied? Or from him by whom even the angels are made spirits, and whose Spirit at the beginning was borne over the waters? Shall I desire the kingdom of one to come whom I have never heard called the King of Glory? Or the kingdom of him in whose hand are even the hearts of kings?

[Note: [Col. 0426D] “Who will give me my daily bread?” Christ clearly forbids us to be anxious about tomorrow, for our life is unstable. Thus the Fourth Council of Toledo, chapter 9, explains it. That bread, moreover, is Christ, as he himself says. But when he said “daily bread,” he signified the need human beings have of his aid, and also warned that life is lived one day at a time, ζωὴν εἶναι ἐφήμερον, “that life is for a day.” LE PRIEUR.]

Who will give me my daily bread: one who does not create even millet for me, or one who also furnished his people daily with the bread of angels from heaven? Who will forgive my sins: one who, by not judging them, does not retain them, or one who, if he has not forgiven them, will retain them for judgment?

[Note: [Col. 0426D] “Who will not allow us to be led into temptation?” The sense [Col. 0427D] is that we pray God not to permit us to succumb to temptation. LE PRIEUR.]

Who will not allow us to be led into temptation: one whom the tempter need not fear, or one who from the beginning condemned the angelic tempter beforehand? According to this sequence, anyone who supplicates another God rather than the Creator does not pray to that god but defames him.

Likewise, from whom shall I ask, that I may receive? With whom shall I seek, that I may find? At whose door shall I knock, that it may [Col. 0425D] be opened to me? Who has anything to give the petitioner except the one to whom all things belong and to whom I myself, the petitioner, also belong? But what have I [Col. 0426A] lost with that god, that I should seek it with him and find it? If wisdom and prudence, the Creator concealed them; therefore I shall seek them with him. If salvation and life, these too are with the Creator. Nothing will be sought elsewhere in order to be found except where it lay hidden in order to appear. Thus I shall knock nowhere else than at the place from which I was dismissed [Note: [Col. 0426D] “Driven away,” Paris.].

Finally, if receiving, finding, and being admitted are the fruits of labor and persistence for the one who asked, sought, and knocked, understand that these things are commanded and promised by the Creator. For that supremely good god, coming of his own accord to bestow benefits upon a human being who was not his own, would have imposed neither labor nor persistence upon him. Indeed, he would no longer be supremely good if he did not give spontaneously to one who did not ask, grant finding to one who did not seek, and open to one who did not knock.

The Creator, however, could impose these things through Christ, so that, because by sinning [Col. 0426B] the human being had offended his God, he should labor, and through persistence in asking receive, through seeking find, and through knocking enter. So too the preceding parable makes the man who asks for bread at night a friend, not a stranger; and one who knocks at a friend’s door, not at that of someone unknown. Moreover, even if he has caused offense, a human being belongs more properly to the Creator than to Marcion’s god. Therefore he knocks at the door of one upon whom he had a claim, whose door he knew, whom he knew to have bread, and who was already lying down with the little children whose birth he had willed.

Even the fact that the knocking occurs late belongs to the Creator’s time. The late hour belongs to him to whom the age and the age’s setting also belong. No one, however, would have knocked late upon a new god who had only just begun to dawn. It is the Creator who long ago had closed the door to the nations, while it had long been knocked upon by the Jews; [Note: [Col. 0426D] *Cluserit*, Venice.] it is he too who rises and gives—even if now not because the man is his friend, yet [Col. 0426C] not as though he were a stranger, but, as he says, because he is troublesome. Yet a recently arrived god could not so quickly have found anyone troublesome.

Acknowledge, therefore, the Father whom you also call the Creator. It is he who knows what his children ask for. For he both gave manna from heaven to those asking for bread and sent quails to those desiring flesh—not a serpent for a fish, nor a scorpion for an egg. Yet it will belong to the one to refrain from giving evil in place of good to whom both belong. Marcion’s god, by contrast, possessing no scorpion, could not have said that he would not give something he did not possess; but the Creator, though possessing a scorpion too, does not give one. Accordingly, he will also give the Holy Spirit with whom there is also an unholy spirit.

When he had cast out a deaf demon—so that in this kind of healing too he might correspond to Isaiah (Isa. 29:3)—he was said to cast out demons by Beelzebul. “If I,” he said, [Col. 0426D] “cast out demons by Beelzebul, by whom do your sons cast them out?” What did he indicate by this more than that he cast them out by the same one [Col. 0427A] by whom their sons also did—that is, by the power of the Creator?

For if you think it must be understood thus: “If I cast out demons by Beelzebul, [Note: [Col. 0427D] “If I cast out demons by Beelzebul.” Although *Beelbebul* is read in Saints Mark and Luke, the etymology of the word nevertheless requires *Beelzebub*, that is, “prince of flies.” For if you read *Beelzebul*, it will either be a name without any meaning, or one with a better meaning than would be appropriate. For *zebul* means “dwelling,” and sometimes “heaven.” LE PRIEUR.] by whom do your sons cast them out?” [Note: [Col. 0427D] “I cast out demons” is omitted by Oberthür.]—as though he were reproaching them for casting demons out by Beelzebul—the preceding thought will oppose you: that Satan cannot be divided against himself. Therefore, they too did not cast them out by Beelzebul, but, as we have said, by the power of the Creator. To make this understood, he adds: “But if I cast out demons by the finger of God, has the kingdom of God therefore drawn near upon you?”

For when Pharaoh employed those magicians against Moses, with whom he was entering into conflict, they called the Creator’s power “the finger of God” (Exod. 8): “This is the finger of God”—a phrase that [Note: [Col. 0427D] *Quo*, Latinus.] also signified something small, yet exceedingly powerful. [Note: [Col. 0427D] *Significarent*, Paris and Franeker.] Showing this same thing, Christ—the commemorator, not the obliterator, of ancient things, evidently his own—called God’s power “the finger of God,” meaning the power of no other god [Col. 0427B] than the one in whose dispensation it had been so called. Therefore, the kingdom of that same God had drawn near whose power [Note: [Col. 0427D] “Whose power was also called the finger.” That elegant interpretation of “the finger of God” as God’s strength and power is employed by everyone. When Pharaoh’s magicians were unable to produce gnats, they attributed this to God’s finger or power, which was hindering them. LE PRIEUR.] was called his finger.

He therefore rightly applied to the parable of that armed strong man, whom another stronger than he overpowered, the prince of the demons, whom above he had called Beelzebul and Satan, indicating that he was overpowered by the finger of God—not that the Creator was subdued by another god. Otherwise, how would the Creator’s kingdom still stand within its own boundaries, laws, and functions, when Marcion’s stronger god might have been thought to have conquered him, even while the world remained intact, unless even Marcionites still died according to the Creator’s law, sinking back into the earth and often being taught even by a scorpion that the Creator had not been conquered?

A woman from the crowd cried out, [Note: [Col. 0427D] “Cries out,” Pamelius.] “Blessed is the womb that bore him, and the breasts that nursed him.” And [Col. 0427C] the Lord replied: “Rather, blessed are those who hear the word of God and do it.” For previously too he had similarly set aside his mother or brothers when he preferred hearers and obeyers of God. Indeed, neither was his mother standing near him here. Thus he had not denied her previously either, since [Note: [Col. 0428D] “Neither,” Junius.] even when he hears this again, he again transfers the blessedness from his mother’s womb and breasts to his disciples—a blessedness he would not have transferred from her if he had no mother.

## CHAPTER XXVII

Elsewhere I prefer to clear the Creator of the charges for which the Marcionites criticize him. Here it is enough if the same things are found in Christ. [Col. 0428A] See, he too is inconsistent and inconstant, capricious, teaching one thing and doing another: he commands that something be given to everyone who asks, yet he himself does not give a sign to those asking for one. For so long an age he hid his light from humanity, yet he denies that a lamp ought to be concealed; rather, he declares that it must be placed upon a lampstand so that it may shine for everyone. He forbids returning a curse—much more, surely, uttering a curse—yet he heaps woes upon the Pharisees and the teachers of the law. Whose Christ is so like my God, unless he is his own Christ?

We have often already established [Note: [Col. 0428D] *Finximus*, Pamelius.] that he could in no way have been characterized as a destroyer of the law if he had proclaimed another God. Therefore, when the Pharisee who had invited [Note: [Col. 0428D] *Vocaret*, Paris, Rigault, Venice.] him to dine was then considering within himself why Jesus had not first washed before reclining at table according to the law, was it because Jesus represented the God of the law? Jesus, moreover, interpreted the law for him, saying that they cleansed the outsides of the cup and dish, [Col. 0428B] but that their own insides were full of plunder and iniquity. He thereby indicated that the cleanliness of vessels was to be understood before God as the cleanliness of human beings, because the Pharisee too had been reflecting within himself about the man, not about an unwashed cup.

“You wash,” he therefore said, “the outsides of the cup,” that is, the flesh, “but you do not cleanse your insides,” that is, the soul, adding: “Did not he who made the outsides,” that is, the flesh, “also make the insides,” that is, the soul? By this statement he plainly demonstrated that the cleanliness of both the outward and inward human being belongs to the same God, because both belong to him—the God who prefers mercy not merely to a human being’s washing, but even to sacrifice (Hos. 6:6). For he adds:

[Note: [Col. 0427D] “Give what you have as alms,” etc. The Author rightly translates “what you have,” as does the Syriac interpreter: this is the meaning of the Greek τὰ ἐνόντα, “the things within,” as though he were saying, in Theophylact’s words, “not what you have seized, but what is your own.” The Vulgate translator had precisely this in view when rendering it “what remains,” namely, what remains beyond plunder and iniquity. Once this sense has been explained, there is no need to read *datis*, “when these things have been given,” instead of *date*, “give,” with Erasmus and Cajetan. Meanwhile, Blessed Cyprian, book 3 *Against Quirinus*, chapter 1, reads neither form; nor does Blessed Ambrose in his *Commentary*, or Blessed Augustine in the *Enchiridion*, chapter 75. Since, however, the Vulgate version also admits the same sense, as we have said, one must repeat here what we have said more than once: Isidore Clarius’s correction, “from the things available to you,” is too free, as though the alternative reading could not possibly be tolerated. PAM.]

“Give what you have as alms, and all things will be clean for you.” Even if another god could also have commanded mercy, nevertheless he could not have done so before he was known. Furthermore, it is apparent here too that they [Col. 0428C] were rebuked not concerning their God, but concerning the discipline of the one by whom both the cleanliness of vessels had been figuratively commanded and works of mercy explicitly enjoined.

So too he reproaches them for tithing garden herbs while passing over the calling [Note: [Col. 0428D] “Calling.” He appears to have read κλῆσιν, “calling,” in place of κρίσιν, “judgment”; or rather to have taken it from Marcion’s Gospel—which denied that judgment belonged to the good God—as an acknowledged point. JUNIUS.] and love of God. The calling and love of which God, except the one to whom they offered rue and mint also, in accordance with the Law’s rule concerning tithes? For the whole point of the reproach was that they attended to small matters for the very one to whom they did not render the greater obligations, though he said (Deut. 6:5): “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart [Note: [Col. 0428D] “Your” is omitted by Semler.] and with [Col. 0429A] all your soul and with all your strength, who called you out of Egypt.”

Otherwise, the time itself would not have permitted Christ to demand so premature—indeed, so immature—a love for a new and recently arrived god, not to say one who had not yet been openly revealed. When he also censures those who seek the foremost seats and the honor of greetings, he administers the Creator’s teaching, who calls such rulers of Sodom *archons* (Isa. 1:23), who also forbids trust in rulers, and indeed pronounces utterly wretched the human being who places his hope in a human being (Ps. 117:8–9).

But if someone seeks preeminence in order to glory in the services of others, then because the Creator forbade such services of hope and trust in a human being (Jer. 17:5), that same Creator also [Note: [Col. 0429D] “Also” is absent from Paris.] rebuked those who seek positions of preeminence.

He inveighs also against the teachers of the law themselves because they burdened others with unbearable burdens [Col. 0429B] which they themselves did not dare to touch even with a finger. He was not disparaging the burdens of the Law as one who detested it; for how could he detest it when he was at that very moment accusing them of neglecting the weightier matters of the Law—almsgiving, the calling and love of God—things that were not burdensome at all, much less the tithing of rue [Note: [Col. 0429D] “And” is absent from Paris.] and the cleansing of dishes? Otherwise, he should rather have judged them excusable if they could not bear unbearable things.

But what burdens does he censure? Those which they themselves heaped up on their own authority, teaching commandments and doctrines of human beings for the sake of their own advantage (Isa. 5:23), joining house to house so that they might seize what belonged to their neighbors, oppressing the people, loving gifts, pursuing recompense, plundering the judgments due to the poor, so that the widow became their spoil and the orphan their prey. Concerning these [Col. 0429C] the same Isaiah says (Isa. 28:14): “Woe to those who are powerful in Jerusalem,” [Note: [Col. 0429D] “Israel,” Oberthür.] and again (Isa. 3:3): “Those who make demands upon you are your masters.” Who more than the teachers of the law? If they displeased Christ also, they displeased him as his own servants.

[Note: [Col. 0429D] “For they were teachers of another’s law.” Here and frequently elsewhere, the teachers of the Law, those learned in the Law, and the Pharisees are called οἱ νομικοί, “the lawyers,” in Greek. LE PRIEUR.]

For he would not have attacked teachers of another’s law at all. Why, moreover, do they also hear “woe” because they built monuments to the prophets whom their fathers had killed? They would instead have deserved praise, because by this work of piety they testified that they did not consent to their fathers’ deeds—unless he was a jealous God of the kind the Marcionites charge him with being, exacting the sins of the fathers from their children down to the fourth generation.

What key, moreover, did the teachers of the law possess except the interpretation of the law? They themselves did not approach its understanding, because they did not believe: “For unless you believe, you will not understand.” Nor did they admit others, [Col. 0429D] since, of course, they taught [Note: [Col. 0429D] “Were teaching,” Semler.] instead the commandments and doctrines of human beings. Therefore, should the one who rebuked them because they neither entered themselves nor granted entrance to others be regarded as a detractor from the law, or as its supporter? If a detractor, then those who barred access to the law ought also to have pleased him; if a supporter, then he is no longer an opponent of the law.

But he heaped up all these things to cast a shadow upon the Creator as cruel, since those who sinned against him would incur woe. Yet who would not fear all the more to provoke a cruel God by defecting [Note: [Col. 0429D] “Despising,” in some copies.] from him? Therefore, he taught all the more that favor must be won from the one whom he represented as an object of fear. Such was necessarily the Christ of the Creator.

## CHAPTER XXVIII

The hypocrisy of the Pharisees therefore rightly displeased him: [Note: [Col. 0430D] That is, loving God with their lips but not with their heart. They followed the letter that kills, not the life-giving spirit of the Law; for they did not possess the key—that is, the interpretation of the Law, as he has just said. While they superstitiously observed certain outward matters, they neglected what was foremost. LE PRIEUR.] they loved God with their lips, not with their heart. “Beware,” he said to his disciples (Luke 12), “of the leaven of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy”—not the proclamation of the Creator. The Son hates those who are rebellious against the Father; he does not wish his own followers to behave like that toward him—not toward some other god, against whom hypocrisy would have been committed, with the Pharisees’ example serving as something the disciples must avoid. [Col. 0430B] Thus he forbids [Note: [Col. 0429D] “Presents,” in other copies.] the Pharisees’ example: he was forbidding such conduct toward the one against whom the Pharisees committed it.

Accordingly, since he had censured their hypocrisy, which concealed the hidden things of the heart and covered over the secrets of unbelief with superficial observances—hypocrisy which, though possessing the key of knowledge, [Note: [Col. 0430D] Pamelius inserts “in.”] neither entered itself nor allowed others to enter—he therefore adds: “But there is nothing covered that will not be revealed, and nothing hidden that will not become known.” No one, therefore, should suppose that he intended the revelation and recognition of a God previously unknown and hidden, since he also includes the things they whispered or discussed among themselves—namely, what they said about him: “This man does not cast out demons except by Beelzebul”—which would be brought into the open and come to be on human lips through the proclamation of the Gospel.

Then, [Note: [Col. 0430D] “Thereafter,” Pamelius.] turning to the disciples, he said: [Col. 0430C] “But I say to you, my friends, [Note: [Col. 0430D] “My” is absent from other copies.] do not be terrified by those who can only kill you and after this have no further power over you.” Isaiah had prophesied of them (Isa. 57:1): “See how the righteous man is taken away, and no one takes notice.” “But I shall show you whom you should fear: fear him who, after he has killed, has power to cast into Gehenna”—plainly signifying [Note: [Col. 0430D] “Signifying” is omitted by Semler.] the Creator. “Accordingly, I say to you, fear him.”

At this point it would be enough for me that, when he commands someone to be feared, he forbids that person to be offended; when he forbids him to be offended, he commands that his favor be won; and the one who commands these things belongs to the very God whose interests he serves by seeing that he is feared, not offended, and won over. But I can also draw evidence from what follows: “For I say to you: Everyone who confesses me before human beings, I shall confess in him before God.”

Those who make their confession in Christ will have to be killed before [Col. 0430D] human beings, and after being killed they will of course suffer nothing further from them. These, [Note: [Col. 0430D] “This one,” Rigault and Venice.] therefore, will be the persons whom he warned above not to fear being merely killed. Thus he first declares that death is not to be feared so that he may add that confession must be maintained [Col. 0431A] [Note: [Col. 0431D] “Endured,” Oberthür.]: “And everyone who denies me before human beings will be denied before God”—plainly by the one who would have confessed the person confessing him. For if he confesses the confessor, he himself is also the one who will deny the denier.

Furthermore, if the confessor is the one who has nothing to fear after being killed, the denier will be the one who has something to fear even after death. Accordingly, since what must be feared after death—namely, the punishment of Gehenna—belongs to the Creator, the denier too belongs to the Creator. But if both the denier and the confessor—the one who after being killed will suffer nothing from a human being but will certainly suffer from God if he denies—belong to the Creator, then Christ too belongs to the Creator, because he shows that those who deny him ought to fear the Creator’s Gehenna.

After discouraging denial, therefore, there follows a warning that blasphemy too must be feared:

[Note: [Col. 0431D] “Whoever speaks against the Son of Man,” etc. Although the Author does not express it, he nevertheless understands “whoever speaks blasphemy,” in place of the wording others have, “whoever blasphemes,” as is clear both from the Greek and Syriac and from the following words: “His Holy Spirit must not be blasphemed.” PAM.]

“Whoever speaks against the Son of Man will be forgiven; [Col. 0431B] but whoever [Note: [Col. 0431D] “But whoever,” Pamelius.] speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven.” If forgiving and retaining sin already indicate God as judge, then the Holy Spirit who must not be blasphemed will belong to that God [Note: [Col. 0431D] “Whose,” Semler.] who, of course, will not forgive the blasphemy—just as above Christ must not be denied because that God will kill even in Gehenna.

But if Christ also turns blasphemy away from the Creator, I do not know how he came as his adversary. Or if by these declarations too he casts a shadow upon the Creator’s severity—because he will not forgive blasphemy and will kill even in Gehenna—it remains that the Spirit of that different god may be blasphemed with impunity, and his Christ denied, and that it makes no difference whether he is worshiped or despised; and just as no punishment follows contempt, so no reward is to be hoped for from worship.

When they are brought [Note: [Col. 0431D] “Produced,” in other copies.] before the authorities, he forbids them to think beforehand, when questioned, about their answer: [Col. 0431C] “For the Holy Spirit,” he says, “will teach you in that very hour what you ought to say.” [Note: [Col. 0431D] “What you are to say,” in other copies.] If an example of this kind belongs to the Creator, the command too will belong to the one whose example preceded it. Balaam the prophet, in Numbers (Num. 22), was summoned by King Balak to curse Israel, with whom the king was going to war, and was at once filled with the Spirit. He pronounced not the curse for which he had come, but the blessing that the Spirit suggested to him at that very hour, having first declared before the royal messengers [Note: [Col. 0432D] “Of the king,” Semler.], and shortly afterward before the king himself, that he would pronounce what God had put into his mouth. These are the new teachings of the new Christ, which the servants of the Creator inaugurated long ago.

Here, plainly, is a different example in Moses and Christ. [Note: [Col. 0432D] Moses intervenes of his own accord between brothers who are quarreling. He calls those “brothers” who were of the same stock of Israel; a little later he takes the word in its proper sense. He also rendered in the single word *injuriosum*, “the wrongdoer,” what in the Greek of Exodus 2 is ἀδικοῦντι, “the one who was doing wrong”; and, remarkably, he rendered as *magistrum*, “master,” what in Greek is ἄρχοντα, for which everywhere else, and properly, he uses “ruler.” PAM.] Moses intervenes of his own accord between brothers who are quarreling [Note: [Col. 0432D] Rhenanus places “and” before this.], and rebukes the wrongdoer: “Why do you strike your neighbor?” But the man rejects him: “Who appointed you master or judge over us?” Christ, however, when asked by someone to mediate between him and his brother [Col. 0432A] concerning the division of an inheritance [Note: [Col. 0432D] “Concerning” is omitted by Venice.], refused his assistance, even in so worthy a cause. Thus my Moses is now better than your Christ, striving for peace between brothers and intervening against wrongdoing.

But of course he is the Christ of the supremely good and non-judging God: “Who,” he says, “appointed me judge over you?” He could find no other plea of excuse, lest he fail to use the very one by which a wicked man and impious brother had rebuffed the champion of uprightness and piety. In short, by using it he approved the wicked statement, and by declining to establish peace between brothers he approved the wicked deed. Or did he perhaps take it amiss that Moses had been driven away by this statement, and therefore, in the parallel case of brothers in dispute, wish to put them to shame by recalling the same statement? Precisely so. For it was he himself who had then been in Moses and had heard such things—namely, the Spirit of the Creator.

I think we have already established sufficiently elsewhere that the glory of riches is condemned by our God, who casts potentates themselves down from the throne and raises the poor from the dunghill (Ps. 112:13). From him, therefore, will also come the parable of the rich man flattering himself over the yield of his fields, to whom God says: “Fool, this night they will demand your soul back; then whose will the things you have prepared be?” [Note: [Col. 0432D] “For whom,” Latinus.] Thus, indeed, the king who boasted among the Persians about the treasuries and storehouses of his pleasures was reproved through Isaiah (Isa. 39).

## CHAPTER XXIX
