## CHAPTER II

Let the matter now be approached from another quarter, so that anything Punic among Romans may neither blush nor grieve. Certainly, changing one’s appearance is a customary function of [Col. 1032B] all nature. Even this world upon which we rest performs that same function from time to time. Let Anaximander answer for it if he thinks there are several worlds; and let anyone else answer if, somewhere among the Meropians, like Silenus, he babbles into the ears of Midas—ears well suited, assuredly, to rather tall tales. But even if there is some world which Plato judges this one to be the image of, that world too must necessarily undergo change in the same manner. For if it is a world, it will consist of diverse substances and functions after the pattern of what this present world is; indeed, it would not be a world if it did not behave correspondingly as a world. Diverse things come together into one; through change, they become different. In short, successive alternations reconcile the discord of diversity. Thus every world will exist by changing, [Note: [Col. 1032D] **Thus every world will exist by changing.** That is, if the world did not change, it would not exist, for it is composed and compacted from diverse substances. SALM.] being both embodied through diversity and tempered through alternation.

## CHAPTER III

RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1036C] “More depressed,” Fran.] where its neck blooms; [Note: [Col. 1036D] **Where its neck blooms.** “To bloom” is said of purple. The hyena, if you observe it, changes sex annually: it alternates between male and female. I say nothing of the stag, because it too, master of its own age, feeds upon a serpent and grows weak from the poison into youth.

There is also a quadruped, slow-moving, wild, lowly, and rough. Do you suppose I mean Pacuvius’ tortoise? I do not. The little beast too receives, along with others, [Note: [Col. 1037C] **Along with others.** *Allis* is used for *aliis*, a usage also found in Catullus. RIG.] a line of verse—quite an ordinary one, but with a grand name. When you hear “chameleon” without prior acquaintance with it, you will already fear something even greater than a lion; but when you find it in a vineyard, usually entirely beneath a vine leaf, you will at once laugh at the audacity and Greek pretension of its name. [Note: [Col. 1037C] **The audacity and Greek pretension of its name.** What lies beyond audacity except shamelessness? Thus, to say something surpassing even shamelessness, he says “Greekness.” There is the familiar verse of Martial:

> But they are Greeks, to whom nothing is denied.

And Juvenal:

> And whatever lying Greece  
> Dares to put into history.

RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1037C] **Greek pretension of its name.** So it is read in the Dijon codex.] [Note: [Col. 1037B] “And already the Greeks,” Rhen.; “distinction,” Salm.] For its body does not even contain fluid, although creatures much smaller than it plainly do. [Note: [Col. 1037C] **Although creatures much smaller than it plainly do.** So the old books read, except that they write *licet* for *liquet*, and I see no reason why anything else should have been changed. He says that the chameleon’s body has no fluid, whereas much smaller insects generally plainly do—that is, much smaller creatures are not without fluid. *Liquet succo* is used impersonally, as in reports of prodigies: “it rains milk,” “it rains blood,” and so forth. The chameleon, [Col. 1037B] [Note: [Col. 1037B] Salmasius inserts *si*, “if.”] a mere skin, nevertheless lives. [Note: [Col. 1037D] **The chameleon, a mere skin, nevertheless lives.** Although it is nothing other than a little skin, it nevertheless lives. RIG.] Its little head springs directly from its back, for it has no neck; it is therefore difficult for it to turn around, but it surveys its surroundings with roving little eyes—or rather, points of light—which whirl about. Sluggish and weary, it scarcely raises itself from the ground; it undertakes its gait in a stupor and moves forward, displaying a step more than completing it. Always fasting, of course, yet never exhausted [Note: [Col. 1037B] “Never weary,” Paris.], it feeds by yawning, ruminates by puffing itself up like a bellows, [Note: [Col. 1037D] **Puffing itself up like a bellows.** Inflated in the manner of a bellows. RIG.] [Col. 1038A] and takes its food from the wind.

Yet the chameleon too can change completely while remaining no other creature. [Note: [Col. 1038B] “Completely, and nothing else,” Fran.] For although it possesses one color as its own, it is suffused with the color of whatever comes near it. This alone has been granted to the chameleon—that which is commonly called “playing with one’s own skin.” [Note: [Col. 1037D] **Playing with one’s own skin.** Martial, against a certain Cerdo, so ostentatiously foolish that from the money he earned by patching hides he furnished gladiators to the people:

> You are drunk, for you would never do this sober, …
