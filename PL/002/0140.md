INHONESTUS (“dishonorable,” “unsightly”). II, 772 A. “Insults proved the flesh unsightly,” i.e. deformed.

INHONOR (“without honor”). II, 108 B, “But to attest that he was without honor.”

INHONORARE (“to dishonor”). II, 393 C, “The man will be dishonored”—although, to be sure, he is honorable by reason of his substance—i.e. he will be deprived of his honor. II, 808 B, “So that not the substance of the flesh, but its acts, may nevertheless be dishonored.”

INHUMANUS (“inhuman”). II, 1047 A, “So that, from a single surrounding garment, I may indeed nowhere be inhuman.” Latinius reads: “being indeed nowhere permitted to be uncivilized.”

INJECTIO (“injection,” “suggestion,” “objection”). II, 17 A, “The introduction of anxiety.” II, 28 A, “As though it were not possible for everyone, when matters have been badly examined, to stumble through error upon the suggestion of some evil”; the Greek is ἐπιβολή (“suggestion,” “attack”). II, 270 C, “Unless room is also given for refuting the other aspects of that objection.” II, 206 A, “By his own objection,” εἰσβολή (“incursion”), that is, by an objection or argument twisted in the opposite direction. II, 190 B, “But this objection of theirs too … has now been blunted.” II, 708 B, [Col. 1319B] “But her—Helen—[he called] his first conception.” Justin expresses it more clearly: “a certain first Mind or Intelligence.” Irenaeus calls her “the conception of his Mind, the mother of all,” through whom he initially conceived in his mind the making of angels and archangels. II, 1003 B, “Since we are not ignorant of his designs,” οὐ γὰρ αὐτοῦ τὰ νοήματα ἀγνοοῦμεν (“for we are not ignorant of his designs”), from 2 Corinthians 2:11.

INJICERE (“to cast in,” “to conceive”). II, 34 C, note 6, “In both cases exposing Christ to censure.” II, 189 A, “Nor are they ashamed to conceive it,” i.e. ἐννοεῖν (“to conceive in the mind”). II, 708 B, “By which he had conceived the creation of angels and archangels,” i.e. he had conceived it in his mind. What follows explains this: “her sharer in this purpose.”

ININTERPRETABILIS (“uninterpretable”). II, 564 A, “By this sole uninterpretable name.”

ININVENTIBILIS (“unsearchable”), ἀνεξερεύνητος. II, 238 B, “His judgments are unsearchable.”

ININVESTIGABILIS (“untraceable”), ἀνεξιχνίαστος. II, 238 B, “His ways are untraceable.”

INIRE (“to enter upon”). II, 893 C, “Therefore those whom he did not separate by remaining silent, he entered upon in the other.” The better reading is: “Therefore those whom he did not divide, by remaining silent he merged into the other” (*inalteravit*), i.e. he placed one within the other, joined and commingled them, so that both were now one and mutually contained one another. He himself says shortly afterward: “He did not separate [the man] from the woman, and by not separating him, joined him to her from whom he did not separate him.” [Col. 1319C]

INLUDIA (“mockeries,” “illusions”). II, 815 B, “He will dream himself nothing other than the mockeries of souls,” i.e. the illusions of the shades, who in vengeance harry their enemies, the instigators of the parricide.

INOBEDIENTIA (“disobedience”), an objection concerning obedience. II, 401 B, “Who could also reproach them for disobedience.”

INOBSCURABILIS (“incapable of being obscured”). II, 652 A, “Fortified there also by the unclouded rule of the divine determination.”

INOFFICIOSUS (“undutiful”), with the genitive. I, 485 A, “Undutiful toward him”—a Greek construction.

INOLESCERE (“to grow into,” “become ingrained”). I, 1269 B, “When he becomes habituated to ashes and sackcloth,” i.e. by habit he makes repentance familiar and almost natural to himself. The metaphor is taken from trees which, after being grafted and budded into other trees, become accustomed to the nourishment and sustenance drawn up and attracted through the trunk. I, 486 A, “It became habituated to every vice and crime.” [Col. 1319D]

INORARE (“to furnish an edge or border”). II, 815 B, “It will also be bordered with the wreaths of its drinker.”

INPRAESCIENTIA (“lack of foreknowledge”). II, 293 C, “Would he not then seem rather to have been deceived through lack of foreknowledge of the future?”

INQUIES (“restlessness”). II, 691 B, “Or if his restlessness has ceased.”

INQUIETARE (“to disturb”). II, 1033 A, “He disturbs,” i.e. “he is disturbed”; so often. “To trouble”: I, 363 A, “They trouble the honor of the divinity.”

INQUIETATOR (“disturber”). I, 654 B, “That charioteer, the disturber of so many souls.”

INQUILINITATUS (“sojourn as a tenant or stranger”). II, 717 A, “Throughout the whole period of his sojourn.”

INRECOGITATIO, i.e. “failure to consider,” as Plautus says, or “inconsiderateness.” II, 919 B, “This very insertion of this counsel, as though it had suffered a failure to consider its own transgression.”

INSANIRE (“to rage,” “be mad”). I, 692 C, “For if you rage, you thereby affirm that he exists,” i.e. if you become angry. [Col. 1320A] I, 1284 A, “And those women who rave at Delphi do not know marriage.”

INSCRIBERE (“to inscribe,” “enroll”). II, 304 C, “But the world itself has been inscribed,” namely with this testimony. The sense is: that catholic and supreme goodness of God has been inscribed upon the tablets of the world. II, 714 A, “When he is now also enrolled under fate,” i.e. counted among its subjects.

INSCRIPTURA (“enrollment”). II, 585 A, “For us, the enrollment of this seed,” ἐγγραφή (“registration”), by which we have been enrolled for salvation in this classification of the psychic seed, as the author said above. Thus “the enrollment of this seed” is the rule laid down for the psychic seed.

INSECUTOR (“pursuer,” “persecutor”). I, 294 A, “Such are always our persecutors: unjust, impious, and shameful.”

INSERERE (“to insert”). II, 581 A, “Into whom they insert Jesus with so much license.” The Valentinians insert that Jesus—Soter, constituted from the first fruits of all the aeons—into the Demiurge’s psychic Christ, thereby making a fertile Christ; and in the same way they thrust the spiritual seed into the psychic.

INSOLESCENS (“swelling,” “becoming excessive”). [Col. 1320B] II, 756 B, “The womb swelling from day to day,” i.e. bulging and protruding in an unusual manner. II, 700 B, “As though it were a clipping of the human race grown insolent.”

INSTANTIA (“pressure,” “persistence,” “persecution”). I, 1265 B, “As often as its persecution became worse than its grounds.” I, 651 B, “Or the persistence of the interrogation.” II, 748 B, “Overcome by the urgent pressure of divine grace.”

INSTAURARE (“to furnish,” “prepare”). II, 363 C, “Among the apostolic writers, Luke and Mark furnish [it],” i.e. equip and prepare it. Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXXI, chapter 2: “Marcus Cicero had erected a monument to himself in his Academy”—as though, indeed, he had not also done so throughout the whole world.

INSTITUERE (“to establish”). II, 51 D, note 7, “Necessity establishes [them]”; understand “them.” For Tertullian very frequently omits an antecedent in this way.

INSTITUTIO (“instruction,” “constitution”). I, 376 A, “By corrupt instructions.” Haverkamp maintains that in these words Tertullian had in view the *Tablet of Cebes*, where the corrupt forms of instruction by which a person is ensnared, i.e. deceived, are δόξαι (“opinions”), ψευδοπαιδεία (“false education”), poets, orators, etc. [Col. 1320C] II, 208 B, “For since their nature has a beginning, it will also be capable of having an end.” II, 810 A, “And so you may believe the restoration of the flesh easier than its original constitution.”

INSTRUCTILIS (“capable of being constructed”). II, 668 A, “It is wholly from itself and no more capable of being constructed from elsewhere than it is divisible from itself.”

INSTRUCTUS (“equipment”), splendid apparatus and an ostentatious domestic establishment. I, 305 A, “In dress, food, and equipment.” “Which we say is born with all its equipment,” as though with all its furnishings and apparatus, with all its property and everything belonging to it. For so he says above. Augustine shows that expressions of this meaning were used especially among Africans, in book II of *Questions on Exodus*, question 47: “The Greek,” he says, “has ἀποσκευήν (‘equipment, baggage’) where the Latin translator rendered ‘property’; our writers sometimes translate it ‘estate,’ just as here we wished to say ‘equipment.’”

INSTRUMENTUM (“document,” “testament,” “apparatus”). I, 377 A, “The document of literature,” i.e. Holy Scripture. I, 381 A, “By those documents.” II, 214 A, [Col. 1320D] “I shall appeal to Moses’ original document.” II, 151 A, “The instruments of imperial power will speak.” II, 267 B, “Nor will his disciples be able to deny what they have in their supreme document.” II, 361 B, “The one belonging to the other document,” or, as it is more commonly expressed, “testament.” II, 169 B, “Who produced all the documents of his apostleship.” II, 472 C, “Rather than from the document of Acts.” II, 484 A, “According to the common document,” i.e. according to that portion of Luke’s Gospel which the Marcionites, together with us, also accept. II, 906 B, “They carry more apparatus to the baths.” Clement, *Paedagogus* III, 5: Τά τε ἀργυρώματα μεθ’ ὧν ἐμπομπεύουσιν ἀπειροκάλως ἐν τοῖς βαλανείοις προτιθέασι (“They set out in the baths the silverware with which they make an ostentatious and tasteless display”). II, 1306 A, “They bestowed on women that apparatus of feminine glory,” i.e. feminine adornment and equipment, from the book of Enoch: κόσμια ταῖς γύναιξιν (“ornaments for women”). II, 1325 B, “How, then, shall we treat chastity without its equipment?” i.e. without gravity; for chastity is better equipped when it has gravity as its companion. [Col. 1321A] II, 850 B, “Then the Apostle too, throughout almost the whole document,” i.e. throughout all his epistles. I, 636 A, “Rather than from the documents of pagan literature.” I, 1202 C, “How great an apparatus it brings to the world”—namely water.

INSUBDITIVUM (“not subject”), that which is not liable to passions and changes, II, 582 A.

INSULA (“apartment block”). II, 550 B, “You would think it Felicula’s apartment block.” An *insula* is a large building surrounded on every side by a public road, ordinarily containing many shops and rented upper rooms.

INSULTATORIUS (“taunting”). II, 497 C, “From whom he received a taunting and triumphal word concerning death.”

INSUMERE (“to expend”). II, 217 B, “If the Holy Spirit expended so much care upon our instruction.”

INTENTATIO, equivalent to *intentio*. I, 500 B, “Against the accusation of every crime.” I, 432 B, “This is enough against the accusation of injured divinity.” II, 1009 B, “Even if you extend the following portions of that epistle to the Apostle’s accusation.”

INTENTIO (“charge,” “accusation”). II, 656 A, “The monstrous accusation of philosophy.” [Col. 1321B]

INTERCALARE (“to intercalate”), to insert a period of time, as formerly in the calculation of the calendar. II, 504 D, “Unless the Creator intercalated [the time] for this reason.”

INTERCEDERE (“to interpose,” “oppose”). II, 555 A, “But Mother Silence interposed,” i.e. placed herself in the way and obstructed him; a forensic expression. Irenaeus: κατέσχε δὲ αὐτὸν ἡ Σιγή (“but Silence restrained him”). II, 293 A, “Because God did not interpose against those things,” i.e. did not oppose them. II, 472 C, “That certain persons had interposed who said…”

INTERCIPERE (“to cut off”). II, 426 A, “The emperor Marcus [Antoninus], already cut off,” i.e. whom fate had carried off before Cybele learned of his death.

INTEREMPTRIX (“destroyer”). I, 650 A, “Those very destroyers of their own modesty.”

INTERFECTOR (“slayer”). II, 750 B, “But now answer here, slayer of truth”: he is addressing Marcion.

INTERIBILIS (“perishable”). II, 229 B, “God would have made nothing perishable out of matter.” Ibid., “From the perishable into the eternal.”

INTERIM. “Sometimes”: I, 309 A, “Those crimes which, when disclosed, will sometimes also provoke human punishment.” [Col. 1321C] II, 1032 B, “Even this world on which we rest sometimes performs the same function.” “At length, eventually”: II, 1045 B, “At length the herald of this one error of yours.” “Nevertheless”: I, 386 A, “And yet the sacred rites of a single prophet.” II, 554 A, “Nevertheless, in the number thirty,” i.e. however great the privileges of those numbers may be in thirty, etc. “Let them meanwhile acknowledge the authority of nature,” i.e. even now. II, 10 B, “Sometimes and always,” i.e. “at times.”

INTERPELLATIO (“interruption,” “attack”). I, 702 B, “And now, for the same reason, an attack of blood”—perhaps a hemorrhage from which Scapula was then suffering.

INTERPRETARI, passive. II, 214 A, “Which can be interpreted as matter.” II, 627 A, “For it is interpreted spiritually in this way.” II, 1222 A, “Nor will it incongruously be interpreted with reference to the figure.” II, 910 A, “We sometimes interpret this as belonging to the devil,” i.e. attribute it to him and credit it to his account. II, 92 A, “To be interpreted as transgression,” i.e. something that ought to be judged and regarded as a transgression.

INTERPRETATIO (“interpretation”). II, 22 C, 23 A, “To contend with the helm of interpretation,” [Col. 1321D] i.e. with reason, with that discipline of reason which must govern every interpretation and always reign within it.

INTERPRETATOR (“interpreter”). II, 937 A, “The same Apostle teaches, as interpreter of both Testaments.”

INTERSTES (“boundary”). II, 441 B, “And the new things began with John as the boundary.”

INTERVENIRE (“to intervene”). I, 1267 A, “Or practice intervenes on behalf of the Lord’s admonitions.” Rhenanus reads *intervenimus* (“we intervene”). We intervene against our errors or the snares of evil when we oppose ourselves to them, not by consenting but rather by avoiding them. Thus formerly a tribune interposed against decrees of the senate. But we intervene on behalf of the Lord’s admonitions when we obey them.

INTERULA, an under-tunic worn beneath the mantle, II, 1047 A.

INTIMARE (“to insert,” “bring inward”). I, 1310 A, “No nail inserts silver into the panels”: it introduces it, fixing and inserting it within by inlaid work. [Col. 1322A] II, 851 A, “He indicated that the Lord must, of course, be brought home to the senses.”

INTINCTIO (“immersion,” “baptism”). II, 1229 A, “He commanded that the baptism of repentance should precede,” i.e. baptism. I, 1270 A, “Toward the occasion of a second baptism,” i.e. martyrdom. I, 1247 A, “Presumption upon baptism brings [this],” i.e. confidence that pardon will be obtained through baptism.

INUNITUS (“united into one”). II, 583 A, “And yet united into one in Adam.” Irenaeus: τὴν ἐν ἑνὶ τῷ Ἀδὰμ συστᾶσαν (“that which was constituted as one in Adam”).

INVENIRE (“to find”). II, 25 B, “It will be found by the seeker,” i.e. what he seeks will be present to the seeker; a Greek expression.

INVENTUS (“discovery”), *inventio*, εὕρημα (“finding”), a Tertullianic form. II, 648 B, “The discovery of truth itself is testimony.”

INVESTIS (“unclothed”). II, 1038 A, “He stood before his potter certainly naked and unclothed.” II, 900 C, “Even as yet unclothed with the maturity of the male,” i.e. ἄρρενος ἀφθόρου (“an uncorrupted male”), one who is still “clothed,” that is, still in chastity and modesty, as Nonius interprets it. II, 906 A, “And the head of the household, although still immature.” II, 746 B, “Suppose now an immature boy.”

INVIDIA (“fixed gaze,” “odium”). I, 487 A, “We beat upon heaven with our fixed gaze.” This is explained as ἀτενισμός (“intent gazing”), from *invidere*, meaning “to look intensely” and to gaze most vehemently at something with the eyes’ concentrated focus. Thus Junius. Rigault explains differently at I, 1159 A: “The souls of the martyrs cry to the Lord with odium,” i.e. by crying out they render him odious; through the blood shed amid so many torments they cast odium upon God. The voices of the martyrs are so resolute and effective that they can wrest from God, even against his will, vengeance for the blood shed on account of his name; indeed, unless he grants their requests, they seem to burden him with the odium of cruelty and mercilessness. II, 113 A, “I prefer to render him odious by perishing through his will rather than provoke his bile by escaping through my own.”

INVISIBILIS (“invisible”). II, 223 A, “Invisible—the earth—as still covered by waters as though by the protection of generative moisture.”

INVOCARE (“to invoke,” “proclaim”). I, 399 B, “Because the remission of the Lord your God has been proclaimed,” ἐπικέκληται (“has been publicly invoked or proclaimed”), promulgated by public declaration.

INVOLUNTAS (“involuntariness”). II, 499 B, “For one sinning from involuntariness or necessity,” [Col. 1322C] i.e. when a sin is alleged to have been involuntary and one into which they were driven by force and necessity.

IRE (“to go”). II, 282 B, “You would have been welcome, O heretical god, if you had gone against the Creator’s dispensation,” i.e. if you had opposed it; for then Marcion would not have been born.

IRRECUPERABILIS (“irrecoverable”). II, 1007 C, “With irrecoverable uncleanness,” i.e. uncleanness from which nothing can be recovered—namely, one curable by no art. Thus below, where he discusses the curable uncleanness of leprosy, he speaks of “recovery and restoration.”

IRROGARE (“to impose”). I, 690 A, “Through them also the punishments prepared for the impious are imposed.” This is corrupt. The truest reading is the common one, also supported by the Agobard manuscript: “Through them also the punishments prepared for the impious remain unknown.” Tertullian says that the unjust savagery of secular authorities against Christians prevents the Christian religion from advancing, and that consequently the punishments God has prepared against the impious remain unknown. Thus all the powers and dignities of this age are not merely alien but hostile to God, and therefore are by no means to be sought or undertaken by Christians, the worshipers of God. [Col. 1322D]

IRRUERE (“to cast in”). II, 701 C, 702 A, “Aethalides, however, and Hermotimus had also cast the bean among the foods held in common,” i.e. had brought or thrown it in; for *ruere* can also be transitive, as Servius says on *Georgics* II.

ISCLA. II, 554 A, “In the Carthaginian *isclae*.” A Carthaginian word; among Syrians, *iscola*; Latin, *schola* (“school”).

ISTER, the Danube. II, 247 B, “More treacherous than the Ister,” because through its depth, swiftness, and the frequency of intervening crags and reefs it very often deceives sailors.

ITERARE (“to repeat,” “transfer again”). II, 1008 A, “When the letters of the same Apostle to the Corinthians repeat [the teaching].” II, 711 B, “Souls transferred again into another body.”

ἸΧΘΎΣ (“fish”). I, 1198 A, “But we little fishes, following our ἰχθύς, Jesus Christ, are born in water.” Ἰχθύς is an *acrostic term* (ἀκροκώλιον), as Junius calls it, derived from the Sibylline verses in this manner: Ἰ, Ἰησοῦς (“Jesus”); χ, Χριστός (“Christ”); θ, Θεοῦ (“of God”); υ, Υἱός (“Son”); σ, Σωτήρ (“Savior”). Cf. I, 150 D, 151 D, 1198 D, 1199 B, C, D, 1200 B, C, D; II, 872 A, 1140 D, 1141 A, B, C. [Col. 1323A]

## J

JEJUNARE (“to fast,” “abstain”). II, 655 B, “In short, although fasting from philosophy, they nevertheless live.” II, 1011 C, “Behold him desiring that souls should fast from the rightful fruit of nature—I mean the fruit of marriage.” II, 631 B, “While all the rest fast from salvation.”

JEJUNATIO (“fasting”). I, 703 C, “When have even droughts not been driven away by our kneelings and fastings?”

JEJUNUS (“empty,” “devoid”). II, 983 C, “For those about to shed tears empty of peace,” i.e. without hope of peace.

JUDICARE (“to condemn”). I, 286 A, “He condemned himself by starvation,” i.e. procured his own death by starvation. I, 352 A, note 2, “Condemned by a thunderbolt,” i.e. punished.

JUDICATO (“by settled judgment”). I, 1235 B, “And to have pronounced him better by settled judgment.” [Col. 1323B]

JUDICIUM (“judgment”). *Domesticum judicium* is a preconceived opinion.

JUPITER. I, 317 A, “A certain Jupiter”: Jupiter Latiaris is meant. II, 974 A, “In the presence of the modern Jupiter, the heretic derived from Pythagoras,” i.e. the god and first founder of the Encratites. See Epiphanius, book II.

JURIDICINA, the period during which someone dispenses justice, II, 1040 A; it means the praetorship.

JURULENTIA (“bloody liquor”). I, 313 A, “So that you may collect the bloody liquor.”

JUSTITIA (“proportion,” “rightness”). II, 1030 C, “Square proportion,” i.e. συμμετρία (“symmetry”). Others read: “and the border of the square garment.” The *instita* was a very narrow band sewn beneath garments for ornament, since such borders were strips of more costly cloth. The remainder is read by others as follows: “But in men, the outward garb of the mantle,” etc. II, 234 B, “The justice of change,” i.e. a just change. II, 880 B–C, “In the bodies themselves he fixed the ready growth of nails and hair according to the proper measure of usefulness and dignity.” [Col. 1323C]

JUVARE (“to please”). II, 1030 C, “When one has leisure and it pleases,” i.e. while it is agreeable and permitted.

JUVENESCERE (“to grow young”). II, 921 B, “It has now grown old since it grew young.”

JUXTA (“equally,” “alongside”). II, 648 B, “That philosophers have sometimes thought equally with us,” i.e. equally in certain matters. II, 987 A, “Those who have not first made profession before the Church are in danger of being judged on a level with adultery and fornication.” II, 1020 A, “It is equally plausible, therefore, that we say John escaped his notice.”

## L

LABARE (“to falter”). II, 998 A, “Who is failing in health?”

LABERIUS, a very ancient poet who wantonly coined words in his compositions. Suetonius, *Julius*, chapter 39; Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book XVI, chapter 7.

LABORARE (“to be hard-pressed”). II, 322 D, “Truth ought to employ all its powers, but not as though hard-pressed.” Virtue is hard-pressed when it is either oppressed by falsehood or overlaid with the appearance of truth. It also happens not infrequently that speaking the truth is dangerous because of the violence and ungoverned force of certain persons. [Col. 1323D]

LACINIOSUS (“many-folded,” “diffuse”). II, 362 A, “And freed from the many-folded works of the law,” i.e. from its manifold works, which kept everyone occupied, just as a many-folded garment hinders one’s step. II, 433 C, “To be free of the impediments of a many-folded and entangled life.” II, 894 B, “Because the discourse is diffuse,” i.e. extended. I, 1225 B, “To occupy oneself with the manifold and superabundant follies of pageants and pleasures.”

LACRYMA (“tear”), the name given to every liquid that drips from trees. Pliny, *Natural History* XII, 14. I, 444 A, “The tears of the Arabian tree,” i.e. grains of frankincense.

LACUNARIA (“coffered ceilings”), paneled work. I, 417 A, “Let another count the coffers of the ceiling.” This concerns the pagans, among whom the ceilings of temples were gilded. See Lipsius, *On the Greatness of Rome*, book III, chapter 5; Meursius, *On Luxury*, chapter 7; and Ricquius, *On the Capitol*, p. 200. Against them he turns back what they used to allege against Christians and Jews: that they counted the clouds. For those pagans themselves, when sacrificing on the Capitol, stood erect not only with hands raised upward but also on tiptoe, as though counting the coffers of the ceiling. [Col. 1324A]

LAESURA (“loss,” “damage”). I, 1260 C, “Nor should their losses be reckoned,” i.e. damage or loss incurred when something from one’s family property is lost.

LAMIUS. II, 545 B, “The towers of Lamia and the sun’s combs.” Children’s tales current at Carthage in Tertullian’s time, by which nurses restrained their little children from crying.

LANA (“wool”), ἔριον or ὠάριον, was a woolly or thick covering (ἐπίθημα) with which they covered the softer parts of the brain while leaving the rest bare. II, 912 A, “For some women do not veil their heads with turbans and pads of wool.”

LANCEA (“spear”). II, 994 B, “But there is need of the whole spear of the sun,” i.e. its ray. [Col. 1324B]

LANCEARE (“to spear”), to pierce through and kill with a spear. II, 618 B, “Who learn to spear before they learn to be lacerated,” concerning Pontic infants; II, 338 A.

LANCINARE (“to lacerate”), to tear apart or cut into pieces. II, 247 B, “Prometheus, the omnipotent god, is lacerated by blasphemies”; others read *laciniatur*, i.e. as though he were pulled and plucked apart strip by strip. II, 618 B, “Who learn to spear before they learn to be lacerated”: infants made irritable by teething lacerate the breasts while sucking. II, 337; 806 B, “Lacerated even by the bedding.”

LANIARE (“to mangle”). I, 274 B, “To order a sacrilegious man to be mangled”—understand “by tortures.” The preceding words should perhaps be punctuated thus: “For this is your custom; you murderers say, ‘Deny it!’” i.e. you are responsible for making them deny.

LANICUTIS (“woolly-skinned”), wool-bearing, εἰροπόκος, II, 1031 B.

LANIUS (“butcher”). II, 199 A, “Among shopkeepers and butchers.” Rigault thinks the text corrupt because butchers are listed separately here, although they were registered in the guild of shopkeepers. Rhenanus and Junius attest that ancient copies read *janeos*. Junius interprets this of hired doorkeepers and cites Festus; Rhenanus interprets it of tavern-haunters, as though Septimius had said *ganeos* for *ganeones*. Nevertheless, since we know that Christians of that age so greatly abhorred blood and gore that they most scrupulously abstained even from blood sausages and other foods of that kind, I can readily be persuaded that Tertullian here excluded butchers from the general designation of shopkeepers, thereby emphasizing more strongly the foulness of the business under discussion. [Col. 1324C]

LANOSITAS (“woolliness”). II, 1039 A, “Where broader shells bristle with mossy woolliness.”

LANX (“platter”). I, 300 A; II, 1048 A, “Likewise, where Sulla handles platters weighing a hundred pounds.” These were serving dishes made from a hundred pounds of silver. Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXXIII, chapter 11.

LAPIDOSITAS (“stony formation”). I, 1311 A, “Just as there is also a certain stony formation in the brains of fish.”

LARGITER (“abundantly”). [Col. 1324D] I, 462 A, “Abundantly of vengeance.” A Hellenism: “abundant vengeance.”

LARISSAEUS HEROS (“the Larisaean hero”): Achilles, II, 1041 A.

LATERCULUM (“register”), the name of a book containing an account of all military and civil offices; it was kept by the *primicerius* of the notaries. See also Pancirolus on the *Notitia Dignitatum*, chapter 92. II, 584 A, “Therefore, from their register.”

LATERE (“to be hidden”). II, 248 A, “Lest it be hidden from anyone”: a Hellenism. II, 774 A, “While this was done, from whom was it hidden?”

LATERENSIS (“attendant at one’s side”). II, 466 A, “He was accustomed to employ almost as many attendants at his side,” i.e. honorary companions and friends accompanying him for honor’s sake, who in civil law are likewise called *a latere* (“from the side”). Others are *laterones*, δορύφοροι (“bodyguards”), who follow in attendance and closely accompany the sides of eminent men in a ministerial, not—as the former—in an honorary office.

LATRINAE (“latrines”). II, 1044 B, “The priest of the latrines, who presides over the goddess Cloacina.” [Col. 1325A] II, 900 C, “It becomes a preparation chamber for latrines.” Jerome also imitated this: “What sort of fast,” he says, “or what sort of refreshment after a fast is it? We are distended by the previous day’s feasts, and our throat becomes a meditation chamber—or rather a preparation chamber—for latrines.” He also cites the Scriptures quoted here: Exodus XXXI, Deuteronomy XXXII and VIII, and Isaiah VI—except that there too the reading should be “God,” since the Greek is Θεόν (“God”), and that the author uses his own expression *salvificator* (“savior”) in place of *salutaris* (“saving”).

LATUS (“side”). II, 1034 A, “The side of Italy”: Magna Graecia. II, 798 A, “Because on this side also the unity of the divinity will be defended”; a metaphor taken from military affairs. II, 443 B, “You also have an observer of marriages, whichever side you please,” i.e. whichever direction you turn. II, 969 A, “On which side, then, do you suppose the Spirit has been confirmed among us?” i.e. by which side of the argument, by what reasoning, and in what direction? He uses the word “confirmed” in reference to a protected flank in an army or in the case of a gladiator.

LAUREOLUS. [Col. 1325B] II, 565 A, “Because she had been trained in no *Laureolus* of Catullus,” λαυρέολον ἀσκηθεῖσα (“trained in a *Laureolus*”): a Hellenism. Catullus the mime-writer composed two mimes, *The Apparition* and *Laureolus*. In the *Laureolus*, a judge was driven onto the cross before a great crowd. Domitian, however, wished a certain defendant, accused on a capital charge and condemned in a genuine trial, actually to be driven onto the cross and there torn apart by a bear. Suetonius, *Caligula*, chapter 57; Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities*, book XIX.

LECTIO (“reading,” “text”). I, 408 A, “And now they criticize them as their readings continue to resound,” i.e. from their writings, which still speak daily. II, 697 B, “And to conclude through readings the questions reconsidered by the pen,” i.e. it was resolved to conclude through written texts the questions reconsidered by the pen, or by this writing of mine, since in those debates the questions treated back and forth in speech could not, and still cannot, be settled.

LEGATORIUS (“testamentary”). I, 638 A, “Among these, in the commemorations even of private persons, are testamentary exhibitions by their parents,” [Col. 1325C] i.e. gifts and games presented by heirs or legatees, to whom the testator had bequeathed by will something from his property for the presentation of games.

LEMNISCUS (“ribbon”). II, 647 B, “Eagerly seeking, even in prison, to crush the ribboned palms of Anytus and Meletus.” A *lemniscata palma* is properly a palm to which ribbons are attached as a mark of higher honor. For *lemnisci* are ribbons customarily used in honors paid to the gods; see Ausonius, Epistle 20. In a decree of the African city of Berenice appears στέφανος ἐλαϊνός καὶ λημνίσκος (“an olive crown and a ribbon”), the latter added to the crown for the sake of higher honor, in Maffeius, *Gallic Antiquities*, p. 7.

LENO (“pander,” figuratively an embellisher). I, 1184 A, “And elaborate styling, the pander of the hair.” Similarly I, 1151 A, “With the Gospel superimposed as the canceller of all former antiquity”; II, 1045 A, “the priest of the platform”; IV, 190, “she purified herself with purgative water”; II, 716 A, “for ruling powers.” Thus there is scarcely any need for the conjecture *lenoninam operositatem* (“pandering elaboration”).

LENOCINATOR (“pander”). II, 272 B, “A pander to grace.”

LENOCINIUM (“allurement,” “pandering”). II, 221 C, “Without any allurement of delivery.” I, 1274 A, “Or subjects his wife to authorities engaged in pandering.” [Col. 1325D] The expression belongs to the jurists. For, according to Ulpian, *On Those Marked with Infamy*, chapter 1, “Athletes”: “A person practices pandering who has owned slaves for prostitution; and so does one who carries on this trade through free persons”—add, in the author’s view, “through his wife.”

LEO (“lion”). II, 77 B, “I know their shepherds too: lions in peace, deer in battle.” A proverbial expression for those who conduct themselves perversely: timid where circumstances demand boldness and courage, fierce where there is no need of ferocity. Livy: “Turbulent in peace, sluggish in war.” Synesius: ἐν μὲν εἰρήνῃ θρασεῖς, ἐν δὲ πολέμῳ δειλοί (“bold in peace, but cowardly in war”).

LEX (“law”). II, 586 B, “That the Julian laws too intervene, as also the Cainian.” Junius reads “Canian,” or “Canuleian.” The Julian law concerned the marriage of the orders and did not permit anyone to remain unmarried, etc. The Canuleian law concerned intermarriage between the orders, allowing plebeians and patricians freely to contract marriages with one another, as Livy relates at the beginning of book IV. This law, however, was needed so that the lower beings beneath the Pleroma might be joined by marriage to the higher beings within the Pleroma. [Col. 1326A] II, 952 A, “It is another matter if they believe that under Christ too proceedings are conducted according to the Julian laws.” See SOLIDUM. II, 327 B, “Evidently, for that shipmaster not indeed the Rhodian but the Pontic law had made provision.” The Rhodian law provided that, in a jettison, no one should suffer more loss through fraud than geometric equality, as it is called, would require, and that no error should be made to the detriment of any passenger. Evidently, says the author, some Pontic law—from Pontus, whence Marcion emerged—had likewise provided that the Jews should not be permitted to err against their Christ.

LIARE (“to smooth”). II, 670 A, “And to smooth a cistern,” λεαίνειν (“to make smooth”), to polish it carefully so that no water can leak out. See Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXXVI, chapter 23.

LIBENS (“pleasing,” “agreeable”), that which pleases its spectators. II, 247 A, “The sun is never pleasing”; perhaps better, however: “The sun does not willingly look upon this region.”

LIBENTER (“willingly”). II, 151 A, “And merely doves willingly wandering,” [Col. 1326B] i.e. straying and flying away from the Lord’s dwellings in heedless simplicity.

LIBERALITAS (“largesse”). II, 76 A, “The largesse of the most excellent emperors,” i.e. the emperors Lucius Septimius Severus, the father, and Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Caracalla, the son, granted the soldiers a liberal donative, as Rhenanus observed.

LIBERTAS (“freedom”). II, 902 A, “So that she may display the honor of holiness in the freedom of her head.” By “freedom of the head” he means what shortly before he called permission regarding the veil, i.e. permission not to wear a veil on the head.

LIBIDO (“inclination,” “appetite”). II, 27 A, “And pour forth every appetite of curiosity.” II, 231 A, “Or their inclinations.” II, 758 A, “The whole uncertainty arising from the impulses of disgust and gluttony,” i.e. from appetites or voluntary movements of aversion or craving, by which pregnant women are generally troubled.

LIBIDINOSUS (“lustful,” “greedy”). I, 516 A, “Greedy for glory.” II, 908 A, “As one greedy for glory”; this occurs through desire and craving for glory. [Col. 1326C]

LIBRARE (“to launch with balanced force”). II, 1031 B, “Launched by no one,” i.e. impelled by no one. II, 234 B, “It was borne along by an onset launched with force.”

LIBRATOR (“balancer”). II, 227 A, concerning the Spirit who was borne over the waters.

LIBRIPENS (“scale-holder”). I, 291 C, “As though the scale-holder for the good emancipated by God.” A *libripens* is properly one who weighs out gold; he was employed especially in emancipations and in manumissions for religious purposes. The author is discussing the emancipation not of a person but of the good, which God transferred to man by emancipation—not by purchase—or transferred as some dignity belonging to man. God therefore gave and emancipated the good to man, i.e. conveyed it from his own hand into man’s. Now, just as in emancipations a scale-holder intervened through a certain legal fiction and representation, weighing out for the person emancipated the property tendered by the emancipator, so, says the author, freedom of choice was assigned by God to man through a legal fiction, like a kind of scale-holder [Col. 1326D] who conveyed and delivered to man the good received from God, so that man might be master of the good bestowed on him by God.

LICENTIA (“liberty,” “power”). II, 942 B, “It possesses along with itself the mind’s liberty,” ἐξουσία (“authority, power”). II, 117 A, “If by tribute we purchased permission for the sect.” II, 649 A, “It preserves nothing of divine power”; namely, “power,” as Latinius confirmed by examples from Arnobius, *Against the Nations*, books I and II.

LICERE (“to be permitted,” sometimes “to be clear”). II, 231 A, “But concerning motion it will be clear elsewhere,” in place of *liquebit*. II, 133 B, “Even Africa was permitted [this].” II, 781 B, “Although it was now also permissible concerning the name ‘man.’” II, 683 A, “Thus it will also be clear that the soul is uniform with the seed,” i.e. *liquebit*.

LICITE (“permissibly,” here “licentiously”). II, 198 A, “He paints licentiously,” i.e. freely or wantonly, contrary to the restriction God placed upon the painter’s art by forbidding the painting of things capable of being worshiped or the setting up of an image of God. Thus he scorns God’s law in his art.

LIGNARIUS (“wooden,” “pertaining to timber”). II, 628 B, “Lest perhaps you suppose some wooden king to be signified.” [Col. 1327A] II, 347 B, “Unless perhaps some wooden king of the Jews is signified.”

LIMAX (“slug”), a land snail, named from mud (*limus*), because it is generated and nourished in it, according to Varro and Festus. II, 663 A, “And with a foaming crawl, as slugs do.”

LIMEN (“threshold”). II, 939 A, “Behold, immediately, as though on the threshold, two guardians of Christian holiness meet us,” i.e. they present themselves at the very commencement of the work and argument. II, 545 B, “To stumble at the threshold” is a proverbial expression for those who err at the beginning of an undertaking they have assumed.

LIMES (“boundary”). I, 526 A, “When, therefore, the end and intervening boundary,” etc. The sense is: when the appointed time and that final boundary lying between temporal and perpetual existence arrive. Rigault thinks that Tertullian calls the span of a thousand years the “intervening boundary.” II, 548 A, “For me, however, the archetypes will form the boundary of the principal teachers,” i.e. the barriers and limits of this disputation of mine will be that I engage with the authentic writings of the leading heretics.

LINEA (“line”). [Col. 1327B] II, 200 B–C, “Because of those who do not understand, of whom Hermogenes is the outermost line,” i.e. the foremost among the ἀνόητοι (“unintelligent”), a metaphor taken from painting. II, 232 B, “Of which he is the outermost line.” II, 233 A, “And here you have fallen outside your own lines,” i.e. you argue against yourself or have strayed from your established purpose. II, 577 A, “Horos marking the outermost line.” II, 175 C, “God is the outermost line of the universe.” II, 599 A, “And let us determine the sum of the question itself with definite lines.” II, 254 C, “Meanwhile we have enclosed it within these lines,” a metaphor taken from games. II, 276 A, “We have led [the argument] along these lines,” i.e. we have demonstrated it by this method and path. II, 781 B, “Let us now direct the encounter toward a single line,” i.e. let us concentrate on one matter and debate it by adducing arguments on both sides. II, 659 B, “But we also inscribe a line for that corporeal soul.” II, 693 B, “I shall now gather my step within our lines.” II, 78 C, “How long shall we draw the saw back and forth along this line?”—a proverbial expression for useless and fruitless labor. The metaphor is taken from sawyers, who draw the saw in such a way that they either do not let it go forward or persist only on the same line: they accomplish nothing, or certainly very little. [Col. 1327C]

LINIAMENTUM (“lineament,” “outline”). II, 812 A, “Such, meanwhile, are the outlines of the divine powers.” II, 866 B, “Walking in holiness according to the lineaments of Christ,” etc., imitating the conduct Christ practiced in the flesh. Thus, in *Against Marcion*, book II, the soul is said to possess God’s lines insofar as it is immortal, free and possessed of its own choice, often endowed with foreknowledge, and capable of understanding and knowledge.

LINUM (“thread”). I, 1314 B, “Ten times a hundred thousand sesterces are inserted upon a single thread.” He means large pearls, which women use after piercing them and placing them on a cord. Scaevola called it “a string of pearls,” book XXVI, *On the Lex Falcidia*. There is a ruling concerning large pearls on a string in Ulpian, book 27, *On the Lex Aquilia*.

LITARE (“to offer propitiatorily”). I, 1265 A, “But what honor shall we offer to the Lord God?”

LITERATURA (“literature,” “Scripture”). I, 515 A, “For the antiquity of divine Scripture established beforehand still benefits my case.” I, 377 B, “The document of Scripture.” [Col. 1327D]

LOCALIS (“local”). I, 701 B, “And meanwhile let us pray that it is local,” i.e. pertains to some single place; it is opposed to “universal.”
