### 134. To contract one’s nostril. Vol. III, Book I, *Against Marcion*, chap. 13, no. 71.

The nose is the seat of mockery. Hence mockers are called “keen-nosed” and are said “to have a nose.” Pliny, Book XI, chap. 37: “Modern manners have consecrated the nose to sly mockery.” Horace:

> To hang someone upon a hooked nose.

Martial:

> And boys have the nose of a rhinoceros.

Persius:

> You laugh, he says, and indulge too freely  
> Your hooked nostrils.

And the Greeks use μυκτηρίζειν, ἀπὸ τοῦ μυκτῆρος, derived from “nose,” for “to mock.” The nostrils also give an indication of anger, since [Col. 0238A] certain animals betray anger by snorting through their nostrils. Theocritus, in *Thyrsis*:

> Καὶ οἱ ἀεὶ δριμεῖα χολὰ ποτὶ ῤινἱ κάθηται.

“Harsh anger always sits upon his nostrils.” Hence those who take offense are said to wrinkle and contract their nostrils. Horace:

> Lest the dirty napkin  
> Wrinkle the nostrils.

### 135. To act with the nose. Vol. III, *Against Marcion*, chap. 25, no. 178.

Cicero: to wound with a ridiculous and scornful taunt.

### 136. To deride with the nose. Vol. V, *On Modesty*, chap. 6, no. 18.

Blessed Paul, Galatians 6: Θεός οὐ μυκτηρίζεται, that is, [Col. 0238B] “is not mocked,” “is not treated with the nose.” For among the Greeks μύζειν, that is, “to mutter” or “murmur,” signifies scarcely anything other than producing a sound through the nostrils with closed lips, whence μυκτήρ. Now the sound μυγμή is generally associated with mockery; and hence one who acts without the sting of censure is called ἀμύγμων. In Homer, throughout, ἀμύμων (“blameless”). Tertullian adds “of flatterers,” so that he appears to mean the *postica sanna*, a covert sneer—that is, secret and concealed mockery expressed by the gesture of those who laugh at someone behind his back or at the back of his head. Thus the sense is: to thrust flattery upon God.

### 137. An inextricable shipwreck. Vol. II, *On Idolatry*, final chapter, no. 169.

In Greek: ναυάγιον ἄφυκτον, “that which cannot be avoided.” [Col. 0238C] Those who strike against rocks and wreck their ship cannot extricate or free themselves from danger. Virgil says of the Labyrinth, “an inextricable wandering.” See “Submerged.”

### 138. To sail among rocks and bays, through shoals and straits. Ibid., no. 168.

It means to be engaged in peril on either side. For rocks—that is, stony mountains or crags projecting from the sea and sometimes concealed beneath the surface of the water—and bays, curved shores embracing the sea, and shoals, shallower places in the sea, dangerous because of the short breakers, and straits, narrow passages of the sea where the water seems to boil with the tide, are treacherous and dangerous to sailors. Book I, *Against Marcion*: “Two Symplegades of his shipwreck.”

[Col. 0238D]

### 139. Darker than a storm cloud. Vol. III, Book I, *Against Marcion*, chap. 1, no. 14.

See Pamelius’s notes on “Cimmerian darkness,” which amount to the same proverb. A *nubilium* is a denser cloud, or a condensation of exhalations possessing the fertile power of producing water. Hence a misty or cloudy sky. “To spread mists or a cloud over something” means to obscure it. But since Tertullian is alluding to Marcion’s character, the expression will signify sadness and the hidden severity of his disposition, as in the Greek comic poet, διασκεδᾷν τὸ νέφος τοῦ μετώπου, “to dispel the cloud from the brow,” which means to banish sadness and assume a cheerful countenance. Likewise “a cloud of the eyebrow,” ὀφρὺς ξυννεφής, denotes gloom. Horace: “Remove the cloud from your brow.” Virgil:

> But his brow was little cheerful, and his eyes were cast down;

and:

> But black night enfolds his head in gloomy shadow.

[Col. 0239A]

### 140. To betray by a nod. (Unless one prefers “to expose by a nod.”) Vol. I, *On the Pallium*, chap. 4, no. 84.

See “To transfix with one’s gaze” and “To point out with one’s finger.” This is the conduct of persons secretly mocking and condemning something among themselves when it would be neither safe nor proper to do so openly. Since it is permissible neither to do it by fixing one’s eyes upon a person or thing nor by extending one’s fingers, it can at least be indicated covertly and by a nod.

### 111. With closed eyes. Vol. I, *On the Pallium*, chap. 2, no. 20; *Apology*, chap. 3, no. 36; Vol. III, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chap. 51, no. 361.

If you understand the verb “it is evident,” the matter is perfectly clear, clearer than the noonday light, so that anyone, willing or unwilling, may see or understand it. If you understand “to do something,” [Col. 0239B] it means to act abruptly, rashly, and indiscriminately. Thus those who, with closed eyes and blinded by hatred, plunge into hatred of the Christian name as into a shipwreck neither investigate the truth of the matter nor take pains to investigate it, but most unjustly indulge their passions; and, according to Terence:

> They know neither justice nor what is good and equitable.

They see nothing—whether better or worse, whether beneficial—except what pleases them.

Blessed Augustine: “What power, then, except blindness and vanity of mind, compels a man, as the saying goes, with closed eyes, to cast something as though against another, which, as soon as he has cast it against him, immediately returns upon himself?” Blessed Jerome: “In the manner of Andabatae, they brandish the sword in darkness”; and: “It is nevertheless better, as the saying goes, to fight with closed eyes in the manner of Andabatae than not to repel direct missiles with the shield of truth.” [Col. 0239C] And Blessed Basil used νυκτομαχίαν, “a battle by night,” of those who attack something without any judgment.

### 142. With Homeric eyes. Vol. I, *On the Pallium*, chap. 2, no. 20.

See Pamelius’s notes. It is the same as the preceding expression. Homer, previously called Melesigenes, acquired the name το̃υ ὁμήρου (“of Homer”) from his blindness—for he is said to have been deprived of sight from early youth. Hence the universal wonder that a man who had never taken in such things with his eyes could have set before our eyes representations of every aspect of human life.

### 143. To plant boundary stakes. Vol. V, *On Fasting*, chap. 11, no. 72.

A boundary stone, and sometimes a stake, is placed at the limits of fields; [Col. 0239D] as Virgil says of a rock:

> Which happened to lie in the field,  
> Set as a boundary to the land, to settle a dispute between fields.

And in the *Georgics*:

> It was not even lawful to mark or divide  
> The field with a boundary.

That stakes were commonly placed instead of stones or rocks is indicated by the word *Palen*, used by the Flemings for the boundaries of fields. Or Tertullian is alluding to the racecourse or equestrian stadium, where there was a finish at which the racers rested after completing their course, and the turning post was a pointed stake placed at the end of the stadium, around which they wheeled in the race. Thus “to plant boundary stakes” means to confine someone within narrow and fixed limits, so that he can neither attempt nor accomplish anything, nor exercise authority any farther.

[Col. 0240A]

### 144. To press upon one side. Vol. V, *On the Trinity*, chap. 23, no. 102.

This is taken from warfare, where the enemy presses with greater violence and persistence upon the right or left flank, wing, or side, as standards are brought against it from every direction. Once those troops are overthrown and driven from their position, victory becomes easy. Caesar, *Gallic War* II: “Caesar proceeded to the right wing, where he saw that his men were being hard pressed,” etc. The author of *On the Trinity* explains himself with the words “had inclined,” “to draw,” and “to overcome,” which are drawn from military usage.

### 145. More tasteless than a melon. Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chap. 32, no. 387.

Πέπονες are said to be soft and yielding, by a metaphor drawn from ripe fruits; or even from cucumbers, [Col. 0240B] which are called *pepones* because they soften and, as soon as they ripen, become flaccid. Hence the expressions μαλακώτερος πέπονος σύκου, “softer than a ripe fig”; and πεπαίτερος μώρων, “riper than mulberries.” There is also the proverbial banquet, by metaphor, πέπονες, τουτέστιν ἀπαλοὶ καὶ ἀσθενεῖς (“soft ones,” that is, “tender and weak”). And ὧ πέπον, τουτέστι, προσφιλέστατε, ἢ, προσηνέστατε (“O dear one,” that is, “dearest” or “gentlest”) is sometimes a coaxing and friendly form of address, derived from ripe and gentle manners, or because τὰ πέπονα, that is, we all love soft and ripe things.

### 146. To have a melon in place of a heart. Vol. III, Book IV, *Against Marcion*, chap. 40, no. 666.

See Pamelius’s notes at this passage, and Feuardent’s on Blessed Irenaeus, Book I, chap. 3, no. 10: “O melons, blameworthy sophists, and not men,” which is taken from [Col. 0240C] Homer’s *Iliad*, β (“Book II”).

> Ὦ πέπονες, κάκ` ἐλέγχε`, ἀχαΐδες, οὐκ ἔτ` ἀχαιοὶ,

that is:

> O soft ones, vile disgraces, Achaean women—not  
> Achaeans.

Virgil imitated this:

> O truly Phrygian women, for you are not Phrygian men.

Thus the proverb means to be heartless, or to have a tasteless heart. For the principal powers of the mind reside in the heart. Hence those whose heart is rightly disposed and strong are “hearty”; those without heart have one soft, flaccid, and wholly unmanly, like a melon. See above: “To have neither brain nor heart,” and “Deer in battle.”

[Col. 0240D]

### 147. To play a part. Vol. III, Book III, *Against Marcion*, chap. 11, no. 77.

See below: “To run through a scene.” *Persona* means an office or part. Cicero: “I have always played these parts of mildness; I have not sought that role of severity.” It comes from the actor’s mask. “To bear, maintain, sustain, or play a part” derives from dramatic performers.

### 148. Fouler than Physcon. Vol. I, *On the Pallium*, chap. 4, no. 79.

This is said of a man who gorges himself upon every kind of luxury and lust. For Ptolemy Physcon, the eighth king of Egypt, who had ruled at Cyrene, succeeded his deceased brother Euergetes. In addition to bestial lust and cruelty—whose crimes Justin recounts in Book 83—his fattened belly and the deformity of his body and face made him more like a beast than a man. [Col. 0241A] This ugliness was heightened, as if deliberately—or rather through lack of restraint—by the thinness of his transparent garment. From the fact itself he received the infamous name Physcon, as though “pot-bellied” and “paunch-bearer,” because of his devotion to his belly. For φύσκων is ὁ γάστρων ἢ παχὺς, ἀπὸ τῆς φύσκης, meaning the large intestine. Persius also says of such a man:

> His fat paunch projects a foot and a half.

### 149. To meet the blow. Vol. III, *Scorpiace against the Gnostics*, chap. 5, no. 35.

This is taken from gladiators, who either evade an opponent’s thrust by turning the body aside or catch it on a small shield, so that the attempt is frustrated. [Col. 0241B] See above: “To direct a blow against another.” Blessed Jerome, *Against Jovinian*: “We seem, as it were, to yield our position and give the adversary an opportunity to strike.” And: “To beat back direct missiles with the shield of truth.”

### 150. To applaud in one’s bosom. Vol. V, *On Modesty*, chap. 9, no. 42.

“If any examples applaud you in your bosom”: that is, if things occur which are so pleasing to you, and which appear to confirm the opinion formed in your minds, that you consequently feel a silent pleasure within yourselves and indulge your own inclination. In Homer: ἐν θυμώ χαιρεῖν (“to rejoice in one’s heart”). Tibullus: “To rejoice in one’s silent bosom.”

### 151. To applaud a side. Ibid., chap. 16, no. 160.

[Col. 0241C] In Tertullian we read, “to applaud,” or “quietly applaud,” “the opposing side,” as though to vote for and subscribe to another’s opinion. It derives from those who make a noise with their feet or express approval by clapping their hands. Cicero: “No one stamped a foot in applause at that trial.”

### 152. More fortunate than Polycrates. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 11, no. 173.

Polycrates, tyrant of Samos, was fortune’s golden offspring, for whom everything flowed according to his nod and desire. Yet, after a course of life prosperous even to the point of provoking envy, he ended miserably, crucified upon the summit of Mount Mycale. Valerius Maximus, Book VI, chap. 11.

### 153. More exalted than Pompey. Ibid.

You have Pompey’s encomium in Cicero’s speech *For the Manilian Law;* [Col. 0241D] and in Pliny, Book VII, throughout chap. 26, who says that Pompey equaled not only the splendor of Alexander the Great’s achievements, but nearly that of Hercules and Liber as well. The surname “Great,” which he shared with Alexander, also gave him the common title of exalted eminence.

### 154. To remain stuck in the midst of the sea’s surge. Vol. III, Book I, *Against Marcion*, chap. 7, no. 50.

Virgil, Book V:

> He stuck fast upon projecting rocks.

And:

> Struggling upon a reef and amid shallow shoals.

And Book X:

> Struck upon the shoals, while it hangs upon its uneven keel.

These expressions, like Tertullian’s, may be taken proverbially. The contrary will be this passage from the same book:

[Col. 0242A]

> It glides on, cleaving the deep, with wave and tide in its favor.

Now *aestus* is sometimes taken for the very movement of the sea as it rises and falls—that is, its advance or increase, or its withdrawal and ebb. Sometimes it means the unstable sand that engulfs and swallows everything. Virgil:

> The surge rages upon the sands . . . .

And:

> The sands are churned by the surge.

Similar expressions are: “To remain stuck upon the same rocks,” “in the water,” “in the shoal,” “in a rough place.” These metaphors are drawn from sailors who, when their ship has struck a shoal, cannot maintain their intended course. Thus, while a ship is driven this way and that by the ebbing and flowing tide and cannot advance, the proverb signifies that a person cannot extricate or free himself, cannot answer an argument, but falls back into the same position. See “Waves on both sides.”

[Col. 0242B]

### 155. To place upon a precipice. Vol. III, Book IV, *Against Marcion*, chap. 37, no. 607.

*Praeruptum* is used substantivally, like *abruptum*, for anything lofty and precipitous: “precipitous rocks” and “a precipitous mountain,” that is, a high mountain. In Pliny; in Greek, τὸ ἀπόκρημνον, whence “a sloping precipice.” Tertullian explains himself: “Knowing that men would go headlong, he himself placed them upon a precipice.” Thus it means to expose someone to an evident danger and fail to take sufficient precautions lest some harm occur, as if the administration of money were entrusted to an avaricious man and a thief, or the chastity of virgins to a lustful youth.

### 156. The unutterable deep. Vol. II, *On Idolatry*, final chapter, no. 169.

Those who are cast from a ship and sink into the depths of the sea [Col. 0242C] scarcely ever escape by swimming from the unavoidable danger. See “Submerged” and “Shipwreck.” The contrary will be Virgil’s expression in Book V:

> Scarcely at last was he restored from the lowest depth;

and in Book VI:

> I swam toward land and was already reaching safety.

### 157. To bring to the rules. Vol. III, Book I, *Against Marcion*, chap. 22, no. 153.

A rule, otherwise called a straightedge, is a craftsman’s instrument by which builders test the length of a structure to see whether it corresponds properly; and Tertullian uses this craftsman’s term for examining God’s goodness. Hence the expressions “straighter than a rule,” “more exact than a measuring-line,” “according to the plumb line and measuring-rule”: to weigh a matter carefully, [Col. 0242D] so that it may be treated perfectly in all its parts and proportions.

### 158. More effeminate than Sardanapalus. Vol. I, *On the Pallium*, chap. 4, no. 79.

Sardanapalus, the last king of the Assyrians, was “a man more corrupt than a woman,” as Justin says in Book I. He was found amid throngs of prostitutes, spinning purple wool upon a distaff and dressed as a woman; and in softness of body and wantonness of eyes he surpassed all women, while distributing their allotted wool among the maidens. Thus, because of his extraordinary effeminacy, his name passed into a proverb among Greeks and Latins.

### 159. To run through and dance out a scene. Vol. III, Book III, *Against Marcion*, chap. 11, no. 7.

The stage is properly the front of the theater, namely the actors’ place, enclosed by curtains, from which the actors came forth upon the forestage to perform. [Col. 0243A] It is frequently used for the play itself, a tragedy or comedy. Virgil:

> Orestes driven mad upon the stage;

that is, celebrated and frequently performed in tragedies. Horace:

> Which does not run over the boards in the loose comic slipper.

“The boards” stands for the stage; that is, “what disorderly and poorly constructed comedies he writes.” Thus “to run through a scene” will mean to complete the whole of the matter undertaken. Cicero: “The points concerning the art that were briefly run through by you.” In Tertullian, “to run through the scene of the phantom” means to prove wholly and conclusively that Christ’s flesh was merely a phantom, and to persist in proving it. Thus Caelius to Cicero, [Col. 0243B] Book VIII: “The scene of the entire affair,” that is, that upon which the whole affair turns and in which it must be brought to completion.

In *Against the Valentinians*, Tertullian calls virtue a “leaping rider,” that is, unstable, insecure, and mobile; and hence he also speaks of “leaping-rider men,” properly borrowing the expression from riders who leap from horse to horse. He likewise uses it in its proper sense, as he does “to run through,” so that “to dance out” means to represent something by mimetic dancing. For in the theater, the orchestra belongs properly to the chorus, just as the stage belongs to the actors, and on it mimes were accustomed to exhibit their performances. ὀρχηστὺς means the art of dancing and the dance itself; thus with each word he alludes to both groups, stage actors and mimes, and draws different metaphors from them.

### 160. To plunge in the scalpel. Vol. I, *On the Pallium*, chap. 11, no. 173.

[Col. 0243C] *Scalpellus*, or *scalpellum*, is a surgical knife, φλεβότομον. I do not know whether *scalpella* is also used, as *pistilla* is in Plautus for *pistillum*; in common speech, “lancet,” a term taken from surgeons. For by the word “bitterness,” an abundance of choleric humor in Vedius seems to be signified. Cicero, *For Sestius*: “It is no cure when a scalpel is applied to a healthy part of the body.” Surgeons commonly plunge in a scalpel to open a vein and draw out an excessive humor. The proverb will be used of the medicine for diseases of the mind, as will what follows in Tertullian: “To administer a cathartic.”

### 161. More just and more soldierly than Scipio. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 11, no. 173.

[Col. 0243D] Pliny, Book VII, no. 27: “Cato was considered the first to have displayed the three highest qualities in a single person: the finest orator, commander, and senator. All these qualities, even if they did not appear earlier, nevertheless seem to me to have shone more brightly in Scipio Aemilianus.” His praise also appears in Cicero’s *On Friendship*. Justice and goodness are commended in the other Scipio, Nasica. Nevertheless, in the same book of Cicero, Laelius attributes devotion both to justice and to military science to the younger Scipio, or Aemilianus. The elder, or Africanus, also excelled in both grounds of praise.

### 162. More savage than a Scythian. Vol. III, Book I, *Against Marcion*, chap. 1, no. 11.

See Pamelius’s notes. In every age, and in the judgment of nearly every nation and author, the Scythian people were born more for war and savagery than for any pursuit of civilized life.

[Col. 0244A]

### 163. To cut down and lop a forest. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 4, nos. 50 and 51; and *Prescription against Heretics*, chap. 37, no. 222.

“By what right do you cut down my forest?”—an expression that transfers everything from civil law; that is: “Why do you invade my possession? Why do you put your sickle into my harvest?” In the *Apology*: “You lop and cut down that entire old and squalid forest of laws with the new axes of imperial rescripts and edicts.” Among the Latins, as among the Greeks with ὕλη, “forest” is used metaphorically for abundance and material. Cicero: “All fertility and, as it were, a forest of eloquence”; and “a forest of facts and thoughts.” This proverb, however, is taken from trees, which are either pruned so that they do not grow wild with shoots and spread excessively in every direction, or are cut out by the roots together with the trunk.

[Col. 0244B]

### 164. To cut down, uproot, and extirpate a forest. Vol. V, *On Modesty*, chap. 16, no. 135.

“How greatly he cuts down, uproots, and extirpates the entire forest of lusts with the axe.” Tertullian adds: “So that he permits nothing to sprout again from the stump.” It means to abolish something utterly, and is the same as the preceding expression. For the trunk is the stem itself stripped of branches and leaves; the stock retains its foliage. Thus “to lop the trunk” and “to extirpate the stock” are the same.

### 165. To stir a vast forest. Vol. V, *On the Trinity*, chap. 16, no. 81.

“To stir a forest of Scriptures concerning something”: to examine many things, draw arguments from every direction, and range over a broad field.

[Col. 0244C]

### 166. What a forest! Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chap. 2, no. 30.

What force! What abundance! What ample and widely extending material!

### 167. Wiser than Socrates. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 11, no. 137.

Socrates is reported to have been judged the wisest and preferred to all others, not merely by human estimation but by the oracle of Pythian Apollo. Pliny, Book VII, chap. 31; Cicero, *On Friendship*.

### 268. The combs of the sun. Vol. III, *Against the Valentinians*, chap. 3, no. 24.

On combs, with which we part or [Col. 0244D] comb our hair, the teeth are called dense or sparse “rays,” from their resemblance to the rays of the sun; and by a reciprocal metaphor, “combs of the sun,” because the sun sends forth rays in every direction, separated like the notches of a comb. Hence in Lucretius, “the missiles of day,” meaning rays projected like weapons; and ἐκήβολος Ἀπόλλων, “Sun.” But since these words, like “the towers of the Lamiae,” signify things used to frighten children, perhaps nurses commonly used these words either to suppress childish wailing or to break the obstinate resistance of children who would not allow their heads to be combed, by ordering the combs of the sun, like scrapers, to be brought to them.

### 169. Written by a ray of the sun. Vol. III, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chap. 47, no. 341.

This means something evident and manifest. For things most firmly established and most fully acknowledged we call “clearer than the sun.” [Col. 0245A] Hence the expressions “To be blind in the sunlight” and “To speak against the sun.” *On Modesty*, chap. 7: “For investigating these matters, there is need not of a lamp’s narrow shaft of light, but of the spear of the whole sun.”

### 170. To stand on solid ground. Vol. V, *On Modesty*, chap. 10, no. 83.

To be secure, well established, and fortified. Tertullian opposes to it “To advance with wavering step,” “The whole matter depends upon one’s footing,” and “To be shaken from one’s course.” Similar expressions are: “To be supported by two anchors”; “To stand in the shallows.” Virgil, Book II:

> Fortune, repeatedly returning with alternating changes,  
> Mocked many and restored them once more to solid ground.

### 171. To correspond to the mirror. Ibid., chap. 8, no. 64; Book IV, *Against Marcion*, chap. 31, no. 511.

[Col. 0245B] He seems to have used “with reference to the mirror of the parable” for the anagogical sense, or anagoge itself: the mystical and hidden sense of Scriptures, parables, and oracles, which lies deeper within and, as it were, beneath the bark. Thus “to correspond to the mirror” will mean—as in craftsmanship, “to correspond to the rule, measuring-line, and plumb line”—that everything proposed in the parable can be fitted to the things signified by it. Or, as Tertullian puts it, that “the examples correspond exactly.”

### 172. Marcion’s sponge. Vol. III, Book V, *Against Marcion*, chap. 4, no. 43.

Painters use a sponge for removing color, as is clear from Pliny, Book XXXV, chap. 10, *On Painters*, in his account of Protogenes: “He had wiped it away repeatedly and changed his brush; finally he dashed the sponge against the objectionable part of the panel.” Ausonius, in one of his letters: “Once the rouge of your correction has been added, may you dash on the sponge that will complete the unfinished representation of a badly foaming horse.” [Col. 0245C] A sponge was also used for wiping clean the counters of money-changers, especially counting boards, upon which they wrote and made corrections by drawing a sponge across them; and upon palimpsests, that is, erasable tablets or parchments upon which we commonly write with a writing stylus. Hence Augustus, speaking of the tragedy *Ajax*, which he had written and then erased, used to say that Ajax had “fallen upon the sponge,” after the example of Ajax, who had fallen upon his sword. Aristides, in one of his speeches, says οὐδὲν δεῖ τῆς Στησιχόρου παλινωδίας, ἀλλὰ σπόγγος ἰάσεται: there would be no need of Stesichorus’s palinode or recantation; rather, the sponge would provide the remedy.

Thus, since Marcion erased whichever books of Holy Scripture he pleased [Col. 0245D]—as was explained above under “To leap across a vast gulf,” and as Tertullian immediately adds here, “what he removed, what he preserved”—Tertullian called that audacity in expunging books and cutting them out with censorious rashness “Marcion’s sponge.”

### 173. A petty thief seeks the dole-basket. Vol. III, Book III, *Against Marcion*, chap. 16, no. 111.

The *sportula*, named after the little baskets of woven rushes that served as containers for money, was a gift—or rather an allowance for food—which wealthy Romans gave to those who attended their morning salutations, while they themselves dined in private. The *recta cena*, a proper dinner, was opposed to it: the more courteous would offer their clients and morning callers a formal banquet. Originally the dole-basket was given only to the poor, but afterward even the rich began [Col. 0246A] to seek and demand it. A man who, though neither a client nor a regular morning caller, thrust himself upon another person and, though unknown, tried to receive the dole-basket is here called a petty thief. The expression applies to one who falsely claims something for himself, as Marcion’s god claims the name of Christ.

### 174. A man sees a speck in another’s eye more easily than a beam in his own. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 39, no. 510.

An evangelical proverb, Matthew 5 and Luke 6, censuring the self-love of those who take offense at even the smallest faults of others but flatter themselves despite their own very great faults. κάρφος; Tertullian’s “speck” is *festuca* in the translator of the common edition. Horace:

> One who asks his friend not to take offense  
> At his own tumors ought fairly to pardon the other’s warts.

And another proverb: “Criticizing another’s pimples while covered with ulcers.”

[Col. 0246B]

### 175. Beneath the fold and the tunic. Vol. V, *On Flight in Persecution*, chap. 12, no. 89.

Tertullian adds “as though by stealth” and opposes it to “before the whole world.” Whatever is done not openly but treacherously and secretly, with witnesses removed and without another’s knowledge, is said to be done “beneath the fold.” Thus “to bring or turn something into one’s fold” means to apply it to one’s private advantage. In Pliny, Scaurus is called “the fold of provincial plunder,” because he turned everything into the abyss of his own avarice. “To rejoice in one’s bosom” means not to publish one’s joy abroad or make it public.

### 176. More fortunate than Sulla. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 11, no. 173.

An omen of good fortune was given to this Sulla by lightning, as Pliny says in Book II, chap. 54. “Alone among men down to this age,” says the same Pliny, Book VII, chap. 44, “he claimed for himself the surname ‘Fortunate.’” Pliny refutes in the same passage this rashness combined with folly.

### 177. Is glass worth as much as a pearl? Vol. II, *Against Marcion*, chap. 4, no. 58.

This is read not only in Tertullian but also in Blessed Jerome. The saying signifies the greatest possible inequality between things. Pearls, otherwise called “unions,” have the highest value among gems. Glass is exceedingly cheap, although it can counterfeit a pearl and deceive the inexperienced. Tertullian explains himself and compares these things with one another: earthly glory and heavenly glory; the reward of human praise and divine recompense.

[Col. 0246D]

### 178. Time reveals all things. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 7, no. 107.

Seneca: “The day discloses truth.” Horace:

> Whatever lies beneath the earth, time will bring into the light;  
> It will bury and conceal what now shines.

χρόνος πάντων βασανιστής, “Time tests all things.” And in Sophocles χρόνος (“Time”) is said πάνθ` ὁρῶν καὶ ἀκούων καὶ πὰντ` ἀναπτύσσειν, “to see and hear all things,” and even to bring them into the open. Hence Truth is aptly called the daughter of Time, because, after remaining hidden for a while, she emerges into the light as time advances. Hence the expression Ἄγει δὲ πρὸς φῶς τὴν ἀλήθειαν χρόνος: “But time draws truth forth into the light.”

[Col. 0247A]

### 179. Sons of the earth. Ibid., chap. 10, no. 162.

See Pamelius’s notes. “The common people,” says Tertullian, “call those whose origin is uncertain ‘sons of the earth,’” γηγενεῖς (“earth-born”); hence the Giants, as though born from the earth. In Juvenal, “a little brother of the Giants” means an unknown person. Persius: “Offspring of the earth.” See above: “He came down from heaven.”

### 180. To call down heavy earth upon someone. Vol. I, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, chap. 4, no. 21.

The ancients were accustomed—as is evident from nearly all the Latin poets—to call down heavy earth upon the shades of a person whose memory, as Tertullian says, they recalled with the sting of some offense and wished to curse; but for the bones and ashes of one to whom they owed gratitude, they prayed for relief. Especially in the month of February, for twelve consecutive days, the people performed purifications and expiatory rites to obtain repose for the shades of the dead. Hence these expressions:

[Col. 0247B]

> May the bones lie softly . . . . .  
> And may earth press lightly upon the ashes.

> I pray that your bones rest peacefully and safely in the urn,  
> And that the soil lie lightly upon your ashes.

### 181. Thales into the well. Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chap. 6, no. 89.

See this proverb discussed by Plato in the *Theaetetus*. It concerns those who do not see what lies before their feet. Thales, one of the seven sages of Greece and the foremost among them, fell into a pit while contemplating the heavens too intently. Hence he was mocked by an old woman, or by a maidservant.

[Col. 0247C]

### 182. More exalted than Themistocles. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 2, no. 173.

Tertullian calls him “the military man.” Hence it seems that one should read “more soldierly,” or “more skilled in warfare or the military art.” His glory first shone forth when he was a young man in the Persian War, at the battle of Marathon, under the command of Miltiades. Then, some years later, as commander of the Athenians, he fought Xerxes’ naval forces at Salamis with memorable distinction.

### 183. To audit a title. Vol. V, *On Monogamy*, chap. 8, no. 55.

Among the jurists, “to audit accounts” means to examine a reckoning, as we commonly say; or, as Papinian describes it, to compare receipts and expenditures. Elsewhere, “to inquire, examine, and set out accounts” is used for “to audit.” [Col. 0247D] “To expunge” is different: it means to surround something with dots and remove it, and, as though the matter were completed, take it out of the register.

Tertullian employs a metaphor derived from accountants and from censors, who enter in their tablets the names and titles of those who register themselves. If one chooses to use the expression proverbially, it will mean to weigh an account or a case, whether it is just or otherwise; or to judge whether someone is fit to discharge some office and sufficiently equipped with the appropriate qualifications. In Book IV of *Against Marcion*, he calls the Creator “the judge and auditor of merits”; in Book V, he speaks of “the auditing of good and evil work.” In *On Patience*: “Whenever any injury encounters patience, it will be audited with the same outcome as a weapon hurled against a rock and blunted”—that is, it will vanish and the attempt [Col. 0248A] will be futile, just as things erased or “audited” vanish, where “audited” is used for “expunged.”

### 184. To engrave a title. Vol. III, Book I, *Against Marcion*, chap. 9, no. 57.

“To engrave” means to record something upon monuments of achievements. Cicero: “To engrave an encomium” (other texts, and Muretus especially, read a variant likewise printed here as “encomium” [reading uncertain]) upon a tomb, bronze, or marble. Pliny, Book VII, chap. 26, in his account of Pompey the Great, records the inscribed titles of his victories and triumphs. Tertullian alluded to this custom, so that the expression means to honor or disgrace someone with a public designation, and to render him renowned by an encomium.

### 185. More eloquent than Tully. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 11, no. 17.

You have Marcus Tullius’s encomium in Pliny, Book VII, no. 30, [Col. 0248B] where he calls him the father of eloquence and Latin letters. Quintilian, Book IX of the *Institutes*, says that the fullness of Plato, the force of Demosthenes, and the charm of Isocrates had been poured into him.

### 186. To repel vanity with vanity. Vol. III, Book IV, *Against Marcion*, chap. 30, no. 496.

It means to outwit artifice with artifice, a lie with a lie; to break down and refute a frivolous argument irrelevant to the matter by one equally frivolous and irrelevant. Very similar expressions are: “To play the fox with a fox”; “To play the Cretan with a Cretan.”

### 187. To draw forth veins. Vol. V, *On the Trinity*, Book I, chap. 2, no. 131.

This metaphor is taken from springs, from which streams and, as it were, veins of water are drawn. Hence the expressions “with a rich vein” or “with a slender vein,” [Col. 0248C] because some springs flow through a narrower channel and others through a greater one, so as to form a river. Or it may derive from metals, whose veins differ sometimes in abundance as well as in kind. Quintilian: “They flow in a fuller channel.” Poets are also said to have drawn veins from Homer as from the fountain of poetry.

### 188. The same road upward and downward. Vol. III, Book II, *Against Marcion*, chap. 28, no. 195.

Tertullian appears to transfer this proverb, used by Heraclitus, to his own method of argumentation. For the expressions “above” and “beneath himself” have the same tendency. These concern dignity, while “upward” and “downward” concern place. For this truly is to construct rival antitheses against Marcion, consisting not only in propositions—“if he was ignorant, he did not know at all”—but also in the words [Col. 0248D] I have cited. Heraclitus perhaps used this obscure saying in teachings concerning the soul, since “upward” and “downward,” in respect of place, belong to the class of things called πρὸς τὶ, or relative terms. Cicero: “To be carried upward in straight lines, and downward along the line.” And thus Tertullian proves his argument, that is, by Heraclitus’s axiom.

### 189. By the living voice. Vol. III, *Prescription against Heretics*, chap. 21, no. 128.

This is immediately opposed to “through letters,” with “by preaching” understood. The living voice is, as it were, lively and effective, received from the very mouth of the speaker; in it native and genuine grace shines forth, and delivery with movement—which is the life of speech—abides. All these things are absent from writing, [Col. 0249A] which only imitates the true and living voice. Quintilian: “Delivery is, as it were, the life of speech; the discovery of material is like the bones; order imitates the sinews; expression represents the flesh, skin, and color; memory serves in place of the spirit. To all these is added pronunciation, like a certain vital motion.” For there is no surer proof of life than motion; hence rivers are called “living” from their motion. Blessed Jerome, in the preface to *Genesis*: “The living voice possesses some hidden energy—I know not what.” Tertullian opposes to it, as I said, “to preach through a letter.” Others speak of “mute teachers,” meaning the writings of those who committed monuments of learned genius to letters for posterity, as though devoid of voice and life.

[Col. 0250A]

### 190. To color a shadow without light. Vol. III, *Against Hermogenes*, chap. 2, no. 12.

This is drawn from the art of painting, which Hermogenes had practiced. “The beginnings of painting,” says Pliny, “consisted in tracing lines around a man’s shadow,” a method called linear drawing; the second stage used single colors and was called monochrome. Thus the first form was without any color, so much so that it became customary to inscribe the names of those whom they painted. Later the lines began to be colored with ground potsherd. The equivalent of this kind of painting in architecture is ὶχνογραφία (“ichnography”), a flat representation and superficial delineation of a site, exhibiting a model of the future work. The expression may be transferred to the obscurity of a discourse, argument, or demonstrated matter.
