[Col. 0319D] I. In preparing to write this third book against Marcion, **ON CHRIST**, he first lays down that Christ must be understood as belonging to no other God than the Creator: both because it was established in the preceding books that no God other than the Creator is to be believed in, and because a review of the apostolic and heretical churches proves that no discussion of another God or another Christ arose before the scandal caused by Marcion.

II. He then argues that Marcion’s Christ is suspect because he came so suddenly, before the Father bore witness concerning the Son—before the sender testified concerning the one sent.

III. Nor is it sufficient that he was supposedly going to prove himself the Son of God by demonstrations of miraculous powers: both because Christ himself shows that faith based on signs is rash, since such signs are very readily found even among false prophets, and because the Creator had already [Col. 0320D] worked miracles before him through his servants.

IV. But if Marcion’s Christ was revealed after the Creator in order to correct him, he ought to have been revealed not before but after the Creator’s Christ, so that he might correct him as well.

V. Now, since he is going to prove from the Scriptures that Christ belonged to the Creator, whose Scriptures were fulfilled by him, he begins by stating that his opponents must recognize two features of prophetic expression: one by which future events are sometimes announced as though they had already occurred, and another by which many things are foretold figuratively.

VI. Thus the Jewish error does not support the Marcionites’ case, on the grounds that the Jews killed the Creator’s Christ as though he were a stranger; for Scripture [Col. 0321A] had foretold that they would fail to recognize him—and this insofar as they regarded him as merely a man.

VII. Both groups, however, are refuted by the two advents of Christ, which the prophets indicated through his twofold condition; they could neither understand nor believe the first, which was more obscure and less honorable.

VIII. He next defends, against the Marcionites and the other authors of their errors—whom the apostle John pronounced antichrists—that Christ truly came in the flesh. Otherwise, among other things, the fruit of the Christian name, Christ’s death, and the resurrection would also have to be denied.

IX. Nor do the Creator’s angels support their position on the pretext that, when they dealt with Abraham and Lot, they acted in the phantasm of an apparent body, yet truly met with them and ate; for those angels were not destined to die for us.

[Col. 0321B] X. But if Christ spurned flesh as something unworthy, he ought also to have disdained its image, since resemblance follows the actual condition of a thing.

XI. And Marcion was wrong to assert that Christ had only an apparent corporeality so as not to be compelled to acknowledge his birth; for he could also have assigned him an apparent birth. Yet neither position can stand, since truth is more fitting for God than falsehood.

XII. Nor does it count against the Creator’s Christ that Isaiah writes that he was to be called Emmanuel, although he was not given that name; for it is enough that Christ was “God with us” in meaning, not in the sound of his name.

XIII. Nor do “the might of Damascus,” “the spoils of Samaria,” and “the king of the Assyrians,” which Isaiah applies [Col. 0321C] to Christ, portend that he would be a warrior; rather, they figuratively represent the eastern Magi presenting Christ in his infancy with gold and incense.

XIV. For elsewhere also—namely, in the Psalms and in John’s Apocalypse—weapons of war are figuratively ascribed to Christ.

XV. He next continues by arguing that the name “Christ” does not belong to Marcion’s Christ: both because it is the proper designation of one person alone, while the Creator’s Christ nevertheless possessed that name; and because one who had no body could not have been anointed, and therefore could in no way be called “Christ,” that is, “Anointed.”

XVI. Much less does the name Jesus belong to him—a Jesus whom the Jews do not await, and whose name belongs to the Creator’s Christ—because another God’s Christ ought to have nothing in common with the Creator’s Christ.

[Col. 0321D] XVII. He then cites, in almost the same words, the same testimonies of Scripture concerning Christ’s contemptible condition during his Passion that he cited above in the book *Against the Jews*.

XVIII. Likewise, from the Pentateuch, concerning his departure through the Passion of the cross.

XIX. Likewise, from the Psalms and the Prophets.

XX. Finally, he proves that the Creator’s Christ was truly the Christ because all the nations have turned to him [Col. 0322A] in accordance with David’s prediction—a prediction that cannot be understood as referring either to David himself or to his son Solomon.

XXI. For the calling not only of the Jews but also of the nations to the Creator’s Christ was foretold in numerous scriptural passages.

XXII. Indeed, it was prophesied that the apostles would be preachers not of another God but of the same God, and that both they themselves and all the faithful would suffer the same things as Christ.

XXIII. Yet because Marcion, together with the Jews, denied that Christ had come, he shows that their end—or destruction—which was foretold as something they would incur because of their impiety toward Christ, has already been fulfilled. This would have happened without cause if they suffered it on account of another God’s Christ, who had been driven to the cross by that God’s rivals, namely, the Creator’s powers; for the Creator could not have exacted vengeance unless the Christ was his own.

XXIV. Finally, taking occasion from Marcion’s claim that Judaea would be restored by his god, he refers the reader to **BOOK IV AGAINST MARCION**, concerning Abraham’s bosom, and to the **WORK ON THE HOPE OF THE FAITHFUL**, concerning the promised restoration of Judaea. Here too he treats at length the millenarians’ dream, concerning which there is a fuller discussion among the Author’s paradoxes. Tertullian is preparing to write against Marcion’s work, composed through the opposition of contrarieties and entitled *Antitheses*, which was designed to enforce a separation between the Law and the Gospel, whereby he divided two different Gods, one belonging to the Old Testament and the other to the New.

**Chapter I.** While first of all not denying the diversity of commandments and disciplines, he demonstrates that they belong to one and the same God, because God the Creator himself, through [Col. 0322C] his prophets, foretold that another and new law—namely, that of the Gospel—would arise in place of the Old, and an eternal covenant in place of a temporary one.

# CHAPTER I

Following the tracks of my former work, which, though lost, we persist in reconstructing, the orderly course of my argument now brings me to Christ—even if this treatment is superabundant after the completion of the defense of the one divine nature.

> **Note:** [Col. 0321D] “For the matter has been sufficiently prejudged.” In the preceding books it has been demonstrated plainly enough that Christ was sent by God the Creator, and was not a Christ belonging to another god than the Creator, as [Col. 0322D] Marcion dreamed. **LE PR.**

For the matter has been sufficiently prejudged: Christ must be understood as belonging to no other God than the Creator, since it has been established that no God other than the Creator is to be believed—the Creator whom Christ so emphatically preached. Thereafter, the apostles announced the Christ of no other God than the God whom Christ preached, that is, the Creator. Thus no mention of another God, and consequently none of another Christ, was ever discussed before the scandal caused by Marcion. [Col. 0322D]

This is most easily proved by a review of the apostolic and heretical churches: the corruption of the rule must plainly be assigned to the place where later origin is found—a point that I also incorporated into my first little book. But this present engagement, which is to examine [Note: “separately” in Semler and Pamel.] Christ as a separate matter, will also have this effect: while we prove that Christ belongs to the Creator, Marcion’s god will thereby be excluded as well.

Truth ought to employ all its powers,

> **Note:** [Col. 0322D] “Not as though it were struggling.” Truth struggles when it is either oppressed by falsehood or covered over with the appearance of truth. It also happens [Col. 0323D] not infrequently that speaking the truth is dangerous because of the violence and lack of self-control of certain people. Thucydides, *Histories* I, spoke in this sense when he said, ἀταλαίπωρος ἡ ζήτησις τῆς ἀληθείας, that is, “the investigation of truth is free from toil.” **LE PR. and EDD.**

not as though it were struggling. [Col. 0323A] In any case, it triumphs through the concise force of preliminary objections. But it has been decided that truth, eager for battle, should encounter its adversary everywhere—an adversary so far gone in frenzy that he more readily assumed the coming of a Christ who had never been announced than of the one who had always been proclaimed.

# CHAPTER II

From here, then, I proceed to the question

> **Note:** “I compare” in the Paris, Franeker, and Pamel editions.

whether he ought to have come so suddenly. First, because he was also his God’s Son; for the proper order was

> **Note:** “would have been” in Semler.

that the Father should declare the Son before the Son declared the Father, and that the Father should testify concerning the Son before the Son testified concerning the Father. Next, because he was also “sent,” in addition to bearing the name of Son.

> **Note:** “By which” in Semler and Pamel.

Accordingly, the authorization of the sender ought to have preceded as testimony on behalf of the one sent. For no one who comes on another’s authority himself defends that authority by his own assertion; rather, he expects himself to be defended [Col. 0323B] by that authority,

> **Note:** “the defense” in Semler; “by the defense” in Pamel.

with an advance declaration from the one who grants the authority preceding him. Otherwise, one will not acknowledge as Son someone whom the Father never named, nor believe that someone was sent whom no commissioning authority ever designated.

> **Note:** [Col. 0323D] “The commissioning authority designated.” The *mandator* is the person who entrusts something to be done; the *mandatarius* is the person who undertakes it. **RHEN.**

The Father would have named him, and the commissioning authority would have designated him, if he had been the Son and the one sent. Everything that has departed from the rule will be regarded with suspicion. The primary order of things does not permit the Father to be acknowledged after the Son, the commissioner after the commission, or Christ after God. In recognition, nothing precedes its own origin, because nothing precedes it in the ordering either.

Suddenly a Son, suddenly one sent, and suddenly a Christ? Yet I would suppose that nothing from God is sudden, because nothing from God is unordered. But if it was ordered, why was it not also proclaimed beforehand, so that it might be proved both ordered by its proclamation and divine by its ordering? Indeed, so great [Col. 0323C] an undertaking—one that was plainly being prepared for human salvation—would not have been sudden, if only because it was to achieve its benefit through faith. For insofar as it had to be believed in order to be beneficial, to that same extent it required preparation in order to be believed, built upon the foundations of ordering and proclamation. Once faith had been formed by this sequence, it could rightly both be commanded to the human being by God and be rendered to God by the human being; it was bound to believe on the basis of recognition, because it was capable of doing so, having learned what to believe from proclamation.

# CHAPTER III

You say that no such order was necessary, because he would immediately prove himself both Son, envoy, and God’s Christ by the realities themselves [Col. 0324A], through demonstrations of miraculous powers. But I shall deny that this form of testimony alone was appropriate to him, since he himself afterward invalidated it. For in declaring that many would come (Matthew 24:24),

> **Note:** [Col. 0323D] “For in declaring that many would come.” He paraphrastically cites Matthew 24: “Many false Christs will come,” etc. So also blessed Augustine, *Against Faustus* XIII.5: “You perform no miracles; although, even if you did, we would be on our guard, since the Lord instructs us [Col. 0324D] and says, ‘Many will arise,’” etc. And *Tractates on John* 14: “Pontius may perform a miracle, and Donatus may pray, and God may answer from heaven: first, they are either deceived or deceivers; finally, grant that they move mountains—if they do not have charity, they are nothing. But how could those who divided unity possess it?” Yet it is clear from *On the Soul* 57 and *Against Marcion* V.16 that the Author did not therefore think that the truth of signs and miraculous powers ever existed, or would ever exist, among heretics. **PAM.**

and that they would perform signs and produce great miracles, even leading the elect astray,

> **Note:** [Col. 0324D] “Even leading the elect astray.” Although I am not superstitious about variant readings, I would nevertheless judge that “the overthrow of the elect” should be read. **LE PR.**

yet nevertheless must not be accepted on that account, he showed that faith in signs and miracles is rash, since they are very readily found even among false Christs.

> **Note:** “Most readily found,” singular, in Franeker and Pamel.

Or what sense does it make if he wished himself to be proved, understood, and received on the very basis—I mean miraculous powers—on which he did not wish the others to be received, although they too were to come just as suddenly and had been proclaimed by no author? If

> **Note:** “But” in Semler and Pamel.

because he came before them and first authenticated demonstrations of power, he therefore seized faith as though it were a place in the public baths, anticipating and appropriating it before all later claimants, take care lest he himself be found in the position of those who came later. [Col. 0324B] For he is found to be later than the Creator, who had already been known beforehand and had accordingly worked miracles beforehand, and had explicitly

> **Note:** “By name” in Latinius.

declared that no others coming after him were to be believed.

Therefore, if coming first and speaking beforehand about later claimants closes the case for credibility,

> **Note:** [Col. 0324D] “Will elicit” in Latinius; “will elude” in others.

then he too will already stand condemned because he is recognized as later; and the authority to establish this rule concerning later claimants will belong to the Creator alone, who could not himself be later than anyone.

Now, since I am about to prove that the Creator formerly produced through his servants the same miracles that you claim as the sole basis for believing your Christ, and that at other times he appointed them to be produced through his own Christ, I may rightly lay down this further preliminary objection: Christ was all the less to be believed on the sole basis of miracles because these miracles, corresponding both to those of the Creator performed through his servants and to those promised for his Christ, could have been interpreted as belonging to no one other than the Creator. [Col. 0324C]

> **Note:** [Col. 0324D] For “corresponding,” Semler reads “for those corresponding,” and Pamel reads “the one corresponding.”

Even if other demonstrations were found in your Christ—new ones, of course—we would more readily believe that the new belonged to the same one to whom the old belonged than to one who possessed only new things, things in need of the proofs afforded by triumphant antiquity. Thus, on this ground too, he ought to have come after being proclaimed: both his own prophecies and his miraculous powers would then establish faith in him. This was especially necessary if he was to come in opposition to the Creator’s Christ, who was fortified by his own signs and prophets,

> **Note:** “Prophecies” in other editions.

so that, as a rival Christ, he might shine forth through every kind of distinction. But how could the Christ of a God who had never been proclaimed [Col. 0325A] himself be proclaimed? This, then, is what requires that neither your god nor your Christ be believed: God ought not to have remained unknown, and Christ ought to have been recognized through God.

# CHAPTER IV

He disdained, I suppose, to imitate the order of our God, as though it displeased him and were precisely what he intended to refute.

> **Note:** “And” in Latinius.

Being new, he wished to come in a new way: the Son before the declaration of the Father, and the envoy before the authority of the commissioner, so that he might introduce that most monstrous faith

> **Note:** “He himself” in Semler and Pamel.

by which it would be believed that Christ had come before it was known that he had existed.

I am also entitled to reconsider why he did not come after Christ. For when I see his lord patiently enduring for so great an age the most bitter Creator, who all the while was announcing his own Christ to humanity—whatever his reason for doing so—and postponing both his revelation and his intervention, I say that, for the same reason, he ought to have extended that patience to the Creator while the latter also carried out his plans in his own Christ. [Col. 0325B] Then, after every operation of the rival God and the rival Christ had been perfected and completed, he might at last have superimposed his own arrangements.

Otherwise, repentance for such great patience caused him not to persevere until the Creator’s course of events reached its end. Did he vainly endure the proclamation of a Christ whom he did not endure to be manifested? Or did he refrain from cutting the Creator off for so long without cause? What restrained him, or what disturbed him? Yet he failed in both respects: he was revealed so late after the Creator, but so precipitately before the Creator’s Christ. He ought long ago to have exposed the one, but not yet the other; he ought not to have endured the one raging for so long, yet ought not to have troubled the other while he was still at rest. [Col. 0325C] With respect to both, he fell away from the title of the Best God, being surely changeable and uncertain himself: lukewarm toward the Creator, hot against Christ, and ineffectual in both cases.

> **Note:** “In each case” in other editions.

For he no more restrained the Creator than he obstructed Christ. The Creator remains entirely what he is, and Christ will come just as he is described. Why did he come after the Creator, whom he was unable to correct? Why was he revealed before the Creator’s Christ, whom he could not recall?

Or, if he corrected the Creator, he was revealed after him so that the things requiring correction might come first. Therefore, if he was likewise going to correct the Creator’s Christ, he ought to have waited for him too, so as to become his later corrector just as he had become the Creator’s. It would be another matter if he himself were to come again after that Christ, so that in his first advent he came forth against the Creator, destroying his Law and [Col. 0325D] Prophets, but in the second he would come forth [Col. 0326A] against Christ, refuting his kingdom. Then, therefore, he would complete his orderly course; then perhaps he should be believed. But if his undertaking has already been completed from this point onward, he will therefore come in vain, since he will accomplish nothing.

# CHAPTER V

I offer these remarks as a preliminary skirmish,

> **Note:** “I have made a preliminary skirmish” in Rigault, Junius, and the Venice edition.

as though I were still at the first stage and still fighting from a distance. But now, as I prepare to fight from here at a fixed position and at close quarters,

> **Note:** [Col. 0325D] “To fight at a fixed position and at close quarters.” It is as though the preceding arguments had been conducted in the manner of a light skirmish; here he promises that he will fight more vigorously, so as to press his adversary under any wager. He therefore appeals to Scripture, which he turns against Marcion. **LE PR.**

I see that certain battle lines must first be drawn, according to which the combat is to be conducted—namely, the Creator’s Scriptures. Since I am going to prove from them that Christ belonged to the Creator, inasmuch as they were afterward

> **Note:** “After” in Junius.

fulfilled in his Christ, I must first set forth the form of their writing

> **Note:** [Col. 0325D] “The form of the Scriptures,” etc.: by explaining, clarifying, and commending it beforehand. **RIG.**

and, so to speak, its nature. Otherwise, if these matters are brought into controversy only when the Scriptures are being applied to the cases at issue, the defense of the Scriptures and that of the cases will become entangled [Col. 0326B] and dull the reader’s attention.

I therefore submit that our opponents must henceforth recognize two features of prophetic expression. The first is that future events are sometimes stated as though they had already occurred. For it befits the Divine Being to reckon as completed whatever he has decreed, since with him there is no difference of time: eternity itself directs before him one uniform condition of the times.

> **Note:** “Of time” in Semler and Pamel.

And it is especially characteristic of prophetic divination to present what it foresees, while foreseeing it, as something already seen and therefore already accomplished,

> **Note:** “As already” in Junius.

that is, as something certain to occur in every respect. Thus Isaiah says (Isaiah 50:6):

> “I gave my back to scourges, and my cheeks to blows with the palms; but I did not turn my face away from spittings.”

For whether, according to us, Christ was even then speaking about himself, or, according to the Jews, the prophet was speaking about himself, [Col. 0326C] he nevertheless described as already past something that had not yet happened.

The second form is that many things are figuratively foretold through riddles, allegories, and parables, and must be understood otherwise than they are written. For we also read that the mountains will drip sweetness (Joel 3:18); yet you are not thereby to expect concentrated grape syrup from stones

> **Note:** [Col. 0325D] “Concentrated grape syrup from stones, or boiled must from rocks,” etc. [Col. 0326D] These two terms mean the same thing, namely cooked must. The distinction is that *sapa* is must boiled down to half its original quantity, while *defrutum* is boiled down to one-third: so Marcus Varro. **LE PR.**

or boiled must from rocks. We hear, too, of a land flowing with milk and honey; yet you are not thereby to believe that you will ever gather cakes and Samian pastries from clods of earth.

> **Note:** [Col. 0326D] “Cakes and Samian pastries,” etc. Others read *psomias*, others *psomia*, from the Greek ψώμιον, “morsel” or “little piece of bread.” Those who read “Samian pastries,” I think, understand it as referring to Samian cakes, which were considered delicacies but bore the name of their inventor, who was called Samius. **LE PR.**

Nor did God’s mouth immediately promise to become a water-diviner and farmer when he said (Isaiah 41:19):

> “I will establish rivers in a thirsty region, and in the desert the cedar and box tree.”

Likewise, when proclaiming the conversion of the nations:

> “The beasts of the field, the sirens, and the daughters of sparrows will bless me” (Isaiah 43:20),

he is certainly not going to receive favorable omens from young swallows and little foxes, or from those monstrous and fabulous songstresses. [Col. 0326D]

Why should I say more about this class of expressions? [Col. 0327A] Even the apostle of the heretics (1 Corinthians 9:9),

> **Note:** “Of the Gentiles” in Latinius.

> **Note:** [Col. 0327D] “The apostle of the heretics.” He means the apostle Paul as edited and mutilated by Marcion. **PAMEL.**

interprets the Law’s indulgence in leaving the mouths of threshing oxen unmuzzled not as referring to oxen, but to us. He declares that the rock that accompanied the people and supplied them with drink was Christ (1 Corinthians 10:4). Accordingly, he teaches the Galatians (Galatians 4:22) that the two narratives concerning Abraham’s sons followed an allegorical course, and he suggests to the Ephesians (Ephesians 5:31) that what was proclaimed in the beginning concerning the man who would leave father and mother, and concerning the two who would become one flesh, is recognized by him as referring to Christ and the Church.

# CHAPTER VI

If these two characteristics of Jewish literature have meanwhile been sufficiently established, remember, reader, that they have been established, so that when we apply any such passage, the dispute will concern not the form of the Scripture but the substance of the case.

When, therefore, [Col. 0327B] heretical madness assumed that the Christ who had never been announced had come, it followed that it should contend that the Christ who had always been proclaimed had not yet come. Thus it was compelled to ally itself with the Jewish error and to construct its argument from it, as though the Jews, themselves certain that the one who came was someone else, had not only rejected him as a stranger but even killed him as an adversary—although they would undoubtedly have acknowledged him and attended upon him with every religious observance if he had been their own.

> **Note:** [Col. 0327D] Evidently that shipmaster had been protected not by Rhodian law but by Pontic law. He reproaches Marcion for his Pontic barbarism, as though Marcion had learned nothing except certain nautical ordinances, and those Pontic—that is, purely barbarous—rather than Rhodian. The Rhodians were admired by everyone for their care in civil law and their justice; but they also appear to have been highly skilled in maritime affairs and to have established most equitable laws concerning everything pertaining to navigation. Strabo records that for this reason they obtained dominion over the sea and flourished in friendship with the Romans. Not a few provisions of their maritime law concerning jettison, and perhaps also concerning the shipmaster’s action, were incorporated into the Pandects. **PAM.**

Evidently that shipmaster had been protected not by Rhodian law,

> **Note:** [Col. 0328D] “Not by Rhodian law,” etc. The laws of the Rhodians, who were highly skilled in maritime affairs, are mentioned in the Digest, under the Rhodian law concerning jettison. Tertullian says this to Marcion ironically. **LE PR.**

but by Pontic law, which forbade the Jews to err concerning their own Christ. Yet even if no such error had been foretold concerning them, the mere fact that human nature is liable to deception would surely have persuaded us that the Jews could have erred, since they were human beings. [Col. 0327C] One ought not immediately to draw a prejudicial conclusion from the judgment of people who could credibly have been mistaken.

Moreover, since it was also foretold that they would fail to recognize Christ, and would therefore kill him, the one whom they were foretold to treat in this way will consequently be the very one whom they failed to recognize and killed. If you demand proof of this, I shall not unfold the Scriptures that declare Christ capable of being killed and thus surely affirm that he could be unrecognized—for, unless he had been unrecognized, he plainly could not have suffered anything. Reserving those passages for the case concerning his sufferings, it will be sufficient for the present to apply those prophecies that prove in the meantime that Christ could be unrecognized. I shall do this briefly by showing that the Creator took away from the people every power of understanding.

He says [Col. 0328A] (Isaiah 29:14):

> “I will take away the wisdom of their wise men, and I will conceal the understanding of their prudent men.”

And again (Isaiah 6:10):

> “With your ears you will hear, and you will not hear; and with your eyes you will see, and you will not see. For the heart of this people has become dull; with their ears they have heard heavily, and they have closed their eyes, lest at any time they should hear with their ears, and see with their eyes, and comprehend with their heart, and be converted, and I should heal them.”

For they had deserved this blunting

> **Note:** “Dulling” in Semler.

of their saving senses, because they loved God with their lips but stood far away from him in their heart.

Therefore, if Christ was being announced by the Creator—who “establishes the thunder,” “creates the spirit” (Amos 4:13),

> **Note:** “Encloses” in Pamel, Rigault, the Venice edition, and Semler; but Latinius prefers “creates,” a reading supported by the Hebrew, Greek, and Latin Vulgate texts of Scripture. **EDD.**

and announces his Christ to humanity, according to the prophet Amos;

> **Note:** “Announcing” in Semler.

and if the whole hope of the Jews—not to mention that of the Gentiles also—was appointed to await the revelation of Christ, [Col. 0328B] then without doubt

> **Note:** [Col. 0328D] Others read “much less” instead of “not to mention.”

 those who were shown to be destined neither to recognize nor to understand what was announced, because the powers of recognition

> **Note:** “Of recognition” omitted by Semler.

and understanding—wisdom and prudence—had been taken away, would fail to recognize that which was being announced, namely Christ. Their chief wise men, that is, the scribes, and their prudent men,

> **Note:** “Their” disregarded by Semler.

that is, the Pharisees, were destined to err concerning him. Likewise, the people would hear with their ears and not hear—surely, they would hear Christ teaching—and would see with their eyes and not see—surely, they would see Christ performing signs. This accords with what he says elsewhere (Isaiah 42:19):

> “And who is blind except my servants? And who is deaf except the one who rules them?”

Likewise, when he reproaches them through the same Isaiah (Isaiah 1:3):

> “I begot sons and exalted them, but they rejected me. The ox recognized its owner, and the ass its master’s manger; but Israel did not know me, and the people did not understand me.”

We, indeed, are certain that Christ always spoke in the Prophets—Christ being, of course, the Creator’s Spirit—as the prophet testifies [Col. 0328C]:

> **Note:** [Col. 0328D] “The spirit of his person is Christ the Lord.” I judge that this must certainly be read instead of “The person of our spirit is Christ the Lord.” For, first of all, the Author would otherwise depart too greatly from the Septuagint at Lamentations 4, where not only the Greek and Latin manuscripts read, “The spirit of our face”—that is, [Col. 0329D] “of our countenance,” or “before our face”—“is Christ the Lord,” but the same words are also cited with the same meaning by blessed Irenaeus, III.11; blessed Justin Martyr, *Second Apology to Antoninus Pius*; and Rufinus, *Commentary on the Creed*. Nor does the Hebrew edition dissent greatly from them, reading, “the spirit of our mouth,” while the Chaldean reads, “the breath of the spirit of life that is in our nostrils.” **PAM.**

> “The spirit of his person is Christ the Lord” (Lamentations 4:20).

From the beginning, acting as the Father’s representative, he was both heard and seen under the name of God. We know that even then it was his voice uttering against Israel reproaches concerning the offenses they were foretold to commit against him:

> **Note:** [Col. 0328D] Semler inserts “and” before “we know.”

> “You have forsaken the Lord and provoked the Holy One of Israel to anger” (Isaiah 1:4).

But if you prefer to refer every account of the Jews’ former ignorance not to Christ but rather to God himself—refusing to admit that even in former times the Creator’s Word and Spirit, that is, his Christ, was despised and unrecognized by them—you are still refuted in this way.

> **Note:** [Col. 0328D] Semler reads, “But if Israel did not,” instead of “But if not.” Since you do not deny that the Creator’s Christ is the Creator’s Son, Spirit, and substance [Col. 0329A], you must concede that those who did not recognize the Father could not have recognized the Son either, because he shares the same substance. If they did not understand the fullness of that substance, much less could they understand a portion of it—at least insofar as that portion shares in the fullness.

Once these matters have thus been considered, it is now apparent how the Jews both rejected and killed Christ:

> **Note:** [Col. 0329C] Franeker omits “both.” not because they understood him to be a foreign Christ, but because they failed to recognize him as their own. For how could they have understood as a stranger someone concerning whom nothing had ever been announced, when they could not understand the one concerning whom proclamation had always been made?

> **Note:** [Col. 0329C] Rhenanus reads “For neither” instead of “For.”

> **Note:** [Col. 0329D] “For that which is capable of being understood is also capable of not being understood.” We have often already pointed out this author’s use of the verb *capit*, “is capable of.” There is a very similar expression in the immediately preceding book: “The condition is also capable,” he says, “of being called an image of the Spirit.” **RHEN.**

For only that which possesses the substance of proclamation is capable of being understood or not understood, since it will also possess the material either of recognition or of error. But that which lacks such material does not admit of any outcome involving wisdom.
