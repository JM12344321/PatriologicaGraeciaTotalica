It is necessary, insofar as you think that this was permitted by your own judgment, that you regarded birth either as impossible or as unfitting for God. [Col. 0756B] But nothing is impossible for God except what he does not will. Let us therefore consider whether he willed [Note: [Col. 0756C] “Did not will,” Seml., Rig., Ven.] to be born—for if he willed it, he both could be [Note: [Col. 0756C] “And could be” omitted by Rhen., Seml.] and was born. I shall take the shortest route. For if God had been unwilling to be born, for whatever reason, he would not have allowed himself to appear human either. For who, seeing a human being, would deny that he had been born? Thus, what he did not wish to be, he would not have wished even to appear to be at all [Note: [Col. 0756C] “To a human being,” Agobardine manuscript.]. Even the mere supposition of anything displeasing is rejected. For it makes no difference whether something exists or does not exist if, although it does not exist, it is presumed to exist. There is certainly this difference [Note: [Col. 0756C] “This” absent from Rig., Ven.]: what truly does not exist does not suffer falsely [Note: [Col. 0756C] “What is false,” Pam., Rhen.].

“But,” you say, “his own consciousness was enough for him. Let human beings see to it if they thought him born because they saw him as a human being.” How much more worthily, then, and how much more consistently would he have endured human estimation if he had truly been born, [Col. 0757A] since even without being born he was going to incur that same estimation, to the injury of his own consciousness! [Note: [Col. 0757D] “Which you reckon as confidence.” He had said: “But, you say, his own consciousness was enough for him.” For Marcion’s excuse was that it made no difference whether God appeared to be human, provided that he truly was not, because, with his own consciousness intact, he could confidently and steadfastly endure the estimation by which he was judged to be human, although this appeared unfitting and unworthy of God. LAC.]—an estimation which you reckon as an occasion for confidence, so that, although unborn, he endured being regarded as born, contrary to his own consciousness. Explain what was so important that Christ, knowing [Note: [Col. 0757C] “Explain, so that knowingly,” Rhen., Seml.] what he was, should present himself as what he was not [Note: [Col. 0757C] “Was,” Rhen., Pam.]. You cannot say: “[Note: [Col. 0757C] Rig., Ven. insert “not.”] If he had been born and had truly put on humanity, he would have ceased to be God, losing what he was while assuming [Note: [Col. 0757C] “Becoming,” Rig., Ven.] what he was not.” For God faces no danger to his own state.

“But this is why,” you say, “I deny that God was truly changed into a human being, so as both to be born and to be embodied in flesh [Note: [Col. 0757C] “To operate,” Rig., Ven.]: because one who is without end must also be immutable. For to be changed into something else is the end of what one was before; change, therefore, is not compatible [Note: [Col. 0757C] Rig., Ven. add “with him.”] with one for whom an end is not compatible.” Certainly, the nature of mutable things is governed by this law: they do not remain in that element of themselves which is changed, and thus, by not remaining, they perish [Note: [Col. 0757C] “They become subject,” Rig., Ven.] when through change they lose what they were. But nothing is equal to God: his nature stands apart from the condition of all things. If, therefore, things that differ from God [Note: [Col. 0757C] Ursinus’ manuscript inserts “or.”] lose what they were when they are changed, where will the difference between divinity and other things lie, unless the contrary holds true [Note: [Col. 0757C] “Contains,” Pam., Par., Fran.]—that is, unless God can both be changed into all things and continue to be what he is? Otherwise, he will be equal to those things which, once changed, lose what they were; and God is surely not unequal [Note: [Col. 0757C] “Equal,” Rhen., Seml., Pam.] to them in every respect if he is not unequal even in the outcome of change.

You have sometimes read and believed that the Creator’s angels were changed into human form, and that they bore such bodily reality that Abraham washed their feet, Lot was rescued from the Sodomites by their hands, [Col. 0757C] and an angel who wrestled with a man, exerting the whole weight of his body, desired to be released by the one [Note: [Col. 0757C] “So greatly,” Rig., Ven., Seml., Rhen.] who held him. What, therefore, was permitted to angels inferior to God—namely, that, although changed into human corporeality, they nevertheless remained angels—you deny to the more powerful God [Note: [Col. 0757D] “You will deny,” Rig., Ven., Par.], as though Christ could not put on true humanity and yet continue to be God. Or did those angels too appear as phantoms [Note: [Col. 0757D] Singular “phantom,” Sem., Pam.] of flesh? But you will not dare to say this. For if the Creator’s angels are such in your view, just as Christ also is, Christ will belong to that God [Note: [Col. 0757D] “Of the same substance,” Pam.] whose angels [Note: [Col. 0757D] Pam. repeats “angels.”] are such as Christ is.

If you had not deliberately rejected some Scriptures that resist your opinion and corrupted others, John’s Gospel would have confounded you on this point [Note: [Col. 0758C] “You from this point,” Pam., Rhen.], when it proclaims that the Spirit, gliding down [Note: [Col. 0758C] “Descending,” Rig., Ven.] in the body of a dove, settled [Note: [Col. 0758C] “Descended,” Pam., Vatican manuscript.] upon the Lord. Although that Spirit was what he was, he was as truly a dove as he was Spirit [Note: [Col. 0757D] “He was as truly a dove as he was Spirit.” In this interpretation of the passage, the author of *On the Christian Struggle*—among the works of Blessed Augustine—chapter 22, agrees with Tertullian. But Augustine himself thinks otherwise in Letter 102 to Evodius and in *On the Trinity*, chapter 5; the blessed Ambrose, *On the Sacraments* I.5, and Chrysostom, Homily 12 on Matthew, also agree with Augustine, drawing this conclusion from the expressions “like a dove” and “in the form of a dove.”], nor had he destroyed his own substance by assuming a foreign substance.

But you ask where the dove’s body is now that the Spirit has returned to heaven. Likewise, the bodies of the angels [Note: [Col. 0758C] “Of the angels also,” Rhen., Seml.] were withdrawn by the same principle by which they had been produced. If [Col. 0758B] you had seen them when they were brought forth from nothing, you would have known what happened when they were withdrawn into nothing. If their beginning was not visible, neither was their end. Nevertheless, the body possessed solidity at the moment [Note: [Col. 0758C] “At whatever moment,” Rig., Ven.] when it was seen as a body. What is written cannot have failed to exist.

## CHAPTER IV

Therefore, if you do not reject embodiment as either impossible or dangerous for God, it remains for you to reject and accuse it as unworthy. Beginning, indeed, with birth itself [Note: [Col. 0758C] Pam. adds “held in hatred.”], go on now, declaim about the filthiness of the generative elements in the womb [Note: [Col. 0758D] “Go on now, declaim about the filthiness of the generative elements in the womb.” Similar words occur in the third book *Against Marcion*: “Go on now, declaim against the most holy and venerable works of nature.” RIG.]: the foul clots of fluid and blood, the flesh that must be nourished for nine months from the same mire. [Col. 0759A] This reverence [Note: [Col. 0759D] “This reverence for nature.” He means the reverence owed to nature. The sense is: what you ought to have revered, you despise. Christ, at any rate, loved that human being clotted amid uncleanness in the womb, brought forth through the private parts, nourished amid humiliations. For his sake he descended; for his sake he preached; for his sake he cast himself down in every humility, even to death, and death on a cross (Philippians 2). He surely loved the one whom he redeemed at great [Note: [Col. 0759C] Pam., Vatican manuscript 3, add “price.”] cost. If Christ belongs to the Creator [Note: [Col. 0759C] “If the Creator is his,” Pam., Rhen.], he rightly loved his own; if he belongs to another God, he loved him all the more, since he redeemed what belonged to another. Therefore, together with humanity, he also loved its birth and its flesh. Nothing can be loved [Col. 0759B] apart from that through which it is what it is. [Note: [Col. 0759C] Pam., Rhen. add “or.”] Remove birth and produce a human being; take away flesh and present the one whom God redeemed. If these things constitute the human being whom God redeemed, then you make shameful for him those very things that he redeemed, and unworthy those things which he would not have redeemed unless he had loved them.

Having refashioned birth [Note: [Col. 0759C] “He refashions birth from death,” Rig., Ven.] by heavenly regeneration, he restores the flesh from every affliction: he cleanses it when leprous, illuminates it when blind [Note: [Col. 0759C] Others read “reilluminates.”], makes it whole when paralyzed, purifies it when possessed by a demon [Note: [Col. 0759C] “Captures,” Rig., Ven.], and raises it when dead—and is he ashamed to be born into it [Note: [Col. 0759C] Others: “And shall we be ashamed of it?”]?

If he had truly wished to come forth from a she-wolf, a sow, or a cow, and, clothed in the body of a wild or domestic animal, to preach the kingdom of heaven, your judgment, I suppose, would prescribe against him: “This is shameful for God and unworthy of God’s Son, and anyone who believes thus is therefore foolish [Note: [Col. 0759C] “Because,” Rig., Ven.].” Let it certainly be foolish, if [Note: [Col. 0759C] “If” absent from Rig., Ven.] we judge God according to our own understanding. But look around you, Marcion—if, that is, you have not deleted the passage—at 1 Corinthians 1:27: “God chose the foolish things of the world to confound the wise” [Note: [Col. 0759C] “Wisdom,” Rig., Ven.; “the wisdom,” Pam., Fran.].

What are these foolish things? A human being’s conversion to the worship [Note: [Col. 0759D] Others: “Human beings’ conversion to the worship of the true God.”] of the true God? The rejection of error? The discipline of justice, chastity, patience, [Col. 0760A] mercy, and innocence? None of these things, certainly, is foolish. Ask, therefore, what things he spoke about; and if you presume that you have discovered them, it will not be so [Note: [Col. 0760C] “Now,” Pam., Rhen.] foolish as [Note: [Col. 0760C] “As” absent from Pam., Rhen.] believing in a God who was born—and born indeed of a virgin, and indeed made flesh—who rolled through those indignities of nature. Let someone say that these things are not foolish and that the things God chose to challenge worldly wisdom were something else. Yet worldly wisdom more readily believes that Jupiter became a bull or a swan than, in Marcion’s system, that Christ became truly human.

## CHAPTER V

There are certainly other things just as foolish, pertaining to the humiliations and sufferings of God; or let them call [Note: [Col. 0760C] “Let me call,” Rhen., Seml.] a crucified God wisdom. Remove [Note: [Col. 0760C] “You remove,” Rhen., Seml.] this [Note: [Col. 0760C] “These things,” Pam., Paris, Fran.] also, Marcion—indeed, remove this above all. For what is more unworthy [Col. 0760B] of God? What is more shameful: to be born or to die? To bear flesh or a cross? To be circumcised or fastened to the cross? To be reared or buried? To be laid in a manger or enclosed in a tomb? You will be wiser if you do not believe these things either. But you will not be wise unless you become a fool [Note: [Col. 0760C] Rig., Ven. insert “in.”] to the world by believing God’s foolish things.

Or did you refrain from excising Christ’s sufferings for the reason that, as a phantom, he was exempt from feeling [Note: [Col. 0760C] “With respect to the perception,” Rhen., Seml.] them? We said above that he could equally have undergone the empty mockeries of an imaginary [Note: [Col. 0760C] “Imaginary and,” Pam., Seml.; “an imaginary thing belonging to imaginary,” Rhen.] birth and infancy. But now answer here [Note: [Col. 0760C] “From here,” Rig., Ven.], you murderer of the truth [Note: [Col. 0759D] “Murderer of the truth.” No one doubts that heretics were formerly branded with harsher language. Our author does so more than once; for in Book I *Against Marcion* he paints that heretic in his true colors and declares him more hideous than every savagery found in his homeland. In a not dissimilar sense, St. Ignatius, in the Letter to the Trallians, calls heretics κακὰς παραθυάδας, “an impious offshoot.” St. Prosper, in his poem *On the Ungrateful*, says of Pelagius:

> The British serpent vomited forth with pestilential speech.

Everyone knows that Blessed Jerome pursued heretics sharply. LE PR.]. Was God not truly crucified? Was he not truly dead, just as [Note: [Col. 0760C] “And,” Seml.] he was truly crucified [Note: [Col. 0760D] “Was he not truly dead, as he was truly crucified?” Christ’s birth is proved from his death. He chose an infamous death and a spectacle full of disgrace so that the truth might be acknowledged by everyone, no one might doubt his real death, and no one might call the resurrection into question. I refrain from citing the Fathers; I mention only St. Ignatius, Letter to the Trallians, where he treats this subject at length. LE PR.]? Was he not truly raised, precisely because he was truly dead?

Was Paul therefore falsely resolved to know among us only Jesus [Note: [Col. 0760C] “Jesus” absent from Rhen., Seml.] crucified? Did he falsely thrust burial upon us? [Col. 0760C] Did he falsely insist upon the resurrection? Then our faith too is false, and everything we hope for from Christ is a phantom [Note: [Col. 0760D] “Will be,” Rig., Ven.]. Most criminal of men, you excuse the killers of God [Note: [Col. 0760D] “You excuse the killers of God.” Marcion, whom Tertullian presses vehemently, proclaimed that Christ had not truly been human but only apparently so, and that he had assumed nothing from humanity. Therefore, if it were certain that he did not take the substance of our flesh and was not a true human being, he certainly would not truly have died; and by that impious and poisonous doctrine Marcion would thus excuse the Jewish executioners. LE PR.]. For Christ suffered nothing from them if he suffered nothing truly. Spare the sole hope of the whole [Col. 0761A] world.

Why [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Who,” Pam., Rhen.] do you destroy the necessary disgrace [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Glory,” Pam., Rhen.] of faith [Note: [Col. 0761D] “Why do you destroy the necessary disgrace of faith?” This may also be said to those who, contrary to the tradition of Tertullian and all the ancient writers, imagine Christ as exceedingly beautiful in stature and countenance. This is unquestionably how the text must be read, and not, as commonly, “glory.” For this is what he undertakes to prove against Marcion: all these things—God being born, God being crucified, God dying, God being buried—which are judged shameful, foolish, and absurd according to human understanding, and which Marcion rejected as unworthy of God, were all chosen by God so that worldly wisdom might be confounded. Septimius therefore says that what we consider disgraces are not disgraces before God, but are in fact necessary to the Christian faith. “Why,” he says, “do you destroy the disgrace necessary to faith?” Georgius Boeticus, in *On Faith*: “You see how beautiful and becoming is the mystery of our salvation. Insofar as he is the Son, he is shown to have been made lesser. Why, therefore, do you reproach as obscuring his divinity what he carries out for its adornment?” RIG.]? Whatever is unworthy of God is advantageous to me. I am saved if I am not ashamed of my Lord. “Whoever is ashamed of me [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Me,” Pam.],” he says, “of him [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Him,” Pam., Fran.] shall I also be ashamed.” Otherwise, I find no grounds for shame that would prove me commendably shameless through contempt for embarrassment and happily foolish.

The Son of God was born: I am not ashamed, because it is shameful. And the Son of God died: it is wholly credible, because it is absurd. And, having been buried, he rose again: it is certain, because it is impossible. But how will these things be true of him if he himself was not real—if he did not truly possess in himself that which could be fastened to the cross, die, be buried, and be raised: namely, flesh suffused with blood, constructed with bones, interwoven with sinews, [Col. 0761B] entwined with veins, which knows how to be born and to die? Human flesh [Note: [Col. 0761C] Accusative “human,” Pam., Rhen.], without doubt, since it was born [Note: [Col. 0761C] Accusative “born,” Pam., Rhen.] of a human being; and therefore [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Because,” Rhen., Seml.] it will be [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Is,” Seml., Pam.] mortal in Christ, because Christ [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Because Christ” absent from Rhen., Seml.] is a human being and the Son of Man.

Or why is Christ a human being and the Son of Man if he has nothing human and nothing derived from a human being? Unless, perhaps [Note: [Col. 0761C] “If” absent from Rig., Ven.], a human being is something other than flesh, or human flesh comes from somewhere other than a human being; or Mary is something [Note: [Col. 0761C] Pam., Fran., Par. add “is.”] other than human; or Marcion’s god is human [Note: [Col. 0761D] “Or Marcion’s god is human”—that is, an imaginary and false human being. For he will presently explain it himself: [Col. 0762D] “So that he bore flesh hard without bones, solid without muscles, bloody without blood, clothed without a tunic, hungry without hunger, speaking without a tongue—so that his speech was a phantom to the ears through the semblance of a voice.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0761C] “Or human” omitted by Pam., Rhen.; [Col. 0762C] “God, Marcion,” Pam.; “In short, Marcion,” Fran.; “Hardly,” Ursinus’ manuscript; “Or Marcion’s god,” Rhen., Seml.].

Otherwise, Christ would not be called human without flesh, nor Son of Man without some human parent—just as he would not be called God without the Spirit of God, nor Son of God without God as Father. Thus the origin derived from each substance displayed him as both human and God: on the one side born, on the other unborn; on the one side fleshly, on the other spiritual; on the one side weak, on the other supremely powerful; [Col. 0761C] on the one side dying, on the other living. This [Note: [Col. 0762C] “By which,” Jun.] distinctive character of the two conditions, divine and human, was certainly reckoned according to the equal reality of both [Note: [Col. 0762C] “Both” absent from Rhen., Pam.; Pam. adds “of each.”] natures, [Col. 0762A] with equal faith in both spirit and flesh [Note: [Col. 0762C] “By faith, spirit and flesh,” manuscripts of Ursinus and Dijon, Seml.]. His mighty works proved the Spirit of God; his sufferings proved human flesh. If the mighty works did not occur without spirit, likewise [Note: [Col. 0762C] “Equally,” Rig., Ven.] neither did the sufferings occur without flesh. If the flesh together with its sufferings was fictitious, then the spirit together with its mighty works was also false.

Why do you divide Christ in half by a lie? [Note: [Col. 0762D] “Why do you divide Christ in half by a lie? He is wholly truth.” Ignatius, Letter to the Trallians: Ἐγὼ δὲ οὐ τῳ δοκεῖν ἔχω τὰς ἐλπίδας ἐπὶ τῷ ὑπὲρ ἐμοῦ ἀποθανόντι, ἀλλὰ τῷ ὄντι· ἀληθείας γὰρ ἀλλότριον τὸ ψεῦδος: “I, however, do not place my hope in the one who died for me merely in appearance, but in reality; for every lie is alien to the truth”—that is, to Christ. Believe it: he preferred to be born rather than to lie in any [Note: [Col. 0762C] Pam., Par., Fran. add “of himself.”] part—and indeed to lie about himself—so that he might bear flesh hard without bones, solid without muscles, bloody without blood, clothed without a tunic [Note: [Col. 0762D] “Without a tunic.” By the term “tunic” he means the skin. He was therefore a phantom even after the resurrection, when he offered his hands and feet for his disciples’ inspection [Col. 0762B] [Note: [Col. 0762C] “Offers,” Rhen., Seml.]: “Look,” he says [Note: [Col. 0762C] “Saying,” Pam., Rhen., Seml.], “that it is I, because a spirit does not have bones, as you see that I have” (Luke 24)—without doubt, hands and feet and bones, which a spirit does not have but flesh does.

How do you interpret this utterance, Marcion, when [Note: [Col. 0762C] “Because,” Rhen., Ven.] you introduce Jesus as coming from a god who is supremely good, simple, and only benevolent? Look: he cheats, deceives, and circumvents everyone’s eyes, everyone’s senses, and everyone’s approaches and touches. Therefore, you ought no longer to have brought Christ down from heaven, but from some troupe of itinerant performers; nor as a God beyond man, but as a man who was a magician; nor as the high priest of salvation, but as a producer of spectacle; nor as one who raises the dead, but as one who summons the living away [Note: [Col. 0762D] “But one who summons the living away.” One who summons the living away from life to death, from right faith to heresy, from God to Satan. ## CHAPTER VI

[Col. 0762C] But certain disciples [Note: [Col. 0762C] “What now,” Rhen., Paris.] of that man from Pontus [Note: [Col. 0762D] “But certain disciples of that man from Pontus.” He means Marcion’s disciples, among whom Apelles was the leader. Marcion is called “the man from Pontus,” just as at the beginning of Book I *Against Marcion* he is called “the Pontic mouse”; for he was a native of Sinope in Pontus. LE PR.], compelled to be wiser than their teacher, concede that Christ’s flesh was real, [Col. 0763A] yet without prejudicing their rejection of his birth. “Let him have had flesh,” they say, “provided only [Note: [Col. 0763C] “The Lord,” Agobardine manuscript, Pithou.] that it was unborn.”

We have therefore come [Note: [Col. 0763C] “We have obeyed,” Agobardine manuscript, Pithou, Rhen., Jun.; “let us produce,” others.], as the saying goes, from the limekiln into the charcoal furnace—from Marcion to Apelles. After Apelles had fallen away from Marcion’s discipline into a woman in the flesh [Note: [Col. 0763C] “Slipped through a woman,” Rhen., Seml.], and thereafter [Note: [Col. 0763C] “And” absent from Rig.] had been spiritually overthrown by the virgin Philumene [Note: [Col. 0763D] “Overthrown spiritually by the virgin Philumene”—that is, seduced by the demon that took possession of that infamous prostitute. LE PR.], he received from her the doctrine he proclaimed [Note: [Col. 0763C] “Proclaiming,” Pam., Rhen.; “he proclaims it as received from him,” Wouwer manuscript.]: that Christ’s body was solid but without birth.

To that angel [Note: [Col. 0763C] “As an angel,” Wouwer manuscript, Pam., Rhen.] of Philumene [Note: [Col. 0763C] “A certain one,” Latin edition; “of that man,” Pam., Fran.; “of Philumene” absent from the Wouwer manuscript; “Philomene,” others.], indeed, the Apostle will reply in the very words with which he foretold even him at that time, saying (Galatians 1:8): “Even if an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel different from what we preached to you, let him be accursed.” But we shall resist the further arguments [Note: [Col. 0763C] “Who,” Pam., Rhen.] they advance [Note: [Col. 0763C] “We have resisted,” Rhen.].

They confess [Col. 0763B] [Note: [Col. 0763D] “Of one confessing,” Pam.] that Christ truly had a body. From what did its material come, if not from the same kind of substance in which it appeared? From what came the body, if the body was not flesh? From what came the flesh, if it was not born? For what comes into being by birth would have to be born.

“He borrowed his flesh,” they say, “from the stars and from the substances of the higher world.” And, of course, they argue that a body without birth should not be considered remarkable, since among us too angels were able to advance into flesh [Note: [Col. 0763D] “In flesh,” Rig., Ven.] without any agency of the womb. We acknowledge that this is indeed what has been related. Yet what sort of thing is it that a faith belonging to one rule should borrow the documentary authority for its arguments from the very faith it attacks [Note: [Col. 0764C] “Should be changed,” Rig., Ven.]? What has Apelles to do with Moses, when he rejects the God of Moses [Note: [Col. 0764C] “Moses, who rejects the God of Moses,” Seml.]? If his God is another, let his affairs also be different.

But [Note: [Col. 0764C] “And,” Rhen., Seml.] let all heretics use the Scriptures of the one whose world they also use: this too will serve as testimony [Note: [Col. 0764C] “This too as testimony of him,” Agobardine manuscript.] against them at the judgment, because they construct their blasphemies from his own examples. It is easy for the truth to prevail even without advancing any such preliminary objection against them. [Col. 0764A]

Therefore, I would like those who set forth the angels as an example for Christ’s flesh—saying that it was flesh, although unborn [Note: [Col. 0764C] “Fleshly, of course,” Pam., Rhen.]—to compare the reasons for which both Christ and [Note: [Col. 0764C] Pam., Rhen. add “the.”] the angels entered into flesh [Note: [Col. 0764C] “In flesh,” Rig., Ven.; “came,” Pam., Fran., Par.; “went forth,” Rhen., Seml.]. No angel ever descended in order to be crucified, to experience death, or to be raised from death [Note: [Col. 0764C] “To be brought to life,” Rig., Ven.]. If the angels never had any such reason for becoming embodied, you have the reason why they received flesh without being born. They had not come to die; therefore neither had they come to be born.

Christ, however, was sent to die and therefore also necessarily had to be born, so that he could die [Note: [Col. 0763D] “Christ, sent to die, also necessarily had to be born so that he could die.” Marius Victorinus was not yet properly a Christian when he wrote thus in Book I of his commentary on Cicero’s *Rhetoric*: “Among human beings, necessity has only as much force as it possesses according to human opinion; otherwise, according to the Christians’ opinion, this is not a necessary argument: ‘If she gave birth, she had intercourse with a man’; nor, again, is this one: ‘If he was born, he will die.’ For among them it is manifest that he was born without a man and did not die.” On the contrary, we confess that our Lord, whom we know to have been truly born, also truly died, and we declare that he also truly rose again. RIG.]. For nothing normally dies except what is born. Birth and mortality stand in reciprocal debt to each other. The manner of dying is the reason for being born [Note: [Col. 0764D] “The manner of dying is the reason for being born.” After saying that there was a reciprocal debt between life and death, he now says that the form is the cause of birth. The “form” of the loan is thus like a certain formal quality in a loan, inhering in it. Tertullian’s meaning, therefore, is this: we die by compact and condition, so that only because we have been born are we bound by this law of death, as though the condition of death were the final cause of birth; for we are not born except under the condition of dying, and the contract of life was made under this condition. So Seneca, *Remedies for Fortune*. LAC.].

If [Col. 0764B] Christ died because of that which dies, and what dies is also what is born, it followed [Note: [Col. 0764C] Rhen., Seml. add “however”; likewise Pam., Fran., Par.]—indeed, it had to precede—that he should likewise be born because of that which is born, since he was going to die because of that very thing which dies because [Note: [Col. 0764C] Rhen., Pam. add “that which.”] it is born. It was not fitting that he should remain unborn in respect of that for which it was fitting that he should die.

“But surely [Note: [Col. 0764C] “But,” Seml.] the Lord himself also appeared to Abraham among those angels [Note: [Col. 0764C] “Those angels,” Rig.] at that time, in flesh but without birth” [Note: [Col. 0764D] “But surely the Lord himself also appeared to Abraham among those angels at that time.” This interpretation, which seems to have arisen from the reading of Genesis 3, “He saw three and worshiped one,” is not approved by Blessed Augustine; the reader should therefore consult *On the Trinity* II.17. Moreover, following Vatican manuscript 3, we again read *atquin* rather than *atqui*. PAM.]. Certainly—because the reason was correspondingly different.

But you do not accept this, because you do not accept that Christ who even then was learning, in the guise of flesh, to address, deliver, and judge the human race—flesh not yet born because it was not yet to die, unless his birth and mortality were first announced. Therefore, let them prove that those angels [Col. 0764C] received [Note: [Col. 0764C] “Flesh,” Rig., Ven.] the substance of their flesh from the stars [Note: [Col. 0764C] “Desired,” Rig., Ven.; [Col. 0764D] “The substance of their flesh” absent from Rig., Ven.]. If they do not prove it—because neither is it written—then neither will Christ’s flesh have come from there, since they apply the example of the angels to it.

It is agreed that the angels did not bear flesh proper to themselves [Col. 0765A] [Note: [Col. 0765D] “It is agreed that the angels did not bear flesh proper to themselves.” That is, they were not altogether and properly fleshly as we are, nor heavy with bodily weight. Above, he calls what belongs to the angels “proper”—namely, proper to themselves—not what is superior to flesh. For he immediately indicates, and elsewhere teaches, that those angels displayed real flesh, though flesh assumed and not proper and natural to them; Augustine and Bernard, when sometimes cited on this matter, are uncertain. LAC.], since they were natures [Note: [Col. 0765C] “For example,” Pam., Rhen.; “a nature,” Rhen., Seml.; “from a nature,” Agobardine manuscript; “of a nature,” Pam., Paris.] of spiritual substance—or, if of some bodily substance, nevertheless of their own kind—yet capable of being transfigured temporarily into human flesh, so that [Note: [Col. 0765C] “So that” omitted by Rig., Ven.] they could be seen and associate with human beings [Note: [Col. 0765C] “Could,” Rig., Ven.].

Therefore, since it has not been related from where they took their flesh, it remains for our understanding not to doubt that this is proper to angelic power: to assume a body for itself from no material. “How much more certain it is,” you say, “that it came from some material!” But nothing concerning that is established, because Scripture does not disclose it. Besides, those who [Note: [Col. 0765C] “Because,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] are able to make themselves what they are not by nature—why should they not also be able to make themselves from no substance [Note: [Col. 0765C] “A substance,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.]? If they become what they are not, why should they not become it from what does not exist? But what does not exist, when it comes to exist, is from nothing.

For this reason it is neither asked nor shown [Col. 0765B] what afterward became of their bodies [Note: [Col. 0765D] “What afterward became of their bodies.” This is thoroughly idiomatic Latin; that is, where they ended up. The preposition *de* is understood. So Cicero, *Against Verres*: “What will become of you? Where will you flee?” Plautus: “What became of the money I gave?” Again Cicero, *For Cluentius*: “What is,” etc. RHEN.]. What came from nothing became nothing. Those who could change themselves into flesh can change nothing itself into flesh. It is a greater thing to alter a nature than to produce material.

But even [Note: [Col. 0765C] “And” absent from Rhen., Pam.] if the angels had necessarily [Note: [Col. 0765C] “Had,” Rig., Ven.] assumed flesh from material, it is surely more credible that it came from earthly material than from any class of heavenly substances, since it proved to be so earthly in quality that it was nourished on earthly food. Suppose now [Note: [Col. 0765C] “Suppose” omitted by Rhen., Seml.; [Col. 0766C] Rhen. adds “if.”] that flesh of stellar origin was similarly [Note: [Col. 0766C] “In the same manner,” Rhen.] nourished on earthly food although it was not earthly, just as earthly flesh was nourished on heavenly food although it was not heavenly—for we read that manna was given to the people for food: “A human being,” he says (Psalm 77), “ate the bread of angels.” Nevertheless, the once-for-all distinct condition of the Lord’s [Col. 0765C] flesh is not invalidated by the reason belonging to a different dispensation. Because he was to become truly human even to the point of death, he had to put on that flesh [Note: [Col. 0766C] “That” omitted by Rhen., Seml.] to which death belongs; [Col. 0766A] and birth precedes that flesh to which death belongs.
