But if anyone acquits them rashly for the sake of gain or pleasure, he acts unjustly, and injustice must necessarily be dear to his heart.” Tertullian uses *eximere*, “to exempt,” just as Livy does, in whom Papirius says of Quintus Fabius, later Maximus: “Quintus Fabius is not exempted from guilt, but, though condemned for guilt, is granted to the Roman people.” Horace, *Odes* II.2.21:

Finally, no one eagerly desires to acquit such a man [Note: [Col. 0277B] Wouwer adds “among you.”]; it is not lawful even to wish it. Therefore no one is compelled to deny. You regard the Christian—a man guilty of every crime [Note: [Col. 0277D] “A man guilty of every crime.” Tertullian notably clears them of all these charges in this *Apology*. He calls them enemies of nature because they were believed to be infanticides, incestuous, and unproductive in commerce. Thus Cicero says of Dolabella, formerly his friend, in *Philippic* XI.10, that perhaps he would not even now be estranged from him had Dolabella not, in addition to his many other crimes, finally proved an enemy of nature and humanity; enemies of the emperors, because the charge of treason and injured majesty was brought against them. Suetonius, chapter XVI, calls them “a class of men belonging to a new and wicked superstition.” As such they were persecuted by impious princes. An ancient inscription from the age of Diocletian and Maximian, in Gruter, p. 280: “THE CHRISTIAN SUPERSTITION DESTROYED EVERYWHERE.” Hence also the inscription of those same emperors in Baronius’s *Annals* and Spanheim’s *On the Use and Excellence of Coins*: “THE NAME OF THE CHRISTIANS, WHO HAD OVERTURNED THE COMMONWEALTH, ERASED.” Enemies of laws and [Col. 0278B] customs, because they neglected public festivals and the worship of idols. Tacitus, *Annals* XV.44: “Accordingly, those who confessed were arrested first, and they were convicted not so much on the charge of arson as of hatred of the human race.” From Tertullian, chapter XXXV, you will learn the source of the hatred charged against them as a crime and why they were called public enemies. Place a comma after “Christian.” HAV.], an enemy of the gods, the emperors, the laws, customs, and all nature—and you compel him to deny so that you may acquit him, although you cannot acquit him unless he has denied [Note: [Col. 0277B] Thus Wouwer.].

You betray the laws [Note: [Col. 0278B] “You betray the laws.” Thus reads the learned manuscript whose owner consulted Scriverius, and not badly. Festus: “Betrayers are named from stepping past.” Thus “to betray the laws” is to step past them. Asconius Pedianus, in the little book included among the ancient grammarians: “He who rejects the bad and retains the good is understood to be a true accuser. But one who does the contrary, so as to impede others and cunningly conceal the matter, is understood to be a betrayer.” These words illuminate Tertullian’s meaning perfectly. For “you wish,” therefore, the Fulda manuscript soon reads “you wish him to deny.” Barraeus’s edition deletes “now.” HAV.]. You therefore wish him to deny that he is guilty so that you may make him innocent—and indeed innocent against his will, and no longer guilty concerning the past. Whence comes this perversity, that you do not even reflect that greater credence should be given to one who confesses voluntarily than to one who denies under force? Or perhaps, after being compelled to deny, he may not have denied in good faith [Note: [Col. 0278C] “May not have denied in good faith.” He falsely pretended that he was not a Christian by supplicating gods whom he rejected in his heart. Emperor Trajan to Pliny, Letter XCVIII: “Nevertheless, anyone who denies that he is a Christian and makes this evident by the act itself—that is, by supplicating our gods—shall obtain pardon through repentance, although he may have been suspected concerning the past.” Not only did the gentiles attempt in this way to persuade Christians to deny their God, as will appear more fully, but there also arose the perverse heresy of the Ossenes, which Epiphanius arraigns for this crime in Book I, Heresy 19:

> ὑποκρίτας δὲ διδὰσκει — “Moreover, he teaches them to be hypocrites…”
