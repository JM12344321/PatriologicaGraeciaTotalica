Great indeed, as we have seen, and surpassing all praise, was the utterly invincible courage of the martyrs, by which they sealed the religion received from Christ with their own blood, as with a public seal. And they certainly shed it all the more willingly because they knew with greater certainty that through martyrdom they would obtain the whole grace of God and the forgiveness of all sins. For why did the Christian so ardently desire martyrdom? “That through it,” says Tertullian, “he may purchase the whole grace of God, and secure from him complete forgiveness by the payment of his own blood” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530). If you ask the reason, he will answer at once: “For all transgressions are forgiven to this deed.” In another [Col. 0974B] book, moreover, he teaches that it is a second baptism, through which the grace of God is bestowed and all sins committed before that most sacred washing with water are wholly and completely forgiven. But let us, if you please, continue to hear him speak for himself:

> We have indeed also a second washing, itself one and unique: namely, that of blood, that is, martyrdom, concerning which the Lord says: “I have a baptism with which to be baptized,” although he had already been baptized. . . . . . . He sent forth these two baptisms from the wound in his pierced side, so that those who believed in his blood might be washed with water, and those who had washed with water might also drink his blood. This is the baptism that both supplies the place of a washing not received and restores one that has been lost.

(Idem, *De Baptismo*, ch. 16.)

We have already seen this asserted by him above (above, ch. 11, art. 5), as it was also by his disciple Cyprian, who furthermore openly proclaims [Col. 0974C] in these words that the grace of this baptism is greater than that of the first:

> We have given the first baptism to believers; let us also prepare each one for the other, making known and teaching that this is a baptism greater in grace, loftier in power, more precious in honor—a baptism in which angels baptize, a baptism in which God and his Christ rejoice, a baptism after which no one sins any longer, a baptism that completes the growth of our faith, a baptism that, as we depart from the world, immediately unites us with God. In the baptism of water the remission of sins is received; in that of blood, the crown of virtues.

(Cyprian, *De Exhortatione Martyrii*.)

Elsewhere he puts it more briefly: “Can the power of baptism,” he asks, “be greater or more excellent than confession, than suffering, so that one confesses before men and is baptized in one’s own blood?” (Id., ep. 73, to Jubaianus.) He [Col. 0974D] repeats the same things elsewhere (Idem, epistle 55; *De Oratione Dominica*), and Augustine confirms them (Augustine, *De Civitate Dei*, book XIII, ch. 7), where he shows by various words of Holy Scripture that martyrdom has as much efficacy as if one were washed in the sacred font of baptism. There he also adds: “For what is more precious than a death through which all transgressions are forgiven and merits are increased more abundantly?” Ambrose, moreover, followed the footsteps of our Tertullian more closely:

> Is there not one baptism? There is one, which the Church here hands down through water and the Holy Spirit, concerning which the Lord Jesus says: “I have a baptism with which to be baptized, which you do not know.” He had, of course, already been baptized in the Jordan, as the preceding words make clear; but let this be the baptism of suffering, by which each person is also [Col. 0975A] cleansed in his own blood.

(Ambrose, *Expositio in Psalmum CXVIII*, sermon 3, § 14.)

More on these matters may be read in the scholastic theologians (*In IV Sententiarum*, distinction 4; and in the *Summa* of St. Thomas, part III, question 66, article 11).

Christians were therefore thoroughly persuaded that they never celebrated a greater triumph over the demons, the enemies of their eternal salvation, than when pagans, incited by the demons’ malicious instigations, killed them for confessing Christ. “For we never triumph more completely over them,” says our Author, “than when we are condemned for our obstinacy in the faith” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 27, p. 434), or, as we said shortly before, for its steadfast and unconquered defense. What that Christian triumph is, and how glorious, he explains in this manner:

> It is a battle for us when, [Col. 0975B] accused because of our Christian profession, we are summoned before the tribunals of the pagans, so that there, at the risk of our lives, we may contend in defense of the truth of this same religion of ours.

“In this battle,” he says, “we conquer when we are killed. In short, we emerge victorious from this battle when we are led away” (*Ibid.*, ch. 50, p. 530), that is, when, led to punishment, we are destroyed and killed. For our Author appears there to have used the word *obducimur* in this sense, although elsewhere, as we shall see, he employed it with another meaning. Since Christians desired nothing more than to die and be slaughtered in defense of their religion, they assuredly conquered when they were killed. “For victory,” Tertullian continues, “consists in obtaining that for which you have contended” (*Ibid.*).

But in this contest the victors gained both glory and spoil, and indeed the greatest of all; for [Col. 0975C] that victory, as Tertullian says, “possesses both the glory of pleasing God and the spoil of living forever” (*Ibid.*). This spoil perhaps seemed all the greater and more welcome to him because, as we noted above (above, ch. 11, art. 5), he believed that the reward of eternal blessedness, deferred for other righteous persons, was bestowed upon the holy martyrs immediately after their death and departure.

Furthermore, those who celebrated so distinguished a triumph over the world and the devil did not lack the attire of triumphers, the palm-embroidered garment, and the chariot. But what, you may ask, were these insignia of the triumphant martyrs? Certainly nothing less than those of Roman generals and emperors. “For when we are burned alive upon the stake of half an axle, of which we spoke shortly before, this,” says Tertullian, “is the attire of our victory, this is our palm-embroidered garment; in such a chariot do we triumph” [Col. 0975D] (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530).

First, he there mentions the “attire of victory,” a term that generally signifies all the ornaments of victory or of triumphers: namely, the golden crown, the curule chair, the ivory staff, the painted toga, the palm-embroidered tunic, and other things frequently mentioned by various writers. For brevity’s sake, however, he passes over most of these ornaments and identifies only two: the palm-embroidered garment and the triumphal chariot. Livy, however, introduces Decius speaking of these and other honors as follows:

> To whom among gods or men can it seem improper to add the insignia of pontiffs and augurs to those men whom you have honored with curule chairs, the purple-bordered toga, the palm-embroidered tunic, the painted toga, and the triumphal crown—whose houses you have made conspicuous among [Col. 0976A] the others by fastening to them the spoils of enemies—and one of whom, adorned in the apparel of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, has been carried through the City in a gilded chariot and has ascended the Capitol?

(Livy, book X, § 7.)

He said “in the apparel of Jupiter” because triumphers, as Servius observes, “possess all the insignia of Jupiter, including the palm-bearing scepter. Hence Juvenal says, ‘in Jupiter’s tunic’” (Servius, on *Eclogue* 10, v. 27, p. 55). Elsewhere Servius speaks thus about the palm-embroidered garment and the chariot:

> It must be known that the curule chair was named from the chariot, because only those who had been carried in a triumphal chariot used it; just as the tunic is called *palmata*, which those who had won the palm from their enemies deserved to wear.

(Idem, on *Aeneid* II, v. 334, p. 637.)

Does he not imply there that it was so named because it was given to those who had deserved the palm? Others, however, think it was so called because palms were woven into it. [Col. 0976B] Martial has these verses concerning it:

> Go, companion, and deserve great triumphs unharmed,  
> And return the general of the palm-embroidered toga—but return him soon.

(Martial, book VII, epigram 1.)

If anyone desires more, let him consult Octavius Ferrari (Ferrari, *De Re Vestiaria*, book II, ch. 8), Aldus Manutius (Manutius, *De Toga Romana*), Lazarus Bayfius (Bayfius, *De Re Vestiaria*, ch. 10), and Albertus Rubenius (Rubenius, *De Re Vestiaria*, book I, ch. 21), who expressly discuss the triumphal chariot. In addition, concerning these and the other insignia and ornaments of triumphers, you may, if you wish and have leisure, consult Onofrio Panvinio’s book *De Triumpho* and Mader’s annotations upon it.

But the glory of the triumph of our martyrs was far greater and altogether extraordinary. For in the midst of the flames of burning [Col. 0976C] bundles of brushwood, scorched on every side as though clothed in a purple, palm-embroidered garment, and bound to the stake and post, they were borne thence as if in a triumphal garment and chariot—not to the Capitol, but to paradise, as we saw above (ch. 11, art. 5)—triumphing over the world, over tyrants, and over the devil.

# CHAPTER XVII

## By what arguments Tertullian demonstrates that Christians were harassed and slaughtered by the pagans with the greatest injustice and contrary to the prescriptions of all laws

### ARTICLE I

#### With what perversity the pagans hated and persecuted Christians, although Christians were entirely unknown to them and although they were unwilling to investigate who or what sort of people they were

[Col. 0976D] What we have thus far said concerning the truth and holiness of the Christian religion, its divine teachings and laws, the excellent morals of its followers, and the utterly invincible courage of the martyrs, demonstrates more than sufficiently that this religion alone is true, while all the others are false and must be utterly rejected. No greater injustice, therefore, can be imagined than that of the pagans, who, because Christians defended this religion of theirs, harassed, tortured, and killed them with the utmost and most dreadful cruelty, as we have seen. But our Tertullian, in order to defend the cause of these same Christians more precisely, undertook to demonstrate two further points: first, [Col. 0977A] that Christians were condemned by the pagans contrary to every natural, divine, and human law; second, that all the crimes of which they were accused were false, and that the pagans themselves were in fact guilty of them.

Let us therefore first see by what reasoning he proved the truth of the first proposition, and then we shall come to the second. “As the first ground of your injustice toward the name of Christians,” he says, “we lay before you, governors of the Roman Empire, the injustice of your hatred—an injustice that is aggravated and refuted by the very title that seems to excuse it: namely, ignorance” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 1; and *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 1, p. 559). Some editions read “hatred” in the nominative instead of “of hatred,” but incorrectly. For “the injustice of hatred” there signifies the same thing as “unjust hatred.” Tertullian therefore first indicts the injustice of the pagans, by which they hated [Col. 0977B] Christians before they knew who or what sort of people they were. For what greater perversity is there than to hate someone when you are wholly ignorant whether he deserves hatred or love?

Our Author plainly shows that the pagans had blazed up with this blind hatred against Christians from the fact that “all who formerly hated them because they did not know the nature of what they hated, as soon as they cease to be ignorant, also cease to hate” (*Ibid.*, passage cited). In Rigault’s edition the words “the nature of what they hated” (or, as some printed editions have it, “the nature of what they had hated”) are entirely omitted. By what right—whether on Rigault’s own authority or through a printer’s error—we do not know; but we know very well that they are found in subsequent and recent editions. That they are Tertullian’s genuine words is established clearly enough both by the common agreement of all the manuscripts [Col. 0977C] and by the Author’s own manner of thought. His argument is this: when the pagans learned who and what sort of people the Christians were, and what their piety and integrity were like, they immediately ceased to hate them. Therefore, they had hated them because they previously did not know who they were.

Although no one wholly deprived of the natural light of reason could deny this, Tertullian nevertheless presses the point and further refutes the pagans by the authority of Anacharsis, who explicitly pronounced that “those without practical wisdom who judge the wise are no less culpable than those without musical knowledge who judge musicians” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 1, p. 265). Laertius presents this philosopher’s maxim as follows: [non-Latin text omitted]. “He said that he wondered how, among the Greeks, craftsmen competed, while those who were not craftsmen acted as judges” (Laertius, book I, *Life of Anacharsis*, § 103). Plutarch reports it somewhat differently: [non-Latin text omitted]. [Col. 0977D] “Anacharsis said this when he had attended an assembly: that he wondered that among the Greeks the wise spoke, but fools passed judgment” (Plutarch, *Life of Solon*, p. 81). You see that all these words express the same opinion, stated only in somewhat different forms: namely, that it is wrong to pass any judgment upon matters one does not understand.

But far more wicked were the pagans, who hated Christians and condemned them for abominable crimes, although [Col. 0978A] they not only did not know what sort of people they were, but, with inflexible obstinacy, were never willing either to learn or to investigate. There was no other cause of this intolerable obstinacy and willful blindness than their fear that, by learning what the Christians’ manner of life was, they would be forced to admit that, after embracing the religion of Christ, they had become better people.

The pagans, however, boldly denied that the integrity of Christians who had abandoned the worship of the gods was genuine. “For,” they said, “something is not therefore good merely because it converts many people” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 1, p. 266), that is, because it transfers them from one profession to another. “And certainly,” they said, “how many are completely transformed from good to evil?” The ancient editions have *praeformantur*, while others and our manuscripts more correctly have *performantur*; or rather, perhaps, *reformantur*, if the authority of [Col. 0978B] some manuscript supported it. The pagans added: “How many deserters go over to perversity?” These are called deserters who flee to the enemy, or who during a truce migrate to those with whom no friendship exists. Yet these men, the pagans continued, having often been converted to perversity, assuredly became worse. Finally, pressing their argument more sharply, they cried out:

> A thing is certainly not to be prejudged as good because it converts a great many people and seizes them for itself. I know of changes of mind in the direction of evil. How many deserters from the good life! How many fugitives to perversity!

(*Ibid.*; and *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 1, p. 559.)

But Tertullian replies that no one denies this; rather, everyone freely admits that very many have also converted themselves from evil to good. Yet this, he says, is readily discerned from the nature of evil and good. For the inseparable [Col. 0978C] companions of evil are fear, shame, evasion, repentance, and lamentation. “Consequently,” our Author says again, “evildoers are eager to remain hidden and avoid appearing” (Id., *Apologeticus*, ch. 1, p. 267). Rigault removed these last two words on his own authority, evidently failing to notice that they are repeated in the book *Ad Nationes* (Id., *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 1, p. 559), as are the words that immediately follow in the *Apologeticus*:

> When discovered, they tremble; when accused, they deny; even under torture they do not readily or invariably confess; when condemned, they certainly grieve; they enumerate against themselves the impulses of an evil mind, yet attribute them either to fate or to the stars.

In the book *Ad Nationes*, however, these latter words are altered as follows: “For they reproach what they were; they attribute the passage of an evil mind from innocence either to themselves or to fate” (*Ibid.*, passage cited). But perhaps in that passage the two words “or to the stars” [Col. 0978D] fell out through a copyist’s pen. Georgius Ambianensis alone, guided solely by conjecture, rather boldly placed *deonerant* in Tertullian’s text instead of *dinumerant*. For his meaning is this: evildoers acknowledge, enumerate, and reproach themselves for the impulses of an evil mind; nevertheless, they impute and ascribe the guilt for them not to themselves but to the stars or to fate. We have already seen (*Dissertation on Minucius Felix’s Octavius*, ch. 6, art. 4) how widespread among the pagans was the false opinion that everything happened by fate. But absolutely none of these marks of evil just mentioned are found in the Christian, who, as we observed above (preceding chapter, art. 2), is neither ashamed of nor sorry for his religion, but, when accused and interrogated, confesses [Col. 0979A] that he is a Christian, rejoices, glories, and, when condemned, gives thanks.

Tertullian therefore rightly asks: “What evil is there in this, among Christians, which does not possess the natural characteristics of evil?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 1, p. 269). In our manuscripts, however, we read: “which the nature of evil does not possess otherwise”; perhaps not altogether badly, if the particle “than” is understood there, as is by no means wholly uncommon among good authors, giving this sense: “which has no other characteristics than those possessed by the nature of evil.” But to many it will undoubtedly appear that the letter *a* was duplicated there through the ignorance or error of copyists. Consider, nevertheless, whether the first reading might not be confirmed from the book *Ad Nationes*, where it is said more briefly but no less clearly: “What kind of evil is this in which the nature of evil is absent?” (Idem, *Ad Nationes*, book I, [Col. 0979B] ch. 1, p. 560.) Indeed, our Author adds that there must be a very great good in a thing “whose accused party rejoices, and whose accusation is his desire.”

He presses the pagans still more sharply (Idem, *Apologeticus*, ch. 1, p. 264) and openly demonstrates that there was nothing evil in Christians, who, having abandoned the worship of false gods and embraced the religion of Christ, had become better people, as we have said. Nor could this be unknown to the pagans themselves, whatever protests they might raise to the contrary. For they knew how great the number of Christians was throughout the whole world; they therefore grieved deeply and complained about it both frequently and publicly. Yet however they had come to know them, they had certainly seen that from evil and dishonorable people they had become good and honorable; from the unchaste, chaste; from the unjust and criminal, chaste [the source reads *eastos*, evidently *castos*], religious, [Col. 0979C] and diligently devoted to every virtue. And certainly, as Tertullian says, they thought “Caius a good man—except that he was a Christian” (our manuscripts read “Gaius Seius”) (*Ibid.*, ch. 3, p. 280; and *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 4, p. 564). They marveled that Lucius was wise, that this woman was chaste, this son obedient to his father, and this servant faithful to his master. They indeed marveled at these and other virtues of Christians, but could not tolerate the fact that these people were Christians.

“Who, then, can deny that those are rather the ones in error,” asks our Author, “who praise what they know, condemn what they do not know, and corrupt what they know by what they do not know?” (*Ibid.*). In all our manuscripts we read *irrumpunt*, “break into,” instead of *corrumpunt*, “corrupt”; but perhaps less correctly. For in the book *Ad Nationes* it is written: [Col. 0979D] “Through the blindness of folly, they praise what they know, condemn what they do not know, and vitiate what they do not know” (passage cited), which, as you see, is the same as “corrupt.” If, however, you prefer and approve the reading “break into,” we shall not wholly oppose it. For Lucan sang concerning rumor:

> An empty rumor also reached your descendants  
> And broke into the minds of the people.

(Lucan, book I, after the middle.)

Thus, with the utmost folly, blindness, and malice, the pagans praised the virtues of Christians that were known to them, but condemned those Christians because they did not know what their name signified. On the basis of this unknown name, moreover, they vitiated or corrupted their virtues, babbling that these were either nonexistent or false. Yet nothing is certainly more unjust [Col. 0980A] than to condemn something unknown to you on the basis of something you have ascertained, or to corrupt something you have recognized on the basis of something unknown. It was no less unjust, however, to condemn and kill Christians merely because of their unfamiliar name, while never wishing to learn and investigate what good or evil there was in them. Indeed, the pagans so greatly abhorred this investigation that their utterly unjust judges never wished to hear Christians when they were brought before their tribunals. This, moreover, was the second principal point of their extreme injustice, which must now be examined.

### ARTICLE II

#### With what injustice the pagans condemned Christians without allowing them to be heard or defended, merely because of their name, and killed them with the utmost cruelty

[Col. 0980B] Tertullian’s second argument against the unbridled violence of the pagans toward Christians is certainly no less forceful and important than the preceding one. It is drawn from the most certain rule governing judicial proceedings, a rule so thoroughly consonant with natural equity that, if it is disregarded, any judgment passed upon any person is utterly unjust, invalid, and null. This, as Tertullian perceptively observes, is the rule that forbids anyone to be condemned altogether undefended and unheard (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 2, p. 276). Fully convinced of the equity of this law, the pagans had ordained that anyone accused of any crime should have recourse both to his own voice, as our Author says, and to hired advocacy—that is, to patrons who, upon receiving a fee, [Col. 0980C] would defend his cause.

But they treated Christians accused of false crimes very differently. For they never granted them any opportunity to defend their innocence either by their own speech or through anyone’s advocacy. Nevertheless, as he perceptively argues, the same treatment should have been applied to the same culpability (*Ibid.*), or, as our manuscripts and ancient editions read, to the same charge (*Ibid.*). What, therefore, could be more unjust than to grant to a pagan what was just, while denying that very thing to a Christian?

Furthermore, according to the prescriptions of all divine and human laws, every person charged with any crime must not only always be heard and questioned, but inquiry must also be made—and [Col. 0980D] even extracted by judicial examination and torture—into the nature of the crime, its place and manner, its time, and who were the accomplices and those privy to it. The pagans did not deny that this was entirely equitable, for they diligently investigated all these matters whenever one of their own was accused by someone of any crime. Yet with the greatest injustice they dismissed and scorned all these things whenever the matter concerned “our indictments,” as Tertullian says (*ibid.*), that is, the denunciation and accusation of Christians. Our manuscripts therefore read *eulogiis*, that is, “commendations,” less satisfactorily—unless one can prove that he used that term ironically. Whatever the case may be, Tertullian’s meaning is plain and evident: so far were the pagans from ever investigating, in the manner equity required, [Col. 0981A] the crimes of which Christians, like everyone else, were accused, that on the contrary every investigation of them was forbidden to all judges.

For when Pliny the Younger asked the emperor Trajan what should be done with Christians, whose number was almost infinite, Trajan, says Tertullian, “replied that this class of persons should not indeed be sought out, but that, when brought forward, they ought to be punished” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 2, p. 273). Trajan’s own words in his letter to Pliny are these: “They are not to be sought out; if they are denounced and proved guilty, they are to be punished” (Pliny the Younger, book X, epistle 102, p. 377); that is, according to our Tertullian’s interpretation, if they are shown and proved actually to be Christians, they are indeed to be punished, but by no means [Col. 0981B] sought out. Eusebius cited this entire passage from Tertullian (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, ch. 33, p. 106), interpreting in Greek the words we have quoted as follows: [non-Latin text omitted]. Rufinus rendered them into Latin: “Christians are not indeed to be sought; yet if any are encountered, they are to be punished” (Rufinus, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, ch. 27). Tertullian therefore had no doubt that this edict pronounced that Christians were not to be sought out, but that as soon as they were brought before the judges and it was established that they were Christians, they were to be punished without any further investigation whatsoever. Nor was he wrong. For Trajan immediately added:

> Yet with this qualification: whoever denies that he is a Christian and makes this manifest by the act itself—that is, by supplicating our gods—[Col. 0981C] although he may have been under suspicion in the past, shall obtain pardon through repentance.

(Pliny, *ibid.*)

He therefore wished no crime of the Christians to be investigated by the judges; instead, merely because they had been brought forward and merely because of their name, he wished them to be punished without any further hearing, unless perhaps they denied that name of theirs and supplicated the false gods.

Tertullian therefore rightly exclaims: “O judgment entangled by necessity!” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 2, p. 273). He deliberately said “by necessity,” because Trajan could not explain it more clearly. Yet it is entangled because Trajan says, as our Author rightly argues (*ibid.*), that Christians must not be sought out, on the ground that they are innocent and guilty of no crime; nevertheless, he orders them to be punished as guilty persons privy to the gravest crimes. This same judgment, therefore, [Col. 0981D] “both spares and rages, overlooks and punishes” (*Ibid.*). For it makes a show of clemency by sparing Christians who were not brought before the judges, but rages against those who were brought forward, although innocent, ordering them to be punished merely because of their name.

Thus, in the entire case of the Christians, nothing else was demanded, as Tertullian further says, but “a confession of the name, not an examination of the crime” (*Ibid.*, p. 271), nor any defense of the accused. To condemn or acquit them, it was enough merely that they should either deny or confess their name—that is, that they were Christians.

He further demonstrates [Col. 0982A] how unjust this was, and how contrary to every divine, natural, and human law, from the judicial examinations that the laws command to be conducted by scourging and torture. For they ought to be employed for no other reason than that crimes may be uncovered and those accused of them may confess that they committed them. Indeed, all enactments of rulers—provided they are not tyrants—just decrees of the senate, and other laws that are not altogether unjust, command, in our Author’s words, “that the evil be brought to light, not concealed; that those who confess be condemned, not acquitted” (*Ibid.*, p. 276; and *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 2, p. 560). He wrote similarly to the judge Scapula: “What further instruction is given to you than to condemn the guilty who confess, but to send those who deny back to torture?” (Idem, *Ad Scapulam*, ch. 4.)

Nevertheless, contrary to this rule for judging defendants, prescribed with such great equity, the pagans [Col. 0982B] compelled Christians by the cruelest torments of judicial examination not to confess any crime, but to deny. They must therefore have been persuaded that the Christians were innocent and guilty of no crime, since they wished them to deny it—or else, which is wicked, they wished them, if they were guilty, to lie outright. For when tortured under examination, they confessed and openly cried out that they were Christians. But the pagans, although it was unlawful for them to torture other defendants further once they had confessed, ordered Christians to be tortured more savagely until they denied or were killed.

Tertullian therefore rightly presses them as follows:

> You see how you yourselves act contrary to your instructions, compelling those who have confessed to deny. You thus acknowledge that we are innocent, since you are unwilling to condemn us immediately upon our confession.

(Idem, *Ad Scapulam*, ch. 4.)

Certainly, through so great a violation of [Col. 0982C] their own laws, these men most manifestly condemned themselves. For they persecuted Christians without hearing or defending them, as though they were guilty of every crime against the gods, the laws, the emperors, morality, and nature itself. Therefore, while striving to force them through torture to deny such things, they assuredly confessed that those crimes were as false as the Christians’ innocence and integrity were supreme. Thus, in this dreadful persecution of those same Christians, there was nothing else, as Tertullian said, but “a battle over the Christian name” (Idem, *Apologeticus*, ch. 2, p. 279), in which those who denied were acquitted, while those who confessed were tortured.

Nor should you think that anything there was either invented or exaggerated by him. For precisely the same public protest had been made not only [Col. 0982D] by Justin Martyr, Athenagoras, and Tatian, as we have seen elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 351, 356, 471, and 523), but also by Melito, as Eusebius reports (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, ch. 26, p. 147). They openly complained that, although no name is good or evil in itself, Christians were nevertheless condemned merely on account of their designation, without their case ever being heard. Minucius Felix, as we showed above (*Dissertation on Minucius Felix’s Octavius*, ch. 8, art. 2), and Cyprian, as we shall say below, pierced the pagans with the same weapon, though turned somewhat differently.

Nor did the pagans take much care to conceal this extreme injustice, which they publicly proclaimed in the judgment issued against Christians on a tablet, such as Cicero [Col. 0983A] (*In Verrem*, book V, p. 179, line 19; oration 39, *Pro Rabirio*, p. 561, final line; oration 23, *Pro Flacco*, p. 369, line 47; oration 37, *In L. Pisonem*, p. 543) and others frequently mention. On this tablet, indeed, no crime of the Christians was recorded, as was done for others condemned to death, but only the name of Christian. “From the tablet,” Tertullian says in addressing them, “you read out that he is a Christian” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 2, p. 279), and nothing more. But since the matter stands thus, he continues, certainly: “If ‘Christian’ is the name of no crime, it is exceedingly absurd if the crime consists in the name alone” (Tertullian, *ibid.*). This is the reading in Rigault’s edition. In our manuscripts, however, it reads: “If the Christian is guilty of the name of no crime, it is exceedingly incestuous”—more recent editions have “uncertain”—“if the crime consists in the name alone.” [Col. 0983B] In some manuscripts and in printed editions earlier than Rigault: “If the Christian is guilty of no crime, the name is exceedingly hostile, if the crime consists in the name alone.” This appears to be Tertullian’s genuine reading; in other manuscripts the word “name” was transposed and the other word, “incestuous,” corrupted. For Tertullian’s meaning is that the Christian name was exceedingly hateful to the pagans, and that merely because of it, and for absolutely no other crime, they tortured and killed Christians so cruelly.

But no hatred of a name, Tertullian insists (*ibid.*, ch. 3, p. 281), can possibly be conceived unless it signifies something barbarous or inherently vicious, or unless hatred is directed toward unknown persons who are called by it. Yet the pagans could discover nothing [Col. 0983C] vicious in Christians, whom they were unwilling to hear even once. Nor was there anything barbarous in the name “Christian,” which, says Tertullian, “is derived from anointing” (*Ibid.*, p. 281). The ignorant pagans made not the slightest advance by prattling that they should be called *Chrestians*, not Christians. For this very name is derived from sweetness or kindness (*Ibid.*). We have discussed elsewhere the etymology of both names (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 17, art. 1).

### ARTICLE III

#### How absurdly the pagans alleged that Christians should be hated because of their own name or that of their founder, although no philosopher is said to hate Platonists, Epicureans, Pythagoreans, Stoics, or Academics; no physician, Erasistratus; no grammarian, Aristarchus; and no cook, Apicius—the founders of their respective schools—because of their name

[Col. 0983D] Since there is nothing clearer even than the noonday sun that the human intellect, blinded by the prejudices of false religion, does not attempt to obscure, it is certainly no wonder that the pagans tried by contentious arguments to evade or weaken the utterly invincible arguments advanced by our Author in defense of Christians. Nevertheless, those who were unwilling to hear Christians must themselves be heard, and their arguments, whatever they may be, must be examined.

They loudly asserted that there was no need to investigate the crimes of Christians, since all of them were comprehended in their one name. [Col. 0984A] But we have already shown that these crimes were utterly false. Yet suppose, if you can, that they were as true as they are false: should a murderer, or any other criminal, be condemned merely because of his name? Are not judges bound to hear him and to investigate where, when, how, and with what accomplices he committed the crime? Christians, however, were condemned contrary to these most equitable laws, as we have said. The judgment of the pagans was therefore utterly unjust (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 2, p. 279; and *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 6, p. 566).

The pagans nevertheless insisted: “The Christian sect is hated under the name, of course, of its founder” (*Ibid.*, p. 282). Should it not therefore have been examined whether that founder or his followers truly deserved to be hated? Tertullian [Col. 0984B] demonstrates most clearly that by this absurd argument the pagans cut themselves down with their own sword, as the saying goes. For they did not hate Platonists because of Plato’s name, Epicureans because of Epicurus’, Pythagoreans because of Pythagoras’, or Stoics because of Zeno’s (*Ibid.*). Yet these men, as we have shown elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation 2, ch. 17, art. 1 ff., p. 1123 ff.), had been the founders of their respective schools. Tertullian then lists the Academics, whom he evidently distinguishes from the Platonists (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 3, p. 282). Should he therefore be said to have been speaking of Arcesilaus and Lacydes, the leaders of the Middle and New Academies, as we observed in the same place? Consider, I ask, and decide.

[Col. 0984C] He continues: did not physicians, he asks, derive their name from Erasistratus, who is called “a renowned physician” by Aulus Gellius (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book XVII, ch. 11), and “the most renowned of the ancient physicians” by Macrobius (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, book VII, ch. 15)? Strabo, moreover, attested that in Smyrna, in the time of his fathers, there existed an Erasistratean school of medicine (Strabo, *Geography*, book XII, p. 580). Plutarch (*Symposium*, book IV, question 1, p. 603; book VII, question 1, p. 698; and *Life of Demetrius*, p. 907), Pliny (*Natural History*, book XXIX, ch. 1, p. 663), Valerius Maximus (book V, ch. 7, § 1), and the author of the Eusebian *Chronicle* (*Chronicle of Eusebius*, at the year 1755) also made mention of this most celebrated physician.
