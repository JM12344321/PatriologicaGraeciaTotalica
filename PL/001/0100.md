Then, if the question concerns modesty—which is the restrained moderation of the desires within the mind—Tertullian says (ibid., ch. 46, p. 500) that no comparison whatever can be drawn between Christians and gentiles. For, as he says, Pythagoras among the Thurians and Zeno among the people of Priene aimed at tyranny. We read in Laertius that Milo’s house, where Pythagoras was then staying, was burned by the people of Croton, [non-Latin text omitted] “because they feared his tyranny” (Laertius, Book VIII, *Life of Pythagoras*, § 38). Tzetzes and Iamblichus mention the kindling of this fire, by which they wished to burn Pythagoras alive, and the war waged against him and his disciples (Tzetzes, *Chiliad* 11, v. 67 ff.; Iamblichus, *Life of Pythagoras*, ch. 35, vol. I, *Apparatus*, [Col. 0932C] p. 1246 ff.). What, then, was the purpose of that fire and war, unless he was attempting revolution and plotting to seize tyrannical power—or unless, as Laertius says, others truly feared that he would attempt this?

We have already observed that Zeno of Elea, when the tyrant Demysius or Nearchus tortured him to force him to reveal some secret, doubtless concerning a conspiracy, spat his own tongue at the tyrant. We are well aware that some, such as Pliny and Valerius Maximus, attribute this strength of spirit to Anaxarchus, while others attribute it to Diomedon. But many others whom we cited in the passage just mentioned ascribe it to Zeno of Elea. One of these is Plutarch, who testifies that Zeno was Parmenides’ [non-Latin text omitted] “disciple” (Plutarch, Book I, *Against Colotes*, p. 1126), or, as others held according to Laertius, his son by birth or adoption; and that he was subjected to such torments by [Col. 0932D] the tyrant Demysius because he had laid a plot against him. Philostratus, however, says: [non-Latin text omitted] [non-Latin text omitted]. “Zeno of Elea, attempting to overthrow the tyranny of Nearchus the Mysian, was detected; and, although subjected to various tortures, he did not betray his fellow conspirators. Instead, he falsely accused those whom he knew to be most loyal to the tyrant and thereby caused them to be condemned to death. He himself liberated Mysia by making the tyranny turn against itself” (Philostratus, Book VII, [Col. 0933A] *Life of Apollonius*, ch. 1, p. 320). For the citizens, making a sudden assault, overwhelmed the tyrant with stones, as Laertius says (Laertius, Book IX, *Life of Zeno*, § 27). Let us add this testimony of the Latin Cicero: “Zeno of Elea endured everything rather than disclose his accomplices in the destruction of the tyranny.” Elsewhere, however, Cicero simply reports that he was killed under torture (Cicero, Book II, *Tusculan Disputations*, p. 146, line 2; and Book III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 252, line 28).

But because several men bore the name Zeno, there is perhaps considerable reason to doubt whether the man whom Tertullian simply calls Zeno is Zeno of Elea, who is said not to have sought tyranny but rather to have wished to overthrow it. Yet one may answer that, while plotting against the tyrant, he was also considering how to seize his kingdom. [Col. 0933B] Moreover, since opinions concerning this Zeno’s death differed greatly, what wonder is it if Tertullian, following other authorities, reported something distinctive about him? In any case, if anyone points out another Zeno to us and proves more securely that he aimed at tyranny, we shall immediately concede the point. But Zeno must still be discussed later.

The Christian, by contrast, is as far removed as possible from that desire to rule and to invade the kingdoms of others, since, as Tertullian says, he does not seek even the aedileship—that is, any magistracy or public office. As Festus notes after Varro, an *aedile* was originally so called because he had charge of sacred and private buildings. But the name was afterward transferred to other magistracies (Tertullian, [Col. 0933C] *Apology*, ch. 46, p. 500; Varro, Book IV, *On the Latin Language*, § 14; Festus, Book XX, *On the Meaning of Words*, s.v. “Aedile”).

Next Tertullian says: “If I enter into comparison concerning equanimity” (our manuscripts written in an ancient hand, as well as the early editions, read *aequanimitate*), that is, concerning the balanced condition of a calm mind, “behold, Lycurgus stands ready before us: he chose death by starvation because the Spartans had amended his laws” (Tertullian, loc. cit.), as we explained above. But the Christian, as our author says, even when condemned by the gentiles on account of his religion, preserves the same equanimity at all times and gives thanks, as we shall show later (below, ch. 16, art. 3).

Our author continues: If, with respect to fidelity, which must always be maintained toward everyone, the Christian is compared with the gentile, “Anaxagoras denied to his guests the property entrusted to him.” Does he not there allude to the story of Pericles related by Plutarch? Pericles, accused of extortion, embezzlement, and expenditure of money that had been deposited with the Prytanes, anxiously expelled Anaxagoras from the city and sent him into exile in order to clear himself (Plutarch, *Life of Pericles*, p. 169, vol. I). But the Christian, Tertullian adds, “is called faithful even toward outsiders” (Tertullian, ibid.); that is, he always preserves inviolate the pledge he has given not only to his fellow Christians but also to all people outside their community. Ambrose discusses at greater length the faithful preservation of deposits by Christians in his work *On the Duties of Ministers* and in another book; there he says: “It makes no difference whether you forswear the deposit or regret [Col. 0934A] having returned it. In either case the peril to life makes fidelity uncertain” (Ambrose, Book II, *On Duties*, ch. 36, p. 105 ff.; and Book I, *On the Death of Satyrus*, § 3, p. 1144).

Moreover, Tertullian says, “if I take my stand upon sincerity,” the gentile sins against it all the more, the more the Christian strives to attain it. For “Aristotle shamefully caused his intimate friend Hermias to depart from his place” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 46, p. 500). If we believe Laertius, this Hermias was a eunuch and king of the people of Atarneus [non-Latin text omitted] (Laertius, Book V, *Life of Aristotle*, § 3). When Aristotle came to him, he so won his friendship that he obtained as his wife Hermias’ daughter—or concubine—whom he loved, as we have noted elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1152). As testimony to his wholly [Col. 0934B] grateful disposition, Aristotle is said to have sacrificed to the woman and to have written a hymn in honor of her and Hermias, on account of which he was accused of impiety (Athenaeus, Book XV, *Deipnosophists*, ch. 16, p. 696; Suidas, s.v. [non-Latin text omitted]). But the more numerous the witnesses who confirm these facts, the more difficult it is to prove why and how Hermias was expelled by him either from his kingdom or from this life. Aristocles, a disciple of Aristotle himself, admits that Aristotle was assailed by many accusations, which he attempts there to refute (Aristocles in Eusebius, Book XIV, *Preparation for the Gospel*, p. 791 ff.). Yet, through an undoubtedly excessive partiality toward his teacher, he could certainly have concealed much. What, then, if this flattery by Aristocles was all the more suspect to Tertullian because he had learned more securely from another source that Aristotle had injured even [Col. 0934C] Hermias, his dearest friend? For Tertullian contrasts him with the Christian, who does not injure even his enemy (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 46, p. 500; ibid., chs. 31 and 37), in obedience to the divine precepts, as has often been observed, by which he is commanded to hate no one, not to repay injury in kind, to love his enemies, and to pray to God for them.

He continues: “The same Aristotle more shamefully flatters Alexander, whom he was to govern, than Plato is put up for sale by Dionysius” (ibid., ch. 46, p. 500). Some, to whom this phrase seemed rather obscure, think that *potitus* should be read there instead of *potius*. But this is futile and contradicts the evidence of all the manuscripts. For what if our author uses the adverb *potius* to mean “sooner,” “earlier,” or even “more,” so that his meaning is: [Col. 0934D] Aristotle, the tutor of Alexander the Great, as everyone knows, more readily and shamefully flattered him than Plato was wickedly put up for sale by the tyrant Dionysius? We have discussed Plato’s sale elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 554, 620, 1144; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 39, art. 10). It is no less certain that Aristotle flattered Alexander, whose ambition—to say nothing of his other vices—he not only failed to restrain but actually fostered and softened by the art of flattery: [non-Latin text omitted], says Plutarch, [non-Latin text omitted] (Plutarch, *Life of Alexander*, p. 668, vol. I).

Tertullian then continues: “Aristippus, clad in purple and beneath a great appearance of gravity, indulges in debauchery”; that is, he lived luxuriously and intemperately. Laertius records [Col. 0935A] that Dionysius ordered each person at a certain banquet to put on a purple garment and dance (Laertius, Book II, *Life of Aristippus*, § 78). Plato refused, but Aristippus readily put on the garment and began to dance, reciting the following verses, which Gregory Nazianzen and Suidas affirm to be by Euripides (Gregory Nazianzen, *Iambic Poem* 18, p. 209; Suidas, s.v. [non-Latin text omitted]):

> . . . . [non-Latin text omitted].
>
> . . . Nor amid the sacred rites of Liber  
> Is the chaste mind of the wise ever corrupted.

You may consult Gregory Nazianzen, who there compares the vices of other philosophers with the virtues of Christians (Gregory Nazianzen, loc. cit.). Moreover, speaking of the same Aristippus but suppressing his name, our Tertullian says elsewhere: “I hear that philosophy has even been practiced in purple” [Col. 0935B] (Tertullian, *On the Pallium*, ch. 4; the same, *Apology*, ch. 46, p. 500).

Finally, he added: “Hippias is killed while laying plots against the state; no Christian ever attempted this on behalf of his own people, although they had been scattered by every kind of savagery.” It is remarkable how corrupted the name of Hippias is in the manuscript codices. In our two older manuscripts it is read *Icthidias*; in two more recent Colbert manuscripts, *Icthiclias*; and in certain others, *Hidias*, which comes closer to *Hippias*, the reading presented in the printed editions. That our author is truly to be understood as speaking of Hippias can be confirmed by these words of Cicero: “The wicked Hippias, son of Pisistratus, who fell in the battle of Marathon bearing arms against his country” (Cicero, Book IX, *Letters to Atticus*, letter 12, p. 306). Herodotus, [Col. 0935C] Pausanias, Suidas, and others provide more information about him (Herodotus, Book I, § 61; Book V, § 93 ff.; Pausanias, Book I, p. 20; Suidas, s.v. [non-Latin text omitted]). But it is not so certain that this is the very man of whom Tertullian speaks as a philosopher, unless it can be proved that he devoted himself to philosophy.

Suidas mentions another Hippias, a sophist and philosopher, the disciple of Hegesidamus, who taught that the mind should be content with its own lot. Is this not the man who, as we have said elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1222), was greatly praised by Cicero and boasted that there was nothing in any art that he did not know? But if this man was the philosopher, neither Cicero nor Suidas has told us by what death he died. It will therefore undoubtedly seem more probable to many that Tertullian was speaking of the former Hippias, unless this philosopher’s [Col. 0935D] name was corrupted in his text by careless copyists.

But it is absolutely certain, as Tertullian added, that Christians never attempted anything against their country, although they were “scattered by every savagery” of the punishments inflicted upon them because of their religion—that is, torn apart and put to death (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 46, p. 500). For, taught by Christ, they imitated the simplicity of doves; harming no one, they strove to deserve well of everyone, even of those who slaughtered them with such cruelty.

Who, then, does not now easily see how rightly Tertullian (ibid.), after establishing his comparisons between gentile philosophers and Christians, concludes with these words: [Col. 0936A] “Accordingly, what similarity”—supply “do they possess” (some editions read: “What similarity, accordingly?”)—“is there between the philosopher and the Christian? Between the disciple of Greece,” where philosophers especially flourished, “and the disciple of heaven,” that is, of the true God and Christ? “What similarity is there between the philosopher, a trafficker in reputation,” or a creature of glory, as was said shortly before, “and the Christian, a trafficker in life” (Rigault’s edition and later editions read “salvation”)? “What between the philosopher, a worker only of words, and the Christian, a worker of deeds? What between the Christian, a builder of realities, and the philosopher, a destroyer? What do the philosopher, the friend of error, and the Christian, its enemy, have in common?” Rigault, however, excised these latter words on his own authority.

Then, in our unpublished manuscripts and the older printed editions, the text reads: “What similarity is there between the interpolator of truth and its restorer and expresser, between its thief and its guardian?” Rigault altered it as follows: [Col. 0936B] “The interpolator of error and the restorer of truth; its thief and its guardian?” But because that emendation rests on the authority of no manuscript, it was doubtless made by him because the words appeared rather obscure and insufficiently elegant. Yet what prevents us from declaring that the words as transcribed in our manuscripts are genuine, and that their meaning is this? The philosopher is an interpolator of the truth handed down in our Sacred Scripture because, by inserting his own errors, he gave it a new form. He is also its thief, since, as we have often said, he plucked many things from it and corrupted them. The Christian, however, is the restorer, expresser, and guardian of truth because he presents, expresses, and guards it entire.

Nor did Rigault restrain his hand there; he also removed these following words: [Col. 0936C] “Truth is more ancient than all of them, unless I am mistaken.” By what right he removed them is sufficiently clear from his other, more audacious corrections. Let it suffice for us to have noted the variant readings of Tertullian’s text, and how rightly he maintains that the Christians’ doctrine is far superior to that of the philosophers, and that their morals are purer and holier than those of any gentiles. We have already treated these comparisons in our dissertation on Minucius’ *Octavius* (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 13, art. 4).

# CHAPTER XIV

## On the Christians’ fasts, works of penitence, prayers, and solemn observance of the Lord’s Day, and how greatly they differed from the gentiles in all these matters

### ARTICLE I

#### How long and severe the Christians’ fasts were; how far removed from this severity of abstinence were the banquets and dinners of the Megarians, tribes, curiae, and decuries; the tenths and sacrificial feasts of Hercules; the Apaturia, Dionysia, and Attic mysteries; and the Serapic dinners, with a discussion of the *sparteoli* and the infamous parasites

[Col. 0936D]

Among the other virtues of the Christians, nothing shone more brightly than their care and solicitude—at once diligent and rigorous—to observe fasts, by which they differed from the gentiles no less than by their other virtues. For very often, and especially amid public calamities, they endeavored to entreat the divine mercy not only for themselves but also for their enemies, [Col. 0937A] not through a perfunctory observance but through the most rigorous practice of a stricter fast. Hear Tertullian himself: “We are parched by fasts,” he says—that is, exhausted and dried up, since at those times they ate only dry foods. For elsewhere he speaks of “late or dry foods,” and somewhat more explicitly: “We observe *xerophagies*, drying our food by excluding all meat, all gravy, and every more succulent fruit, so that we neither eat nor drink anything containing wine; we also abstain from the bath, in harmony with our dry diet” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 40, p. 478; *On Fasting*, chs. 1 and 13).

If you ask Epiphanius what this dry food and dry diet was, he will answer that it consisted of nothing but bread, salt, and water. [Col. 0937B] Throughout those days of fasting and *xerophagy*, as he calls them, Christians were not permitted to taste these foods—however unpalatable and lacking in savor they might be—at just any time of day (Epiphanius, *Exposition of the Faith*, § 22, p. 1015). Rather, “deferred from every fruit of life,” as our author again says—that is, from every kind of food (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 40, p. 487)—they took it only late, at the beginning of night. Nor is this our own explanation of these words, but that of Tertullian himself, who elsewhere says most clearly: “Abstaining from those things which, unlike certain heretics, we nevertheless do not reject but merely postpone” (the same, *On Fasting*, ch. 15); and thus “we prolong our fasts until evening.”

Indeed, according to Eusebius, Christ had taught his followers most willingly to abstain from food and wine for many days, to lead a hard and severe [Col. 0937C] mode of life, and had impelled an immense multitude not only of men but also of women to despise bodily nourishment and seek instead the rational food of divine readings (Eusebius, *Oration in Praise of Constantine*, ch. 17, p. 661). What wonder, therefore, if the same Tertullian says that they were “wasted by all that continence” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 40, p. 487), that is, so exhausted that their bodily strength failed?

But the gentiles’ dinners—prepared at lavish expense—their immoderate banquets, drinking parties, revels, and gluttonous feasts were utterly and completely at variance with this Christian frugality, abstinence from food, and fasting. First, Tertullian says, the intemperance of the Megarians is plainly rebuked by this saying of Diogenes: [Col. 0937D] “The Megarians buy provisions as though they were going to die the next day, but build as though they were never going to die” (ibid., ch. 39, p. 473). Yet if you trust Laertius, Empedocles said this of the people of Acragas (Laertius, Book VIII, *Life of Empedocles*, § 33); if Aelian, Plato said it of those same people (Aelian, Book XII, *Various History*, ch. 28); if Plutarch, Stratonicus lashed the Rhodians with this reproach (Plutarch, *On the Love of Wealth*, p. 525, vol. II). But who will deny that several people could have said and repeated it concerning several nations? Whoever these peoples were, they were exceedingly far removed from Christian sobriety and fasting.

Undoubtedly it was not concerning them but many others that our author added: “With so many tribes, curiae, and [Col. 0938A] decuries belching” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 39, p. 473)—that is, exhaling excessive food and drink that could not be digested in the stomach—“the air turns sour.” But could this, you will ask, have been written without great hyperbole? Yet what, I ask you, prevents it from being said of the public banquets customarily set out on the forum, public squares, streets, and crossroads of cities during the solemnities of certain great days, especially those honoring emperors? For then individual cities, and indeed entire cities, presented the appearance of taverns and cookshops, as we shall see below. What wonder, then, if the air turned sour from the belching of such a multitude of people exhaling food that had been taken immoderately and remained undigested? Turnebus therefore thought in vain that by the word *curiae* Tertullian meant the banquets given by curiales [Col. 0938B] in the senate-house itself (Turnebus, Book XII, *Adversaria*, ch. 13). For Tertullian must undoubtedly be understood as referring to those public banquets.

Tertullian later says: “The Salii,” whom we have discussed elsewhere, “will need a creditor when they are about to dine” (Tertullian, loc. cit.), because no one was capable of paying their expenses immediately. If, however, you do not believe us (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 20, art. 3) and wish the point to be proved to you, hear these words of Festus: “The Salii were so called from dancing; because sumptuous dinners are set before them on every day and wherever they stay, any other dinners that are magnificent are also called ‘Salian’” (Festus, s.v. “Salii”). Hence Horace sang:

> Now is the time for drinking; now with unfettered foot  
> The earth must be struck; now was the time,  
> Companions, to adorn the gods’ couch  
> [Col. 0938C] With Salian feasts.  
> (Horace, Book I, Ode 27.)

He calls rich, highly exquisite feasts prepared at great expense “Salian.” The gods’ couch was burdened with them, and afterward they were devoured by the Salii. It was for precisely this reason, as Suetonius relates, that the emperor Claudius, “struck by the savor of a luncheon being prepared in the nearby temple of Mars, abandoned the tribunal, went down to the priests, and reclined at table with them” (Suetonius, *Life of Claudius*, § 33). Apuleius, moreover, says that his horse had been filled with such an abundance of barley that it thought it had dined like a Salius (Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, Book IV, after the middle).

The Salii, however, were priests of Hercules, concerning whom Tertullian adds: [Col. 0938D] “The accountants calculated the expense of the tenths of Hercules and of the *pollucta*” (the manuscript codices read *pollinctorum*); the same printed and other unprinted codices read “stable-keepers” instead of “accountants” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 39, p. 473). *Pollinctores* were those who anointed the bodies of the dead and arranged funerals. But Tertullian does not appear to have spoken of them. Therefore *polluctorum* is the better reading, as can be confirmed by the following verses of Plautus, which shed no little light upon this passage:

> He must come here as quickly as possible,  
> So that I may offer a tenth part to Hercules.  
> (Plautus, *Stichus*, act II, scene 1, v. 80.)

For the gentiles, as our author had said above, consecrated a tenth part of their goods to Hercules, placed it upon his altar, and afterward [Col. 0939A] feasted magnificently. Hence the same Plautus says: “Purchase provisions lavishly for the sacrificial feast” (the same, *Mostellaria*, act II, scene 4, v. 11). Festus says: “To offer goods as a *polluctum* . . . spelt, meal, wine. To Hercules, however, they offered every kind of food as a *polluctum*” (Festus, s.v. “Pollucere”); that is, they offered a tenth part. On this tithe, sacrifice, and feast of Hercules, see Varro, Plutarch, and Macrobius, who also calls it a “lavish sacrificial dinner” (Varro, Book V, *On the Latin Language*, p. 58; Plutarch, *Roman Questions*, p. 267; Macrobius, Book III, *Saturnalia*, chs. 12, 15, and 17).

The accountants who kept the accounts of cities and provinces, as is evident from the Theodosian Code, where an entire title concerns accountants, would calculate how great the expense of this dinner was. Our author therefore means that the expense of these dinners was so great that [Col. 0939B] only public accountants, accustomed to calculating the largest sums of expenditure, could scarcely reckon it. This reading is therefore preferable to “stable-keepers,” that is, innkeepers and tavern-keepers, who in their establishments were accustomed more easily to calculate expenses, whether great or small, incurred by their diners.

Tertullian continues: “At the Apaturia, the Dionysia, and the Attic mysteries a levy of cooks is proclaimed” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 39, p. 473). The [non-Latin text omitted] was a solemn festival celebrated annually by the Athenians for three days in the month of October. On the first day, the members of the tribes or associations enrolled in this solemnity dined sumptuously together. On the second day they sacrificed to Jupiter and Minerva. Finally, on the third day, they registered boys and girls in the curiae. [Col. 0939C] Herodotus recorded that Homer, invited by the associates to this dinner, actually reclined at their table. Elsewhere the same historian also mentions this festival (Herodotus, *Life of Homer*, § 29 ff.; and Book I, § 147), concerning which see also Pollux, Suidas, Petrus Castellanus, Joannes Fasoldus, and especially Joannes Meursius (Pollux, Book VI; Suidas; Castellanus and Fasoldus, *On Greek Festivals*; Meursius, *Festivals of Greece*, s.v. [non-Latin text omitted]).

The Dionysia, or [non-Latin text omitted], were festivals celebrated by the Attic people in the eighth month in honor of Dionysus or Bacchus; they were also called the Bacchanalia. Tertullian called them both the Dionysia and the Attic mysteries, in order to signify that these were the same festivals for which the Athenians prepared dinners with such an abundance of the most delicate foods that more highly skilled cooks had to be selected to prepare them.

[Col. 0939D] Tertullian then returns to the Romans, who celebrated the festivals of Serapis with equal or even greater intemperance in dining: “At the smoke of a Serapic dinner the *sparteoli* will be aroused” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 39, p. 473). The *sparteoli* were a certain class of soldiers who, according to an ancient commentator on Juvenal, kept watch at Rome for the protection of the city, equipped with hooks and water for extinguishing fires (Juvenal, Satire 14, v. 313). According to some, they were so called from their tunics and footwear, or more probably from the vessels made of esparto grass in which they carried water. But Suetonius, if Casaubon is to be believed, declared in the following words that they had been instituted by the emperor Augustus: “As a safeguard against fires, he devised nocturnal watches and a corps of [Col. 0940A] watchmen” (Suetonius, Book II, *Life of Augustus*, § 30).

Who, then, does not easily understand that Tertullian is speaking of these men, who, since they were common and lowly people and walked through the city at night, were aroused by the smoke of the sumptuous dinner laid out for the festival of Serapis and ran to it? These festivals and sacred rites, called the Serapia or Serapida, or the rites of Serapis or Isis, were customarily celebrated in the month of April. But we have discussed them in our preceding dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 22, art. 1).

Undoubtedly worse than the *sparteoli* were the parasites, the most disgraceful attendants at every table. They “aspire,” our author says, “to the glory of a servile liberty under contract” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 39, p. 475); that is, as may be gathered from Cicero, Suetonius, [Col. 0940B] and others, for something like a stipend, price, or payment in return for having their bellies fattened amid insults (Cicero, Book I, *On Duties*, p. 372, line 3; Suetonius, Book III, *Life of Tiberius*, § 7). In another book he calls them “laborers for the belly, whom a patience inspired by the devil binds to insulting patrons through the subjection of their liberty” (Tertullian, *On Patience*, near the end, ch. 16). But Terence depicts this breed of utterly worthless men more vividly than anyone else. If you do not begrudge the trouble of consulting him, you will find there an explanation of whatever appears obscure in Tertullian’s words (Terence, *Eunuch*, act II, scene 2).

Christians utterly abhorred all these most intemperate and wanton dinners and banquets of the gentiles. They mortified their bodies with such long and rigorous fasts, as we have said, and their own [Col. 0940C] dinner was wholly frugal and exceedingly modest, as we shall show later.

### ARTICLE II

#### How different were the penitential works of the Christians and the gentiles: by the former the heavens were assailed with the sight of their misery, while the latter continually provoked the true God through impious superstitions

It was not enough for the Christians to placate God’s anger through so severe and austere an observance of fasts, not only amid public calamities but also on various other days. Rather, Tertullian says, “rolling in sackcloth and ashes” (*volvitantes* is incorrectly written in our two older manuscripts, through the misplaced addition of the letter *i*), “we batter heaven with the sight of our misery” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 40, p. 487). Thus, when they were about to entreat God, they rolled themselves in [Col. 0940D] sackcloth and ashes, just like others who performed public penance in the churches for some grave crime.

For the discipline of such persons, as our author relates elsewhere, “commands, with respect to their very clothing and food, that they lie in sackcloth and ashes, obscure the body with filth, and cast down the spirit with grief” (the same, *On Repentance*, ch. 9). And a little later: “One must dwell apart from gladness, amid the roughness of sackcloth, the horror of ashes, and the wasting of the face through fasting” (the same, ibid., ch. 11). Cyprian indeed exhorts the lapsed to repentance in these words: “One must pray more earnestly and make supplication; spend the day in mourning; pass the nights in vigils and weeping; occupy all one’s time with tearful lamentations; lie prostrate [Col. 0941A] and cling to the ground; roll in ashes, haircloth” (elsewhere: “in ashes and haircloth”), “and filth” (Cyprian, *On the Lapsed*, near the end). That not only those guilty of a crime but others too performed penance of this kind is clear from one of Celerinus’ letters published among those of Cyprian. In it he testifies that he mourned his lapsed sister’s crime in this manner: “On account of her deeds, I spent—and to this day continue to spend—the joyful season of Easter weeping, day and night, tearfully in haircloth and ashes” (the same, Letter 21).

Thus the other Christians, although conscious of no crime, nevertheless “battered heaven with the sight of their misery,” as Tertullian says, through the acts and dress of public penitents (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 40, p. 487). If the word *invidia* carries any obscurity there, some light may be cast upon it by what he says [Col. 0941B] elsewhere concerning the gentiles themselves: “In certain colonies, by an annual rite, they are clothed in sackcloth and sprinkled with ashes, and display before their idols, as supplicants, the sight of their misery . . . a Ninevite cessation of business, I suppose.” Of our holy martyrs he says: “The souls of the martyrs beneath the altar cry out to the Lord with the spectacle of their suffering: ‘How long, Lord, will you not avenge our blood upon the inhabitants of the earth?’” (the same, *On Prayer*, ch. 5). He also calls their scars “provocative of Christ’s regard,” because they had desired to conquer (the same, *On Modesty*, at the end). Indeed, he similarly said of himself: “I prefer to bring the sight of my suffering before him”—that is, before God—“by perishing according to his will, rather than provoke his anger by escaping according to my own” (the same, *On Flight in Persecution*, ch. 10).

Why say more? Statius, when lamenting his father’s death, sang:

> [Col. 0941C] Surely I shall assail the other gods above and Tartarus  
> With a still greater display of grief.  
> (Statius, Book IX of the *Silvae*, *Lament for His Father*.)

Again, in the lament for his son:

> . . . Shall I not, with the horror of my grief, assail  
> The gods above themselves and unjust Tartarus?  
> Shall I not mourn you, dear boy?  
> (Ibid.)

It is clear that the word *invidia* in all these passages is not taken in its ordinary sense of hatred and ill will, or grief at another person’s prosperity. Such emotions neither apply to God nor touch or move him to mercy. Cicero indeed acknowledges that the word *invidia* is ambiguous: “This word,” he says, “is derived from looking too intently upon another person’s fortune, as in the *Melanippus*:

> [Col. 0941D] Who has cast an envious eye upon the flower of my children?

The Latin seems incorrect, yet Accius writes excellently: for just as we say ‘to see,’ so ‘to look enviously upon the flower’ is more correctly said than ‘to envy the flower.’ Custom forbids us, but the poet maintained his right and spoke more boldly” (Cicero, Book III, *Tusculan Disputations*, p. 151, line 10). What, then, if Tertullian used the word *invidia* in the same sense as Accius, to mean the beholding, sight, and appearance of the wretched condition and dress of the Christian penitents, by which they endeavored to touch and move the divine mercy (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 40, p. 487)?

We cannot deny that the gentiles also performed certain [Col. 0942A] works of penance amid public calamities, by which they thought they could bend and appease their gods. For at such times they proclaimed rites for obtaining rain, barefoot processions, laid aside the insignia of their magistracies and ranks, and did other things, all of which we shall discuss below (below, ch. 23, art. 4). But they wrongly invoked and entreated an artificial Jupiter and others who were not gods. Provoking the true God by those impious acts and superstitions, they could undoubtedly obtain nothing from him, just as they could obtain nothing from their false gods.

### ARTICLE III

#### How the Christians prayed to God without a prompter, with hands extended toward heaven, the head uncovered, the soul innocent, and facing east; and how differently the gentiles prayed to their gods and the Persians worshiped the sun

[Col. 0942B]

By the severe penitential labors we have described, Christians prayed to God not only at times of public calamity but also on individual days, especially on appointed days, both for themselves and for others, “without a prompter,” as our author says, “but from the innermost breast” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 30, p. 443). He deliberately said “without a prompter,” because it was the gentiles’ custom for someone to lead aloud all those who were praying and to prompt them beforehand. Some think that the following verses of Statius should be referred to this custom:

> Hence the fathers’ prayers are entrusted to you, and the noble youth  
> Is governed with you as prompter, while you recount the customs  
> And deeds of those who came before.  
> (Statius, Book V, *Silvae*, v. 488.)

[Col. 0942C] But that passage does not concern any prayers recited under the leadership of a prompter. Pliny comes closer to the matter in these words: “We have seen the highest magistrates offer supplication with prescribed prayers; and, lest any word be omitted or spoken out of order, one person reads in advance from a written text, another is assigned as a supervisor to listen attentively, and yet another is appointed to order that favor be shown with the tongues”—that is, that all remain silent (Pliny, Book XXVIII, *Natural History*, ch. 2, p. 556).

If anyone objects that this passage treats of magical rites and of words then pronounced in advance, he certainly cannot deny that Plutarch speaks of the gentiles’ solemn prayers when he informs us that Numa Pompilius commanded that, whenever magistrates and priests performed any divine rite, a herald should precede them and cry out in a loud voice: “Attend to this!” [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0942D] [non-Latin text omitted] (Plutarch, vol. I, *Life of Coriolanus*, p. 225). By that cry, Plutarch says, he ordered everyone to direct their attention to the sacred rites and not to be interrupted by any work or business.

Juvenal also bears upon the point. Speaking of a woman who prayed to Janus and Vesta that Pollio might win, he sang:

> . . . She stood before the altar and did not think it shameful  
> To veil her head for a lyre-player, and repeated the words  
> Dictated to her, as custom requires.  
> (Juvenal, Satire 6, after the middle.)

If you have leisure and inclination, consult Brissonius in his book *On Formulas* (Brissonius, Book II, *On Formulas*, p. 68). Christians, however, admitted no such prompter, since they prayed to God not only with mouth and lips but from the breast, [Col. 0943A] and with the inward and sincere affection of the heart.
