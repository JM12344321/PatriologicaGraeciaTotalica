It is therefore necessary that the Apostle still be shown to them—a man whom I shall defend in the Second Epistle to the Corinthians as being such as I have known him in all his letters. In the First Epistle too, the first thing he did was to consecrate the temple of God: “Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and that the Lord dwells in you?” (I Cor. 3:16). And, to safeguard and purify the temple, he wrote a law for its sacristan: “If anyone defiles the temple of God, God will destroy him; for the temple of God is holy, which you are” (ibid. 17). Come now: whom has he ever restored to God after he was utterly defiled—that is, delivered to Satan for the destruction of the flesh—when he therefore added beneath it: “Let no one deceive himself” (ibid. 18), that is, let no one presume that one defiled can be restored to God once again? Likewise, when elsewhere—indeed, before all else—he denies that adulterers and fornicators [Col. 1010D], the effeminate, and men who lie with men will obtain the kingdom, he prefaced it with: “Do not [Note: [Col. 1010D] “Nor,” Rhen., Oberth., Leop.] be deceived” [Col. 1011A] (I Cor. 6:9–10), namely, by supposing that they will obtain it. But those from whom the kingdom is taken away are certainly not permitted the life that exists within the kingdom.

He presses the point as well: “And such indeed you were; but you have been washed, but you have been sanctified in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ and in the Spirit of our God” (ibid. 11). The more he makes those offenses antecedent to the reception of the washing, the more he establishes them as unpardonable after the washing, since it is not permissible to be washed a second time.

Recognize Paul also in what follows, an immovable pillar of discipline: “Foods are for the belly, and the belly for foods; but God will bring both it and them to an end; the body, however, is not for fornication, but for God” (ibid. 13). “Let us make man,” God says, “in our image and likeness”; and “God made man in the image and likeness of God he made him” (Gen. 1:26–27). “The Lord is for the body” (I Cor. 6:13); for “the Word became [Col. 1011B] flesh” (John 1:14). “But God both raised the Lord and will raise us through his power” (I Cor. 6:14), namely because of the body’s union with him. And therefore: “Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ?” (ibid. 15), because Christ also is God’s temple: “Destroy this temple, and I will raise it up in three days” (John 2:19). “Shall I take away the members of Christ and make them members of a prostitute? Do you not know that he who is joined to a prostitute becomes one body? ‘For the two,’ it says, ‘will be one flesh.’ But he who is joined to the Lord is one spirit. Flee fornication” (I Cor. 6:15–18).

If it can be revoked by pardon, why should I flee it, since I may become an adulterer again? I shall have gained nothing if I flee it; I shall become one body with the person to whom I shall be joined through intercourse. “Every offense that a man commits is outside the body; but he who fornicates sins against his own body” (ibid. [Col. 1011C] 18). And lest you seize upon this saying as a license for fornication, as though you would sin against something that is your own rather than the Lord’s, he takes you away from yourself and attaches you to Christ, just as he had determined: “And you are not your own,” immediately adding: “For you were bought with a price,” namely, the Lord’s blood; “glorify and bear the Lord in your body” (ibid. 19–20).

Consider whether the man who commands this has pardoned one who dishonored the Lord and cast him out of his own body—and by incest, at that. If you wish to drink in the Apostle’s whole teaching, so as to understand with how mighty an axe of judgment he cuts down, uproots, and clears away the entire forest of lusts, permitting nothing from the fallen stock [Note: [Col. 1011D] “Remaining stock,” Obert., Seml.] to sprout, behold him desiring souls to fast even from the lawful fruit of nature—I mean the fruit of marriage. “Now concerning the matters about which you wrote: it is good for a man not to touch a woman; but because of fornication, [Col. 1011D] let each man have his own wife; let the husband render what is due to his wife, and the wife to her husband” (I Cor. 7:1 ff.).

Who does not know that he reluctantly loosened the clasp of this “good,” in order to forestall fornication? If he has granted, or does grant, indulgence to anyone for fornication, he has certainly undermined the purpose of his remedy; and henceforth he will be held responsible for bridling marriages with continence, if fornication—the reason for which they are permitted—is not to be feared; for what is pardoned will not be [Col. 1012A] feared. And yet he declares that he has granted the use of marriage as an indulgence, not commanded it, for he wishes everyone to cleave to himself (ibid. 7). If even lawful things are granted as indulgences, who can hope that unlawful ones will be?

To the unmarried and widows also he says that it is good to persevere after his example; but if they should fail, they should marry, because it is better [Note: [Col. 1011D] “It is preferable,” Lat.] to marry than to burn (ibid. 8–9). With which fires, I ask, is it worse to burn: those of desire, or those of punishment? Yet if fornication possesses pardon, its desire does not burn; but it is more characteristic of the Apostle to provide against the fires of punishment. But if it is punishment that burns, then fornication has no pardon, since punishment awaits it.

Meanwhile, in prohibiting divorce, he provides instead either perseverance in widowhood or the reconciliation of peace, according to the Lord’s command against adultery, because “he who dismisses his wife except on account of adultery causes [Col. 1012B] her to commit adultery; and he who marries a woman dismissed by her husband commits adultery” (Matt. 5:32). How many remedies the Holy Spirit institutes to prevent the renewed commission of that which he does not will to be pardoned anew!

Again, if he says everywhere that it is best for a man to remain as he is: “Are you bound to a wife? Do not seek release,” lest you give adultery a place. “Are you released from a wife? Do not seek a wife,” so that you may preserve your opportunity. “But even if you marry a wife, you do not sin; and if a virgin marries, she does not sin; nevertheless, such persons will have affliction of the flesh” (I Cor. 7:26–28). Here too he permits by way of forbearance. Moreover, he establishes that “the time has been shortened,” so that “those who have wives should be as though they had none” (ibid. 29); for “the fashion of this world is passing away” (ibid. 31), since it now no longer demands: “Increase and multiply” (Gen. 1:28).

Thus he wishes us to live free of anxiety, because the unmarried [Col. 1012C] care for the things of the Lord, how they may please God, while the married think about the things of the world, how they may please a spouse (I Cor. 7:32–34). Thus he declares that the man who preserves his virgin does better than the man who gives her in marriage (ibid. 40); thus too he judges happier the woman who, after losing her husband and entering the faith, [Note: [Col. 1011D] “Entering the faith.” Tertullian adopts Montanist views, thus condemning the marriage of a woman who became a widow after entering the faith. EDD., from PAM.] has cherished the opportunity of widowhood; thus [Note: [Col. 1012D] “If,” Oberth., Seml.] he commends all these counsels of continence as divine: “I think,” he says, “that I too have the Spirit of God” (ibid.).

Who is this most audacious champion of all immodesty, this altogether faithful advocate of adulterers, fornicators, and the incestuous, who has undertaken this case against the Holy Spirit in order to honor them, so that he may recite false testimony concerning his Apostle? Paul granted no such indulgence, for he strives to erase the whole necessity of the flesh even under honorable titles. He grants indulgence, certainly, not to adulteries but to marriages; [Col. 1012D] he spares, certainly, matrimony, not debauchery; he attempts not even to pardon nature, lest he flatter guilt; he labors to restrain the intercourse of blessing, lest the intercourse of curse be excused. This alone remained for him: to purify the flesh at least from filth, for from stains he cannot purify it.

But this is the usual practice of the perverse, the ignorant, and heretics—and now of all the *psychici* as well—to seize upon the occasion of some ambiguous chapter and arm themselves against [Col. 1013A] an army of pronouncements drawn from the entire body of Scripture.

## CHAPTER XVII

Summon him to the apostolic battle-line; examine his Epistles. All of them take the field for modesty, chastity, and holiness; all hurl their weapons against the business of luxury, wantonness, and lust. What, finally, does he write to the Thessalonians? “For our exhortation was not from deceit, nor from uncleanness” (I Thess. 2:3); and: “This is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from fornication; that each one know how to possess his own vessel in sanctification and honor, not in the lust of desire, like the nations that do not know God” (I Thess. 4:3–5).

Here he weighs the gravity of fornication and proves it to be the greatest offense from the Apostle. By this testimony, certainly, those are convicted who exempt it from sin in order to indulge their disease and vice. Modesty bids that no one be identified here. What, moreover, do the Romans learn more emphatically than not to abandon the Lord after faith? “What, then, shall we say? Shall we persevere [Note: [Col. 1013D] “Shall we endure,” Gelen.] in transgression, so that grace may abound? Far be it! We who have died to transgression, how shall we still live in it? For in that he died to transgression, he died once; but in that he lives, he lives to God. Therefore, since Christ died once, no one who has died [Note: [Col. 1014D] “After” absent from Seml.] after Christ to transgression—and especially to so grave a transgression—can come to life again in it; or, if fornication and adultery can be committed anew, Christ too will be able to die anew.

The Apostle presses on, forbidding transgression to reign in our mortal body (ibid. 12), whose weakness of flesh he knew. “For just as you presented your members as servants to uncleanness and iniquity unto iniquity, so now present them as servants to righteousness unto [Col. 1013D] sanctification” (ibid. 13). For although he denied that good dwelt in his flesh (Rom. 7:18), that was according to the law of the letter under which he had been; according to the law of the Spirit, however, to which he attaches us, he liberates us from the weakness of the flesh: “For the law,” he says, “of the Spirit of life has set you free from the [Col. 1014A] law of transgression and death” (Rom. 8:2).

Although he may seem in part to be arguing from Judaism, he nevertheless directs toward us the integrity and fullness of the disciplines, for our sake who were laboring under the law through the flesh: God sent his Son in the likeness of the flesh of transgression, and on account of transgression condemned transgression in the flesh; “so that,” he says, “the right requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the Spirit. For those who walk according to the flesh mind the things of the flesh; and those who walk according to the Spirit, the things of the Spirit” (ibid. 3–5).

Moreover, he affirmed that the mind of the flesh is death (ibid. 6). Then he called it enmity even toward God, and said that those who are in the flesh—that is, in the mind of the flesh—cannot please God (ibid. 7). And: “If you live according to the flesh,” he says, “you are going to die” [Col. 1014B] (ibid. 13). What do we understand by the mind of the flesh and the life of the flesh, if not whatever it is shameful to pronounce? For the Apostle would have named the other things of the flesh.

Likewise, when reminding the Ephesians of their former state, he admonishes them concerning the future: “Among whom we too once conducted ourselves, carrying out the desires and pleasures of the flesh” (Eph. 2:3). Finally, censuring those who had denied what they were—Christians, that is—because they had delivered themselves over to the practice of every uncleanness, he says: “But you did not so learn Christ” (Eph. 4:20). And again he says: “Let him who used to steal steal no longer” (ibid. 26–28). So too, let him who used to commit adultery commit adultery no longer; and let him who used to fornicate fornicate no longer. He would have added these also if he had been accustomed to extend pardon to such persons, or had wished it to be extended at all—he who, unwilling to be defiled even by a word, says: “Let no [Col. 1014C] foul speech proceed from your mouth” (ibid. 29).

Likewise: “But let fornication and all uncleanness not even be named among you, as becomes saints”—so far is it from being excused—“knowing this, that no fornicator or unclean person has the kingdom of God. Let no one deceive you with empty words. Because of this the wrath of God comes upon the children of unbelief” (Eph. 5:3–5). Who deceives with empty words, if not one who publicly preaches that adultery is pardonable? He does not see that the Apostle has even dug away its foundations by restraining drunkenness and revelry, as here too: “And do not become drunk with wine, wherein is debauchery” (ibid. 18).

He also shows the Colossians which members they are to mortify upon earth: fornication, uncleanness, lust, evil desire, and foul speech (Col. 3:5). Now set against so many and such great pronouncements that single passage to which you cling: [Col. 1014D] [Note: [Col. 1014D] “The few are overshadowed by the many, the doubtful by the certain, the obscure by the manifest.” They are darkened and hidden, so that the few, compared with the many; the doubtful, compared with the certain; and the obscure, compared with the manifest, are no longer visible at all. LE PR.] “The few are overshadowed by the many, the doubtful by the certain, the obscure by the manifest.” Even if it were certain that the Apostle had granted fornication to that Corinthian, it would be another case in which, for reasons belonging to the time, he once acted against his own rule [Col. 1015A] [Note: [Col. 1015D] “For reasons belonging to the time,” etc. He wrongly asserts that, if we admit pardon was granted to the Corinthian, it was done for reasons belonging to the time, as was Timothy’s circumcision; for the two matters have nothing in common. ## CHAPTER XVIII

“But these things,” he says, “will pertain to the prohibition of all immodesty [Note: [Col. 1015D] “And the adjudication of all modesty.” A praetor *addicit*—“adjudicates”—when he orders that something belong to someone as his property. RIG.] and to the adjudication of all modesty, while nevertheless leaving room for pardon. Pardon is not immediately denied if offenses are condemned, since the time for pardon may coincide with the condemnation that excludes it.”

It followed that the *psychici* would think this too; and therefore we have reserved for this place those testimonies that even from antiquity were directed expressly toward denying ecclesiastical communion in cases of this kind. For Solomon too, in the Proverbs, which we call *Paroemiae*, speaks specifically of an adulterer who can nowhere be expiated: “But the adulterer,” he says, “through deficiency of understanding acquires the destruction of his soul; [Col. 1015B] he sustains pains and dishonors. His disgrace will not be abolished forever. For indignation full of jealousy will not spare a man on the day of judgment” (Prov. 6:32–36).

If you suppose this was said of a pagan, you have certainly already heard with regard to the faithful, [Note: [Col. 1015D] “In vain.” Latinius conjectures “through Isaiah,” very plausibly, unless the reading “in vain” is confirmed by the authority of ancient manuscripts, so that it means “vainly, fruitlessly.” RIG.] in vain [Note: [Col. 1015C] “Through Isaiah,” Lat., Leop.; “through the cause,” Oberth., Seml.]: “Depart from their midst and be separated, and do not touch what is unclean” (II Cor. 6:17).

RIG.]—this concerns the church of evildoers—“and I will not sit with the ungodly”; and, “I will wash my hands with the innocent and encircle your altar, Lord” (Ps. 30:4–5), [Note: [Col. 1015D] “So that he alone might be many.” Once again he lashes the Catholics [Col. 1016D] and consoles himself for his schism, as though, surrounded by his own innocence, he alone were in God’s sight the equivalent of many. Instructed by this, therefore, the Apostle too says: “I wrote to you in the Epistle not to associate with fornicators—not, certainly, with the fornicators of this world,” and the rest: “otherwise you would have had to leave the world. A little [Note: [Col. 1015D] “The medicinal Eucharist, namely,” Fran.] leaven, certainly, ruins the taste of the whole lump (I Cor. 5:9 ff.).

Likewise, to Timothy: “Lay hands hastily on no one, nor share in the offenses of others” (I Tim. 5:22). And more insistently still: “Do not share in the fruitless works of darkness; rather, even convict them, since the things done by them in secret are shameful even to declare” (ibid. 11). What is more shameful than acts of immodesty?

Moreover, if he commands the Thessalonians to withdraw even from a brother who walks idly (II Thess. 3:6), how much more from a fornicator? For these are the carefully considered provisions [Note: [Col. 1015D] “Counsels,” Obert., Leop.] of Christ, who loves the Church [Col. 1016B] and delivered himself for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing of water in the word, that he might present to himself a glorious Church, having neither spot nor wrinkle—certainly, after the washing—but holy and without reproach (Eph. 5:25–27): thereafter, namely, [Note: [Col. 1016D] “Without the wrinkle of old age.” This too is directed against the Catholics, who opposed their own antiquity to the novelty of Montanus, as we observed above. RIG.] without the wrinkle of old age [Note: [Col. 1015D] “Vanity,” Lat.; “truth,” Fran.], as a virgin; without the stain of fornication, as a bride; without the disgrace of vileness, as one cleansed.

What [Note: [Col. 1015D] “But,” Ven.] if you should undertake to answer here too that communion is indeed taken away from sinners—especially those polluted in the flesh—but is presently to be restored, namely, on the ground of repentance, in accordance with that mercy of God by which he prefers the sinner’s repentance to his death (Ezek. 33:11)? For this foundation of your opinion must be battered down everywhere.

We therefore say: if it had accorded with divine mercy that a manifestation [Note: [Col. 1016C] Thus Lat. emends the text: “if it had thus been fitting for restoration to be granted even to his own.”] of itself should be granted even to those who have fallen after faith [Col. 1016C], then [Note: [Col. 1016D] “Or,” Lat.] the Apostle would have said: “Do not share in the works of darkness, unless they have repented”; and, “With such persons do not even take food, unless they have first wiped clean the sandals of the brethren by rolling at their feet”; and, “He who defiles the temple of God, God will destroy, unless he has shaken from his head in the Church the ashes of every hearth.” For he ought similarly to have qualified what he had condemned, since [Note: [Col. 1016D] “Where,” Oberth., Seml., Leop.] he would have condemned it only [Note: [Col. 1016D] “Only” absent from Ven.] conditionally, if he had condemned it with temporal and conditional, rather than perpetual, severity.

But since in all his Epistles he forbids the admission [Col. 1017A] of such a person after faith and drives one already admitted away from communion, without hope of any condition or term, he supports our position more strongly, showing that the repentance the Lord prefers to the sinner’s death is that which occurs before faith and before baptism—the sinner being washed only once through the grace of Christ, who died once for our sins.

For the Apostle establishes this even in his own person. When he affirms that Christ came for this purpose, to save sinners, of whom he himself had been the first, what does he add? “And I obtained mercy because, being ignorant, I acted in unbelief” (I Tim. 1:16). Thus that mercy of God which prefers the sinner’s repentance to his death concerns those who are still ignorant and still unbelieving, for whose deliverance Christ came—not those who have already known God and [Col. 1017B] learned the sacrament of faith.

But if God’s mercy applies to those still ignorant and unbelieving, then repentance too invites mercy to itself—while preserving that form of repentance after faith which, for lighter offenses, may obtain pardon from the bishop, but for greater and unpardonable offenses from God alone.

## CHAPTER XIX

But how long shall we speak of Paul, when even John seems somehow to applaud the opposing side? It is alleged that in the Apocalypse he manifestly assigned the aid of repentance to fornication, where the Spirit commands the angel of the Thyatirans that he has this against him: that he tolerates the woman [Note: [Col. 1017C] “The woman Jezebel,” Paris.] Jezebel, who calls herself a prophetess and teaches and seduces my servants to fornicate and to eat things sacrificed to idols. “And I granted her a space of time [Col. 1017C] to enter upon repentance, but she does not wish to enter upon it” concerning fornication. “Behold, I shall cast her onto a bed, and her adulterers with her into the greatest affliction, unless they repent of her works” (Apoc. 2:20–22).

It is well, however, that this accords with the Apostles and the rules both of faith and discipline. “For whether I,” he says, “or they, so we preach” (II Cor. 15:11). The whole sacrament, therefore, depends upon believing that John granted nothing Paul denied. Whoever observes this equality of the Holy Spirit will be led by the Spirit himself into John’s meanings.

For he was introducing covertly into the Church a heretical woman who had undertaken to teach what she had learned from the Nicolaitans, and was covertly introducing her into [Col. 1018A] the Church; with good reason he urged her to repentance. For who doubts that a heretic deceived by instruction, who afterward recognizes his fall and expiates it by repentance, both obtains pardon and [Note: [Col. 1017D] “Is restored to the Church.” A person is said to be restored to the Church when the Church is restored to him—that is, communion in ecclesiastical peace. RIG.] is restored to the Church?

Hence among us the heretic, being equal to a pagan—indeed, worse than a pagan—is admitted only when [Note: [Col. 1017D] “Through the baptism of truth.” Although he rightly asserts that a heretic deceived by instruction obtains pardon after recognizing his fall and expiating it through repentance, he nevertheless adds “purified through the baptism of truth,” and thus . . . he followed the error of those who thought that heretics coming to the Church had to be baptized. PAM.] purified through the baptism of truth from both persons [Note: [Col. 1017D] “Purified from both persons.” For pagans baptized by heretics were regarded as pagans—that is, as something worse than pagans because of the heretics. Therefore, if they asked to be received into the orthodox Church, they were baptized anew, so that they might be purified from both persons: the former pagan and the superimposed heretic. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1017C] “Name,” Jun., Lat.].

Or, if you are certain that this woman, after living in the faith, afterward lapsed into heresy, so that you may claim pardon through repentance for her not as a heretic but as a faithful woman who sinned [Note: [Col. 1017D] “He may claim,” Seml., Oberth.], then [Note: [Col. 1017D] “Let her indeed repent, but so as to bring her adultery to an end.” Let her repent in such a way that she ceases from adultery; for, as he said above, cessation of the offense is the root of pardon. For thus he interprets the Apostle’s words. RIG.] let her indeed repent, but so as to bring her adultery to an end; she is not, however, to obtain restoration as well. For this will be the repentance that we too acknowledge to be due all the more, though we reserve the matter of pardon to God.

Finally, that same Apocalypse in its later passages [Col. 1018B] decreed that the shameless and fornicators, like the cowardly, unbelieving, murderers, sorcerers, and idolaters—[Note: [Col. 1018D] “Who have been such a thing while in the faith.” That is, those who committed such a sin after baptism. RIG.] those who have been such a thing while in the faith [Note: [Col. 1018C] “Has been,” Fran.]—should enter the lake of fire, without any conditional form of condemnation. For it cannot appear to be speaking about pagans when it has declared concerning the faithful: “Those who overcome will possess these things by inheritance [Note: [Col. 1018D] “This inheritance,” Paris.], and I shall be their God, and they shall be sons to me”; and then added: “But for the cowardly, unbelieving, [Note: [Col. 1018D] “The shameless.” The author uses *propudiosos* to translate the Greek ἐβδελυγμένοις, for which the Vulgate translator has “the accursed”; and he cautiously omitted “sinners and liars,” because not much later he again acknowledges certain pardonable sins, including lying either from embarrassment or necessity. Likewise again: “Blessed are those who act according to the commandments, so that they may have authority over the tree of life and may enter through the gates into the holy city. Outside are the dog, the sorcerer, the fornicator, and the murderer” (Apoc. 22:14–15)—certainly those who do not act according to the commandments; for it belongs to those who were inside [Col. 1018C] to be cast outside. Moreover, the preceding question was: “What have I to do with judging those who are outside?” (I Cor. 5:12).

They immediately seize upon John’s Epistle as well. It is said: “The blood of his Son cleanses us from every offense” (I John 1:7). Therefore we shall sin always and in every manner, if he always cleanses us from every offense; or, if not always, then not after faith either; and if not from every offense, then not from fornication either.

But where did he begin? He had previously declared that God is light and that there is no darkness in him, and that we lie if we say we have communion with him while walking in darkness [Col. 1019A]. “But if,” he says, “we walk in the light, we shall have communion with him, and the blood of Jesus Christ our Lord cleanses us from every offense” (ibid. 5 ff.). Do we therefore sin while walking in the light, and shall we be cleansed while sinning in the light? By no means. For he who sins is not in the light but in darkness.

Hence he also shows how we shall be cleansed from offense while walking in the light, in which an offense cannot be committed: so clearly does he say that we are cleansed, not insofar as we sin, but insofar as we do not sin. For while walking in the light and not sharing in darkness, we shall live as persons cleansed—not by laying aside an offense, but by not admitting one. For this is the power of the Lord’s blood: after it has cleansed people from offense and thereafter established them in the light, it thereafter keeps them clean, provided that they persevere in walking in the light.

“But he adds,” you say [Col. 1019B]: “If we say that we have no offense, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our offenses, he is faithful and just to forgive them to us and to cleanse us from every unrighteousness” (ibid. 8–9). Does he say “from uncleanness”? Or if so, then also from idolatry. But the meaning is otherwise.

For behold, once again: “If we say,” he says, “that we have not sinned, we make him a liar, and his word is not in us” (ibid. 10). More emphatically still: “Little children, I have written these things to you so that you may not sin; and if you do sin, we have as advocate before God the Father Jesus Christ the righteous, and he is the propitiation for our offenses” (I John 2:1–2).

“According to these words,” you say, “it will be established both that we sin and that we possess pardon.” What, then, will happen when, as I proceed, I find something different? For he denies that we sin at all and treats this point at great [Col. 1019C] length, so as to grant nothing of the kind. He sets forth that offenses were erased once by Christ and would have no pardon afterward; in this [Note: [Col. 1019D] “Which,” Seml., Oberth.] sense he entrusts us to the admonition of chastity:

“Everyone,” he says, “who has this hope purifies himself, because he too is pure. Everyone who commits an offense also commits iniquity, and offense is iniquity. And you know that he was manifested in order to take away offenses”—those, certainly, which were committed up to that point, since he adds: “Everyone who remains in him does not sin; everyone who sins has neither seen nor known him. Little children, let no one deceive you. Everyone who practices righteousness is righteous, just as he is righteous. He who commits an offense is from the devil, because the devil sins from the beginning. For this purpose the Son of God was manifested, that he might dissolve the works of the devil.”

And indeed he dissolved them, liberating man through the washing and canceling [Col. 1019D] the written bond of death. “And therefore everyone who is born of God does not commit an offense, because the seed of God remains in him; and he cannot sin, because he has been born of God. In this the sons of God and the sons of the devil are manifest” (I John 3:3–10). In what respect, unless the former do not sin from the time they were born of God, while the latter sin because they are from the devil, just as though they had never been born of God?

[Col. 1020A] But if he says, “He who is not righteous is not from God,” and likewise he who is not chaste, how will one who has ceased to be from God become so again? It follows, therefore, that we must say John contradicted himself: in the first chapter of his Epistle (1:8), indeed, denying that we are without offense, but now laying down the rule that we are not to sin at all; and there, indeed, soothing us with one statement concerning pardon, but here strictly denying that those who have sinned are sons of God.

Far be it! For neither have we ourselves fallen away from the distinction between offenses from which we digressed. Here too John commended that distinction: there are certain offenses of daily incursion to which we are all exposed. For to whom does it not happen either to become unjustly angry, and beyond sunset; or even to strike someone; or readily to curse; or to swear rashly; or to break the faith of an agreement; or to lie from embarrassment or necessity? [Col. 1020B] [Note: [Col. 1019D] “In business, in duties, in trade.” From this it is clear that even those early Christians were human beings—namely, liable to these more human failings. RIG.] In business, in duties, in trade, in food, in sight, and in hearing, how greatly are we tempted, so that if there were no pardon for these things, salvation would belong to no one! For these, therefore, there will be pardon through Christ, the Father’s intercessor.

But there are also offenses opposed to these, as being more serious and deadly, which cannot receive pardon: murder, idolatry, fraud, denial, blasphemy, and certainly adultery and fornication, as well as any other violation of God’s temple. [Note: [Col. 1019D] “For these Christ will no longer be an intercessor.” Augustine’s opinion was more humane, in the book *On Faith and Works* [Col. 1020D]: “But those who suppose,” he says, and so forth, “make compensation by alms” [the reading is uncertain]; nevertheless, they should not doubt that three sins are mortal and require excommunication until they are healed by a humbler repentance: immodesty, idolatry, and murder. RIG.] For these Christ will no longer be an intercessor. One who has been born of God will not commit them at all, since he will cease to be a son of God if he commits them.

Thus John’s account of the difference will remain coherent, as he establishes [Note: [Col. 1019D] “John . . . establishing,” Wouw.’s manuscript.] the distinction between offenses, at one moment affirming and at another denying that the sons of God sin. For he was looking ahead [Col. 1020C] to the conclusion of his letter and preparing these meanings for it, since at the end he was to say more explicitly:

“If anyone knows that his brother is committing an offense not unto death, he shall ask, and the Lord will give life to him who sins not unto death. There is an offense unto death; I do not say that anyone should ask concerning it” (I John 5:16).

He himself also remembered that Jeremiah had been forbidden by God to intercede for a people committing mortal offenses (Jer. 7:16; 11:14; 14:11). “Every unrighteousness is an offense, and there is an offense unto death. But we know that everyone who is born of God does not sin” (I John 5:17–18)—that is, does not commit the offense that is unto death.

Thus nothing now remains except either to deny that adultery and fornication are mortal offenses, or to confess that they are unpardonable, since it is not permitted even to pray for them.
