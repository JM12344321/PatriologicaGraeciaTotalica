**11.** With IMP. CAES. L. SEPT. SEVERUS PERTINAX AUGUSTUS II and D. CLODIUS SEPT. ALBINUS CAESAR II as consuls, in the ninth year [Col. 0051C] of St Victor, Roman pontiff, and the second year of Severus himself, it is likely that TERTULLIAN was still practicing as an advocate at Carthage. To this year must be referred his words in the book *To Scapula*: “Thus we are also defamed in matters concerning the emperor's majesty; yet no Christian could ever be found among the Albinians, Nigrians, or Cassians. The very people who until the day before had sworn by their guardian spirits, and who had both sacrificed and vowed sacrifices for their safety, were discovered to be their enemies” (*To Scapula* 2).

For in this year Imperator Caesar C. Pescennius Niger Justus Augustus was killed near Cyzicus—or Issus according to Herodian. After this Severus immediately killed those senators who had supported Niger's party and had served with Niger under the title of generals or tribunes. He killed all the others as well, except the soldiers, whom he received after offering them impunity, [Col. 0051D] and for the same reason deprived the Antiochenes of their privileges and the Neapolitans of civic rights. Several kings of barbarians, according to Onuphrius, who had aided Niger were afterward defeated by Severus; hence he was acclaimed imperator II, III, and IV. Our author calls them all Nigrians. This last point confirms our opinion that the Arabs, Parthians, and Adiabenians were defeated in this year; the ancient inscriptions on coins and marble quoted above therefore belong here, as do the author's words “the barbarians shut out” (*On the Pallium* 2).

Indeed, constitutions issued under these consuls in the names of Severus and Antoninus Augusti, dated 25 February, 21 April, 7 July, 26 September, and 22 October, confirm what we said above about Antoninus as colleague in the Empire from the beginning. They survive in Code 8.14.1; 6.2.2; 2.1.2; 2.24.1; and 8.16.1. We judge that the Jewish and Samaritan war, which we mentioned above from Eusebius and which Freculphus of Lisieux also reports, belongs to this year; Severus was accordingly offered the cognomen Judaicus. Our author seems in part to look back to it when speaking of the Jews: “Scattered, wandering, exiles from their own sky and soil, they roam through the world, without man, without God as King; they are not permitted even under the law of strangers to greet their native land with so much as a footprint” (*Apology* 21). From this I infer that the occasion of the war was their desire to return to Judaea, from which the emperor Hadrian had barred them.

Here too belongs what Nicephorus writes in *Ecclesiastical History* 5.2: “At the beginning of Severus's reign our affairs were in a fairly good condition, and the profession of the faith received daily increase, advanced by the writings and teaching of the ecclesiastical doctors whom we have named. This occurred especially in all the greatest cities: Alexandria, Antioch, Aelia, and throughout Palestine; Ephesus and Caesarea; and toward the West, Thessalonica, Athens, Corinth, Gaul, and especially Rome. Whole multitudes and entire households were coming to the faith, ready to do and suffer anything rather than act or innovate against our religion.” This gives occasion to list here the ecclesiastical writers of this age whom both Nicephorus and Eusebius mention, especially since our author undoubtedly owed his conversion to their writings and teachings: at Alexandria, Demetrius, the twelfth bishop, and the presbyters Pantaenus and his pupil Clement of Alexandria; at Antioch, Serapion, the ninth bishop; at Jerusalem, Gordius, the thirty-fourth; Theophilus of Caesarea in Palestine; Polycrates of Ephesus; Palmas of Pontus; Beryllus and Bacchylus of Corinth; Agrippinus at Carthage in Africa; St Irenaeus at Lyon in Gaul; and finally St Victor, pontiff at Rome.

Thus our reading from the manuscript codex is confirmed in the author's words: “What kind of laws, then, are these that only the impious, unjust, disgraceful, savage, vain, and mad enforce against us—laws which no Pius and no Verus ever imposed?” (*Apology* 5), that is, down to the time when he published the *Apology*.

## § VI. When, on what occasion, and by what considerations he became a Christian

**12.** With Q. Flavius Tertullus Scapula and T. Flavius Clemens as consuls, in the tenth year of St Victor, Roman pontiff, and the third year of Severus, TERTULLIAN, in my opinion, gave his name to the Christian faith.

[Note: [Col. 0052D] This opinion of Jacques Pamelius is rejected by Pierre Allix in his dissertation *On the Life and Writings of Tertullian*, Paris, 1680, and by Tillemont, *Mémoires*, vol. III, article 2, p. 87. Some, as the distinguished Fr Berti reports, *Dissertationes historicae*, vol. II, century III, dissertation 4, §2, p. 413, think that he converted to the Christian faith around the time when he wrote *On the Pallium*, namely after the year 208. But many works of Tertullian written earlier survive and display the faith he professed. Fabricius places Tertullian's conversion in 196, Oudin in 170, and Cave in 185, though they hesitate and rely only on conjectures. See Lumper, ibid. EDD.]

The Empire had now been completely pacified, Antoninus, Severus's son, and Clodius Albinus ruling as Caesars with Severus; hence Tertullian calls it a threefold Empire in the words quoted above, to which we refer the reader, for several details there pertain to this year. His conversion occurred not at the beginning of the year but near its end, after “every aconite of hostility had already been uprooted” (*To Scapula* 3; *Apology* 23)—that is, after the destruction of Byzantium. Herodian, Zonaras, and Michael Glycas attest that, after a three-year siege begun in Severus's first year while Niger was still alive, the city was finally taken by famine. It was stripped of all freedom and assigned to the Perinthians, who had suffered much from Niger; its walls were demolished and it was reduced to the status of a village. Our author also mentions it: “Caelius Lapella, at the fall of Byzantium, exclaimed, ‘Christians, rejoice!’” (*To Scapula* 3).

He himself apparently indicates the occasion of his conversion: the testimony of some god who, adjured by Christians, confessed that it was a demon (*Apology* 23). “These testimonies of your gods,” he says, “are accustomed to make Christians; by trusting them most of all, we believe in Christ the Lord. They themselves kindle faith in our writings; they themselves build confidence in our hope.”

[Note: [Col. 0053C] Pamelius attributes Tertullian's conversion to the answer of an oracle, George of Amiens to a vision; both [Col. 0053D] appear to savor of fable. From the author's own testimony it is pleasing to draw out three principal causes of his conversion. First, the unconquered constancy of the martyrs amid the cruelest torments: “Everyone,” he says, “who beholds so great an endurance is struck by some doubt and is set afire to inquire what its cause may be; once he has learned the truth, he immediately follows it himself” (*To Scapula*, final chapter). Second, their astonishing power over demons and the demons' images and oracles: “What is more manifest than this work? What proof more trustworthy? The simplicity of truth stands openly before you: from bodies too they depart at our command, unwilling and grieving, and put to shame in your presence” (*Apology* 23). Third, fear of divine judgments: “We fly over to this discipline,” he says in *On the Soul* 2, “through fear of the judgment foretold.” To these I would add what Pamelius gives above: the authority of Scripture and the divine majesty of predictions confirmed by their truth; indeed, the calamities of the Empire, raging more fiercely as mad brutality against Christians grew more savage; and finally apologetic discourses, weighty in substance no less than sharp in words, published by many teachers who flourished during that storm of the Church. EDD.]

Nor did this arise from that alone, but also from faith in the sacred Scriptures, whose credibility the gods approve by their testimony. In particular, as he himself testified when already a Christian, the greatest antiquity vindicated their authority, because “the repository of the single prophet Moses preceded by several centuries all the substances and materials, the origins, orders, and veins of every ancient style; most peoples and cities; the notable subjects of histories and records; finally, even the very forms of letters, the indicators and guardians of things; the gods themselves, the temples themselves, the oracles and rites themselves.”

The truth of their predictions also confirmed their divine majesty: “Whatever happens,” he says, “was foretold. That earthquakes swallow cities; that seas rob away islands; that foreign and civil wars tear peoples apart; that kingdoms strike against kingdoms; that famine and pestilence, every local disaster, and the great frequency of deaths lay waste; that even the functions of the seasons and the tasks of the elements go astray; that the form of nature is disturbed by monsters and portents—all were providently written” (namely Matthew 25). “While we suffer them, they are read; when we recognize them, they are proved.” All these matters are treated more fully both in *On the Pallium* 2 and *Apology* 40, to which and to our notes we refer the reader.

**13.** Here we shall add only that, among other things, he alludes to the great plague at Rome and in many provinces, and to the earthquake in which Smyrna, a city of Asia, collapsed—events Eusebius records in the *Chronicle* under the emperors Marcus and Verus—and to the lightning that struck the Capitol under Commodus and set fire to the library, and to the civil wars of that age, which we have already mentioned. He likewise alludes to the famine, of which he writes in *To Scapula*: “Likewise under the governor Hilarianus, when they had shouted concerning the open spaces used for our burials, ‘Let there be no burial grounds,’ their own threshing floors ceased to be; for they did not gather their harvests” (*To Scapula* 3).

Finally he alludes to the portent mentioned in the same place: “For that sun too, in the assembly at Utica, with its light almost extinguished, was such a portent that it could not have suffered this from an ordinary eclipse, being situated in its own elevation and domicile” (*On the Testimony of the Soul* 1).

We also think that the writings against the Gentiles of many Fathers since Christ contributed to his conversion. Elsewhere he alludes to them: “Some among us,” he says, “in whom the labor of curiosity and the continuity of memory from their former literary culture endured, composed little works recalling and calling to witness, in order to censure, the origins, traditions, and grounds of opinions by which it can be recognized that we have embraced nothing new or monstrous for which common literature does not also act as advocate in our support, whether we have cast out some error or admitted some equitable thing” (*Against the Valentinians* 5). Among these should be named Quadratus, Aristides, Anaxagoras, Melito, Theophilus of Antioch, Apollinaris, Tatian, Clement of Alexandria, and Apollonius the Senator; and finally those cited somewhere by the author: Justin, philosopher and martyr; Miltiades, ecclesiastical sophist; and Irenaeus, “the most inquisitive investigator of every branch of learning.” That Tertullian labored through all their writings both before and after accepting the faith is evident from the *Apology*, in which from time to time he imitates them almost word for word.

Converted to faith in Christ with all these supports and already initiated, he put on the Christian pallium, as we touched upon earlier. When a friend reproached him with inconstancy because he had passed from the toga to the pallium, he delivered a speech at Carthage, which he entitled *ON THE PALLIUM*. In it he commends the pallium's antiquity, simplicity, ease, carefree freedom, philosophical gravity, and holiness, and shows through various examples that he should not be blamed for changing his attire. On that occasion he attacks the various kinds of lust through which nature is altered and the indecent adornment of matrons; he applies the cautery to the vices of the forum, judges, and soldiers, and also to ambition and gluttony.

A constitution of Severus and Antoninus dated under these consuls on 11 March and surviving in Digest, book 9, title 1, law 1, further confirms our opinion that Antoninus the son was a colleague in the Empire.

## § VII. From what sources he drew his comprehensive knowledge of Christian teaching

**14.** With C. Domitius Dexter II and L. Valerius Messalla Thrasea Priscus as consuls, in the eleventh year of St Victor, Roman pope, and the fourth of Severus, the writings of TERTULLIAN, which will be treated later, bear more than sufficient testimony to the occupations to which, now a Christian, he devoted himself wholly. From them it is first evident that he was so practiced in reading the sacred writings and had so committed every passage of Scripture individually to memory that he sometimes embraced several in a single period; this may readily be discovered from our Index of Scriptures by comparing it with the author.

In citing them, as regards the Old Testament he everywhere follows the edition of the Seventy translators, which, as he himself attests, was restored by Ezra (*On the Apparel of Women* 3; *Apology* 18). He does so not only in the Pentateuch of Moses and the other historical books, but also in the predictions of the prophets. “Those whom we have called preachers,” he says, “are called prophets from their office of foretelling. Their voices, and likewise the miraculous works they produced as evidence of divinity, remain in the treasuries of literature, and these are not now hidden. The most learned of the Ptolemies, surnamed Philadelphus, also most keenly interested in every kind of literature, when in his enthusiasm for a library he emulated Pisistratus, as I think, among other records supported in fame by either antiquity or some curiosity, at the suggestion of Demetrius of Phalerum, then the most approved of grammarians and the man to whom he had entrusted its administration, also requested books from the Jews—namely their own native writings, which they alone possessed. For the prophets had always spoken from among them and to them, that is, to the household nation of God by the grace of their fathers. Those formerly called Hebrews are now called Jews; hence both their letters and speech were Hebrew. But lest knowledge be lacking, this too was supplied to Ptolemy by the Jews, seventy-two translators being granted; Menedemus the philosopher, defender of Providence, also admired them because of their agreement in judgment. Aristeas likewise affirmed these things to you. Thus the records were openly left in Greek form. Today they are displayed in Ptolemy's library at the Serapeum together with the Hebrew writings themselves.” We have cited this at somewhat greater length against those who do not shrink from diminishing the authority of the Seventy translators.

[Note: [Col. 0055D] Johann Salomo Semler, a man sometimes daring in counsel and rather impatient of the well-trodden path, published at the end of his edition a dissertation *On the Various and Uncertain Character of Tertullian's Books*. One may see especially with what vehemence he dares to assert that Tertullian neither used Greek codices nor wished to use them; that his entire concern was to prefer his Latin reading to any foreign one, as if Greek books had already at that time been corrupted by heretics; and that the conviction thus more easily became fixed in Latin minds that Latin books possess especial excellence and greater antiquity, because Latin exemplars had their first beginnings and recent rudiments earlier. He even asserts that various new interpretations and glosses survive of which Tertullian was the first and certain author, and that from him they flowed into all [Col. 0056D] Latin codices. However that may be, one must diligently guard against the two aims this author sets before himself: to discredit the commonly received reading of the sacred books, and further to assert that certain books of the ancients—those of Irenaeus or Clement—came from one and the same workshop, so that there is no longer sufficient reason to continue dividing them among different authors, since they were composed by one. See *Tertulliani opera*, ed. Semler, Halle, 1828, vol. V, pp. 185–211. EDD.]

In the New Testament, however, it is evident from his distinctive rendering—often greatly different from all others—that our author, deeply learned in Greek, used not so much Latin translations as old Greek codices. For the benefit of professors of sacred literature, we have everywhere noted this divergence. [Col. 0055D] St Ambrose—or whoever wrote the commentary on Paul's Epistle to the Romans—long ago pointed it out at Romans 5: “It is established,” he says, “that certain Latins in former times translated from old Greek codices, which the simplicity of those times preserved and approved as uncorrupted. But after disagreement among dissenting minds and the disruptions of heretics began to twist questions, many things were altered according to human judgment, so that the letters would contain what seemed right to a man. Hence the Greeks themselves possess differing codices. I judge true, however, that reading in which reason, history, and authority were observed. For what is today censured in the Latin codices is found so placed by the ancients: TERTULLIAN, Victorinus, and Cyprian.” Thus far that author.

Our author further spent night and day examining not only the writers of apologies for the Christian faith whom we cited above but, without doubt, other ecclesiastical writers as well. Apart from Apollonius the Senator and St Victor, all were Greek. I mean Philo; Josephus, whom he calls “the native champion of Jewish antiquities” (*Apology* 19); Apion and Hegesippus, Jews; Clement of Rome and St Polycarp, whom he recognizes as ordained bishops, the former of the Romans by Peter and the latter of Smyrna by John (*On the Prescription Against Heretics* 32); St Ignatius of Antioch; St Dionysius the Areopagite of Gaul; Papias, bishop of Hierapolis; Agrippa Castor; Dionysius of Corinth; Pinytus and Philip, bishops of Crete; Musanus; Modestus; Pantaenus of Alexandria; Rhodo of Asia; Serapion; and a certain Proculus, whom he calls “the dignity of Christian eloquence,” about whom more will be said later; and finally Sextus, Apion, Candidus, Arabianus, Maximus, and Heraclitus (see also *Against the Valentinians* 5; *Song Against Marcion* 9; *On the Prescription Against Heretics* 38; *Apology* 35).

To these I add the other earliest successors of the Roman pontiffs in the chair of Peter, whose decretals survive and whom the author mentions together with Linus and Cletus: Anacletus, Evaristus, Alexander, Sixtus, Telesphorus, Hyginus, Pius, Anicetus, likewise Soter—whom he omitted because no occasion offered itself—and Eleutherius, whom he calls Blessed. I think all these wrote in Greek, judging from the same Latin style, which appears to belong to one translator; for they knew Greek better than Latin, as did St Irenaeus. Consequently, as even Rhenanus notes, no one should wonder at the author's Greek manner of speaking: even if some works had been translated into Latin, he preferred, as they say, to drink from the springs rather than the pools.

Certain writers say that in this first year, on 15 May, the anniversary of the beginning of the reign, Severus named his son Antoninus Caesar Augustus at Viminacium before setting out for war against Albinus. Nevertheless, the rescripts of the preceding years in the names of Severus and Antoninus Augusti show that he had already once been called Augustus from the beginning. Meanwhile, the author too mentions Albinus's conspiracy against Severus in this year in the words: “Whence came the Cassiuses, Nigers, and Albini?”

## § VIII. When and amid what circumstances he composed certain poems

**15.** With T. Sextius Lateranus and M. Marius, son of Marcus, Cuspius Rufinus as consuls, in the twelfth year of St Victor, Roman pontiff, and the fifth year of the emperor Severus, it is likely that TERTULLIAN, to exercise his memory, composed in verse *GENESIS* and *SODOM*—indeed, in my opinion, the complete history of the sacred books of the Old Testament. The former poem is mutilated, and the opening of the latter sufficiently suggests that he also described the flood of the whole world. He also composed the poem *TO A SENATOR WHO RETURNED FROM THE CHRISTIAN FAITH TO THE SERVICE OF IDOLS*, five books *AGAINST MARCION* in verse, and a book *ON THE JUDGMENT OF THE LORD*.

In this year, on 19 February, Imperator Caesar D. Clodius Septimius Albinus Augustus was defeated in battle by the emperor Severus and killed near Lyon. Because Lyon had previously received Albinus, as Herodian attests, it was plundered and burned. In that campaign, as various inscriptions establish, Severus was acclaimed imperator VIII, IX, and X. Once Albinus had been defeated, Severus first ordered the execution of all the nobles of Gaul, Spain, and Britain who had aided him; then, returning to Rome, he sought out Albinus's friends everywhere and ordered forty senators among them to be killed, together with all who had conspired against Pertinax. The author accordingly calls them Albinians in the words quoted above.

He indicates that the investigation of them lasted from AD 195 through several years: “Even now,” he says, “the accomplices or applauders of criminal factions are revealed daily, a surviving gleaning after the harvest of parricides”—apparently as far as the Parthian triumph, and thus Severus's ninth year, as is explained there more fully. Elsewhere he likewise alludes to the murdered senators: “There is no one who cannot suffer for the sake of a man what he hesitates to suffer for God's cause. Let the present times themselves be our proof: how many and what sort of persons meet unexpected ends for the sake of a man, ends unlooked-for in view of their birth, rank, physical condition, and age—either at his own hand if they acted against him, or at the hand of his enemies if they stood for him” (*To Scapula* 2; *Apology* 35; *Against Marcion*, final chapter).

One of the said consuls, Lateranus, was so great a friend of the emperor Severus that he built him the most magnificent residence in Rome, which we still call the Lateran Palace. St Jerome later writes in the *Epitaph of Fabiola* that Lateranus was slain by a Caesarean sword, perhaps that of Antoninus Caracalla, who punished many of his father's friends.

Under these consuls the second letter of St Victor, Roman pontiff, was written to the African bishops and dated 1 September; the letter of his predecessor St Eleutherius was dated 11 July under the consuls Maternus and Bradua. We have therefore necessarily designated this as the twelfth, not the preceding, year of Victor's pontificate, especially because St Damasus writes that Victor occupied the pontifical seat from the consulship of Commodus V and Glabrio—under whom his first letter to Theophilus of Alexandria was dated 20 July—until Lateranus and Rufinus. From 1 June of that consulship, the day he was created pontiff, until 28 July of the following year, there are twelve years, one month, and twenty-eight days.

In the said second letter, rebuking the Africans' disagreement in the sacraments, Victor seems to me to allude to the observance of Easter. In his first letter he had commanded that, according to what his predecessors had also decreed, Easter must be celebrated on a Sunday, whichever Sunday fell between the fourteenth and twenty-first moon of the first month. In this matter some Africans perhaps at first followed the contrary custom with Polycrates of Asia, though they afterward came to one agreement. For of Blastus, a Quartodeciman heretic, the author writes: “In addition to all these comes Blastus, who secretly wishes to introduce Judaism. For he says that Easter must be observed only according to the law of Moses, on the fourteenth of the month. But who does not know that evangelical grace is nullified if he brings Christ back under the Law?” (*On the Prescription Against Heretics* 53, heresy 22; *On Fasting* 14 and 13).

This passage of the same author concerns the same observance: “We celebrate Easter in the first month in the annual cycle”; as does this: “Moreover, throughout the Greek lands councils drawn from all the churches are held in fixed places.” Although he speaks generally, we think this chiefly refers to the councils celebrated shortly before in Palestine under Theophilus of Caesarea, in Pontus under Palmas, at Corinth under Bacchylus, among the churches in Osrhoene, at Alexandria also under Theophilus, and finally in Asia. Nicephorus, *Ecclesiastical History* 4, final chapter, writes that at the Asian council the Fathers followed the said judgment of St Victor and the Roman Council, with which the council of the Gauls under Irenaeus also agreed.

## § IX. The publication of the books Against the Jews

**16.** With T. Haturius, son of Titus, Saturninus and C. Annius, son of Appius, Trebonianus Gallus as consuls, in the first year of St Zephyrinus as pope at Rome and the sixth year of the emperor Severus, there seems to us to have occurred that disagreement between TERTULLIAN and a certain Jew on account of which he indicates that he wrote the book *AGAINST THE JEWS*. “Very recently,” he says, “a disputation took place between a Christian and a Jewish proselyte. By turns, each pulling on the contentious rope, they prolonged the day into evening. Some spectators also shouted interruptions, and the truth was overshadowed by a kind of cloud surrounding each man's arguments. It therefore seemed good to examine more carefully what could be elucidated less fully through the clamor of the debate, and to settle in writing the questions considered anew” (*Against the Jews* 1).

Among other things, it should be noted how beautifully he traces the spread of the faith through almost the whole world even at that early date: “In whom else have all the nations believed except Christ, who has already come? ... Parthians, Medes, Elamites,” and so forth, as is read in Acts 2, “and the other nations, including the various peoples of the Gaetulians and the many territories of the Moors; all the boundaries of Spain and the diverse nations of Gaul; places among the Britons inaccessible to the Romans but subject to Christ; the lands of the Sarmatians, Dacians, Germans, and Scythians; many hidden nations and many provinces and islands unknown to us and which we can scarcely enumerate—in all these places the name of Christ, who has already come, reigns” (*Against the Jews* 7).

His account of the limits of the Roman Empire at that time is also memorable: “The Germans are still not permitted to cross their frontiers. The Britains are enclosed within the circuit of the Ocean. The nation of the Moors and the barbarity of the Gaetulians are blockaded by the Romans so that they do not pass beyond the boundaries of their lands. What shall I say of the Romans, who fortify their Empire with the garrisons of their legions and cannot extend the power of their kingdom beyond these nations?” Herodian touches on this very matter concerning the Germans in *History* 2, when he says that Severus was chosen by all the armies that, keeping watch against the barbarians along the banks of the Danube and Rhine, protected the Roman Empire.

We place this as the first year of the pontificate of St Zephyrinus—Habundius, son of Habundius, a Roman by birth—because, after St Victor died and the see had been vacant twelve days, he was created pontiff on 10 August under these very consuls. St Damasus himself attests this, writing that Zephyrinus occupied the see in the times of Severus and Antoninus, from the consulship of Saturninus and Gallus to the consuls Praesens and Extricatus.

How St Victor is nevertheless reckoned fifth among the martyrs when the persecution of the Christians had not yet begun is not without difficulty. This moved Eusebius and other chronographers to extend his pontificate into Severus's eighth, ninth, or tenth year. Yet my statement, and St Damasus's opinion on the dates of both pontiffs, is confirmed by Zephyrinus's decretal letters issued under these same consuls Saturninus and Gallus, the former dated 20 September and the latter 9 November. Nor was it unusual in the primitive Church for persecution to begin with the Roman populace or the prefects of the City.

What is more, the author expressly asserts this concerning the present time: “Severus knew that women and men of the highest rank belonged to this sect; not only did he not harm them, he even adorned them with his testimony and openly resisted the raging populace to its face” (*To Scapula* 4; *Apology* 1–2). Before the following year, moreover, the law of the deified Severus and Antoninus must have been issued that no absent person should be punished; it is mentioned in Digest, book 1, title *On Searching for Accused Persons*. Without doubt the author alludes to it in these words: “What loss do the laws suffer in their own dominion if the truth is heard? Or will their authority boast more if they condemn truth unheard?” And again: “Since it is not lawful for people wholly undefended and unheard to be condemned.”

## § X. The Apology, The Testimony of the Soul, and Fate, against the Gentiles; TERTULLIAN's priesthood and the books arising from it: On Repentance, On Prayer, On Circumcision, On the Garments of Aaron, On Clean and Unclean Animals, On the Trinity, and On Baptism

**17.** With P. Cornelius Anullinus II and M. Aufidius, son of Marcus, Fronto as consuls, in the second year of St Zephyrinus, Roman pontiff, and the seventh of the emperor Severus, the emperor departed with his sons Antoninus and Geta—whom before his departure he had declared Caesars, Augusti, and imperatores—for the second Arab, Parthian, and Adiabenian war discussed more fully above, after again giving a largess to the people and a donative to the soldiers. Meanwhile, the Roman magistrates and priests continued to persecute the Christian religion, a persecution begun by St Victor the pontiff. In imitation of others who had done so before him, and especially Blessed Justin Martyr, TERTULLIAN sent to Rome an *APOLOGY FOR THE CHRISTIANS*, in which, as even Lactantius attests, he argued this case fully.

The author himself indicates that, even before Severus's departure, the emperor had both amended and published many laws: “Do you not yourselves daily, as experiments illuminate the darkness of antiquity, lop and cut down that whole old and squalid forest of laws with the new axes of imperial rescripts and edicts? Did not Severus, most steadfast of the Caesars, only yesterday abolish after so venerable an old age the utterly vain Papian laws”—here he alludes to the *lex Papia Poppaea*, so called from its proposers, the consuls M. Papius, son and grandson of Marcus, Mutilus and Q. Poppaeus, son and grandson of Quintus, Secundinus—“which compel people to beget children before the Julian laws compel them to contract marriage?” (*Apology* 4).

How the abolition of those laws can be attributed to Severus although it is afterward ascribed to the emperor Constantine, we have treated more fully in our notes on this passage. If our conjecture concerning Digest, *On Sexual Violation and Adultery*, law 1, is accepted—that it establishes that sexual violation and adultery can be charged and punished even in the case of a betrothed woman—then that law must have been enacted under these consuls.

Our author also supports the fact that the second Parthian war had not yet been completed—a war Cassiodorus too records as begun under these consuls—when he counts the Moors and Marcomanni and even the Parthians among the enemies of the Roman Empire (*Apology* 37). Again he celebrates the spread of the Christian faith in these words: “We are of yesterday, and we have filled all that is yours: cities, islands, fortresses, towns, market-places, the camps themselves, tribes, decuries, the palace, the senate, the forum.” Shortly before, he confirms what we said under the preceding year concerning popular persecution: “How often, even apart from you, does the hostile mob attack us in its own right with stones and fires? In the very frenzy of the Bacchanals they do not spare even dead Christians, but tear them from the repose of burial, from a kind of asylum of death—already altered, no longer whole—and cut and rend them apart” (*Apology* 37).

To the same matter belongs this: “None demand Christians for punishment more than the mob,” and the popular cry so often quoted by the author: “The Christians to the lion!” Many other matters in the *Apology* deserve mention, but lest we grow too lengthy here, we refer the reader to our notes. We shall add only that the occasion of the persecution arose from false accusations, which Tertullian refutes magnificently, especially three charges: infanticide, feeding on human blood, and incest. “We are called,” he says, “the most criminal of men because of the sacrament of infanticide and the meal made from it, and because of incest after the banquet, since dogs overturn the lamps—as procurers, of course, of darkness—and provide for the shamelessness of impious lusts” (*Apology* 7).
