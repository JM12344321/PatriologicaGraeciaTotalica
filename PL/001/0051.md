### ARGUMENT.—

Christians are therefore wrongly called public enemies merely because, though in all other respects dutiful toward the emperors, they celebrate the emperors’ solemn festivals in accordance with conscience rather than wantonness, observing them modestly, decorously, chastely, and piously. The Romans themselves behave otherwise: among them not only does the common people often tear Caesar apart with abuse, desire a new emperor day after day for the sake of profit—and this during the very distribution of the largess—but the senators themselves, the knights, and the leading men of the city and the camps threaten Caesar’s life and conspire with the Cassii, Nigri, and Albini, meanwhile concealing their treason, until it breaks out, beneath a grand display of rejoicing. No Christians are found among these men. Nor do Christians consult astrologers about the safety of the Caesars, since that cannot be done except from a desire for political revolution, from which Christians wholly recoil.

Christians, then, are public enemies because they do not pay the emperors vain, deceitful, or rash honors; because, as men of true religion, they celebrate even the emperors’ solemn festivals in accordance with conscience rather than wantonness. A splendid duty, to be sure: to bring hearths and couches out into public, to feast street by street, to turn the city into the likeness of a tavern, [Col. 0452A–B] to make mud with wine,

> **Note [Col. 0452B–0453B].** Rhenanus, Gelenius, and Scaliger read *obole facere*; for *habitu*, Havercamp preferred to write *habitu*. The Fulda codex has: “to change the city into the likeness of a tavern.”
>
> “To make mud with wine.” The copyists did not understand this either. They therefore altered it to “to compel the drunken,” as in the Leiden codex, or “to produce drunkenness,” as presented by the Fulda codex. But “to make mud with wine” means to soak and intoxicate the ground with a great quantity of wine, as a sign of insolence and luxury. Horace touches upon this when censuring the miser, of whom Publilius Syrus says in verse 59:
>
> > The miser does nothing rightly except when he dies.
>
> For thus Flaccus, *Odes* II.14:
>
> > A worthier heir will consume the Caecuban wine  
> > guarded by a hundred keys, and with unmixed wine  
> > will stain the splendid pavement,  
> > wine finer than at pontiffs’ banquets.
>
> Cicero too, *Philippic* II: “Everything resounded with the voices of drunken men; the floors swam with wine; the walls were wet.”
>
> This, at any rate, seems to me to be the meaning of the present passage. La Cerda refers it to the novel custom of making fountains of wine, but I doubt that he can prove that this was also practiced among the ancients. There was also a festival of Venus at which much wine was poured out before the goddess’s temple. Plutarch, *Roman Questions* XLV, names Aeneas as the author of its institution and adds the reason for the pouring:
>
> > Ἦ καὶ τοῦτο σύμβολόν ἐστι τοῦ χρῆν νήφοντας ἑορτάζειν, ἀλλα μὴ μεθύοντας, ὡς τῶν θεῶν μᾶλλον τοῖς ἐγχέουσι χαιρόντων τὸν πολὺν ἄκρατον, ἣ τοῖς πίνουσι — “Or is this too a sign that festivals should be celebrated soberly and not drunkenly, since the gods delight more in those who pour out abundant unmixed wine than in those who drink it?”.
>
> For this signifies that festivals should be celebrated soberly, not drunkenly, since the gods take far greater pleasure in the pouring out of wine than in human drunkenness. This may perhaps also have given rise to the custom in question. The Greeks would call it πηλὸν ποιεῖν (“to make mud”), as does the man in *Anthology* II, chapter XL, epigram 3, who wished to be given while alive what is done uselessly for the dead:
>
> > Ζῶντί μοι εἴτε θέλεις, χάρισαι· τέφρην δὲ μεθύσκων,
Πηλὸν ποήσεις, κοὔχ ὁ θανὼν τίεται· — “If you wish, show me kindness while I live; but by making my ashes drunk, you will make mud, and the dead man is not honored.”.
>
> That is: “Give me, while I live, whatever you wish to offer; but by making the ashes drunk you will merely make mud, and the dead man receives no honor.” **HAV.**

to run about in bands committing outrages and shameless acts and pursuing the enticements of lust. Is public joy expressed in this way, through public disgrace? Are these things fitting for the solemn days of rulers [Col. 0453A], though they are not fitting on other days?

> **Note [Col. 0453C–0454B].** “Are fitting for rulers.” The manuscripts are extraordinarily confused here. Where Pamelius and others have, “Are these things fitting for the solemn days of rulers, though they are not fitting on other days?” the Fulda manuscript most strangely has: “I have said these things on the solemn days of rulers, and they decree that it is not fitting on other days.” These words, which are manifestly corrupt, acquire a new sense in the Leiden manuscript: “Will these things proclaim the solemn days as the beginning, which they will not proclaim on other days?” In the last phrase *decent*, “are fitting,” should be read. “To proclaim a beginning” is to provide an omen for what remains. For these vows were made chiefly on the Kalends of January, so that they might thus extend throughout the whole year.
>
> Tacitus, *Annals* IV: “Caesar, in a letter, offered vows for the Kalends of January, the solemn beginning of the year.” La Cerda cites a parallel passage from the jurist Gaius. In Dio, book LIX, p. 742 of Stephanus’s edition, we likewise read that under Gaius Caligula, on the first day of the year, a certain slave named Machaon ascended to the sacred couch of Jupiter Capitolinus, uttered many dreadful prophecies there, killed the puppy he had brought with him, and afterward killed himself. This certainly constituted a dreadful beginning and portent of the future, since it signified that Gaius—who, like a good physician, ought to have healed the wounds inflicted on the Roman commonwealth by Tiberius—would both destroy the commonwealth and himself perish by a bloody death.
>
> Thus that day was, to speak with Petronius, the “title” of the year; in Petronius the stork is the “title” of warm weather. The emperor was also said to “open the year.” Statius, *Silvae* IV, poem I.2, concerning Domitian:
>
> > Germanicus opens the distinguished year.
>
> And there, verse 20:
>
> > Thus is it fitting to enter the years.
>
> On this rite one may consult the learned short commentary by Giovanni Pietro Bellori, *On the Auspices of the New Year*, concerning a unique coin of Antoninus Pius inscribed: **S. P. Q. R. A. N. F. F. OPTIMO PRINCIPI. PIO**, that is, “a new, auspicious, and happy year.” It was published first at Rome, and afterward at the end of Cardinal Carpegna’s *Selected Coins*, Amsterdam, Wetstein.
>
> I have nevertheless not ventured to alter the reading of the Agobardine manuscript: “which are not fitting on other…,” with “days” deleted. **HAV.**

> **Note [Col. 0454B–C].** “We overshadow our doorposts with laurels.” Rhenanus reads “we shade,” less correctly. Tacitus, writing of the discovery of the conspiracy against Nero’s life, says in *Annals* XV.17:
>

One man, after the killing of his son; another, of his brother; another, of a relative or friend, gave thanks to the gods, adorned his house with laurel, threw himself at Nero’s knees, and wearied his right hand with kisses.”

>

>

>
> > Ράμνόντε καὶ κλάδον δάφνης
Ύπἐρ θύρην ἐθἤκεν — “He placed buckthorn and a branch of laurel above the door” [the recovered Greek contains uncertain spacing and diacritics].,
>
> that is, “placed buckthorn and a branch of laurel upon the door.” **HAV.**

It is an honorable thing, when a public solemnity demands it, to dress your house in the guise of some newly opened brothel.

> **Note [Col. 0454A].** “To bring in,” Paris.

Yet in this religion also of a secondary majesty—a religion on whose account we Christians are arraigned for a second sacrilege, because we do not celebrate the Caesars’ solemn festivals with you in a manner which neither modesty, decorum, nor chastity permits, but which an opportunity for pleasure rather than any worthy reason has recommended—I should like to demonstrate your loyalty and truthfulness, lest perhaps even here those who refuse to regard us as Romans, but call us enemies of the Roman rulers, should be found worse than Christians.

I summon the Quirites themselves, the native common people of the seven hills themselves, and ask whether that Roman tongue spares any Caesar of its own.

> **Note [Col. 0454C–0455B].** “Whether that Roman tongue spares him.” Although such conduct is readily to be expected from the common rabble and dregs of humanity, who possess neither a reputation they can stain nor property they can lose, those jesters in Trebellius Pollio’s *Gallienus* teach the point sufficiently. Peering with astonishing eagerness into the face of every person, they said that they were looking for the emperor’s father. This was Valerian, who had been captured by the Persians, whom Gallienus absurdly paraded in a procession while neglecting vengeance for his father.
>
> No one described such things more precisely than Suetonius Tranquillus, who committed the conduct of the Caesars to writing with freedom equal to that with which they themselves had lived. In *Nero* XXXIX: “As Nero passed by, Isidorus the Cynic had rebuked him in public with a loud voice because he sang well of Nauplius’s misfortunes but managed his own prosperity badly.”
>
> In the same passage, an actor in Atellan farces, when given the words ὑγίαινε πάτιρ, ὑγίαινε μήτερ (“Farewell, father; farewell, mother”) [πάτιρ is an uncertain recovered form]. in a certain song, had illustrated them by gestures representing drinking and swimming, thereby signifying the deaths of Gaius, Claudius, and Agrippina; and in the final line,
>
> > Orcus guides your feet,
>
> he indicated the senate by a gesture. Suetonius there records even the most notorious verses posted against Nero; Claudius before him and Domitian afterward continually suffered the same treatment.
>
> Even Vespasian, the best of the emperors and himself sufficiently witty, did not escape the sharpness of this tongue. Since his strained expression resembled that of a man exerting himself—that is, needing to defecate—he wished to hear some witty remark about himself from a certain man of polished wit. He had to hear: “I shall speak when you have finished emptying your bowels.”
>
> Indeed, even at Vespasian’s funeral, when mimes were employed according to custom, the dead man could not be spared. As Suetonius notes in chapter XIX, Favor, the chief mime, wearing Vespasian’s mask and imitating, as customary, the deeds and sayings of the living man, openly asked the procurators how much his funeral procession cost. When he heard “ten million sesterces,” he cried out that they should give him one hundred thousand sesterces and throw him into the Tiber if they liked.
>
> Here the jester seems to me to have had in mind that saying and action of Vespasian which Suetonius likewise relates in chapter XXIII. When envoys informed him that a colossal statue of no moderate cost had publicly been decreed to him, he ordered them to set it up immediately, holding out his cupped hand and saying that the base was ready. **HAV.**

The Tiber is witness,

> **Note [Col. 0455B].** “And the Tiber is witness.” The Fulda manuscript and Heraldus have “is” in place of “and.” The most distinguished Gothofredus, in his notes on *To the Nations* I.17, refers this to the Tiberian house, or palace of the Caesars. But in ancient times, as I think, the common people lived along the banks of the river, while the more powerful occupied the hills, safe from floods. Since Tertullian is speaking of the common people, he could indicate nothing more suitably than the place where they lived.
>
> The French translator Giry dreams of some statue of the Tiber—I do not know which: “the placards that are attached to the statue of the Tiber.” Finally, the Leiden manuscript seems to suggest a third possibility: “There, Vesta, the Tiber, and the schools of beasts are witnesses”; perhaps it intended “and the statues,” since in *To the Nations* he also speaks of statues. **HAV.**

and so are the schools of beasts.

> **Note [Col. 0455C].** “Schools of beasts.” Rigault reads “school.” Some, including Scriverius, take “schools of beasts” to mean that Roman enthusiasm by which men gathered for combats of beasts and spectacles of hunting as though attending a school. There was much license of speech there, as in all spectacles. Or he indicates the place where those condemned to the beasts were trained; there was room for abuse there, since nothing more dreadful than that death could be feared. Chrysostom also condemns the theaters under this heading, *Homily I on Isaiah* VI.1: “Is it from the insults, abuse, and mockery with which the spectators of such things bespatter one another?” **HAV.**

Now, if nature had drawn some transparent material over human breasts so that they could be seen through, whose inmost heart would not appear engraved—

> **Note [Col. 0455C–0456B].** “Now, if over breasts.” The Fulda manuscript shapes the reading in this way: “Now if, for the purpose of seeing through human breasts, nature had drawn over them some transparent material….” Hence Scriverius, in the margin of my book, and Meursius, in his *Critical Exercises*, emend it from Juvenal and others to “some transparent material.”
>
> I, adhering more closely to the traces of the manuscript’s reading, prefer: “Now if nature, when it first formed them, had drawn over human breasts some transparent material, etc.” For the word “human” seems to need adding from the Fulda manuscript, and τὸ (“the [word]”) signifies that ancient time when the human being received the form which all his descendants preserve.
>
> Momus, of course, had long before found fault with this and had wished the human breast to be furnished with windows, so that its more hidden thoughts could not remain concealed. From this philosophy Euripides wished men to be double-tongued, so that the sound of truth and falsehood might be disclosed in each man. For in *Hippolytus*, verse 925, Theseus speaks thus:
>
> > Φεῦ, χρὴν βροτοῖσι τῶν φίλων τεκμήριον
Σαφές τι κεῖσθαι, καὶ διάγνωσιν φρενῶν,
Ὅστις τ` ἀληθής ἐστιν, ὅστε μὴ φίλος — “Alas! Mortals ought to have some clear token of their friends and a means of discerning their minds—who is a true friend and who is not.”.  
> > Δίσσας δὲ φωνὰς πάντας ἀνθρώπους ἔχειν,
Τὴν μὲν δικαίαν, τὴν δ` ὅπως ἐτύγχανεν,
Ὡς ἡ φρονοῦσα τ` ἄδικ` ἐξηλέγχετο
Πρὸς τῆς δικαὶας, κοὐκ ἂν ἠπατώμεθα — “And every person should possess two voices, one truthful and the other whatever it happened to be, so that the voice devising injustice might be exposed by the truthful one and we should not be deceived.”.
>
> That is: “Alas! Men ought to have some visible mark of their friends and a means of discerning the mind, showing who is a true friend and who is not; and every man should possess a double voice, one truthful and the other answering circumstances, so that the voice which devised unjust things might be convicted by the truthful voice, and we should no longer be deceived.”
>
> For already in Euripides’s time even the Spartans were such, as he says in *Andromache*:
>
> > Εὖ λέγοντες ἄλλα μὲν — “Speaking one thing plausibly”.  
> > Γλώσσῃ φρονοῦντες δ` ἄλλ` ἀνευρισκεσθ` ἀεὶ — “with the tongue, but thinking another, always to be found out”.
>
> Our Publilius, as though interpreting this, expressed it thus in verse 265:
>
> > A man always bears one thing upon his lips and thinks another.
>
> Sallust too, in his customary brief and vigorous manner, said in *Catiline*: “To have one thing shut within the breast and another ready upon the tongue.” For sometimes, beneath the human form—these are Seneca’s words—lurks the spirit of a beast, indeed one more fickle and savage than the beasts themselves. **HAV.**

>
> **Note [Col. 0459C].** “Freshly.” Observe the ancient custom of employing especially fresh laurels, and so great a quantity and bulk of them that the entrances of the doors were virtually barricaded.

With how lofty and brilliant an array of lamps did they cloud their vestibules?

> **Note [Col. 0459C–D].** “Lofty.” I understand this of the rows of lamps and the raised structure, as our author spoke above: it seems that rows of these lamps were constructed in vestibules or on doorposts in several levels, as though upon platforms.

With what richly adorned and splendid couches did they divide the forum among themselves?

> **Note [Col. 0459D–0460D].** “Richly adorned and splendid couches.” Scriverius notes from an old manuscript the reading “bulls,” referring the passage to sacrifices. This does not please me, for the word must be taken as at the beginning of this chapter: “A splendid duty, to bring hearths and couches out into public.” See Heraldus there.
>
> Tertullian says that they “divided” the forum, evidently seizing the most convenient and desirable place like so many greengrocers or shopkeepers. No one describes the luxury and ornamentation of couches or litters better than Martial, book XI, epigram 16:
>
> > Zoilus is ill; his coverlets cause this fever.  
> > If he becomes well, what will his scarlet fabrics do?  
> > What will his couch from the Nile do, or the linen dyed with fragrant purple?  
> > What but illness displays his foolish wealth?  
> > What have you to do with physicians? Send away every Machaon.  
> > Do you wish to become well? Take my coverlets.
>
> The same poet also teaches us at what time of day this spectacle was dismantled and the couches brought home, book IV, epigram 8:
>
> > The ninth hour commands the erected couches to be broken up.
>
> For after that hour a respectable man did not readily roam through the streets but withdrew home for dinner and study. Prostitutes, however, customarily opened their brothel then, and were consequently called *women of the ninth hour* by Persius at the end of *Satire* I:
>
> > If a wanton woman of the ninth hour plucks a Cynic’s beard. **HAV.**

They did so not to celebrate public rejoicings, but to proclaim their own wishes already, and in another man’s solemn festival to inaugurate an example and image of their own hope, changing the ruler’s name in their hearts.

> **Note [Col. 0459C].** Rigault and Havercamp read “proclaim” in other editions, followed by “and an example,” etc. Others read “learn by heart”; the second Lyon codex reads “expound, lest in another’s solemnity both an example,” etc.
>
> **Note [Col. 0459C].** Rhenanus and Gelenius read “imitating”; the Lyon codex, “inviting.”

The same duties are also paid by those who consult astrologers, soothsayers, augurs, and magicians concerning the Caesars’ lives—arts which Christians, because they were disclosed by apostate angels and forbidden by God, do not employ even for their own affairs. But who needs to search into Caesar’s safety except one by whom something against it is being devised or desired, or by whom something following it is hoped for and awaited?

> **Note [Col. 0459C].** Havercamp reads “separated” instead of “awaited.”

For one does not consult concerning loved ones in the same frame of mind as concerning masters. The anxious curiosity of blood relationship is one thing; that of servitude another. [Col. 0459B]

## CHAPTER XXXVI

> **Note [Col. 0460D].** “And those who consult astrologers.” Tertullian touches upon this charge because under Severus many men perished on account of astrologers. Spartianus, in *Severus*. **LAC.**
>
> **Note [Col. 0460D–0461B].** “Thus piety and religion.” Up to this point he has refuted them from their own opinion; now he states clearly that the piety, religion, and loyalty owed to emperors do not consist in those duties and practices by which hostility is veiled, covered, and disguised—as the gentiles do, who openly say that they are Romans but secretly lay plots. **LAC.**

That those Christians may be regarded as enemies who breathe nothing hostile against either the Roman Empire or the Roman emperor—indeed, who are toward others just what they are toward the emperor, since they are not permitted to wish evil, devise evil, against others, much less against the emperor, who has been raised by God to so great a height.

If these things are so, so that men who were called Romans are discovered to be enemies, why are we, who are deemed enemies, denied the name of Romans?

> **Note [Col. 0460C].** “Why should we be denied,” Paris.

Can we not both be Romans and not be enemies,

> **Note [Col. 0460C].** Pamelius and others read: “both not be Romans and be enemies.”

when men who were regarded as Romans are found to be enemies?

Thus the piety, religion, and loyalty devoted to the emperors do not consist in duties of this kind—duties which hostility can more readily employ as a covering for itself—but in those forms of conduct which civic duty must display as truly toward the emperor as toward everyone.

> **Note [Col. 0460D].** “Changing in their hearts.” Rhenanus reads “imitating”; the Leiden manuscript, “inviting.” Neither pleases me. This is very well illustrated by the accusation with which Valerius Omulus burdened Marcus Antoninus’s mother before Antoninus Pius. Julius Capitolinus relates in his *Life of Marcus Antoninus the Philosopher*:
>
> “Marcus was held in the highest favor by his adoptive father, although there were some who whispered things against him, particularly Valerius Omulus. When Omulus saw Lucilla, Marcus’s mother, in a garden venerating an image of Apollo, he whispered: ‘She is now asking that you end your day and that her son become emperor.’” **HAV.**
>
> **Note [Col. 0460C].** Wouweren restored this reading from a manuscript, and Rigault, Havercamp, and others accepted it. The earlier editions read: “in those things which divinity commands, as truly as,” etc. Havercamp and Ritter, however, transposed the words to read: “civic duty must display as truly toward the emperor as,” etc.
>
> **Note [Col. 0460C].** “Must” is absent from the Paris edition.

For these works of a good mind are not owed by us to emperors alone. We perform no good with partiality toward persons, because we render it for our own benefit, since we seek credit for neither praise nor reward from man, but from God, the exactor and remunerator of impartial kindness.

We are the same toward emperors as we are toward our neighbors.

> **Note [Col. 0460C].** Havercamp emends this from the reading: “from himself, because also toward our neighbors.”

For we are equally forbidden to wish evil, do evil, speak evil, or think evil concerning anyone. Whatever is not lawful against the emperor is not lawful against anyone; and what is lawful against no one is perhaps all the more unlawful against the emperor himself, who is so great through God. [Col. 0460B]

## CHAPTER XXXVII

> **Note [Col. 0460].** This note appears at the foot of column 462, but the callout letter, (d), has not been printed in the text.
>
> **Note [Col. 0462B].** “From the repose of burial.” A notable passage against the heretical followers of the Donatists in our own age, who dug up the buried bodies of Christians, indeed of kings, although burial is rightly called by Tertullian an asylum of death. And certainly the most severe penalties against violators of tombs were established long ago even by the pagan emperors themselves. **PAM.**

That nothing evil is to be expected from the Christians may be evident from this: among them it is not lawful to repay evil with evil. Yet if they wished to avenge unjust hatred and insults, they could do so very easily, both because of their enormous numbers, with which the world has been filled in a short time, and because of the contempt for death which they display even under torture for the Faith—whether they preferred to act by fires, by wars, or finally by mere withdrawal, thereby laying waste the commonwealth. But now, having actually become benefactors of their enemies, they deliver from the assaults of demons those whom they have nevertheless preferred to call enemies of human error rather than of the human race.

If, as we said above, we are commanded to love our enemies, whom have we to hate?

> **Note [Col. 0461A].** “Whom shall we have,” Paris.

Likewise, if, when injured, we are forbidden to retaliate, lest we become equal to them in deed, whom can we injure?

> **Note [Col. 0461A].** The Fulda codex adds “likewise.”

For consider the matter yourselves. How often do you rage savagely against Christians, partly obeying your own passions and partly the laws? How often, even bypassing you, does the hostile mob attack us on its own authority with stones and fires?

> **Note [Col. 0461A].** “When you have passed by,” Paris.
>
> **Note [Col. 0461B–C].** “With stones and fires.” Such punishment is familiar to the mob; compare John 8:59; 10:31; 11:8; Luke 20:6; Acts 5:26; 14:19. Eusebius, bishop of Samosata, was killed in this manner by an Arian woman, who threw a roof tile upon his head. On this deed see Nazianzen, *Oration XI against the Arians*, and concerning himself:
>
> > ὃν ἐκ τῆς ὑπερορίας ἀχθέντα λίθοις ὲδῖδαξασδε μεσούσης ἡμέρας, ἐν μέσῃ πόλει — “Whom, when he had been brought back from exile, you received with stones at midday, in the middle of the city” [ὲδῖδαξασδε is corrupt in the recovered text; its rendering follows the clear context].,
>
> that is: “When I was brought back from exile, you received me with stones, at midday itself and in the middle of the city.”
>
> In Petronius: “Some of those strolling in the porticoes threw stones at Eumolpus while he was reciting.” In the same work: “And so I am not surprised if the people pursue you with stones.”
>
> Indeed, even images of emperors and gods, and the temples themselves, were stoned by the raging mob, as Suetonius teaches in *Caligula* V concerning the death of Germanicus.
>
> Cyprian, book IV, epistle 10, presents a remarkable example of both kinds of fury in Numidicus, whom he commends to the presbyters of Carthage. By his exhortation Numidicus sent before him a great number of martyrs killed by stones and flames; and he joyfully watched his wife, who clung to his side, burned together with the others—or, I would rather say, preserved. He himself, half-burned, overwhelmed with stones, and abandoned for dead, afterward remained behind against his will when his daughter, seeking her father’s body with the anxious devotion of piety, found him half alive, revived him, and drew him away from the companions whom he himself had sent ahead. **HAV.**

In the very frenzy of the Bacchanals they do not spare even dead Christians, but tear them from the repose of burial, from that asylum, as it were, of death—by then altered, by then no longer whole—and cut and drag them apart.

> **Note [Col. 0461C–D].** “In the very frenzy of the Bacchanals.” He alludes to the description of Bacchus, whom afterward, as blessed Justin Martyr says in his book *On Monarchy*, they call “raving.” As for the rites of Bacchus—how shameful and how full of frenzy they were—let the reader consult Augustine, *The City of God* VII.21, and the annotations of Vives there. **PAM.**
>
> **Note [Col. 0461D–0462B].** “From the repose of burial.” Yet he says almost the same thing immediately afterward in “from a certain asylum of death.” For just as temples of the gods made those who fled into them safe and inviolable, preserving their lives, so death and the tomb can furnish corpses with nothing but repose.
>
> The reading of the Fulda codex, “from the remains of burial,” might therefore be preferred, were he not again saying virtually the same thing immediately afterward in “now others, now not even whole.”
>
> Nazianzen severely censures the Arians in the passage cited because they exposed the naked bodies of the saints to wild beasts and forbade burial of the dead:
>
> > τίνας (“what [beasts]”), he says, ἐπαφήκαμεν θῆρας ἁγίων σώμασιν; ἅς τινες τὴν ἀπάνθρωπον φύσιν δημοσιεύσαντες — “did we set upon the bodies of the saints?—beasts which certain men did set upon them, publicly displaying their inhuman nature” [the recovered construction is incomplete and ἅς is grammatically uncertain]..
>
> That is: “What wild beasts have we set upon the bodies of the saints? Certain men did this, publishing and prostituting human nature.”
>
> And shortly afterward: “Whom have we charged with a crime because they buried the dead—dead whom the very beasts themselves would have revered?”
>
> Certainly, as the same author says in praise of Hero, *Oration* XXXIII, burial should not be denied even to impious and criminal men. **HAV.**

Yet what retaliation for injury have you ever detected among men so closely united, so resolute even unto death, when a single night and a few little torches could abundantly accomplish vengeance, if it were lawful among us to balance evil with evil?

> **Note [Col. 0461A].** “Do you detect,” Franciscus.
>
> **Note [Col. 0461B].** “Repaid,” Franciscus.
>
> **Note [Col. 0461B].** Havercamp conjectures “little torches.”
>
> **Note [Col. 0461B].** Thus the Fulda codex, Rigault, and others; another reading is “for the abundance of vengeance.”

But far be it from us either that a divine sect should be avenged by human fire or that it should grieve to suffer the very thing by which it is proved.

If we wished to act as open enemies, rather than merely hidden avengers—

> **Note [Col. 0462A].** “Excluded,” Paris.
>
> **Note [Col. 0462B–C].** “Hidden avengers.” As Christians are now, entrusting vengeance to God. Or does he mean treacherous supporters of opposing factions? Elsewhere those too may be called hidden avengers who, being presently prevented from avenging themselves by lack of strength, seize a suitable time for vengeance, as Theognis urges in verse 362:
>
> > Εὗ κώτίλλε τὸν ἐχθρὸν — “Soothe your enemy well”.  
> > ὂταν δ` ὑποχεὶριος ἔλθῃ,
   Τίσαί νιν, πρόφασιν μηδεμίαν Θέμενσς — “but when he comes under your power, take vengeance upon him, admitting no excuse” [Θέμενσς is corrupt in the recovered text; the rendering follows the clear contextual sense]..
>
> That is: “Soothe your enemy well; but after he has come under your power, take vengeance upon him, admitting no excuse.”
>
> The sharp saying of Publilius Syrus, verse 156, teaches the same:
>
> > Do not speak evil of an enemy, but think it.
>
> On this passage one may consult the observations from the posthumous works of Janus Gruter which we have published. This political advice is fitting for gentiles, however, not for Christians. Sophocles speaks humanely, of course, in *Ajax the Flagellifer*, verse 747.
