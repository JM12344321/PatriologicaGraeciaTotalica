After this Martha confessed him to be the Son of God, and erred no more than Peter (Matt. 16:16) and Nathanael; and even if she had erred, she would immediately have been instructed. For behold: when about to raise her brother from the dead, the Lord looked up to heaven and to the Father: “Father,” he said (John 11:42)—[surely the Son]—“I thank you that you always hear me. [Note: Lat. adds “But.”] Because of these crowds standing around, I have said this, so that they may believe that you sent me.”

And also, in the agitation of his soul: “And what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour. Yet it was for this very reason that I came to this hour. But, Father, glorify your name” [Col. 0184C] (John 12:27)—the name in which the Son also was. [Note: Seml. omits “also.”] “I,” he says (John 5:43), “have come in my Father’s name.” The Son’s voice addressed to the Father had, of course, been sufficient. Behold, out of superabundance [Note: Latin reads “abundantly.”], the Father answers from heaven [Note: Fran. reads “answered.”] and bears witness concerning the Son (Matt. 17:5): “This is my beloved Son, in whom I have been well pleased; listen to him.”

Thus also in this saying, “I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again” [Note: Seml. and Venet. punctuate: “I will glorify it; again.”], how many persons do you perceive, most perverse Praxeas, if not as many as there are voices? You have the Son on earth; you have the Father in heaven. This is not separation, but divine ordering. Certainly, we know that God is even within the abysses and is present everywhere, though by force and power; and that the Son likewise, as inseparable, is everywhere with him. Yet within the economy itself, the Father willed that the Son should be present on earth, while he himself should be in heaven. Looking up to that place [Note: Seml. reads “which.”], the Son himself both prayed and made requests of the Father; [Col. 0184D] toward that place he also taught us to raise ourselves in prayer: “Our Father who art in heaven” (Matt. 6:9). And although he is also everywhere [Note: Seml. reads “heaven: although”; others add “indeed.”], the Father willed this to be his seat. He made [Col. 0185A] the Son a little lower than the angels (Ps. 8:6) [Note: Seml. reads: “He willed it. The Father made the Son lower.”] by sending him down to earth, though intending to crown him with glory and honor by taking him back into heaven.

He was already granting him these things when he said, “I have glorified it, and I will glorify it.” The Son petitions from earth; the Father promises from heaven. Why do you make both Father and Son liars, if either the Father was speaking from heaven to the Son while he himself was the Son on earth, or the Son was praying to the Father while he himself was the Father in heaven? What sense would it make for the Son likewise to petition himself by petitioning the Father [Note: Seml. reads “should petition” and omits “if”], if the Son was the Father; or, conversely, for the Father to promise himself something by promising it to the Son, if he was the Father? Even if we were thereby to call them two divided beings, as you keep proclaiming, it would be more tolerable to speak of two divided beings than to preach one shape-shifting God.

Accordingly, the Lord then [Note: Seml. reads “now.”] declared to those people [Col. 0185B] (John 12:30): “This voice came not for my sake, but for yours,” so that they might believe in both Father and Son, each under his own names, persons, and locations. But Jesus further cries out and says: “He who believes in me does not believe in me, but believes in him who sent me”—because through the Son one believes in the Father, and the Father is the authority for believing in the Son—“and he who sees me [Note: Rhen. omits “me.”] sees him who sent me.”

How so? Because, of course, “I have not spoken from myself; but the Father who sent me himself gave me a command as to what I should say and what I should speak.” For “the Lord gives me the tongue of instruction, so that I may know when it is fitting to speak the discourse that I speak” (Isa. 50:4). “As the Father has spoken to me, so I also speak” (John 12:50).

John, the evangelist and surely so illustrious a disciple, knew [Col. 0185C] better than Praxeas how these things had been said [Note: Seml. reads “names.”]. Therefore, expressing his own understanding, he says: “Now before the feast of Passover” [Note: “Now before the feast of Passover.” He cites these words from John 13 in transposed order; otherwise he agrees with the remaining text, even reading with the Vulgate translator “in himself,” although the Greek is *ἐν αὐτῷ* (“in him”). PAM. The expression *ἐν αὐτῷ* is certainly not found in our copy, published at Paris in 1549, but, much more correctly, *ἐν ἑαυτῷ*, that is, “in himself,” as in the Vulgate (*Καινὴ Διαθήκη* [New Testament], p. 177, l. 11). Tertullian could therefore have had before his eyes a Greek reading similar to ours, to which the Vulgate was subsequently conformed. EDD.] (John 13:1), “Jesus, knowing that all things had been handed over to him by the Father, and that he had come forth from God [Note: Seml. reads “to be.”] and was going to God.”

Praxeas, however, would have the Father himself come forth from himself and depart to himself, so that the devil put into Judas’s heart not the betrayal of the Son, but of the Father himself. This works well neither for the devil nor for the heretic, because the devil did not effect the betrayal in the Son for his own benefit. For it was the Son of God who was betrayed—the one who was in the son of man—as Scripture adds: “Now the son of man has been glorified, and God has been glorified in him.”

What [Col. 0186A] God? Surely not the Father, but the Father’s Word, who was in the son of man, that is, in the flesh, in which he too had already been glorified; but in power and word even before Jesus [Note: Seml. reads “Jesus” in the nominative]. “And God,” he says, “has glorified [Note: Jun. reads “will glorify.”] him in himself”—that is, the Father, having the Son within himself [Note: Seml. adds “whom.”], even though extended down to earth, soon glorified him through the resurrection [Note: Seml. omits “will glorify.”], once death had been conquered.

## CHAPTER XXIV

There were indeed people who even then did not understand. Thomas too was unbelieving for a time: “Lord,” he says (John 14:5), “we do not know where you are going, and how can we know the way?” And Jesus replied: “I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me. If you had known me, you would also have known the Father; but [Note: Seml. adds “also.”] from now on you know him and have seen him.”

And now we come [Col. 0186B] to Philip, who, stirred by the hope of seeing the Father, did not understand how he had heard that the Father had been seen: “Show us the Father,” he says, “and it is enough for us.” And the Lord answered: “Philip, have I been with you so long, and you have not known me?”

Whom does he say they ought to have known—for this alone must be examined—as the Father or as the Son? If as the Father, let Praxeas show that during all the time Christ had lived among them he could ever have been—not to say understood, but even supposed—to be the Father. [Note: “All the Scriptures, both the ancient ones,” etc. He alludes to all those ancient Scriptures in which Christ is called God, such as Psalm 2, Isaiah 45, Lamentations 4, and Daniel 9. PAM.] For us, all the Scriptures—both the ancient, which define him as the Christ of God, and the new, which define him as the Son of God [Note: “And the new define him as the Son of God.” Passages of this kind from the New Testament may be found at the beginnings of the Gospels of Mark, Luke, and John, and elsewhere in those same writers; Matthew 4; Acts 2 and 9; the Epistles to the Romans, Galatians, Ephesians, and Hebrews; and finally Revelation 2. PAM.]—establish this beforehand.

This was proclaimed in earlier times; this was declared by Christ himself; indeed, by now it had also been declared openly by the Father himself from heaven, confessing the Son and glorifying the Son: “This is my Son”; and, “I have glorified it and will glorify it.” [Col. 0186C] This was believed by the disciples; this was not believed by the Jews. Desiring them to believe this about himself, at every moment he named the Father, set the Father before them, and honored the Father.

If this is so, therefore, it was not the Father who had lived among them so long whom they had failed to know, but the Son. And when the Lord reproached Philip for not knowing him, he surely wished that one to be recognized whom he reproached them for having failed to recognize for so long—that is, the Son.

It will now also become apparent how the words “He who sees me sees [Note: others read “has seen.”] the Father also” were spoken: namely, in the same sense as the earlier statement, “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30). Why? Because “I came forth from God and have come”; and “I am [Col. 0187A] the way” (John 14:6): “no one comes to the Father except through me”; and “No one comes to me unless the Father has drawn him”; and “The Father has handed all things over to me”; and “As the Father gives life, so also does the Son”; and “If you have known me, you have also known the Father” (John 6:44 and related passages).

According to these sayings, he had shown himself to be the Father’s representative, through whom the Father might both be seen in deeds and heard in words, and might be known in the Son who administered the Father’s deeds and words. For the Father is invisible, as Philip too had learned in the Law and ought to have remembered: “No one shall see God and live.” He is therefore rebuked for desiring to see the Father as though the Father were visible, and is taught that the Father becomes visible in the Son through acts of power, not through a representation of his person.

Finally, if Christ wished the same being to be understood as both Father and Son by saying, “He who sees me sees the Father,” how does he add, “Do you not believe [Note: Seml. reads “Do you not believe” in the singular.] that I am [Col. 0187B] in the Father, and the Father is in me?” For he ought to have added: “Do you not believe [Note: same variant.] that I am the Father?” Or how did he reinforce his point if he did not disclose what he wished to be understood, namely, that he was the Son?

Rather, by saying, “Do you not believe that I am in the Father, and the Father is in me?” he reinforced precisely this point: lest, because he had said, “He who sees me sees [Note: Seml. reads “has seen.”] the Father also,” he should be thought to be the Father—something he never wished to be thought, since he always confessed himself to be the Son and to have come from the Father.

He therefore made clear the conjunction of two persons, so that the Father should not be sought separately as though visibly present to the sight, and so that the Son should be regarded as the Father’s representative. Moreover, he also interpreted how the Father was in the Son and the Son in the Father: “The words,” he says, “which I speak to you are not mine”—surely [Col. 0187C] because they are the Father’s—“but the Father who remains in me performs the works.”

Thus, by the works of power and the words of teaching, the Father who remains in the Son is seen through those things by which he remains and through him in whom he remains. In this very statement the distinctive property of each person appears, when he says: “I am in the Father, and the Father is in me.” And indeed: “Believe,” he says. What? That I am the Father? I do not think that is written; rather: “That I am in the Father, and the Father is in me. Otherwise, believe at least because of the works.” Those works, surely, through which the Father in the Son was seen not by sight, but by understanding.

## CHAPTER XXV

After Philip and the entire substance of this question, John continues to the end of his Gospel in the same mode of discourse, by which Father and Son are distinguished in their own proper identity [Note: Seml. reads “is distinguished.”]. Christ also promises that, when he has ascended to the Father, he will ask the Father for the Paraclete and send him—another Paraclete indeed, though we have already explained in what sense he is another.

Moreover, “He will take from what is mine” (John 16:14), he says, just as he himself takes from what belongs to the Father [Note: “Just as he himself from what belongs to the Father.” The sentence is elliptical, as he has often spoken above; “takes from that which belongs to the Father” is to be understood. Similarly below: “Do you not know,” he says, “that I must be among the things of my Father?” where “things” is understood. RHEN.].

Thus the connection of the Father in the Son and of the Son in the Paraclete produces three who cohere, one from another. These three are one, not one person, in the sense in which it was said (John 10:30): “I and the Father are one”—with reference to unity of substance, not singularity of number.

Continue through the passage, and you will find that the one whom you believe to be the Father is spoken of as acting in the Father’s stead. And you surely suppose the Father, the vineyard keeper, to have been on earth [Note: Jun. reads “and whom.”]. Yet again you acknowledge him to be in heaven when the Son, looking up there, entrusts [Note: Seml. reads “entrusted.”] his disciples to the Father.

But even if it had not been [Note: Seml. reads “is.”] revealed in this Gospel: “My God, why have you forsaken me?” (Matt. 27:46), and, “Father, into your hands I commit my spirit,” nevertheless, after the resurrection and the glory [Col. 0188B] of conquered death [Note: Seml. reads “glory”; another variant reads “necessity” as an accusative], when the necessity for every humiliation had been laid aside, he could by then have revealed himself as the Father to that faithful woman who, from love, not from curiosity or the unbelief of Thomas, attempted to touch him. Yet he said (John 20:17): “Do not touch me, for I have not yet ascended to my Father; but go to my brothers”—indeed, even in this he shows himself to be the Son [Note: Seml. reads “because even in this he shows himself to be the Son”], for he would have called them sons if he had been the Father—“and say to them: I ascend to my Father and your Father, my God and your God.”

Is it the Father going to the Father, and God going to God; or the Son going to the Father, and the Word to God? Why, too, does the concluding sentence of the Gospel attest that these things were written, if not “so that you may believe,” it says, “that Jesus Christ is the Son of God”?

Therefore, whatever among these sayings you may think can serve you as proof that Father and Son are the same, you are striving [Col. 0188C] against the Gospel’s definitive declaration. For they were not written so that you might believe [Note: Rhen. inserts “in.”] Jesus Christ to be the Father, but so that you might believe him to be the Son.

## CHAPTER XXVI

Because of Philip’s single statement and the Lord’s answer to him, we see [Note: Rhen. reads “we seem.”] that we have traversed the Gospel of John, lest one statement overturn so many things plainly declared both before and afterward. That one statement must instead be interpreted in accordance with all the others, rather than against them all—and even against its own meaning.

But, not to introduce the other Gospels, which confirm the same teaching [Note: Rhen. reads “faith.”] concerning the Lord’s nativity, it is enough that the one who was to be born of the virgin was announced by the angel himself and expressly defined as the Son of God: “The Spirit of God will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the holy thing that will be born from you [Col. 0188D] will be called the Son of God” (Luke 1:35).

They will indeed attempt to argue here also, but the truth will prevail. “Certainly,” they say, “the Son of God is God, and the power of the Most High is the Most High.” Nor are they ashamed to foist in an assertion which, if it were true, would have been written explicitly. Whom was the angel afraid of, that he did not openly declare, “God will come upon you, and the Most High will overshadow you”?

But by saying “the Spirit of God,” although the Spirit [Note: Fran. reads the accusative “Spirit.”] of God is God, yet without directly naming God, he wished a portion of the whole to be understood, which was to assume the name of Son. This Spirit of God will be the same as the Word. For just as, when John says, “The Word was made flesh,” we also understand the Spirit in the mention of the Word, so here too we recognize the Word under the name of Spirit. For the Spirit is the substance of the Word, and the Word is the operation of the Spirit, and the two are one.

Otherwise, John will confess that one being was made flesh and the angel that another was to become flesh, unless the Spirit is also the Word and the Word the Spirit. Therefore, just as the Word of God is not himself the one whose Word he is, so neither is the Spirit; and even if he is called God, nevertheless he is not himself the one whose Spirit he is said to be. Nothing belonging to someone is itself the one to whom it belongs.

Certainly, when something is from him, and is his precisely because it is from him, it can be something of the same kind as he from whom it comes and to whom it belongs. Therefore, the Spirit of God [Note: Rhen. omits “of God.”] is God, and the Word of God [Note: same omission.] is God, because each is from God; yet neither is himself the one from whom he comes.

But if he is “God of God,” as a substantival entity, he will not be God himself, but God to this extent: because he is from the substance of God himself, by virtue of which [Note: Lat. reads “because”; Rhen. reads “which.”] he too is a substantival entity, and is, as it were, a portion of the whole.

Still more, “the power of the Most High” will not be the Most High himself, because power is not even a substantival entity, as the Spirit is; nor are wisdom and providence. For these are not substances, but attributes of each substance. Power is an attribute of the Spirit, and will not itself [Col. 0189C] be the Spirit.

Therefore, when these realities—whatever precisely they are—the Spirit of God, the Word, and the power, were brought together in the virgin, what is born from her is the Son of God. [Note: Rhen. adds “if.”]

In these Gospels too he himself testifies to this about himself immediately from childhood: “Do you not know,” he says (Luke 2:49), “that I must be among the things of my Father?” Satan also recognizes him as such in the temptations: “If you are the Son of God.” From that point onward the demons too confess it: “We know who you are: the Son of God.” He himself worships the Father. He does not deny that Peter recognized him as the Christ of God.

Exulting [Note: Venet. reads “insulting.”] in the Spirit, he says to the Father: “I confess to you, Father, because you have hidden these things from the wise” (Matt. 11:25). Here too he affirms that the Father is known to no one except the Son. He affirms that the Father’s Son will confess before the Father those who confess him and deny those who deny him. He presents the parable of the son—not the father—sent into the vineyard [Col. 0189D] after several servants, killed by the wicked tenants, and vindicated by the Father. He himself does not know the final day and hour, which are known to the Father alone. He assigns a kingdom [Col. 0190A] to his disciples just as, he says, one was assigned to him by the Father. He possesses the power, if he wished, to request legions of angels from the Father as assistance. He cries out that God had forsaken him and places his spirit in the Father’s hands. After the resurrection, he promises to send his disciples the Father’s promise. Finally, he commands them to baptize into the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit—not into one person. For we are baptized not once but three times, at each name into each individual person.

## CHAPTER XXVII

Why do I linger [Note: Rhen. reads “shall I linger?”] over matters so manifest, when I must address the passages by which they seek to obscure what is manifest? For, hemmed in on every side by the distinction between Father and Son—which we order while preserving their conjunction, like that of sun and ray and of spring and river, yet according to the inseparable number of two and three—they nevertheless attempt to interpret this distinction according to their own opinion. Thus they distinguish both Father and Son within one person, saying that the Son is the flesh, that is, the human being, that is, Jesus; while the Father is the Spirit, that is, God, that is, Christ.

And those who contend that Father and Son are one and the same now begin [Note: “To divide them rather than unite them.” Rhenanus says that Tertullian uses the simple verb *unare*, from which we derive *adunare* and *coadunare*. Although [Col. 0190D] three Vatican manuscript codices read *unire*, we have retained *unare*, since it has a stronger flavor of African usage. PAM.] to divide them rather than unite them. For if Jesus is one and Christ another, the Son will be one and [Note: Rhen. omits the second “one/another.”] the Father another, since the Son is Jesus and the Father Christ. Perhaps they learned such a monarchy from Valentinus: to make two, Jesus and Christ.

But this objection of theirs has already been blunted by what was discussed beforehand [Note: Rhen. reads “reconsidered”]: namely, that the one whom they make the Father is called the Word of God, or the Spirit of God, and the power of the Most High. For these are not themselves the one whose they are said to be, but are from him and belong to him. Nevertheless, they will also be refuted in another way in this chapter. [Col. 0190C]

“Behold,” they say, “it was proclaimed by the angel: ‘Therefore the holy thing that will be born will be called the Son of God.’ Accordingly, the flesh was born, and the flesh surely [Note: Rhen. reads “therefore.”] will be the Son of God.”

No: it was said of the Spirit of God. For certainly the virgin conceived from the Holy Spirit, and what she conceived she bore; therefore, what had been conceived and was to be delivered was what had to be born—that is, the Spirit, whose name will also be called Emmanuel, which is translated “God with us.”

The flesh, however, is not God, so that it should have been said of the flesh, “The holy thing that will be born will be called [Note: Rhen. reads “will be called holy.”] the Son of God.” Rather, the one who was born in it is God, of whom the Psalm also says (Ps. 87:5): “For God was born as man in her, and he built her by the will of the Father.”

What God was born in her? The Word and the Spirit, who was born with the Word by the Father’s will. Therefore, since the Word was in the flesh, we must also inquire concerning this [Note: Latin reads “Finally, this.”]: how was [Col. 0190D] the Word made flesh? Was he transformed, as it were, into flesh, or clothed with flesh? No: he was clothed with it.

For God must be believed immutable and incapable of change of form, since he is eternal. Transformation is the destruction [Col. 0191A] of the former condition. [Note: “Transformation is the destruction of the former condition.” This passage supports transubstantiation, which Catholics affirm in the sacrament of the Eucharist but deny in the assumption of Christ’s humanity, since, according to the Creed of blessed Athanasius, he is one “not by conversion of the divinity into flesh, but by assumption of the humanity into [Col. 0192D] God.” PAM.]

For everything that is transformed into something else ceases to be what it had been and begins to be what it had not been. But God neither ceases to be nor can be something else. The Word, moreover, is God, and “the Word of the Lord remains forever,” surely by continuing in his own form.

If he is not capable of being transformed [Note: Rhen. reads “conformed.”], it follows that he must be understood to have been made flesh in this sense: that he comes to be [Note: Fran. reads “is.”] in flesh, and is manifested, seen, and handled through the flesh. For the other considerations also require that the passage be understood in this way.

For if the Word was made flesh by transformation and alteration of substance, Jesus will now be one substance from two—a kind of mixture of flesh and Spirit, like electrum from gold and silver. He begins to be neither gold, that is, Spirit, nor [Col. 0191B] silver, that is, flesh, since the one is changed by the other and some third thing is produced.

Jesus will therefore no longer be God: for the Word, who was made flesh, has ceased to exist. Nor will he be flesh, that is, man [Note: Rhen. reads “nor man flesh”]; for one who was the Word is not properly flesh [Note: Rhen. reads “because.”]. Thus from both he is neither; he is something else, a third thing far removed from either.

Yet we find him expressly set forth as both God and man, as this very Psalm indicates: “For God was born as man in her, [Note: Rhen. inserts “and.”] and he built her by the will of the Father.” He is assuredly everywhere called both Son of God and son of man, since he is without doubt God and man, with each of the two substances remaining distinct in its own proper identity: for the Word is nothing other than God, and the flesh nothing other than man.

So too the Apostle (Rom. 1:3–4) teaches concerning both his substances: “Who was made,” he says, “from the seed of David.” This will be the man and son of man, who “was designated Son of God according to the Spirit.” This will be God and the Word of God, the Son.

We see a twofold condition, not confused [Note: “Not confused but conjoined in one person,” etc. God and man are, of course, conjoined while the proper identity of each substance is preserved, *οὐχὶ ἐκ συγχύσεως τῆς οὐσίας*—“not from a confusion of the substance.” These words do not seem very far removed from the Creed of blessed Athanasius. LE PR.], but conjoined in one person, Jesus, God and man. Concerning “Christ,” however, I defer discussion [Note: Seml. reads “I discuss.”].

And so fully is the proper identity of each substance preserved that the Spirit performed in him its own activities—that is, powers, works, and signs—while the flesh underwent its own sufferings: hungering under the devil’s temptation, thirsting with the Samaritan woman, weeping for Lazarus, being distressed even to death, and finally dying.

But if he had been some third thing, confused from both like electrum, such distinct proofs of each substance would not appear. Rather, through the interchange, the Spirit would have performed fleshly acts and the flesh spiritual acts; [Col. 0192A] or else [Note: Rhen. reads “or.”] neither fleshly nor spiritual acts would have occurred, but acts of some third form arising from [Note: Venet., Fran., Paris., and Rig. omit “from.”] the confusion.

Indeed, either the Word would have died, or the flesh would not have died, if the Word had been changed into flesh. For either the flesh would have been immortal or the Word mortal. But because both substances, each distinctly, acted in its own condition [Note: Paris. reads “distinct.”], their respective works and outcomes therefore befell them.

Learn, then, with Nicodemus (John 3:6): “What is born in [Note: Latin reads “from.”] the flesh is flesh, and what is born of the Spirit is spirit.” Flesh does not become Spirit, nor Spirit flesh. They certainly can exist in one being. Jesus consisted of these: from the flesh, man; from the Spirit, God. The angel then declared him the Son of God with respect to that part by which he was Spirit, while preserving “son of man” as the designation of the flesh.

So too the Apostle, by calling him the mediator of God and human beings (1 Tim. 2:5), confirmed both substances. Finally, you who interpret the Son of God as the flesh: show what the son of man is. Or will it perhaps be the Spirit? But you wish the Spirit to be regarded as the Father himself, because “God is Spirit”—as though there were not also the Spirit of God, just as the Word is God and there is also the Word of God.

## CHAPTER XXVIII

And so you make Christ the Father, most foolish man, without even examining the meaning of this title—if indeed “Christ” is a name and not rather an appellation; for it means “anointed.” [Note: Rhen. reads “for rather ‘anointed.’”] “Anointed,” however, is no more a name than “clothed” or “shod”: it is a circumstance added to a name.

Suppose that, on the basis of some consideration, Jesus were also called “clothed,” just as he is called Christ from the sacrament of anointing. Would you likewise say that Jesus was the Son of God, but believe that “the clothed one” was the Father?

Now concerning Christ. If the Father is Christ, the Father is anointed, and surely by another. Or, if by himself, prove it. But the Acts of the Apostles do not teach this, in that exclamation of the Church [Note: Rhen. omits “of the Church.”] to God: “For all assembled” (Acts 4:27) “in this city against your holy Son Jesus [Note: Rhen. omits “Jesus.”], whom you anointed—Herod and Pilate together with the nations.” Thus they both testified that Jesus was the Son of God and that the Son was anointed by the Father.

Therefore, Jesus [Note: Seml. reads “the Lord.”] will be the same as Christ, who was anointed by the Father; he will not be the Father, who anointed the Son. So too Peter says (Acts 2:36): “Therefore let the whole house of Israel know with absolute certainty that God has made this Jesus whom you crucified both Lord and Christ”—that is, anointed.

John, moreover (1 John 2:22), brands as a liar the person who denies that Jesus is the Christ; conversely, he says that everyone who believes Jesus to be the Christ has been born of God. Therefore he also exhorts us to believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ, so that we may have communion with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ.

Thus Paul too everywhere sets forth God the Father and our Lord Jesus Christ. When writing to the Romans (1:8), he gives thanks to God through our Lord Jesus Christ. When writing to the Galatians (1:1), he declares that he is an apostle not from human beings nor through a human being, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father.

And you have all his writings [Note: Rhen. reads “his whole document.”], which declare matters in this way and set forth two: God the Father and our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father’s Son. They also set forth Jesus himself as the Christ, the Son of God under this second designation as well. [Col. 0193B]

For hence, by the principle that both names belong to one being, namely the Son of God [Note: Rhen. reads “Son.”], either one without the other also belongs to that same being. Whether only “Jesus” is used, “Christ” is also understood, because Jesus [Note: Rhen. adds “Christ.”] is anointed. Or whether only “Christ” is used, the same one is Jesus [Note: Rhen. omits “only” and “Jesus.”], because Jesus is the one anointed.

Of these names, one is proper, the name imposed by the angel; the other is circumstantial, belonging to him by reason of the anointing—provided, however, that Christ is the Son, not the Father.

Finally, how blind is the person who does not understand that even in the name “Christ” another God is indicated, if he ascribes the name Christ to the Father [Note: Seml. reads “the name of Christ to the Father”]? For if Christ is God the Father, he who says (John 20:17), “I ascend to my Father and your Father, and my God and your God,” plainly shows another Father and God above himself.

Likewise, if the Father is Christ, then another is the one [Col. 0193C] who establishes the thunder, creates the Spirit, and announces his Christ among human beings (Amos 4:13). And if “the kings of the earth took their stand, and the rulers assembled together against his Christ” (Ps. 2:2), then another will be the Lord against whose Christ the kings and rulers assembled.

And if: “Thus says the Lord to my lord Christ” (Isa. 45:1), another will be the Lord who speaks to the Father of Christ. And when the Apostle writes (Eph. 1:17), “That the God of our Lord Jesus Christ may give you a spirit of wisdom and knowledge,” another will be the God of Christ Jesus, the giver of spiritual gifts.

Certainly, not to wander through every passage, “he who raised Christ will also raise our mortal bodies” (Rom. 8:11). Thus the one who raises will now be another than the dead Father and the Father who was raised [Note: Rhen. reads “as though another”; Wouw.’s codex reads “surely another”], if Christ, who died, is the Father.

## CHAPTER XXIX

[Col. 0193D]

Let this blasphemy fall silent—let it fall silent. Let it suffice to say that Christ, the Son of God, died; and this only because it is so [Col. 0194A] written. For the Apostle too, when declaring—not without gravity—that Christ died, added [Note: others read “adds.”], “according to the Scriptures,” so that he might soften the harshness of the declaration by the authority of the Scriptures and remove the stumbling block for the hearer.

Yet since two substances are reckoned in Christ Jesus, divine and human; and since it is established that the divine is immortal, just as that which is human is mortal [Note: Jun. reads “it mortal”; Rhen. reads “since mortal”], it is apparent in what respect he calls him dead: namely, insofar as he is flesh, human being, and son of man—not insofar as he is Spirit, Word, and Son of God.

Finally, by saying “Christ died”—that is, “the anointed one”—he shows that what was anointed died, namely, the flesh.

“Therefore,” you say, “when we declare by the same reasoning that the Father died, just as you declare the Son did [Note: Seml. omits “the Father died”; Rhen. reads “we” for “you”], we do not blaspheme against the Lord God; for we say that he died not in his divine substance but in his human substance.”

On the contrary, you blaspheme not only because you say that the Father died, but also because you say that he was crucified. For by transferring to the Father the curse of the crucified, which according to the Law applies to the Son—because Christ became a curse [Note: Fran., Paris., and Rig. read “by a curse.”] for us, not the Father—and by transforming Christ into the Father, you blaspheme against the Father.

We, however, when we say that Christ was crucified, do not curse him, but report the curse of the Law; for the Apostle did not blaspheme when he said this either. Just as something which a subject is capable of having said about it may be said without blasphemy, so if something of which it is not capable is said of it, that is blasphemy.

Nor, therefore, did the Father suffer with the Son. For, being afraid of direct blasphemy against the Father, they hope to lessen it in this way, now conceding that Father and Son are two: if [Note: Rhen. reads “since.”] the Son suffers, the Father suffers with him. They are fools [Col. 0194C] in this too. For what is “to suffer with” except to suffer together with another?

Further, if the Father is incapable of suffering, he is surely also incapable of suffering with another. Or, if he is capable of suffering with another, he is surely capable of suffering. You gain nothing for him even by this fear of yours [Note: Seml. reads “as though dead.”]. You are afraid to call him capable of suffering, though you call him capable of suffering with another.

The Father, however, is just as incapable of suffering with another [Note: Seml. reads “capable of suffering with another.”] as the Son too is incapable of suffering in respect of that condition in which he is God.

But how did the Son suffer, if the Father did not suffer with him? He was separated [Note: Rhen. reads “is separated.”] from the Son, not from God. For even if a river is contaminated by some disturbance, although a single substance flows down from the spring and is not severed from the spring, nevertheless the injury to the river will not extend to the spring.

And although it is the water of the spring that suffers in the river, yet because it suffers not in the spring but in the river, it is not the spring that suffers, but the river which comes from the spring. Thus, even if the Spirit of God [Col. 0194D] could suffer something [Note: Seml. reads “can”; Paris reads “Thus also he who suffers.”] in the Son, yet because it would suffer not in the Father but in the Son, the Father would not be regarded as having suffered.

But it is enough that the Spirit of God suffered nothing in his own proper capacity; for if he suffered anything, he suffered it in the Son, [Col. 0195A] in whom the Father also was [Note: Semler reads “indeed was as Father”], while the Son [Note: Semler reads “in the Son.”] suffered in the flesh—because this has already been discussed [Note: Semler reads “discussed” in agreement with a masculine subject].

Nor will anyone deny it, since even we cannot suffer for God unless [Note: Seml. omits “not”; another Seml. variant reads “if.”] the Spirit of God is in us, who also speaks from within us the words belonging to confession—yet without himself suffering, but enabling us to suffer.
