67. To direct one’s step. Vol. III, Book IV Against Marcion, [Col. 0225C] chap. 37, no. 601.

It is to direct the force of one’s intellect toward something, to prepare oneself to examine something.

68. To plant one’s step. Vol. II, book On the Veiling of Virgins, chap. 1, no. 86; and Vol. III, Book IV Against Marcion, chap. 2, no. 18; Book V Against Marcion, chap. 10, no. 154; book Against Praxeas, chap. 8, no. 56, and chap. 22, no. 135; Vol. V, book On Fasting, chap. 11, no. 71.

RHENANUS. A metaphor from athletes engaging in a contest; a proverbial expression. It means to take one’s stand steadfastly; and in so many passages of Tertullian it means vigorously to defend an opinion once adopted and to persist in it; or to press one’s argument, as Cicero puts it; or to dwell upon the careful consideration of a single matter [Col. 0225D] and make no digressions into things irrelevant to the subject.

69. To plant one’s step in the battle line. Vol. V, book On Flight in Persecution, chap. 11, no. 80.

Frequent in Virgil is “Stand, men,” an exhortation that they halt their flight; hence the name of Jupiter Stator. And in the *Aeneid*, Book 10:

> Do not trust in your feet: through the foe with steel  
> A path must be broken.

Proverbially: not to be moved by the opponents’ arguments, to persist stubbornly in one’s opinion, and vigorously to defend one’s cause. In the book *On Fasting*: “Stand firm in that position, if you can.”

70. To move one’s step. Vol. V, book On Fasting, chap. 13, no. 78.

Tertullian interprets it thus: “You have moved your step by departing [Col. 0226A] from tradition, since you observe practices that have not been established.” It therefore means to depart from one’s established course or prescribed standard, and to yield in defeat to one’s opponent. Virgil, Book II:

> And, with his voice, checked his backward step.

71. To block the approach. Vol. I, *Apology*, chap. 27, no. 424; Vol. II, book On the Veiling of Virgins, chap. 15, no. 120; Vol. III, book On Prescription Against Heretics, chap. 15, no. 99; and book On the Resurrection of the Flesh, chap. 48, no. 344.

“We therefore block this approach above all: they must not be admitted to any debate concerning the Scriptures”; that is, we raise this as our first objection and exclude them from the encounter. In the *Apology*: “When challenged to sacrifice, we block the approach of faithlessness” (another reading: “on behalf of the faith”) “to our conscience”; that is, we forestall faithlessness, lest it creep into our minds. [Col. 0226B] Cicero: “to block the refuges of the wicked.” In the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*: “so that what our opponents block in the very first battle line, we might overthrow in the final encounter.”

72. To hold one’s ground. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 23.

Those who are attacked are said to withstand, or endure, hostile force and assault. Hence, proverbially, it means to undertake or sustain the defense of something. Tertullian transferred it thus to literary controversy: “Another little book will hold this ground against the heretics.”

73. To remain upon a single step. Vol. III, *Scorpiace* Against the Gnostics, chap. 8, no. 52.

Tertullian adds by way of explanation: “On this point alone [Col. 0226C] we issue our challenge.” To halt, to withdraw one’s foot, and to advance farther are opposites. Cicero: “To halt is to remain in a certain position.” Proverbially: not to depart from the debate as instituted, but to apply oneself to the investigation of a single matter.

74. This position will stand. Vol. III, book On the Soul, chap. [number omitted], no. 83.

Tertullian: “But neither will this position stand”; that is: this reasoning, definition, or argument will not be firm or hold its ground; it will readily be refuted and weakened. Affirmatively: I shall undertake to defend this against every line of reasoning; I shall champion the truth of this proposition.

75. To meet the present position. Vol. III, Book I [Col. 0226D] Against Marcion, chap. 19, no. 125.

Cicero: “to meet and oppose someone’s designs.” In Tertullian it means, as occasion requires, to parry an argument with an answer and a contrary line of reasoning.

76. To skirmish from the first position. Vol. III, Book III Against Marcion, chap. 5, no. 14.

Tertullian adds: “still, and as though from a distance”; immediately opposing to it: “to fight decisively and at close quarters,” expressions explained above that bear upon this one. Before their proper contest with an antagonist, gladiators customarily performed a preliminary display in order to exhibit their skill and entertain the spectators, as Acestes does in Virgil:

> Displaying alike his skill and the sounding bow.

It therefore means to bring forward light and almost playful [Col. 0227A] and weaker arguments at the outset, before the real strength of the arguments is produced.

77. To be at a stand. Vol. V, book On Modesty, 6. 10, no. 83.

To dwell upon a single argument. Tertullian contrasts with it “to proceed.” We may interpret the latter as drawing an inference from premises or propositions and arriving at the final point of the matter to be proved. A man may be said to be wholly “at a stand” when he makes propositions but reaches no conclusion. One who employs a sorites, or cumulative argument, lingers over its beginnings and remains far from its final conclusion.

78. To lead into a position. Ibid., chap. 21, no. 183.

To prepare oneself for a new investigation of the matter; after explaining one point, to advance to another.

[Col. 0227B] 79. Back into position. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 9, no. 155.

When, carried and drawn rather far away from our subject by a digression or ἐκβασιν (“digression”)“, we return to the point from which we turned aside and once again discuss and examine the same matter.

80. To issue a challenge upon the very ground. Vol. III, Book V Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 12.

Taken from those who challenge another to fight: once they have taken their stand, they await their opponent and provoke him with a threatening weapon. It therefore means to attack the main point of the matter, not to trace the question farther back or fetch it from afar, but to investigate from the outset the matters over which the struggle is being waged and not depart from their investigation.

[Col. 0227C] 81. With the reins let loose. Vol. V, Letter on Jewish Foods, chap. 4, no. 14.

*Habenae* are reins attached on either side to the bridle for guiding a horse. Hence the metaphors “to give the loosest reins to friendship.” Cicero. Virgil, Book V:

> He sets the foaming  
> Bridles upon the beasts, and with his hands lets all the reins run free.

(Properly Book XII:)

> Without distinction he arouses slaughter  
> And lets loose all the reins of wrath.

[“Translate” appears in the source.] An orator is also said, in his peroration, to open all the springs of eloquence and let loose all the reins of emotion. The contrary is “to cast on the reins,” meaning to restrain and check an impulse. “To let go, give, or loosen the reins” corresponds to the Greeks’ δοῦναι χαλινοὺς πᾶσαν ἀφιέναι, ἢ ὃλην ἐφιέναι [Col. 0227D] τὴν ἡνίαν, meaning to indulge excessively and allow excessive freedom. “With the reins let loose” means with the greatest license, freedom, and lack of restraint, or immoderately. Novatian likewise, in the book *On the Trinity*: “He poured forth the broad folds of his indulgence”; and: “For under the yoke of the natural law given to all, some things are drawn back as though recalled by bridles, while others are driven forward as though spurred on with reins let loose.”

82. More unstable than a Hamaxobius. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 12.

See Pamelius’ annotations. The Hamaxobii, or Hamaxobitae, a people of Sarmatia, are celebrated by historians and poets. Horace describes them thus in Book III, Ode 24:

> The Scythians of the plains live better,  
> Whose wagons duly draw their wandering homes,  
> And the hardy Getae,  
> [Col. 0228A] For whom unfenced acres bear free  
> Crops and grain,  
> Nor does cultivation lasting longer than a year please them.

It will therefore be said not only of one who continually changes his dwelling and everywhere plays the part of a wanderer, but also of one who is inconstant through the changeableness of his opinion.

83. Colder than winter. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 14.

Cicero says that words, judgments, and men are “cold”; and, using opposed metaphors, “to be heated in a very cold matter and cold in a very heated one”; and “slow in speaking and almost cold,” of a tasteless man of sluggish and stupid intellect, just as “hot” is said of one with a lively and animated spirit. And “hot counsels” means precipitate and rash counsels.

[Col. 0228B] 84. An unbreathable underwater depth. Vol. II, book On Idolatry, final chapter, no. 170.

See Pamelius’ annotations: ῶ [an isolated, apparently corrupt character from the preceding mixed-script word]; ὑποβρύχιον, τὸ ὑπὸ τοῦ κυμάτων καλυπτόμενον, “that which is covered by waves.” Homer, *Odyssey*:

> Τονδ` ὑπόβρυχα θῆκε πολὺν χρόνον (“He kept this man submerged for a long time”)“,

where ὑπόβρυχα is used poetically for ὑποβρύχιον, τὸν καταδεδυκότα εἰς τὸ ὕδωρ, “one who has gone beneath the water.” Such, substantially, is Paulus Leopardi, *Miscellanies*, Book VIII, chap. 23. But since Tertullian says: “an ineffable deep for those thrown off, an inextricable shipwreck for those dashed against it, an unbreathable underwater depth for those swallowed up,” he seems to mean a place—namely, the very bottom of the sea—to which both those thrown off who cannot swim and those dashed away, clinging neither to planks nor rocks, are carried down. The proverb will therefore signify extreme peril, in which every hope of safety and of emerging [Col. 0228C] from one’s troubles has been taken away.

85. To consider another blow. Vol. III, *Scorpiace* Against the Gnostics, chap. 5, no. 35.

“This stroke has been met; let us consider another blow,” etc. Others punctuate the passage very differently. Taken from gladiators. Cicero, *First Catilinarian*: “How many attacks of yours, launched so that they seemed impossible to evade, have I escaped by a slight turn and, as they say, by a movement of the body?” And Virgil, Book V, concerning the boxer:

> Now this approach, now that, he ranges through, and every place  
> He explores with skill, pressing vainly with varying assaults.  
> Entellus, rising, showed his right hand and raised it high;  
> The other swiftly foresaw the blow descending from above  
> And, slipping away with agile body, withdrew.

Thus it means, after one argument has been refuted, to seek how another may be weakened [Col. 0228D] and to devise within oneself a line of reasoning by which it may be disproved.

86. More deceitful than the Ister. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 15.

See Pamelius’ annotations. Who would say that this is taken from an animal called *histrix* [reading uncertain]? The inhabitants of the region of Istria are called not *Istri* or *Histri*, but *Istrii*; and I cannot discover why anyone should attribute deceit to them. It remains, therefore, that the river Ister, otherwise called the Danube, gave rise to this proverb, since Tertullian assigns Marcion so many titles and honorific appellations derived from places, peoples, Pontus, regions neighboring his homeland, and things peculiar to it. The Ister will be called deceitful either because of its winding bends—like the Maeander, whence poets call it twisting and sinuous—[Col. 0229A] or because of the multitude of its mouths, among which one is the *Pseudostoma*, as though “false” or “deceitful mouth,” which misleads those navigating on their intended course; or because, by a sudden inundation, it often overwhelms neighboring inhabitants unexpectedly. Tibullus:

> The deceitful witness too,  
> The Pannonian who dwells beside the Ister.

Ausonius:

> And the source of the Ister, unknown to Latin annals.

(For “unknown” breathes the same sense: unknown because it deceives and lies hidden.)

87. To shake off the yoke; and: to free one’s neck from the yoke. Vol. V, book On Modesty, chaps. 10 and 12, nos. 81 and 95.

The yoke is what oxen wear when they draw a plow or cart. Cicero: “Oxen’s necks were born for the yoke.” [Col. 0229B] Metaphorically it signifies slavery or a heavy burden. Hence the expression “to send under the yoke.” “To cast the yoke from men’s necks” means to free them from violent domination and the heavy burden of servitude. The contrary appears in Virgil, Book XII:

> Unless they confess that they must accept the bridle and obey in defeat.

Tertullian, in the book *On Idolatry*: “He loosened the bond and yoke for us”; and in the book *On Modesty*: “The yokes of works have been cast off, not those of discipline.”

88. The towers of Lamia. Vol. III, book Against the Valentinians, chap. 3, no. 23.

Politianus says much about the Lamiae in the preface to his *Dialectic*, as do Brassicanus, his commentator; Hadrianus Junius, Adage 34, Century 2; and Caelius Rhodiginus, chap. 5, Book XXIX. There, after much has been said about the mistress of Menippus the Lycian, who was now on the point of being taken as his wife, [Col. 0229C] a certain philosopher says: “This beautiful bride is one of the ῶ [an isolated, apparently corrupt character from the preceding mixed-script word]; τῶν Ἐμπουσῶν, ἅς λαμίας τε καὶ μορμολυκίας οἱ πολλοὶ νοοῦσιν εἶναι (“Empusae, whom most people understand to be Lamiae and Mormolyciae”)“”; that is, one of the Empusae, whom many also identify with the Lamiae and Mormolyciae. Tertullian mentions towers because, according to the tale, Lamia, a queen in the regions of Libya, inhabited an enormous cavern at the foot of a cliff. The expression will therefore be used of things terrifying in appearance but containing no danger: names invented to inspire terror, old wives’ tales.

89. It is defended on this flank. Vol. III, book On the Resurrection of the Flesh, chap. 2, no. 21.

In a battle line—that is, an army arrayed for combat—there were the front, rear, and two flanks, also called wings and horns. “A covered flank” is said of those protected by arms and [Col. 0229D] defenses; “an exposed flank,” of those exposed to danger. Terence, *The Self-Tormentor*:

> I triumph if I am allowed to get away with my flank covered;

that is, in such a way as to bring no danger upon myself. Caesar, Book II of *The Gallic War*: “This circumstance also fortified one flank of the camp with the banks of the river”; and: “with almost the whole camp exposed at the front and on the left side”; and: “some of whom surrounded the legions on their exposed flank.” Nennius in the *Miscellades*—if I remember rightly—and Muretus after him, on Terence, maintain that it is taken from gladiators, for whom, even if they cannot inflict a ἀντιπάλῳ (“upon their opponent”)“ wound, it is honorable enough to leave with their flank covered—that is, whole and uninjured. They also used the expression “to present an exposed flank” when they did not evade the blow but freely offered themselves to the executioner and to death. “To be defended on this flank,” therefore, means to be established by these and like [Col. 0230A] arguments, reasonings, and proofs; in the same passage Tertullian uses the word “fortified,” likewise a military term.

90. From either flank. Vol. V, book On the Trinity, chap. 26, no. 112.

Words frequent among jurists. Ulpian, Book III of the *Digest*, title *On Procurators*: “so that he may be regarded as plaintiff from either side.” And Justinian, Book V of the *Code of Law*, says: “Authorities on either side engaged in a great contest.” Tertullian: “From either flank truth confines us to this conclusion”; that is, on both sides and from both aspects of the argument.

91. On whichever flank you wish. Vol. III, Book IV Against Marcion, chap. 34, no. 544.

The same as the preceding, “from either flank,” which Tertullian [Col. 0230B] explains: “You have, on whichever flank of marriage you wish, one who looks ahead”; that is, in whatever direction you turn.

92. On which of the flanks? Vol. V, book On Fasting, chap. 11, no. 72.

On which side of the argument? By what reasoning? In which direction? Here too he uses the word “confirmed,” so that the expression may be referred to a covered flank in an army or gladiator.

93. Lions in peace; see above: Deer in battle. Vol. II, book On the Soldier’s Crown, chap. 1, no. 18.

It applies to those who behave perversely: where the situation demands boldness and courage, they are timid; they are fierce where there is no need of ferocity. Livy: “turbulent in peace, sluggish in war.” Synesius: ἐν μὲν ἐιρήνῃ θρασεῖς, ἐν δὲ πολέμῳ δειλοί, fierce in peace, fearful in war.

[Col. 0230C] 94. To meet one at the threshold. Vol. V, book On Monogamy, chap. 8, no. 52.

To present oneself at the very entrance to a work and a disputation.

95. To stumble at the threshold. Vol. III, book Against the Valentinians, chap. 3, no. 22.

RHENANUS. A proverb concerning those who err at the beginning of an undertaking they have assumed. Similar to: “The gelding at the gate.” Comparable expressions are: “To strike against something in port”; “To fail at the threshold.” Virgil:

> Why do we fail ingloriously at the very first threshold?

That is, to err at the very commencement of the work and business undertaken. It is transferred from those who, when leaving a house, [Col. 0230D] strike their foot against the threshold. To have struck the threshold, to have one’s garment catch upon it, or to slip and fall were inauspicious portents and omens of evil, frequently in the poets. Ovid:

> She is said to have fallen forward when her foot struck against it.

96. To enact the lines. Vol. III, book On the Soul, chap. 43, no. 480.

“To enact daily, most especially, the lines of human beginning and end.” The following proverbs drawn from lines have been transferred partly from geometry, which accomplishes everything by lines; partly from racecourses and wrestling grounds, whose spaces were enclosed by fixed lines; and partly from painters, who customarily draw lines with the brush before they begin to add shadow without color. [Col. 0231A] Tertullian seems to derive this first expression either from the beginning and end of the courses in the racecourse or from painting, since he adds: “extending the hand of faith, which is more easily assisted through images and parables of things as well as words.” Then he sets the matter before you, as though placing it before your eyes in a painting: “even through an image of death.”

97. To draw certain lines beforehand, upon which the contest will be waged. Vol. III, book Against Marcion, chap. 5, no. 14.

This is taken from those who meet in a duel or single combat, or from those who race in a stadium; it means to define beforehand a fixed rule for the subject matter.

98. To delimit by fixed lines. Vol. I, book Against the Jews, chap. 2, no. 6.

Tertullian adds: “the substance of the question itself.” It comes [Col. 0231B] from the same lines of contestants, within which they take their stand to fight. In the book *On Modesty*: “We fix the same boundary of the threshold.” There *limen* means “line,” as in Virgil, “and they leave the threshold.” It means to prescribe fixed bounds and fixed rules for a debate already instituted.

99. To enclose within lines. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 7, no. 51.

“Although the whole subject concerning two Gods is being contested, for the present we have enclosed it within these lines; within them we shall engage.” It therefore means to define beforehand certain fixed arguments, within which, as though within barriers, the freer wandering of discourse may be confined.

100. To lead within lines. Ibid., chap. 25.

Cicero: “To this point the case is brought.” “I have brought the matter to this point,” that is, I have brought it to this state or place. [Col. 0231C] Tertullian: “As regards the question of goodness, within these lines we have brought it to the conclusion that it is in no way equal to God”; that is, we have treated it within these limits.

101. To draw fixed lines beforehand for a single form of encounter. Vol. III, *Scorpiace* Against the Gnostics, chap. 4, no. 32.

Tertullian first says: “We, fighting elsewhere concerning God.” It means to prescribe fixed rules for oneself, within which the debate concerning one and the same matter may run its course.

102. The final line. Vol. III, book Against Hermogenes, chap. 3, no. 19, and chap. 38, nos. 132–133; and book Against Praxeas, chap. 16, no. 100.

RHENANUS. In ancient courses where races were held publicly, the beginning and end were marked by lines. Hence [Col. 0231D] everything that is last began proverbially to be called “the final line.” Therefore, when Tertullian here calls Hermogenes “the final line of those without understanding,” take it as meaning the most ignorant of all. Horace closes his epistle to Quintus thus:

> Death is the final line of things.

Turnebus also discusses this in *Adversaria*, Book XII, chap. 10, where he explains a similar passage in Terence; it was also called the “chalk line,” because it was marked with lime or chalk. Beyond the chalk line nothing remains toward which the race may tend. Yet when God of the universe, in the book *Against Praxeas*, is called “the final line,” the author seems rather to allude to Revelation I and XXII: “I am α (“alpha”)“ and ω (“omega”)“,” or “the beginning and the end.” In the book *Against Hermogenes*: “If it is delimited, it has a final line; [Col. 0232A] and since you are properly a painter, you recognize that the final line is the end of everything that has one.” In Terence, “to love to the final line” means to love most intensely: ἥ ἄκρα γραμμὴ, “the highest line and furthest limit,” so that supreme love is meant there. In the same way Euripides, when he meant the furthest limit of evils and miseries, said ἐπ ἄκρον ἥκομεν γραμμὴν τῶν κακῶν (“We have reached the utmost limit of evils”)“. Yet it would not be inept to understand “the final line in love” in Terence as the mere sight of the beloved or desired object—as though loving along long lines, that is, from afar. Erasmus thinks that Tertullian calls Hermogenes “the final line” as though he were the last and latest of the heretics of his time who still survived in Tertullian’s day.

[Col. 0232B] 103. To fight at the line. Vol. V, book On Modesty, chap. 6, no. 59.

“At this line now our encounter will be fought.” He then explains himself: “Yet I must prescribe a rule for you, lest you extend your hand toward former matters, lest you look back.” It therefore means, in disputation, not to wander from the matter proposed.

104. To pass beyond the lines. Vol. III, book Against Hermogenes, chap. 39, no. 137.

To stray from one’s purpose and proposition. The metaphor seems taken from those who, failing to keep upon the lines, fall out or pass beyond them—that is, put a foot beyond the line during the contest.

105. Beyond the line. Ibid.

Beyond the prescribed limit of the disputation.

[Col. 0232C] 106. There is one line. Vol. III, *Scorpiace* Against the Gnostics, chap. 11, no. 84.

There is one goal toward which the substance of the whole disputation must be directed.

107. To direct toward a single line of encounter. Vol. III, book On the Flesh of Christ, chap. 17, no. 135.

To dwell upon one matter and debate it by presenting arguments from either side, as above: “To fight on the same ground.”

108. To stand upon the line. Vol. III, Book IV Against Marcion, chap. 17, no. 250.

Its opposite was given above: “To pass beyond the lines.” It means to remain within one’s undertaking and not wander. For, as Turnebus says, men sometimes fought at close quarters in such a way that a foot always had to remain [Col. 0232D] upon a drawn line and could not be withdrawn from it.

109. To fight within the same lines. Vol. III, Book V Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 12.

To remain within fixed enclosures of arguments, or to conduct one’s debate within the same bounds.

110. Back to the lines. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 9, no. 55.

That is, to return to the proposition and undertaking after a digression or παρέκβασιν (“digression”)“. Taken from those who, after falling away from the lines, gather their stance again. See above: “Back into position.”

111. To gather one’s stance within the same lines. Vol. III, book On the Soul, chap. 26, no. 345.

[Col. 0233A] St. Jerome, in the *Dialogue Against the Luciferians*: “Return again to your position and the lines.”

112. To enclose within a final line. Vol. III, book Against Hermogenes, chap. 38, no. 134.

This seems to be taken rather from geometers, whose subject is quantity, place, surface, and line. Tertullian is speaking about Matter, which Hermogenes made spatially infinite, yet assigned it a place surrounding it—that is, enclosing it within a final line. The proverb will therefore signify, if one pleases, to comprehend and delimit something within fixed bounds.

113. To draw lines. Vol. III, book On the Soul, chap. 36, no. 432.

In Greek, ἄγειν ἤ γράφειν γραμμὴν (“to draw or inscribe a line”)“. And line drawing is [Col. 0233B] ἡ γραφὶς (“the drawing”)“. Hence the expression “to draw the first lines,” an elegant metaphor from painters meaning merely to present the outline of something and sketch in passing what one wishes to be understood. He too draws the first lines who prescribes a formula for beginning a business. And “to cast a line” means merely to outline a matter and propose it for investigation by certain conjectures. For from the drawing of lines we also learn the full art of painting. Hence the verse:

> A line that would proclaim the ancient Apelles from afar.

Pliny, Book XXXV, chap. 10, mentions the lines of Apelles. For painters customarily, as Rhenanus also noted, draw lines with a brush before they begin to add shadow without color.

[Col. 0233C] 114. To draw straight lines. Vol. III, book Against Hermogenes, chap. 36, no. 126.

He adapts these proverbs, derived from the art of painting, to Hermogenes, who had practiced painting before his heresy: “Except that, if the lines you draw are as straight, Hermogenes, as this argument is, there is no painter more stupid than you.” It may be applied to one who performs his duty properly, does not depart from the precepts of upright conduct, and is skilled in his art. Its opposite may also be “to fall away from the lines.”

115. To trace lineaments. Vol. III, book On the Resurrection of the Flesh, no. 35.

Cicero: “Pigments scattered at random upon a panel can form the lineaments of a face.” In Greek they are περιγρὰφαι (“outlines”)“ [Col. 0233D] and σκιογραφίαι (“shadow-drawings”)“. Likewise Cicero, in the *Orator* addressed to Brutus: “Rhythm brought to speech, as it were, a certain training ground and its finishing lineaments”; that is, beauty applied for display and, as it were, the final touch. Tertullian interprets it as “to express,” “to draw lines”: namely, to set a matter more clearly before the eyes and demonstrate it.

116. To fall away from the lines. Vol. III, book Against Hermogenes, chap. 39, no. 137.

Besides the preceding interpretation derived from athletes, he applies this rather to Hermogenes as a former painter. For painters who stray from the lines drawn with the brush extend their painting beyond the line. Thus it means to deviate from one’s purpose, or even to depart from one’s duty, be deceived, and err.

[Col. 0234A] 117. To draw the saw back and forth along the same line. Vol. II, book On the Soldier’s Crown, chap. 3, no. 28.

Rhenanus: It has the appearance of a proverb concerning useless and vain labor. The metaphor is taken from sawyers of timber: if they draw the saw in such a way that they either do not let it go forward or persist only along the same line, they will accomplish nothing, or certainly very little. Tertullian indicates useless labor concerning one and the same matter, as with those who are immoderate upon the same argument and, as it were, are always painting the cypress. The following expressions have the same tendency: “Always to wander over the same string”; “To strike the same anvil night and day.” In Virgil, “the shrill blade of the saw” implies reciprocating movement. Thus fullers are said “to chatter with their feet,” that is, to tread and wash cloth with alternating, back-and-forth steps.

[Col. 0234B] 118. Light-shunners. Vol. III, book On the Resurrection of the Flesh, chap. 47, no. 341.

*Lucifuga*, whether masculine or feminine in form, is a name of infamy. Such a person, naturally contrary and perverse in relation to nature, sleeps by day and stays awake by night. Jurists call him a “bat,” because once the course of the day is finished, he creeps forth toward night in the manner of a bat. In Greek, λυχνόβιος (“one who lives by lamplight”)“; by some he is called *Tenebrio*, as though a lover of darkness. Cicero, in the *Catilinarian*, calls them “sleepers,” opposing them to those who keep watch. Tertullian speaks of “light-shunners of the Scriptures,” who stumble amid the supreme clarity of the writings and amid passages that—to use Tertullian’s own words—“shine as though written by the very ray of the sun”; as the saying goes, they are blind in sunlight.

[Col. 0234C] 119. To carry a lamp about at midday. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 19.

Proverbially it will mean the same as to bring or light a lamp at midday: to do something at the wrong time, or to hunt for obscurity in a clear matter. It arose from Diogenes, who searched for a human being at midday with a lighted lamp.

120. Deprived by having his eyes gouged out. Vol. V, book On the Trinity, chap. 28, no. 127.

Virgil, Book III, concerning Polyphemus:

> He washed away the flowing gore from his gouged-out eye.

And a little earlier: “whose eye had been taken away”; and: “with a weapon we pierce his eye.” See above: “A blind man strikes against a rock.” Novatian adds by epexegesis: “Let him be wholly [Col. 0234D] overcome in the blindness of his doctrine.” Thus it may proverbially be said of one blinded by the depravity of doctrines or errors, in whom no sound judgment or understanding remains. Or, closer to Novatian’s meaning, of one who has no subterfuge left to which he may resort when defeated by arguments, and can discern no means within himself by which to defend his error.

121. To stray from the light through darkness. Vol. V, book On Modesty, chap. 8, no. 59.

The verb “to stray from the track” derives from *orbita*, the rut left by a wheel moving along a road. The phrase means to turn aside from the way and, because of the blindness of error, to be unable to perceive true doctrine, which shines of itself. In the book Against the Gnostics: “They strayed from him”—God—“into idolatry.”

[Col. 0235A] 122. Evil, begone! Vol. III, book Against the Valentinians, chap. 10, no. 119.

The cry of one who detests and recoils from something and prays that evil and misfortune may be averted. Virgil, Book II:

> May the gods first turn that omen  
> Upon him himself.

And:

> May the gods grant better things to the pious,  
> And that error to our foes.

“Gods, ward off these threats”; “Gods, avert such a fate.” Among the ancients, Hercules ἀλεξίκακος—that is, “the repeller of evils”—was customarily placed in the vestibules of houses; and sometimes, for the sake of a good omen, μηδὲν κακὸν εἰσί τω, “let no evil enter,” was inscribed there. Moreover, wicked and profane persons were customarily excluded from sacred rites by the officiants’ cry. Virgil:

> Far hence, far hence, be gone, profane ones!

[Col. 0235B] Theocritus, in the idyll:

> Ἀεισω συνετοῖσι, θύρας δ` ἐπιθέσθε βέβαλοι.

That is:

> I shall sing to the initiated; close the doors, profane ones.

123. To detain by laying on a hand. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 6, no. 44.

Properly, in the ancient language of law, “to lay on a hand” applies whenever, without waiting for the authority of a judge, we lay claim to something owed to us or even to something that is ours; and a lord was accustomed to lay his hand upon a benefice. A hand is laid upon someone and upon his goods or merchandise when, by some authority or edict, they are forbidden to depart or be moved from there. Thus “to retain the laying-on of a hand” means to retain the power of claiming back an alienated thing. Virgil:

[Col. 0235C]

> The Fates laid their hand upon him,

that is, they destined him for death. Tertullian explains himself: “Furthermore, here too the same rule of supreme greatness will champion us, since it claims the whole standing of divinity.”

124. To extend a hand. Vol. III, book On the Soul, chap. 23, no. 408; book On the Resurrection of the Flesh, chap. 53, no. 382; and Book IV Against Marcion, chap. 41, no. 677.

A sufficiently common expression meaning “to give assistance.” Cicero, in the speech *After His Return*: “He was the first, when I lay afflicted and prostrate, to extend to me the pledged loyalty and right hand of a consul.” Thus God is said by Tertullian “to extend a hand to faith”; that is, to strengthen it and preserve it in its firmness. Virgil, Book III:

> He gives his right hand and strengthens his courage with the present pledge.

The extension of hands also [Col. 0235D] signified approval of another person’s opinion, which in Greek is χειροτονία, τὸ χειροτονεῖν (“a show of hands, the act of voting by a show of hands”)“.

125. To give a hand. Vol. V, book On Modesty, chap. 15, no. 126.

He uses it in the same sense as the preceding. Virgil, Book VIII:

> He received him by the hand and, clasping his right hand, held fast.

In Cicero, “to hand over from hand to hand” means to entrust and commend.

126. To give one’s hands. Vol. V, book On the Trinity, chap. 11, no. 55.

Cicero, in *On Friendship*: “At last let him give his hands and suffer himself to be defeated.” Virgil:

> For he himself would not have given his hands despite his fierceness.

It therefore means to acknowledge oneself defeated and yield to one’s opponent. [Col. 0236A] For those who surrender to a victor so that they may not be killed freely offer their hands for bonds. Virgil also used “to stretch out” and “to extend” in Book XII:

> He, humbled and suppliant, extending his eyes  
> And his pleading right hand—

and:

> You have conquered, and the Ausonians have seen  
> The conquered man stretch forth his palms.

127. To hold between one’s hands. Ibid., chap. 1, no. 95.

It means to possess something as certain, to have it, as it were, set before one’s eyes. Thus we say: “To hold victory in one’s hands.” Virgil:

> All else that lies shattered in the ruin of affairs  
> Is before your eyes and between your hands.

As though he said: These things need no narration; they are manifest and clear to everyone.

128. The napkin dropped. Vol. III, book Against the Valentinians, chap. 36, [Col. 0236B] no. 381.

That is, as though some signal had been given. Tertullian alludes to a custom observed at the Roman circus games, by which the napkin used by tragic actors was dropped by the praetor as a signal for the chariot race and contest to begin. Juvenal touches upon this custom in Satire II:

> Meanwhile the spectacles of the Megalesian napkin  
> Celebrate the Idaean solemnity.

And Martial:

> When the praetor wished to drop the chalked—or, in another reading, waxen—napkin.

And Suetonius, in *Nero*: “when some freedman dropped the napkin.” See Pamelius’ annotation on the book *On the Spectacles*, chap. 16, no. 143, Vol. II.

129. More inhuman than a Massagete. Vol. III, Book I Against Marcion, chap. 1, no. 13.

[Col. 0236C] See Pamelius’ annotations. They are a Scythian nation beyond the Hyrcanian Sea, later called Alans, Abasgi, and Augi, and by the Hebrews Magogs; they are exceedingly hostile to strangers and can maintain neither lasting treaties of friendship nor good faith with neighboring peoples. They drink mare’s milk mixed with blood. Moreover, they are of so monstrous and savage a nature that they have customarily cut those exhausted by old age into pieces and fed upon their flesh mixed with that of livestock. Hence this celebrated name for inhumanity.

130. Μὴ θεομαχεῖν. Vol. I, book To Scapula, chap. 4, no. 22.

“Do not wage war against God.” Cicero, in the book *On Old Age*: “For what else is it to wage war against the gods in the manner of the Giants, if not to fight against nature?” Hence θεομάχοι: enemies of God, or those who struggle against God. It seems to be drawn from the very familiar [Col. 0236D] fable of the Giants warring against Jupiter. It may be used where someone, relying upon his own strength, rashly attempts things that should not be attempted; and it may be used to warn that whatever is undertaken by violence against divine and human laws turns out disastrously. Horace in an ode:

> Why weary a spirit too small  
> For eternal counsels?

Virgil:

> Trust God.

And:

> Alas, when the gods are unwilling, no one may rightly trust in anything.

Twice in the *Acts of the Apostles* Λ [an isolated, apparently corrupt character intruding into “Apost.”]: μὴ θεομάχωμεν (“let us not fight against God”)“ … θεομάχοι εὑρὲθητε (“you may be found fighters against God”)“. Plato said that such men were of the race of the Titans.

131. To stuff in crumbs. Vol. III, book On the Soul, chap. 6, no. 85.

Something may be gathered from the comparison between Plato and Aristotle instituted by Tertullian. Plato is varied, manifold, [Col. 0237A] and copious, with singular eloquence and sweetness of speech. In his dialogues, although the language is removed from verse, nevertheless, because it moves with greater animation and employs the most brilliant ornaments of diction, it should be regarded as poetry rather than the work of comic poets. Aristotle excelled in brevity of expression—or rather maintained a certain dryness and thinness in speech; that is, he favored language that was narrow, compressed, and concise, whereas Plato’s is ample, copious, and overflowing. Hence the expressions “honey-water from Plato’s eloquence” and “crumbs from Aristotle’s minute style” may be understood proverbially: the latter kind of discourse is dry and nothing can readily be taken away from it, while the former is overflowing, and if you remove or alter something in it you will have taken nothing from its meaning. [Col. 0237B] Nevertheless, we may also interpret “to stuff in crumbs” as to distribute something sparingly and frugally, while “to pour in honey-water” means to do so lavishly and generously.

132. To pour in honey-water. Ibid.

From *aqua mulsa*, ὑδρομέλι, ἢ μελίκρατον (“hydromel, or honey-water”)“, water mixed with honey. Thus he alludes not only to Plato’s copious eloquence, but also to its honeyed or mellifluous quality. In the book *On the Soul*, concerning the same Plato: “because of the honey of his eloquence.” By contrast, what is dry is very easily crumbled and broken into tiny crumbs. The words “to pour in” and “to stuff in” point in the same direction. Perhaps “to stuff in crumbs” may also correspond to the Ciceronian expression “to put pre-chewed food into someone’s mouth,” which means to present a matter in teaching with excessive elaboration, not piecemeal enough, and to explain it coarsely; [Col. 0237C] in Greek, Μικρολογεῖν, λεπτολογεῖν (“to speak of trifles, to discuss minute details”)“, or, as Cicero writes to Atticus, περὶ μικρά σπουδὰζειν (“to be preoccupied with small matters”)“, meaning to be anxiously preoccupied with minute details.

133. To spur a mule from an ass. Vol. III, book Against the Valentinians, chap. 19, no. 237.
