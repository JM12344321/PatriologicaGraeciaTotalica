As for his tomb, mentioned by our Minucius and by his intermediary Cyprian, [Col. 0560A] the Cretans, according to Lucian, affirmed that “Jupiter not only lived and was reared among them, but they even show his tomb: τὸν τάφον δεικνύουσι, ‘they show the tomb.’” Cicero, moreover, distinguished three Jupiters, of whom “the third,” he says, was reckoned “the Cretan, son of Saturn, whose tomb is shown on that island.” And Varro, as Solinus notes, “in the work entitled *On Coasts*, affirms that even in his own time Jupiter’s tomb was visited there.” Callimachus, however, sings that the matter is doubtful and accuses the Cretans of lying; but when he says there that Jupiter has always existed, he himself lies absurdly. Read also, if you wish, Theophilus of Antioch, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Tertullian, Arnobius, Lactantius, Eusebius, and [Col. 0560B] Epiphanius. Vossius, however, thinks there were two Cretan Jupiters: one, the most ancient king of Crete, from whom Crete received its name; the other, a younger one who abducted Europa, and about whom he thinks Cicero and Lactantius speak (Cypr., *De Idol. Vanit.*, p. 11; Lucian, *De Sacrific.*, p. 185, line 39; Cicero, *De Nat. deor.*, bk. III, p. 247, line 34; Solinus, *Polyhist.*, ch. 11, p. 29; Callimachus, *Hymn to Jupiter*, p. 535, vol. I, *Poet. Graec.*; Theophilus, *Ad Autol.*, bk. II, p. 81; Clement of Alexandria, *Admonit.*, p. 24; Origen, *Contra Celsum*, bk. III, p. 137; Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 25, p. 27; Arnobius, *Adv. Gent.*, bk. IV, p. 135; Lactantius, *Instit.*, bk. I, ch. 11, p. 64; Eusebius, *Praepar. Evang.*, bk. III, ch. 10, p. 107; Epiphanius, *Ancoratus*, § 108, p. 108; Vossius, *De Orig.* [Col. 0560C] *et prog. idolol.*, bk. I, ch. 1, p. 61). For us, however, it is sufficient to have proved what our author handed down. Something more must be said about Jupiter later.

How rightly Minucius also placed the Delphic Apollo after Jupiter can again be plainly confirmed from Cicero himself. For he enumerates several Apollos, of whom “the third,” he says, “was born of the third Jupiter and Latona, and is said to have come from the Hyperboreans to Delphi.” Lucian, moreover, says: “They worship those gods as distributed among various regions, and receive them into their own cities. Thus, ὁ μὲν Δελφὸς τὸν Ἀπόλλω, Delphi and Delos claim Apollo, and the Athenians Minerva, displaying and making them their own citizens.” But Diodorus Siculus relates more expressly that most of the gods came forth from Crete: [Col. 0560D] “Ὡσαύτως δὲ μὲν Ἀπόλλωνα πλεῖστον χρόνον φανῆναι περὶ Δῆλον, καὶ Λυκίαν, καὶ Δελφούς. Likewise, Apollo spent a very long time on Delos, in Lycia, and at Delphi.” Delphi was a city of Phocis in Achaia, near Mount Parnassus, exceedingly famous—as everyone knows—for Apollo’s oracle. If you wish to read still other writers on this subject, consult Pausanias, Plutarch, Strabo, and other mythographers. Nevertheless, if you believe Macrobius when he interprets the various names of the Sun, “they call him Ἀπόλλωνα Δελφῶν, ‘Apollo of Delphi,’ because he reveals obscure things by the brightness of light, ἀπὸ τοῦ δηλοῦν ἄφανη, ‘from making invisible things manifest’; or, as Numenius holds, as though he were one and alone. For he says that in the ancient Greek language δελφόν meant ‘one.’ Hence, he says, a brother is called ἀδελφός, as though ‘not one’” (Cicero, *De Natur. deor.*, bk. III, p. 248; Lucian, *De Sacrific.*, [Col. 0561A] p. 185; Diodorus Siculus, *Biblioth.*, bk. V, p. 237; Pausanias, bk. X, p. 320 ff.; Plutarch, *De oracul. Defect.*, p. 433; Strabo, *Geogr.*, bk. IX, p. 417 f.; Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 17).

Minucius next mentions Pharian Isis. But if the writings of Euhemerus, whom he praises, had come into our hands, we would surely know whether he had indicated for us the homeland or the tomb of Pharian Isis, or both. For Herodotus, by whom she is called Ἰοῦν, Io, Diodorus Siculus, and others testify that there were various opinions about these matters (Herodotus, bk. I; Diodorus Siculus, *Biblioth.*, bk. I, pp. 9, 14, and 17, and bk. V, p. 32). Yet by these two words, “Pharian Isis,” our author signifies with sufficient clarity that her tomb was situated on Pharos, the most celebrated [Col. 0561B] island of Egypt, where she was worshiped with the greatest honor. Diodorus Siculus indeed admits (*ibid.*, bk. I, p. 16) that certain writers had asserted that Isis and her husband Osiris were buried at Nysa in Arabia, and that columns had been erected there in honor of each. On one of these the following inscription, engraved in sacred characters, was read:

> I am Isis, queen of this place, instructed by Mercury. No one can abrogate the laws established by me. I am the youngest daughter of Saturn, the latest god. I am the wife and sister of King Osiris. I am she who first discovered grain for mortals. I am the mother of King Horus. I am she who rises in the constellation of the Dog. For me the city of Bubastis was built. Farewell; rejoice, Egypt, my nurse.

But he makes no secret of the fact that he did not place great confidence in these claims.

[Col. 0561C] And certainly, according to Herodotus, the Egyptians assembled in great numbers more than once each year, above all “ἐς Βουβάστιν πόλιν τῇ Ἀρτέμιδι, in the city of Bubastis in honor of Diana. Δεύτερα, ἐς Βούσιριν πόλιν τῇ Ἴσι, in the second place, in the city of Busiris in honor of Isis. In that city is the greatest ἱρόν, temple of Isis; the city itself is situated in the middle of the Egyptian Delta. Isis is she who in the Greek language is called Δημήτηρ, Demeter, that is, Ceres” (Herodotus, bk. II, § 59).

From Strabo, moreover, we learn that Busiris was a city of the Busirite province, which has a harbor at Pharos guarded by herdsmen (Strabo, *Geogr.*, bk. XVII, p. 802). May it not therefore be gathered that this is the Isis whom our author calls Pharian, or [Col. 0561D] Parian—for thus you will see her named on the coins exhibited by Federico Ubaldini? (Ubaldini, *De Symbol. Dian. Ephes. statu.*, p. 365, vol. VII, *Antiq. Graec.*)

Furthermore, since Isis, as the recently cited Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus, and very many others have related, is the same as Ceres, Tertullian doubtless mentioned her in these words: “How is Attic Pallas, or Pharian Ceres, distinguished from the upright beam of a cross?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 17).

Pharian Isis was distinguished only by a surname—or rather by the places where she was worshiped—from Eleusinian Ceres, whom our author also mentions after Euhemerus. Strabo left the following in writing: Εἶτ’ Ἐλευσὶν πόλις, ἐν ᾗ τὸ τῆς Δήμητρος ἱερὸν τῆς Ἐλευσινίας, καὶ ὁ μυστικὸς [Col. 0562A] σηκός, ὃν κατεσκεύασεν Ἰκτῖνος, ὄχλον θεάτρου δέξασθαι δυνάμενον. “Next comes the city of Eleusis, in which is the temple of Eleusinian Ceres and the mystic sanctuary, capable of holding a crowd as large as one ordinarily finds in a theater, the work of Ictinus” (Strabo, *Geogr.*, bk. IX, p. 395). Add Cicero as well, whose words are these:

> I pass over that holy and august Eleusis,
>
> Where peoples from the farthest shores are initiated.  
> (Cicero, *De Natur. Deor.*, bk. II, p. 12, line 38.)

Who, then, would not join our Minucius in laughing at and rejecting those fictitious divinities—Ceres, Isis, and the others—whom the pagans admitted to have died and been buried?

## ARTICLE III

**Minucius’s argument is examined, by which he proves that the gods of the pagans were fictitious from Alexander the Great’s letter to his mother, in which he testified that a secret concerning the gods had been disclosed to him, and which made Vulcan their chief; and the sistrum of Isis beside the swallow, and the empty tomb of Serapis, or Osiris, are discussed.**

[Col. 0562B]

Someone will doubtless be surprised that Minucius cites Alexander the Great as a witness to the fictitious divinity of the pagan gods: “In a notable volume,” he says, “which he wrote to his mother, Alexander testified that through fear of his power a priest had disclosed to him the secret that the gods were human beings.” But Cyprian transferred this into his own book *On the Vanity of Idols*, word for word as an unquestionable truth, as we have already seen (Cyprian, *De idolor. Vanit.*, p. 12; above, ch. 2, [Col. 0562C] art. 4, p. 35).

Athenagoras had said earlier: Ἡρόδοτος, καὶ Ἀλέξανδρος Φιλίππου ἐν τῇ πρὸς τὴν μητέρα ἐπιστολῇ (ἑκάτεροι δὲ ἐν τῇ Ἡλιουπόλει, καὶ Μέμφει, καὶ Θήβαις, εἰς λόγους τοῖς ἱερεῦσιν ἀφῖχθαι λέγονται), φασὶ παρ’ ἐκείνων, ἀνθρώπους αὐτοὺς γενέσθαι, μαθεῖν. “Herodotus and Alexander son of Philip, in his letter to his mother—both of whom are said to have entered into conversation with the priests in various Egyptian cities, Heliopolis, Memphis, and Thebes—say that they learned from them that the gods had been human beings” (Athenagoras, *Legat. pro Christian.*, p. 31). Thus, what Minucius and Cyprian after him call a “notable volume,” Athenagoras calls a letter. So too does Augustine, who teaches that this priest, as Minucius likewise says, was Egyptian by birth [Col. 0562D] and surnamed Leo (Augustine, *De Civit.*, bk. VIII, ch. 5, p. 194, and final chapter, p. 218; bk. XII, ch. 10, p. 309; and *De Cons. Evang.*, bk. I, ch. 3).

Rigaltius and Cellarius, however, protest against this. They contend that Minucius and the other writers praised by us erred, and that they substituted Alexander the Great of Macedon for Alexander Polyhistor. They attempt to establish their opinion by two arguments: first, because Polyhistor published *Aegyptiaca* (Egyptian histories); and second, because Tertullian said: “I now offer something from your own literature: what the Egyptians relate, Alexander arranges, and his mother reads” (Tertullian, *De Pallio*, ch. 3, p. 134).

But apart from the improbability that Polyhistor could, through fear of his power, have extorted from an Egyptian priest [Col. 0563A] the secret that the gods were human beings and written a book or letter about it to his mother, Plutarch—whom Rigaltius and Cellarius perhaps trusted more than the Fathers of the Church—certainly relates the same thing in his *Life of Alexander the Great*: Αὐτὸς δὲ Ἀλέξανδρος ἐν ἐπιστολῇ πρὸς τὴν μητέρα, φησὶν γεγονέναι τινὰς αὐτῷ μαντείας ἀπορρήτους, ἃς αὐτὸς ἐπανελθὼν φράσει πρὸς μόνην ἐκείνην . . . . λέγεται δὲ καὶ Ψάμμωνος ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ τοῦ φιλοσόφου διακούσας, ἀποδέξασθαι μάλιστα τῶν λεχθέντων, ὅτι πάντες ἄνθρωποι βασιλεύονται ὑπὸ Θεοῦ· τὸ γὰρ ἄρχον ἐν ἑκάστῳ καὶ κρατοῦν, θεῖόν ἐστιν. “Alexander himself says in a letter to his mother that certain secret oracles had been imparted to him, which on his return he would disclose to her alone. . . . He is also said, after hearing the philosopher Psammon in Egypt, to have approved especially this among his sayings: that all [Col. 0563B] mortals are ruled by God, since whatever commands and exercises dominion in each thing is divine” (Plutarch, *Life of Alexander*, vol. II, p. 680). The man whom Minucius, Cyprian, and Athenagoras call a priest, and Augustine calls Leo, Plutarch there calls the philosopher Psammon. He did not indeed say that Alexander wrote to his mother what he had received from Psammon, but neither does he deny it. What prevents Athenagoras, Minucius, and the others from having obtained more definite information about the matter from another source? Writers of such great authority ought not, therefore, to have been condemned as mistaken on the strength of such slight conjectures.

The words that immediately follow in our author are greatly corrupt and extremely obscure. We read them thus: “There [*Illic*; Royal MS: *Illi*, ‘to him’] he makes Vulcan the chief of all, and afterward the lineage of Jupiter. Look down upon [*Despice*; Royal MS: *et de spicis*, ‘and from the ears of grain’] the sistrum of Isis beside the swallow, and the empty tomb of your Serapis or Osiris, its members scattered [*dispersis*; Royal MS: *adspersis*; Royal MS: *Osiridis*].” From these various readings you doubtless perceive whether some scholars are wrong to consider the passage so corrupt that they have despaired of its being restored without the aid of another, sounder manuscript.

The sense of our author’s first words, insofar as it may be gathered from the reading of the Royal manuscript, which is not unreasonably to be preferred to the others, is this: “To him”—or rather, “that Alexander,” and perhaps better, “there,” that is, in the cited letter—Alexander makes Vulcan the chief of all, namely, [Col. 0563D] of all the gods, and afterward the lineage of Jupiter. This can be excellently confirmed by the following words of Diodorus Siculus transcribed by Eusebius: Ἔνιοι δὲ τῶν ἱερέων φασὶ πρῶτον Ἥφαιστον βασιλεῦσαι πυρὸς εὑρετὴν γενόμενον. “Some of the priests hold that Vulcan, the inventor of fire, should be reckoned the first king, and that Saturn, Jupiter’s father, succeeded him in power” (Diodorus, *Biblioth.*, bk. I, p. 8; Eusebius, *Praepar. Evang.*, bk. II, ch. 1). Whether this is true will be examined in the following article. Yet we think that no one will fail readily to admit that there were considerable disagreements among the pagans concerning the origin of their gods. For most others thought that the whole lineage derived its origin from Saturn, as we shall explain a little later. But whether it began with Saturn or Vulcan, [Col. 0564A] neither these men nor their sons and grandsons could ever have been true immortal gods.

What Minucius afterward said about Isis and her sistrum beside the swallow is certainly very difficult to explain. Some think Minucius’s meaning is this: in his letter Alexander makes the sistrum of Isis from ears of grain, producing a sound like that of a swallow. For ears of grain, when shaken, agitated, and struck together, give forth a sound like that produced by the shaking of a sistrum.

Salmasius, however, suspects that Minucius read in some Greek author’s book that among the emblems of Isis were numbered Χελιδονίαν, σεῖστρον, that is, a chelidonian asp and a sistrum. He translated this “a sistrum beside the swallow,” as though the sistrum made with its rattle a sound like that of a swallow, just as κερκίδες, the rods or battens of a loom, are called [Col. 0564B] χελιδόσιν εἰκελόφωνοι, “similar in voice to swallows,” in a Greek epigram (Salmasius on Solinus, p. 343). From this he judges that our author’s text should be corrected thus: “Aspice sis Isidis ad hirundinem sistrum”—“Consider, if you please, the sistrum of Isis in relation to the swallow.” But if Minucius read some work by a Greek author, it would appear to be none other than the letter of Alexander the Great cited by him, or books by Plutarch or others. Yet who can divine what those works contained on this subject? The text, therefore, ought not to be emended by so uncertain a conjecture against the authority of the most ancient manuscript and all the editions.

If, then, you ask what Minucius meant there, we shall frankly confess that we have nothing certain. But if you ask what we conjecture, then, to oblige you, it is useful first to observe what Plutarch related: [Col. 0564C] that Isis was transformed into a swallow (Plutarch, *De Isid. et Osirid.*, p. 356; *ibid.*, p. 376). He next depicts the sistrum as rounded in its upper part, with a cat in human form represented at the top of its curve, and on the lowest part, below the pieces that are shaken, sometimes the face of Isis and sometimes that of Nephthys. But sistra were not of merely one kind; they were of various kinds and diverse forms, as Tollius rightly demonstrates against Bossius (vol. VI, *Antiquit. Graec.*, p. 411 f.), as do others. Moreover, the figure of Isis was represented carrying a sistrum in her right hand. She herself, as we have seen, is the same as Ceres, who was said to have discovered crops. With these points explained, consider, please, whether the following can be said to be our author’s meaning: Alexander wrote in his letter [Col. 0564D] that Isis or Ceres was represented bearing upon her head ears of the grain which, as they said, she had discovered; beneath them a swallow, into which she had been transformed; and in her right hand a sistrum. If, however, something better occurs to you and you suggest it to us, we shall immediately and without the least resistance subscribe to your opinion. Yet it must be admitted that Minucius rightly concludes from this that the divinity of Isis is altogether ridiculous and ought to be rejected.

Finally, in the same letter of Alexander—or “volume,” as our author calls it—it was written that “the tomb of Serapis or Osiris was empty, his limbs having been scattered” or “sprinkled about.” Not without good reason, certainly. For Diodorus Siculus relates that some handed down that Typhon cut the body of his brother Osiris, whom he had cruelly murdered, [Col. 0565A] into twenty-six parts and distributed them among the same number of accomplices in his fratricide (Diodorus Siculus, *Biblioth.*, bk. I, p. 12; and in Eusebius, *Praepar. Evang.*, bk. II, ch. 1, p. 46). Isis later found them and enclosed each part in a fabricated human image. She then summoned the priests of all Egypt and distributed one part to each tribe, for each priest to bury in his own locality. Hence every Egyptian tribe believed that it possessed the tomb of Osiris, although, as our author says, it was empty and vacant. Plutarch says more simply and briefly that Isis, pretending to give the corpse of Osiris, gave each city only an individual image; or, as others think, buried the several parts of his corpse just as they came to hand (Plutarch, *De Isid. et Osirid.*, p. 358).

[Col. 0565B] “Granted,” someone will say; “I concede that the tomb of Osiris was empty. But why did Minucius add, ‘or of your Serapis’?” We shall answer the questioner in Plutarch’s words (*ibid.*, p. 362): “It is better to identify Osiris with Bacchus, and Serapis with Osiris, who was so called after he changed his nature, because Serapis is common to all.” He next observes that most priests taught that Osiris and Apis were the same, and that Apis “should be understood as a beautiful image of the soul of Osiris.” Elsewhere we have shown how the name Serapis was formed from Apis (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. 2, ch. 4, art. 1, p. 1090). Hence Lactantius doubtless agreed with Plutarch when he said: “There is Osiris, whom the common people call Serapis and Serapides. For the names of the dead are customarily [Col. 0565C] changed when they are consecrated, I suppose lest anyone think that they had been human beings” (Lactantius, bk. I, ch. 21, p. 118). To both let us add Diodorus Siculus, who should have been cited first, in whom we read these words: “They think Osiris to be now Serapis, now Dionysus, sometimes Pluto, sometimes Ammon, at times Jupiter, and not rarely Pan: τὸν Ὄσιριν οἱ μὲν Σάραπιν . . . . νενομίκασι” (Diodorus, *Biblioth.*, bk. I, p. 15).

Why Minucius added “your” you will easily understand if you observe that he is disputing against Caecilius, who was still a pagan and who, as is related at the beginning of this book, had greeted a statue of Serapis that he encountered with impious worship. But whether the tomb was full of the bones of Osiris or Serapis, [Col. 0565D] or empty, it is most certainly established from this that neither they nor others who likewise died could partake of divinity. For God is eternal, having life from himself alone, and therefore can never lose it.

# CHAPTER XVI

## HOW STRONG ARE THE OTHER ARGUMENTS BY WHICH OCTAVIUS PROVES AGAINST CAECILIUS THAT THE GODS OF THE PAGANS ARE FALSE AND NONEXISTENT?

[Col. 0566A]

## ARTICLE I

**How clearly Octavius shows from the first origin of the gods that they were not gods but mere human beings; and, first, concerning their chief Saturn, who fled from Crete and was received by Janus in Italy, where he taught human beings many things—especially how to mint coins and impress letters—and from whose name Saturnia was so called.**

In order that Minucius might forever suppress the utterly intolerable boasting of Caecilius and the other pagans [Col. 0566B] concerning their gods, he demonstrates in the following way that they were by no means gods, but mere human beings from their very first origin: “All the Greek and Roman writers of antiquity handed down that Saturn, the chief of this race”—that is, the race of gods—“was a human being. Nepos and Cassius know this in their histories, and Thallus and Diodorus say it.”

In precisely the same way Tertullian strikes down and vanquishes those same enemies of the Christian religion: “Before Saturn,” he says, “there is no god among you. From him derives the whole lineage of divinity—or at least of the more powerful or better-known divinity. Accordingly, whatever is established concerning the origin will also apply to the posterity. Thus, insofar as literature teaches, neither Diodorus the Greek nor Thallus, [Col. 0566C] neither Cassius Severus nor Cornelius Nepos, nor any commentator on antiquities of this kind, proclaimed Saturn to be anything other than a man” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 10, p. 12). He repeats the same thing in another book, except that someone or other wrote “Tacitus” in place of “Thallus,” as we shall note in its proper place (*ibid.*, *Ad Nationes*, bk. II, ch. 12, p. 75; *Dissert. in Tertullian.*, ch. 27, art. 1). For Lactantius, who follows him and our Minucius, said this about the same Saturn in almost the same words: “All—not only poets but also writers of histories and antiquities—who committed to memory his deeds in Italy agree that he was a human being: among the Greeks, Diodorus and Thallus; among the Latins, Nepos, Cassius, and Varro” (Lactantius, bk. I, ch. 13, p. 76).

[Col. 0566D] Thus to the four writers praised by Minucius, as also by Tertullian, Lactantius added a fifth, Varro. In this way those vigorous champions turned their enemies’ own forces against them and utterly destroyed and routed them.

But why, you will ask, did those writers dare to affirm so confidently that Saturn was the first and chief of all the gods? Did Alexander, instructed by a priest, not write in the letter cited by us a little earlier that Vulcan was the chief of the gods, and then came the lineage of Jupiter? But what wonder is it if pagans wandering in error held various opinions about the first origin of their false gods? Moreover, this was the judgment of a single priest—or, as Minucius says, a secret. Who, then, would say that he should be believed rather than so many [Col. 0567A] celebrated writers cited by our author? Finally, what is to be said below will sufficiently show that Vulcan could not be called either the first or the chief of all the gods (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 755).

Minucius then relates, doubtless from the same writers, that Saturn, as we have noted elsewhere, fled from Crete and took refuge in Italy through fear of his son. There, having been received by Janus, he taught “uncivilized people” many things, as Diodorus Siculus, praised by Minucius, also testifies—especially how to impress letters, mint coins, and make implements. In addition, he wished the place in which he had lain hidden to be called Saturnia, just as Janus called his place the Janiculum. Tertullian had previously committed all these things to writing, as Cyprian and Lactantius did afterward (Diodorus, *Biblioth.*, bk. III, p. 136, [Col. 0567B] and bk. V, pp. 231 and 233; Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 10, p. 12, and *Ad Nationes*, bk. II, ch. 12, p. 75; Cyprian, *De Idol. vanit.*, p. 11; Lactantius, bk. I, ch. 13, p. 76).

Concerning the city of Saturnia and the Janiculum, Virgil sang as follows:

> Moreover, you see these two towns with their shattered walls,
>
> Relics and monuments of men of old:
>
> Father Janus founded this city, Saturn that;
>
> This was named Janiculum, and that Saturnia.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VIII, line 354 ff.)

But regarding these matters and the minted coin, Ovid—also praised by Lactantius (*Divine Institutes*, bk. I, ch. 13, p. 76)—questioned Saturn thus:

> Much indeed have I learned, but why is one side
>
> Of the bronze stamped with a ship, the other with a double head?  
> [Col. 0567C]

He represented Saturn as answering thus:

> You could have recognized me in the double image, he said,
>
> Had the long passage of time not worn away the work.
>
> The reason for the ship remains: in a ship the sickle-bearing god
>
> Came to the Tuscan river, after wandering through the world.
>
> I remember Saturn being received in this land;
>
> He had been driven by Jupiter from the celestial kingdoms.
>
> Hence the name Saturnia long remained for the people;
>
> The land was also called Latium, because the god lay hidden.
>
> But a dutiful posterity fashioned a ship’s stern upon the bronze,
>
> Attesting the arrival of its guest.
>
> I myself inhabited the ground whose left side is gently washed
>
> By the wave of the sandy Tiber.
>
> Here, where Rome now stands, a lofty forest then flourished,
>
> And that mighty city was pasture for a few cattle.
>
> My citadel was the hill which the common people call by our name,
>
> And which this present age calls the Janiculum.  
> (Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. I, line 231 ff.)

But by what reasoning, someone will object, is Saturn said to have taught the art of minting coins? Does not Servius, on the cited verses of Virgil, ascribe it to Janus alone in this observation: “Janus lived on the Janiculum; because he came as an exile in a single ship, his coinage was stamped on one side with the head of Janus and on the other with a ship” (Servius, p. 509)? But Servius is discussing there both Saturn and Janus, as is his author Virgil. Since, therefore, Saturn, and by no means Janus, “came as an exile in a single ship,” must not Servius be understood to speak of coinage stamped in the name of both?

“Granted,” another will insist; “yet Draco of Corcyra, in Athenaeus, with whom Macrobius agrees, explicitly affirms: νόμισμα χαλκοῦ πρῶτον χαράξαι, ‘a bronze coin was first stamped by Janus’”; and that this is why many cities in Greece, Italy, and Sicily [Col. 0568A] struck money impressed on one side with a two-headed face and on the other with either a raft, a ship, or a crown (Athenaeus, *Deipnosoph.*, bk. XV, ch. 18, p. 692; Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 7). But why, please, was the image of a raft or a ship stamped on this coin? Was it not because, as we have said and as Plutarch states, it was “ἐπὶ τιμῇ Κρόνου πλοίῳ διαπεράσαντος εἰς Ἰταλίαν, in honor of Saturn, who was conveyed to Italy by ship”? (Plutarch, vol. II, *Roman Questions*, p. 274.) What, then, if Janus struck a coin in honor of Saturn, and Saturn another in memory of his host? Whatever the case, Minucius certainly seems to have had as an earlier authority the Ovid cited by us, and more expressly Tertullian, who did not hesitate to commit the following to writing concerning Saturn himself: “By him coinage was first stamped with a tablet and image, [Col. 0568B] and therefore he presides over the treasury” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 10, p. 12). Cyprian follows his master, as he also follows our Minucius, closely in these words: “He was the first in Italy to institute the impressing of letters and the minting of coins; hence the treasury is called the treasury of Saturn” (Cyprian, *De Idolor. Vanit.*, p. 11). Let us add, if you please, Polydore Vergil, who attempts to confirm this opinion by the authority of Eutropius (Polydore Vergil, *De Invent.*, bk. II, ch. 20).

Minucius therefore rightly concludes from what has been said: “Certainly Saturn was a man, since he fled; certainly a man, since he hid.” Lactantius draws the same conclusion: “Does anyone,” he says, “consider one who fled and hid to be God? No one is so foolish. For one who flees and hides must necessarily fear both violence and death” (Lactantius, [Col. 0568C] *Divine Institutes*, bk. I, ch. 13, p. 76).

## ARTICLE II

**How perversely the pagans said that Saturn was born of heaven and earth; whence this opinion arose; whether the explanation of it given by Minucius is rightly refuted by Lactantius; and whether it may nevertheless be concluded from it that Saturn was not a god but a mere human being.**

It was demonstrated abundantly enough in the preceding article that Saturn was not God, since, as Minucius says, he “was born from a human being.” Indeed, says Tertullian, because he was begotten “from a human being, surely not from heaven and earth,” as the pagans everywhere proclaimed both orally and in writing, at home and abroad—indeed, where did they not? (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 10, p. 12, and [Col. 0568D] *Ad Nationes*, bk. II, ch. 12, p. 75.) If you ask our author why they said with so empty a boast that he had sprung from heaven and earth, he will give you this reason: “Saturn is said to have been the son of earth and heaven because he appeared among the Italians with his parents unknown; just as to this day we call those who unexpectedly appear ‘sent from heaven,’ and persons of obscure and unknown origin ‘sons of earth.’” But this explanation did not please Lactantius, and he refutes it thus:

> Minucius Felix, in the book entitled *Octavius*, argued as follows: that when Saturn had been driven out by his son and had come to Italy, he was called a son of heaven because we customarily say that those whose excellence we admire, or who have arrived suddenly, have fallen from heaven; and a son of earth because we call those born of unknown parents sons of earth. [Col. 0569A] These things indeed resemble the truth, but are not true, because it is established that he was so regarded even then, when he reigned. He could have argued thus instead: since Saturn was a most powerful king, in order to preserve the memory of his parents he gave their names to heaven and earth, which had previously been called by other names; in the same manner we know that names were imposed upon mountains and rivers.  
> (Lactantius, *Institutes*, bk. I, ch. 11, p. 67.)

Then, after enumerating the names of certain rivers, he concludes thus: “It is therefore apparent that he was not born from heaven, which is impossible, but from the man whose name was Uranus; Trismegistus is the authority that this is true.” He adds that these matters were unknown to Minucius, who therefore diverted the history into another interpretation. Finally, he considers it truer that Jupiter, after contemplating earth and heaven from a lofty [Col. 0569B] mountain, gave to the ether above the world the name *Coelum*, or Οὐρανός, Uranus, by which his grandfather was called. But he needed to prove that Saturn’s father, while he was still alive, was actually called “Heaven” by the Italians. This opinion of Lactantius, however, must be discussed in its proper place.

Thus he judges Minucius’s reasoning to be defective. For Minucius ought rather to have said that Saturn was called the son of heaven and earth because, when he was a most powerful king, he imposed the names of heaven and earth upon his parents in order to preserve their memory. He attempts to prove this by the authority of Trismegistus, who had recorded that Saturn’s father was surnamed Uranus, or Heaven. But Trismegistus does not show there whether he said when and from whom Uranus [Col. 0569C] received his name. He could therefore more truly have written that uncivilized people, overwhelmed by Saturn’s supreme royal power, believed that he derived his origin from heaven and earth. For the Sibyl sang this in the following verses, cited by Athenagoras (*Legat. pro Christian.*, p. 14):

> Καὶ βασιλεὺς Κρόνος, καὶ Τιτὰν, Ἰαπετός τε,
>
> Γαίης τέκνα φέριστα, καὶ οὐρανίους ἐκάλεσσαν
>
> Ἄνθρωποι, γαῖάν τε, καὶ οὐρανὸν οὔνομα θέντες,
>
> Οὕνεκα οἱ πρώτιστοι ἔσαν μερόπων ἀνθρώπων.
>
> Then Saturn, Titan, and Iapetus
>
> Reigned; human beings said they had been created
>
> By heaven and earth, calling them by the names
>
> Of earth and heaven, because in excellence
>
> They surpassed all mortal men.  
> (*Sibylline Oracles*, III, line 48 ff.)

But Minucius does not appear to have omitted this explanation altogether. For he distinguished noble men from the ignoble, and said that the latter were surnamed sons of earth and the former sons of heaven [Col. 0569D] by Italians to whom their parents were unknown.

To Lactantius, however, it appears more plausible than true that people born of unknown parents and arriving unexpectedly were named after heaven and earth, from which they were believed to have sprung. Nevertheless, this is proved by other writers, both pagan and Christian. The first pagan writer who may be cited is Titus Livy, who relates that in founding the city of Rome Romulus employed “the ancient policy of founders of cities, who, by gathering around themselves an obscure and lowly multitude, falsely claimed that an offspring had been born to them from the earth” (Livy, bk. I, 58). But how could they have falsely claimed this unless the origins of that obscure multitude had been unknown? Who, moreover, will deny that the same thing must be said of [Col. 0570A] parents and children? Yet if some difficult and captious person wishes this to be proved to him specifically of parents, let him hear Servius’s words concerning Achelous: “He is called a son of earth, as is customarily said of those whose parents are hidden by antiquity” (Servius on Virgil, *Georgics*, bk. I, line 9).

Still more pertinent to our subject concerning Saturn is Aurelius Victor—or whoever was the author of the book *On the Origin of the Roman People*:

> Saturn is believed to have been the first to come into Italy. . . . Such, moreover, is the simplicity handed down concerning the people of old down to that time, that when strangers came to them who, provided only that they were endowed with prudence and wisdom, contributed something toward the ordering of life and the formation of character, because their parents were unknown, they not only themselves believed them to have been produced by heaven and earth, but also affirmed this to posterity—as, for example, this very Saturn, whom they called a son of heaven and earth.  
> (Aurelius Victor, *Orig. gent. Rom.*, beginning.)

Add Tertullian, who left this written about Saturn not merely in one book but in two:

> Because his parents were unknown, it was easy for him to be called the son of those whose children we may all be thought to be. For who does not call heaven and earth mother and father, either for the sake of veneration and honor or from the human custom by which unknown persons, or those appearing unexpectedly, are said to have come down from heaven? Accordingly, because Saturn appeared suddenly everywhere, it fell to him to be called a heavenly being. For the common people also call those whose lineage is uncertain “sons of earth.”  
> (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 10, p. 12, and *Ad Nationes*, bk. II, ch. 12, p. 75.)

Whether these passages suffice to establish our Minucius’s opinion, and whether it was not entirely rightly refuted by Lactantius, we shall await the judgment of more learned men. For we readily admit that it is not ours to settle disputes among men so numerous and of such venerable antiquity. Whatever may be determined, it is certainly established from this, by an altogether unanswerable inference, that this Saturn—whom the pagans babbled was the parent of their other gods—was by no means a god but merely a human being, begotten from a human being just like all other human beings.
