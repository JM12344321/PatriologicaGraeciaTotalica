Therefore, if it is established from whom persecution comes, we can now [Col. 0107A] resolve your question and determine from this very preliminary discussion that one must not flee during persecution. For if persecution comes from God, that which comes from God must by no means be fled. This conclusion is upheld by a twofold argument: what comes from God ought neither to be avoided nor can it be escaped. It ought not to be avoided, because it is good. For everything that has seemed good to God must necessarily be good. And was it not for this reason that Genesis repeatedly states, “And God saw that it was good” (Gen. I, passim)—not because he would not have known it was good unless he had seen it, but so that by this expression it might be declared good because it was seen by God? There are indeed many things that come from God and come to someone’s harm. Rather, a thing is good precisely because it comes from God, as something divine and rational. For what is divine that is not rational and good? [Col. 0107B] What is good that is not divine?

But if it appears otherwise to someone’s perception, human perception does not prejudge the objective condition; rather, the condition judges perception. For the condition of each thing is something determinate, and it gives perception the rule that the thing is to be perceived as it actually is. If, then, what comes from God is objectively good—for nothing from God is not good, because it is divine and rational—yet appears evil to perception, the condition will remain secure and the fault will lie in the perception. Chastity, truth, and justice are objectively excellent things, though they displease the perception of many. Is their condition therefore surrendered to perception? So too persecution is objectively good, because it is a divine and rational dispensation, though it displeases the perception of those to whose harm it comes.

Do you see that even that evil has a rationale with God, when someone in persecution is overthrown from salvation, just as that good [Col. 0107C] occurs rationally when someone advances toward salvation through persecution? Unless someone either perishes before the Lord or is saved irrationally, he will not be able to call persecution evil, since even on the side of evil it is good, insofar as it is administered rationally. Thus, if persecution is good in whatever respect, since its objective character is established, we rightly determine that what is good ought not to be avoided, because it is a sin to refuse what is good—all the more so what has seemed good to God. Nor, indeed, can it be avoided, because it comes from God, whose will cannot be escaped.

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “Resolve your question,” etc. *Inducitur*, says Rhenanus, properly means “what is erased”; here *consultationem inducere* means to remove the doubt involved in the question. He explains himself when he adds “and determine.” In what follows there is an excellent reconsideration of the same point. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “Upholds”: *depends*, Pamelius, Semler, Oberthür.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “Prejudged,” Franeker.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “Each one,” Rhenanus.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “But gives,” Semler, Oberthür.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “In the whole,” Rhenanus, Rigault, Venetian edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “Rational,” Semler, Oberthür.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “And now among evils,” Pamelius.]

[Note: [Col. 0107D] “Among evils it is an equal good,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.]

Therefore those who think one should flee either reproach God with evil, if they flee persecution as an evil—for no one avoids what is good—or consider themselves stronger than God, since they think they can escape if God has willed such a thing to occur.

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “Therefore those who think one should flee.” In the Dijon manuscript: *Eo ad igitur*. In the margin there likewise: *Eo ad*, that is, *adeo* (“to such a degree”). RIG.]

## CHAPTER V

[Col. 0108A]

“But,” he says, “I do what belongs to me: I flee, lest I perish if I deny him. It belongs to God, if he wishes, to bring me back into public view even while I am fleeing.”

First answer me this: Are you certain that you will deny him if you do not flee, or uncertain? If you are certain, you have already denied him; for by presuming that you will deny him, you have pledged yourself to that which you presumed. And now you flee in vain lest you deny him, since, if you are going to deny him, you have already done so. But if you are uncertain, why, given the equal uncertainty of your fear concerning either outcome, do you not also presume that you may confess him, and that by not fleeing you may instead be saved—just as you presume that you will deny him, in order that you may flee?

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “By presuming,” Rigault.]

Now either both outcomes are within our power, or the whole matter lies with God. If it is within our power to confess or deny, why do we not presume what is better, namely, that we shall confess? [Col. 0108B] Unless, though you wish to confess, you do not wish to suffer. But to be unwilling to confess is to deny. If, however, the whole matter lies with God, why do we not leave it wholly to his judgment, acknowledging his strength and power—that just as he can bring us back into public view when we flee, so he can also overshadow us when we do not flee, indeed even when we move about amid the people? What sense does it make to honor God in connection with flight, because he can bring you into public view even while you flee, but to dishonor him in connection with bearing witness, by despairing of his power to protect you?

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “Bring forth,” Pamelius, Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “For standing firm,” Rhenanus.]

Why do you not rather say, on the side of steadfastness and confidence in God: “I do what belongs to me: I do not depart. If God wishes, he himself will protect me”? It is more properly ours to stand under God’s judgment than to flee under our own.

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “Why”: “How much,” Rhenanus.]

[Col. 0108C] The most holy martyr Rutilius, after fleeing persecution so many times from place to place, and even buying off the danger—as he supposed—with money, was unexpectedly arrested after all the security he had contrived for himself, brought before the governor, torn apart by tortures—I believe as punishment for his flight—and then given to the flames. He ascribed to God’s mercy the suffering he had avoided. What else did the Lord intend to demonstrate to us by this example than that one must not flee, since flight is of no use if God does not will it?

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “The most holy martyr Rutilius.” He suffered in Africa with Silvanus and Maximus, as is clear from the martyrology, where the name is incorrectly given as *Rutilus*. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “By the mercy,” Rhenanus.]

## CHAPTER VI

“Rather,” he says, “he fulfilled the command by fleeing from city to city.” For thus a certain man, himself also a fugitive, chose to argue. Such people accordingly refuse to understand the meaning of that pronouncement of the Lord, so that they may use it as a covering for their cowardice, although [Col. 0109A] it had its own persons, times, and causes: “When they begin to persecute you, flee from city to city” (Matt. X, 23).

[Note: [Col. 0108D] “It,” Codex Wouwerianus.]

We maintain that this properly concerns the persons of the Apostles, their times, and their circumstances, as the preceding and following meanings will prove, since they apply only to the Apostles: “Do not go into the way of the nations, and do not enter a city of the Samaritans; but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (Matt. X, 5–6).

[Note: [Col. 0109D] “Person,” Pamelius, Rhenanus.]

[Note: [Col. 0109D] The words enclosed in parentheses were added by Pamelius and accepted by Rigault.]

But the way of the nations lies open to us: upon it we were found, and along it we continue to walk until the end. Nor is any city excepted in which we preach throughout the whole world. Neither has special care for Israel been imposed upon us, except insofar as we must preach to all nations. Even if we are arrested, we shall not be brought before their councils [Col. 0109B] or flogged in their synagogues, but will of course be placed before Roman authorities and tribunals.

Thus the Apostles’ circumstances also required the command to flee, since the lost sheep of the house of Israel had to be preached to first. Accordingly, so that the preaching might be completed among those with whom it first had to be completed, and so that the children might receive the bread before the dogs, he therefore commanded them to flee temporarily at that time. This was not for evading the danger posed personally by persecution—for he had, on the contrary, foretold that they would suffer persecutions and taught that these must be endured—but for the advancement of the proclamation, lest, if they were immediately crushed, the dissemination of the Gospel should also be destroyed.

[Note: [Col. 0109D] “Among us,” Venetian edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0109D] “Crushing,” Rhenanus, Semler, etc.]

[Note: [Col. 0110D] “For the name,” Junius.]

[Note: [Col. 0110D] “Condition,” Rhenanus, Junius.]

Nor were they to flee silently into some city, but as men who would proclaim everywhere and therefore suffer persecutions everywhere, [Col. 0109C] until they had filled the cities with their teaching. Finally he says (Matt. X, 23), “You will not complete the cities of Israel.” Thus the command to flee was contained within the boundaries of Judea. But no limitation of preaching to Judea applies to us, now that the Holy Spirit has been poured out upon all flesh.

[Note: [Col. 0110D] “Their teaching,” Pamelius.]

Accordingly Paul and the Apostles themselves, mindful of the Lord’s command, declare this before Israel, whom they had now filled with their teaching: “It was necessary that the word of God should first be delivered to you. But since you have rejected it and judged yourselves unworthy of eternal life, behold, we turn to the nations” (Acts XIII, 46). And thence, having themselves turned, as their predecessors had ordained, they went into the way of the nations and entered the cities of the Samaritans, so that indeed their sound might go out into all the earth and their voices to the ends of the world [Col. 0109D] (Ps. XVIII, 1).

If, therefore, the exclusion of the way of the nations and of entrance into the cities of the Samaritans has ceased, why should the command to flee, issued at the same time, not also have ceased? Indeed, from the time when Israel had been filled and the Apostles passed over to the nations, they neither fled from city to city nor hesitated to suffer.

Indeed Paul, who had permitted himself to be lowered through the wall to escape persecution while the command still applied, later, at the conclusion of his office and the fulfillment of the command, [Col. 0110A] refused to yield to his disciples’ earnest entreaties that he not expose himself at Jerusalem to the sufferings Agabus had prophesied there. On the contrary, he said: “What are you doing, weeping and troubling my heart? For I would choose not only to suffer bonds, but even to die at Jerusalem for the name of my Lord Jesus Christ.” And so they all said: “The Lord’s will be done” (Acts XXI, 13–14). What was the Lord’s will? Certainly that he should no longer flee persecution. Otherwise, those who preferred that he avoid persecution could have invoked the Lord’s earlier will, by which he had commanded flight.

Therefore, since even under the Apostles themselves the command to flee was temporary, like the other injunctions, that which ceased among our teachers [Col. 0110B] cannot continue among us, even if it had not been issued specifically to them. Or, if the Lord wished it to continue, the Apostles sinned by failing to take care to flee until the end.

[Note: [Col. 0110D] “Abandoned,” some witnesses, incorrectly.]

## CHAPTER VII

Let us now see whether the Lord’s other decrees agree with a perpetual command to flee. First, if persecution comes from God, what sense does it make for the same one who inflicts it to command that it be fled? If he wished it to be escaped, it would have been better not to send it, lest he seem to transgress his own will. For either he wished us to suffer persecution or to flee it. If to flee, how are we to suffer? If to suffer, how are we to flee?

Again, how great is the inconsistency between the sayings of one who commands flight and invites to suffering, which is the opposite of flight! “Whoever has confessed in me, I too will confess in him before [Col. 0110C] my Father” (Matt. X, 32). How will one fleeing confess? How will one confessing flee? “Whoever has been ashamed of me, I too will be ashamed of him before the Father” (Mark VIII, 38; Luke IX, 26). If I avoid suffering, I put confession to shame. “Blessed are those who have suffered persecution for my name’s sake” (Matt. V, 11). Unhappy, therefore, are those who, by fleeing according to the supposed command, will not have suffered. “Whoever endures to the end will be saved” (Matt. X, 22). Why, then, when commanding me to flee, do you wish me to endure to the end?

Since so great a diversity of pronouncements does not accord with divine gravity, it is also apparent from these statements that the command to flee possessed at that time the rationale we have explained.

“But,” he says, “the Lord, foreseeing the weakness of certain persons, nevertheless in his humanity also pointed out the harbor of flight.” As if he were not capable of saving during persecution without flight—a protection so shameful, unworthy, and servile—those whom he knew to be weak! On the contrary, he does not cherish the weak but always rejects them, [Col. 0110D] first teaching that persecutors are not to be fled, but rather not to be feared: “Do not fear those who can kill the body, but can do nothing to the soul. Rather fear him who can destroy both body and soul in Gehenna” (Matt. X, 28).

And thereafter what does he prescribe for the fearful? “Whoever values his life more than me is not worthy of me”; and, “Whoever [Col. 0111A] does not take up his cross and follow me cannot be my disciple” (Luke XIV, 26). Finally, in the Apocalypse he does not offer flight to the cowardly, but assigns them, among the other reprobates, a portion in the lake of sulfur and fire, which is the second death (Rev. XXI, 8).

## CHAPTER VIII

He himself sometimes withdrew from violence, but for the same reason that he had commanded the Apostles to flee: namely, until he should complete his teaching. Once it was completed, I do not merely say that he stood his ground; he did not even desire assistance from the Father in the form of armies of angels, and he even rebuked Peter’s sword.

[Note: [Col. 0111D] “He himself sometimes withdrew from violence.” By various arguments and examples, including even the flight of Christ the Lord, which he explains elegantly, he proves that one must not flee. LE PR.]

He himself did indeed profess that his soul was sorrowful unto death and that his flesh was weak, in order to show you, first, that both human substances existed in him, from the distinctive sorrow of the soul and the weakness of the flesh; [Col. 0111B] lest you interpret either the flesh or the soul of Christ as something different, as certain people have now introduced. Next, having demonstrated their conditions, he wished you to know that in themselves, without the spirit, they have no strength.

And therefore he gives precedence to the words, “The spirit is willing” (Matt. XXVI, 41), so that, considering both conditions of both substances, you may understand that strength of spirit is also within you, just as is weakness of flesh. From this you may now know what you are to do from which source, and which you must subject to which—the weak, namely, to the strong—lest, as you now do, you plead the weakness of the flesh while suppressing the firmness of the spirit.

He too asked the Father that, if it were possible, the cup of suffering might pass from him. You too may ask—but while standing firm as he did, merely asking, and adding what followed: “Nevertheless, not what I will, but what you will” (Matt. XXVI, 39). [Col. 0111C] But how will you ask this while fleeing, when you yourself secure for yourself the removal of the cup, doing not what the Father wills but what you will?

## CHAPTER IX

The Apostles certainly taught everything according to God; they wished to proclaim everything as gospel. Where do you show that they renewed the command to flee from city to city? They could not have established anything so contrary to their own examples, commanding flight when they wrote to the churches chiefly from bonds or islands, where they were confined because they confessed the Name, not because they fled it.

[Note: [Col. 0111D] “They reviewed all the matters of the Gospel,” first edition; Rhenanus approves; Junius.]

Paul commands that the weak be supported—certainly not while fleeing, for how could they be supported while absent? Or does he mean that they are to be borne with patiently whenever they stumble through weakness of faith? So too [Col. 0111D] the fainthearted are to be consoled, yet not sent into flight.

But when he admonishes us, “Give no place to evil” (Eph. IV, 27), he does not suggest a plan of flight, but teaches restraint of anger. And when he says that the time must be redeemed because the days are evil (Eph. V, 16), he wishes us to gain time for our sojourn, not through flight, but through wise conduct.

[Note: [Col. 0111D–0112D] “He wishes us to gain time for our sojourn?” By *sojourn* he means our delay in this world. He wishes us to “gain” our sojourn, that is, to count as gain for ourselves every day we pass innocently or, as he says, in wise conduct. RIG.]

Moreover, he who commands us to shine [Col. 0112A] like children of light does not command us to be hidden by flight like children of darkness. He instructs us to stand immovable—certainly not made mobile by flight—and to be girded: for flight, or for advancing to meet the Gospel’s conflict? He also points out weapons that would not be necessary for those about to flee, among them the shield by which you may extinguish the devil’s darts (Eph. VI, 16), doubtless by resisting and receiving the full force of his assault.

Likewise John teaches that we must lay down our lives even for the brethren (1 John III, 16), much more for the Lord. This cannot be fulfilled by fugitives. Finally, mindful of his Apocalypse, in which he had heard the fate of the cowardly, he too admonishes us in the same vein that fear must be cast out. “Fear,” he says, “is not in love. But perfect love casts fear outside, because fear has punishment”—surely the fire of the lake. “But whoever [Col. 0112B] fears is not perfect”—in the love of God, that is. Now who will flee persecution except one who is afraid? Who will be afraid except one who has not loved?

But if you consult the Spirit, what does the Spirit more strongly attest by that utterance? For nearly all its words exhort to martyrdom, not to flight. Let us recall this one as well: “You are exposed to public view,” it says; “it is good for you. For whoever is not exposed before human beings is exposed before the Lord. Do not be ashamed: righteousness brings you into public view. Why are you ashamed when you bear praise? Power comes into being when you are seen by human beings.” Likewise elsewhere: “Do not desire to depart amid beds, miscarriages, and gentle fevers, but amid martyrdoms, so that he who suffered for you may be glorified.”

## CHAPTER X

But some, passing over the divine exhortations, [Col. 0112C] instead apply to themselves that little Greek verse of worldly wisdom: “The man who fled will fight again” (*ἀνὴρ ὁ φεύγων καὶ πάλιν μαχήσεται*)—perhaps so that he may flee again as well. And when will he conquer, who, when he fled, was defeated? Does he prove himself a good soldier to Christ his commander, when, though so fully armed by the Apostle, upon hearing persecution’s trumpet he deserts the day of persecution?

I too will answer with something from the world:

> “Is it, then, so very wretched to die?”  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid* XII, 646)

Let him die in whatever manner, either defeated or victorious. For even if he has fallen by denying, he has nevertheless fought against the tortures. I prefer what calls for pity to what calls for shame. A soldier lost in battle is nobler than one saved in flight.

Christian, do you fear a human being—you whom angels ought to fear, since you are to judge angels; whom [Col. 0112D] demons ought to fear, since you have received power even over demons; whom the entire world ought to fear, since in you the world too is judged? You have put on Christ, since you were baptized into Christ. By fleeing the devil, you have depreciated Christ, who is in you. You have rendered yourself a fugitive together with the devil.

But by fleeing, you reproach the Lord with the futility of the counsel of all who flee him. A certain courageous prophet also fled the Lord: [Col. 0113A] he crossed from Joppa toward Tarshish, as though he could sail away from God. But I find him not only in the sea and on land, but even in the belly of a beast, where he could neither die for three days nor even in that way escape God.

How much better is the servant of God who, if God’s enemy threatens, does not flee him but rather despises him, confident, of course, in the Lord’s protection; or, if he fears God, stands all the more beneath his eyes, saying: “He is the Lord; he is mighty; all things are his. Wherever I may be, I am in his hand. Let him do what he wills; I will not depart. And if he wishes me to perish, let him himself destroy me, provided that I preserve myself for him. I prefer to provoke his envy by perishing through his will than his anger by escaping through my own.”

## CHAPTER XI

Every servant of God ought to think and act in this way, even one of lesser rank, so that he may become one of higher rank [Col. 0113B] if he ascends to some degree through endurance of persecution.

[Note: [Col. 0113D] “So that he may become one of higher rank.” Thus in the book *On Baptism* he calls those admitted into the ecclesiastical order “higher persons.” RIG.]

But when the authorities themselves—that is, the deacons, presbyters, and bishops themselves—flee, how will a layman be able to understand the rationale of the saying, “Flee from city to city” (Matt. X, 23)?

[Note: [Col. 0113D] “But when the authorities themselves.” By “authorities” he means the superiors and teachers of the Church, in whose hands lies the authority for governing the Church. In the Glosses of Philoxenus, *auctor* is ἀρχηγός (“leader”), αὐθέντης (“master”); and in the books of the land surveyors, *auctores* is used absolutely for teachers and masters. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0113D] “The deacons, presbyters, and bishops themselves flee.” He reproaches this disgrace in the book *On the Crown*: “I know their shepherds too—lions in peace, deer in battle.” RIG.]

Thus, when the leaders flee, who from the rank and file will endure them urging him to plant his foot firmly in the battle line?

[Note: [Col. 0113D] “When the leaders flee.” That is, the authorities, as he has just said, and the shepherds who are accustomed to be lions in peace and deer in battle, as he reproaches them in *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter I. To these I add the line of Aristophanes: “At home lions, but in battle foxes” (*Οἴκοι μὲν λέοντες, ἐν μάχῃ δὲ ἀλώπεκες*). LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0113C] “To urge,” Rhenanus, Semler; “to urge that it be planted,” Pamelius.]

Certainly the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. Thus Moses, although Christ the Lord had not yet been revealed, and even though Christ was foreshadowed in himself, said: “If you destroy this people, destroy me together with them” (Exod. XXXII, 32). Moreover, since Christ confirms his own prefigurations, he who flees when he sees the wolf and abandons the sheep to be torn apart is a bad shepherd (John X, 12). Such a shepherd will be cast out of the estate; the wages for his service will be withheld from him [Col. 0113C] as compensation; indeed, restitution for the master’s loss will also be exacted from his former private property. For “to him who has, it will be given; but from him who does not have, even what he seems to have will be taken away” (Matt. XIII, 12).

[Note: [Col. 0114D] “The wages for his service will be withheld from him as compensation.” That is, as compensation for the sheep torn apart by the wolf through the shepherd’s negligence or treachery, although it had been entrusted to his loyalty and vigilance for an agreed wage for the commission. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0113C] “Harvest,” Latinus.]

[Note: [Col. 0113D] Some insert “or.” Latinus.]

[Note: [Col. 0113D] “For his own compensation,” Pamelius.]

Thus Zechariah threatens: “Arise, sword, against the shepherds, and tear away the sheep; I will bring my hand upon the shepherds” (Zech. XIII, 7). Against them Ezekiel (Ezek. XXXIV, 2) and Jeremiah also declaim with the same threats, because they not only feed shamefully upon the sheep, feeding themselves instead, but also [Col. 0114A] cause the scattered flock to become prey for all the beasts of the field, since it has no shepherd. This never occurs more than when, during persecution, the Church is deserted by the clergy.

[Note: [Col. 0114C] “And,” Semler, Rhenanus.]

If anyone has also recognized the Spirit, he will hear it denouncing fugitives as well. Moreover, if it is neither fitting nor permissible for those who preside over the flock to flee when wolves rush in—for he who declared such a shepherd evil certainly condemned him, and everything condemned has without doubt been made unlawful—then the superiors of the Church ought not to flee in persecution. Moreover, if the flock were obliged to flee, the superior of the flock ought not to remain, for he would remain without cause to protect a flock that, under the permission to flee, would have no need of such protection.

[Note: [Col. 0114C] “The superiors of the flock ought to remain, since they would remain without cause,” Rhenanus.]

## CHAPTER XII

[Col. 0114B]

As far as your problem is concerned, brother, you now have both the answer expressing our judgment and the exhortation. Moreover, whoever asks whether persecution ought to be fled must necessarily also anticipate the following question: if it ought not to be fled, may it at least be bought off? I shall therefore volunteer advice to you on this matter too, determining that persecution, which admittedly must not be fled, likewise must not be bought off.

The price makes the difference. Yet just as flight is redemption without payment, so redemption by money is flight. This too is certainly a counsel of cowardice. You buy off what you fear; therefore you flee. You remained standing with your feet, but ran with your money. By the very fact that you stood your ground through payment, you fled.

[Note: [Col. 0114D] “Cowardice,” Codex Wouwerianus.]

[Note: [Col. 0114D] “You remained standing with your feet, but ran with your money.” This concerns the *libellatici*, who offered the judges a certificate in which they professed themselves Christians and therefore declared that they were not permitted to be defiled by the rites of the profane, but bought off the penalty by paying money. The Church treated them not much more leniently than the *lapsi*, or those “who had sacrificed to idols” (*ἐπιτεθυκόσι εἰδώλοις*). LE PR.]

But to buy with money the freedom of your person, whom Christ redeemed with his own blood—how unworthy of God and of his dispensation! He did not spare his own Son [Col. 0114C] for you, so that he might become a curse for us, because “cursed is everyone who hangs upon the tree”; he was led like a sheep to slaughter, and like a lamb before the shearer, so he did not open his mouth; he offered his back to scourges, his cheeks to blows, and did not turn his face away from spitting; he was numbered among the lawless (Isa. LIII) and delivered over to death, even death on a cross. All this was so that he might gain us away from our sins!

The sun gave way to the day of our purchase. In the lower regions was our manumission, and in heaven our formal covenant. [Col. 0115A] The everlasting gates were lifted up so that the King of Glory, the Lord of hosts, might enter (Ps. XXIII, 9), having purchased humanity from the earth—indeed, from the lower regions—for heaven.

[Note: [Col. 0114D–0115D] “The sun gave way to the day of our purchase. In the lower regions was our manumission, and in heaven our formal covenant.” He says that the sun represented the day of our purchase, the day on which Christ the Lord rose from the dead. Thus Sunday gave way to the day of our redemption. Through Christ’s death our manumission was formally completed. Christ dying is the purchaser of the household—himself both purchaser and price. The merchandise and household is humanity. God in heaven is the party receiving the formal promise. Christ, the guarantor and price of our purchase, returning to the Father, carried into heaven the earnest of the entire sum. RIG.]

Who is there now who struggles against him—indeed, who depreciates and defiles his acquisition, procured at so great a cost, namely, by the most precious blood?

[Note: [Col. 0115D] “Indeed, depreciates the merchandise.” By wishing what was redeemed to be valued at a few paltry coins. He calls the human being redeemed by Christ’s blood his “acquisition.” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0115C] “From the price,” Semler.]

It is therefore now better to flee than to become cheaper, if a human being will not value himself at as much as he cost the Lord. The Lord redeemed him from the angels that rule the world, from the powers, from the spiritual forces of wickedness, from the darkness of this age, from eternal judgment, and from perpetual death. But you bargain over him with an informer, a soldier, or some petty thief of a governor, under the tunic and in the fold of the garment—as the saying goes—as though he were stolen goods, though Christ bought him before the whole world and indeed set him free!

Will you therefore assess this free man at a price and possess him [Col. 0115B] at a price other than that which, as we have said, he cost the Lord—namely, his own blood? Why, then, do you buy Christ from a human being, in the human being in whom Christ dwells?

[Note: [Col. 0115D–0116D] “Why, then, do you buy Christ from a human being, in the human being in whom Christ dwells?” Septimius means this: Why do you buy from a pagan person—an informer, a soldier, or a petty-thief governor—permission for the Christian sect for yourself, yourself, I say, a Christian who, if you are a Christian, have Christ within you? Why do you buy with money the spirit of Christ—that is, permission for you to live as a Christian? Thus Septimius will soon say: “It is not fitting that Christ should have a monetary price.” RIG.]

Simon likewise attempted no different thing when he offered the Apostles money for the spirit of Christ. Therefore this man too, who by redeeming himself redeems Christ’s spirit, will hear: “May your money perish with you, because you thought the grace of God could be obtained for a price” (Acts VIII, 20).

Who would not despise such a denier? For what does that extortioner say? “Give me money”—obviously so that he may not betray the man, since he sells nothing other than what he will supply in return for your payment. When you give, you plainly intended not to be betrayed. Yet if you had not been betrayed, you would have been brought forward. Therefore, by being unwilling to be betrayed, you are unwilling to be brought forward; [Col. 0115C] by that unwillingness you have denied what you were unwilling to be brought forward as being.

[Note: [Col. 0115D] “Nevertheless, a denier,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler; “a thing from the denier,” Latinus; “let him be despised,” Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0115D] Latinus adds “not.”]

“Rather,” you say, “by being unwilling to be brought forward as what I am, I have confessed that I am what I am unwilling to be brought forward as—namely, a Christian.” Can you therefore claim to be a martyr? To have steadfastly displayed Christ? By buying yourself off, you did not display him. If perhaps you confessed before one person, then by being unwilling to confess before many you denied him.

Your very preservation will judge that the person has fallen away while escaping. The person who preferred to escape has therefore fallen away. Refusal of martyrdom is itself denial. A Christian is saved by money and, for this purpose, has coins so that he may not suffer, while being rich against God. But Christ was rich in blood for him. “Blessed, therefore, are the poor,” he says, “for theirs is the kingdom of heaven” [Col. 0116A] (Matt. V)—those whose soul alone is held in the treasury.

[Note: [Col. 0116D] “They have only their soul held in the treasury.” Suetonius used the word *confiscatum*, where, in speaking of money “confiscated,” he means money stored in chests (*fisci*). LE PR.]

If we cannot serve God and Mammon, can we be redeemed by God and by Mammon? For who will serve Mammon more than the man whom Mammon has redeemed?

Finally, what example do you employ in support of buying off betrayal? When did the Apostles, harassed by persecutions, free themselves by negotiating with money? They certainly did not lack it: the proceeds of estates were deposited at their feet, and many wealthy believing men and women supplied them with relief as well. When did Onesiphorus, Aquila, or Stephen assist them in this way during persecution?

[Note: [Col. 0115D–0116D] “Negotiating,” added by Rhenanus and Semler. Pithoeus restored the passage thus: “When did the Apostles, harassed by persecutions, free themselves by negotiating with money?” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0115D] “Negotiating” is absent here in Rhenanus and Semler.]

Indeed, when the governor Felix hoped that he would receive money for Paul from the disciples, a matter about which he also conferred privately with Paul himself, neither Paul paid for himself nor did his disciples pay for him. Those were, of course, the disciples who wept because he persisted in going to Jerusalem and did not guard against the persecutions foretold for him there, but finally said: “The will of God be done” [Col. 0116B] (Acts XXI, 14).

[Note: [Col. 0116C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür add “nor.”]

[Note: [Col. 0116D] “The will of God be done.” Justin, *Second Apology for the Christians*: “Let that which is pleasing to God come to pass” (*ὃ φίλον τῷ Θεῷ, τοῦτο γενέσθω*). RIG.]

What will was this? Certainly that he should suffer for the Lord’s name, not that he should be bought off. For just as Christ laid down his life for us (1 John III, 16), so must we do for him; and not only for him, but indeed also for the brethren on his account. Teaching this, John pronounced that money was not to be paid even for the brethren, but that one should rather die. It makes no difference whether you ought not to redeem a Christian or purchase one. And that this is indeed God’s will is plain.

Look at the condition of kingdoms and empires, certainly disposed by God, [Col. 0116C] in whose hand is the king’s heart (Prov. XXI, 1). Every day so many means are devised for increasing the treasury—censuses, customs duties, levies, and taxes. Yet never to this day has any such plan been devised concerning Christians, to make them liable for some payment to redeem their lives and sect, although an immense revenue might be reaped from so great a multitude, which is unknown to no one.

[Note: [Col. 0116D] “From the summit,” Pamelius, Paris edition, Franeker.]

[Note: [Col. 0116D] “With the condition arranged,” Pamelius, Franeker, Paris edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0116D] “Nor has any such plan ever yet been devised concerning Christians,” etc. But it is gathered from what is read in Cyprian concerning the *libellatici* that this was done not long afterward. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0116D] “Under some payment for redemption,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.]

Bought with blood, rewarded with blood, we owe no coin for our head, because Christ is our Head. It is not fitting that Christ should have a monetary price. How could martyrdoms take place for the Lord’s glory [Col. 0117A] if we purchased permission for our sect by a tax? Therefore, whoever bargains for that permission by payment opposes the divine dispensation.

[Note: [Col. 0116D] “Counted,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.]

Since, then, Caesar has imposed upon us no such arrangement for a tax-paying sect, nor could such an arrangement ever be imposed, with Antichrist now drawing near and panting for Christians’ blood, not their money, how can Scripture be cited to me: “Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s”?

[Note: [Col. 0117C–0117D] “With Antichrist now drawing near.” Judas, similarly extending his work on Daniel’s weeks to Severus’s tenth year, appears to have predicted Antichrist’s advent at that time. The blessed Jerome reports this in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, as does Nicephorus, Book V, chapter 35. In this, says blessed Jerome there, he is convicted of error; but he wrote this because the magnitude of the persecution threatened the immediate destruction of the world. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0117C] “Can you,” Junius.]

A soldier, informer, or enemy extorts me while exacting nothing for Caesar; indeed, he acts against Caesar when, for payment, he releases a Christian who is guilty under human laws. The denarius I owe Caesar is a different one: it belongs to Caesar and was the subject then under discussion—the tribute coin, owed by taxpayers, of course, not by free persons.

Or how shall I render to God the things that are God’s? Surely, likewise, by rendering his image and coin, [Col. 0117B] stamped with his name—that is, the Christian human being.

[Note: [Col. 0117D] “His image and coin, that is, the Christian human being.” Ignatius, *Epistle to the Magnesians*: “The God-fearing human being is a coin struck by God” (*ὁ θεοσεβὴς ἄνθρωπος, νόμισμον ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ χαραχθέν*). RIG.]

And what do I owe God, corresponding to the denarius owed Caesar, except the blood that his Son poured out for me? But if I owe God both the human being and my blood, and I am now at the time when he demands what I owe him, I plainly defraud God by acting so that I need not pay what I owe. I have admirably observed the command—rendering to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s while denying to God the things that are God’s!

[Note: [Col. 0117C] “But,” Oberthür, Semler, Rhenanus.]
