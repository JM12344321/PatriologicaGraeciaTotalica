The whole crux of this most serious Cyprianic controversy turns upon what Cyprian thought concerning the episcopate; and it is clear that he derived this from the conception of the apostolate that he had formed for himself. Both matters must therefore be investigated briefly. Cyprian had this deeply rooted preconception in his mind: that the Apostles, upon whom the Church rested and was built, were by no means separated from one another, each separately possessing his own rights and authority from the one Christ; rather, all and each were fused into one and the same body, agreeing from the same will, in the same power, toward one end; and that the one Peter was the head, center of unity, and summit of the entire apostolic body. There was therefore not merely one head of the apostolic college, but also one apostolic person. Hence, amid the very turmoil over rebaptism, Cyprian wrote these words to Jubaianus:

> “First to Peter, upon whom he built the Church, and from whom he established and manifested the origin of unity, the Lord gave this power: that what he loosed on earth should be loosed in heaven. And after the resurrection he also speaks to the Apostles, saying: ‘As he sent me,’ etc. [Note: [Col. X] Epistle LXXIII, no. 7, vol. III, col. 1114.] . . . The Lord cries out that whoever thirsts should come . . . to the Church, which is one and was founded by the Lord’s voice upon the one man who also received its keys. [Note: [Col. X] Epistle LXXIII, no. 11, vol. III, col. 1116.] There is one Holy Spirit, and one Church, founded by Christ the Lord upon Peter as the origin and principle of unity. [Note: [Col. X] Epistle LXX, no. 3.]”

Hence, to repeat the passage already quoted:

> “In order to manifest unity, he established one chair, and by his authority arranged that the origin of that same unity should begin from one. Certainly the other Apostles were what Peter was, endowed with an equal partnership in honor and authority; but the beginning proceeds from unity, and primacy is given to Peter, so that the one Church of Christ and the one chair may be shown. And all are shepherds, yet one flock is shown, which is fed by all the Apostles with unanimous agreement, so that the Church of Christ may be shown to be one. [Note: [Col. XI] Book *On the Unity of the Church*, col. 501.]”

And, a few words later:

> “We must firmly maintain and vindicate this unity, especially we bishops who preside in the Church, so that we may also prove that the episcopate itself is one and undivided. . . . The episcopate is one, a share of which is held in its entirety by each individual bishop. [Note: [Col. XI] Ibid., loc. cit.]”

This, then, is Cyprian’s true and genuine doctrine concerning both the Apostles and the bishops: as the apostolate was one in Peter, so too the episcopate is one; as there was one chair among the many Apostles, so among the many shepherds there is one flock, one Church; as in the apostolic college there was one mouth and one head, Peter, so the episcopate is one person, possessed in its entirety by each individual. Hence the bishop is in the Church and the Church in the bishop. Hence the beginning proceeds from unity, rather than gradually coalescing from plurality into one. We ourselves were hewn from the Rock; we did not bring that Rock together into our midst. It is not the final stone brought by the builders, but the stone upon which the whole structure is laid and rises. It is not merely the topmost stone at the final summit, but also the cornerstone and foundation stone. The one Church did not arise from many churches; rather, many churches arose from the one. Thus the order of things and their true principle are turned completely upside down in that successive and adventitious unification of Christianity into one body which, as we said above, has been fabricated from Cyprian.

## VIII. 3rd. This One and Catholic Church Is, Furthermore, Roman, Founded upon Peter, Neither Distinct from the Apostolic See nor Different from the Supreme Pontiff

You may perhaps be troubled by our earlier confident assertion that Cyprian was the standard-bearer of Roman theologians. Indeed, a recent anonymous author among the pseudo-Reformed of Germany, though of a very different mind, judged that St. Cyprian was in fact the first to introduce that dogma: “There is one Church, and outside it there is no salvation.” [Note: [Col. XI] *Neither the Christian Religion nor the Roman . . . Catholic Church Is the Only One That Brings Salvation*. Leipzig and Frankfurt, 1791.] Yet before him no one—not even Irenaeus excepted—commended more splendidly that very “chair of Peter, the principal Church, from which sacerdotal unity arose [Note: [Col. XI] Epistle LV, otherwise XII to Cornelius, vol. III, cols. 818–821.], the root and mother of the Catholic Church. [Note: [Col. XI] Epistle XLV, otherwise IV to Cornelius, vol. II, col. 710.]”

Before him no one—not even Tertullian excepted—extolled with greater praise “the supreme priest, the bishop of bishops”: whether Cornelius, “made bishop by the judgment of God and of his Christ, when the place of Fabian—that is, when the place of Peter and the rank of the sacerdotal chair—was vacant [Note: [Col. XI] Epistle LII, otherwise X to Cornelius, vol. III, cols. 771–772]”; or Stephen himself, “who ought to send the fullest letters to his fellow bishops established in [Col. XII] Gaul, and whom it does not become to allow anyone to mock the bishops and pass judgment on the majesty and dignity of the Church. [Note: [Col. XII] Epistle LXVII, to Stephen, vol. III, col. 993.]”

Neither before nor after him did anyone assail more sharply and vehemently those who departed from the Roman Church, as in the case of Novatian:

> “Maddened by the frenzy of discord, he rends the Church, destroys the faith, disturbs peace, scatters charity, and profanes the sacrament. He bears arms against the Church and fights against God’s ordinance. An enemy of the altar, a rebel against Christ’s sacrifice, faithless under the guise of faith, sacrilegious under the guise of religion, a disobedient servant, an impious son, a hostile brother—despising the bishops and abandoning the priests of God, he dares to establish another altar, to offer another prayer with unlawful words, and to profane the truth of the Lord’s sacrifice through false sacrifices. [Note: [Col. XII] Passim in the book *On Unity*, no. 8 ff.] What an offense it is, which not even the baptism of blood can wash away! What a crime, which martyrdom cannot expiate! [Note: [Col. XII] *On the Lord’s Prayer*, vol. IV.] An inexpiable fault! That stain is not washed away even by blood. [Note: [Col. XII] Book *On the Unity of the Church*.] Though they burn in flames, though handed over to the fires or cast to the beasts, though they lay down their lives, that will not be the crown of faith but the punishment of faithlessness; not a glorious end, but destruction born of despair. Such a man can be killed; he cannot be crowned. [Note: [Col. XII] Ibid., col. 511.]”

## IX

What more need be said? Much indeed remains—namely, the matters we have not yet touched, those propositions we boldly proposed should be established from Cyprian: that in his mind there was no distinction between the one Catholic Church and the Roman Church, between the source of the episcopate and the Apostolic See, between Peter and Christ sitting and teaching upon earth, between Peter and his see and his successors—in a word, between Peter and Peter.

Rather, attend and hear how one who communicates with Cornelius thereby communicates with the Catholic Church:

> “You also wrote,” he says, “that I should transmit a copy of the same letter to our colleague Cornelius, so that, all anxiety laid aside, he might now know that you communicate with him—that is, with the Catholic Church. [Note: [Col. XII] Epistle LII, otherwise X to Cornelius, vol. III, col. 768.]”

Hear and attend how one “opposes and resists the Church”—these are his own words—“who abandons the chair of Peter, upon whom the Church was founded; whoever he may be, let him no longer trust that he is in the Church. [Note: [Col. XII] Book *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 500.]”

Hear that the whole principle and constitution of the Church, as it was founded by Christ, is placed in Peter and his successors. Nor does he say this incidentally or hastily, but frequently, both in his letters and in his special book *On the Unity of the Church*:

> “The Lord speaks to Peter: ‘I say to you,’ he says, ‘that you are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my Church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it. . . .’ And again, after his resurrection, he says to the same man: ‘Feed my sheep.’ Upon that one man he builds his Church, and to him he entrusts [Col. XIII] his sheep to be fed. And although after his resurrection he grants equal authority to all the Apostles and says, ‘As the Father sent me, so I send you . . .,’ nevertheless, in order to manifest unity, he established one chair, and by his authority arranged that the origin of that same unity should begin from one. The other Apostles were what Peter was, endowed with an equal partnership both in honor and authority; but the beginning proceeds from unity, and primacy is given to Peter, so that the one Church of Christ and the one chair may be shown. [Note: [Col. XIII] Book *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 500.]”

Amid the very turmoil over rebaptism, as we have just said, he writes these words to Jubaianus:

> “First to Peter, upon whom he built the Church, and from whom he established and manifested the origin of unity, the Lord gave this power: that what he loosed on earth should be loosed in heaven. [Note: [Col. XIII] Epistle LXXIII, vol. III, col. 1114.] The Lord cries out that whoever thirsts should come and drink from the rivers of living water that flowed from his belly. To whom is the thirsty man to come? To the heretics . . . or to the Church, which is one and was founded by the Lord’s voice upon the one man who also received its keys? This is the one Church that holds and possesses all the authority of her bridegroom and Lord. [Note: [Col. XIII] Epistle LXXIII, vol. III, col. 1116.]”

He solemnly established these same things in the addresses and decrees of the Carthaginian synods—even at the risk of condemning himself—when he said:

> “There is one Holy Spirit, and one Church, founded by Christ the Lord upon Peter as the origin and principle of unity. [Note: [Col. XIII] Council of Carthage under Cyprian V, *On Baptism* II–III, col. 1043.]”

## X

Those who defected from the Roman faith left no stone unturned in their effort to disperse this mass of testimony. Some, gnawing at the words as though with an envious tooth, loudly boast that certain expressions are missing from certain manuscripts. But what does it matter if one or two words have fallen out of these passages, provided the rest stand? And they do stand—indeed, even more passages stand; they stand unconquered and inescapable.

Others distort the meaning and, treating Peter as a shadowy symbol fit only for derision, find in him nothing but an empty type of any bishop whatever, or an almost vanishing image of the whole episcopate, of which not even a faint shadow passed on to his successors.

Others—especially among our own Gallicans—distinguishing by a certain subtler perception the See from the one seated upon it, lavishly attribute to the See whatever is holy, great, and primary, while granting to the Pontiffs who occupy it weakness, impotence, and error. They maintain that venerable antiquity, and Cyprian above all, fully embraced, cherished, and taught these quibbles of scholastics itching for distinctions.

Yet let the bishop and martyr himself be our witness, for he plainly affirms:

1. that Christ founded the unity of faith and Catholic communion upon Peter;
2. that Cornelius, Lucius, and Stephen succeeded to Peter’s place by a legitimate and most complete right;
3. that one who communicates with them by name—and not merely with their See, vainly apprehended in the abstract—communicates with the Catholic Church;
4. but that one who has departed from those [Col. XIV] Pontiffs, not merely from the shadow of their see, has rent the Church, destroyed the faith, fought against the Church and God’s ordinance, become faithless under the guise of faith, and cannot be expiated even by the confession of that very faith through martyrdom.

Let this suffice to establish, as we intended, our proposition: namely, that in Cyprian’s mind the one Catholic Church was not separated from the Roman See.

## XI. 4th. This One, Catholic, Roman Church, According to Cyprian’s Mind, Is the True and Sole, Most Certain Rule of Faith, Neither Stained nor Capable of Being Stained by Any Taint of Error

Although these propositions may appear to many like prodigies, it is clear that they flow from what has already been said and are necessarily deduced from it. Will not the true and sole rule of faith be found where there is “the one and perfect dove of God”; where there are “one Lord, one faith, one God”; where “the episcopate is one and undivided”; where there is “the spotless bride of Christ, his one house, the sanctity of one bridal chamber”; where there is “one principle of life and salvation”—so much so that “outside her no one can live; no one has God as Father who does not have her as mother; nor does one hold God’s law or the faith of the Father and the Son if one does not hold her as the sole rule of faith”?

Is not that Church the one and true rule of faith, free from error, which, “flooded with the Lord’s light, extends its rays throughout the entire world, and is joined throughout the whole world by the bond of unity”? I mean that one, Catholic, Roman Church “from which sacerdotal unity arose, the root and mother of all churches,” from which no one departs without destroying the faith, rending the Church, disturbing peace, bearing arms against God, becoming a rebel against Christ and faithless under the guise of faith.

I mean that one and infallible rule of faith of the Roman Pontiffs, with whom to communicate is the same as communicating with the Catholic Church; with whom the whole structure of the Church, which the gates of hell shall not prevail against, is established as in the one Peter: namely, one chair, the origin of that same unity, and primacy given to Peter, so that the one Church of Christ and the one chair may be shown.

Indeed, this same infallible authority of the Roman Church is openly proclaimed not merely by way of deduction but in Cyprian’s own words. For behold these exceedingly clear and decisive words, addressed to Pope Cornelius concerning the Novatianists, which stop the mouth of every opponent:

> “After all this, moreover, with a pseudo-bishop established for themselves by heretics, they still dare to set sail and to carry letters from schismatics and profane men to the chair of Peter and to the principal Church, from which sacerdotal unity arose; nor do they consider that these are the Romans whose faith was praised by the Apostle’s proclamation, to whom faithlessness has not yet been able—indeed, can never at any time be able—to gain access. [Note: [Col. XIV] Epistle LV, otherwise XII to Cornelius, vol. III, col. 818.]”

Surely none of the theologians most devoted to the Roman See has spoken more copiously or splendidly concerning Roman infallibility.

## XII

We shall now refrain from adducing further testimonies. Yet we must very briefly meet the commonplace objection on everyone’s lips, arising from Cyprian’s disagreement with Stephen. [Col. XV]

We have no intention, nor is there any necessity, of defending St. Stephen as though he were accused of error or crime. There have been quite enough who vigorously undertook that task, as may be seen in this very work; quite enough who, vindicating the holy Martyr from the unjust abuse of Vossius, Blondel, Basnage, Antonio de Dominis, Launoy, Dupin, and others, firmly established that he not only did not fall into heresy but was the defender both of antiquity and of truth, and deviated neither from the true path of doctrine nor from the straight apostolic course.

## XIII

Cyprian and the other supporters of rebaptism require much more defense. Different writers have paved different paths toward this end. Most have found nothing but a disciplinary blemish in a matter not yet defined, nothing but an offense against a single Pontiff—while preserving the majesty of the supreme See—which could in some fashion be reconciled with Catholic communion and episcopal zeal.

But in defending Cyprian’s cause, acknowledging no advocate more expert or benevolent than Augustine, we judge that only the twofold argument he employed should be used. Either we must candidly confess with him that Cyprian erred and afterward corrected his less-than-sound opinion, and that the Father furthermore cleansed by the pruning-hook of martyrdom whatever in him required correction; or we must plainly affirm that Cyprian neither thought nor wrote the things that audacious interpolators of his letters attributed to him.

Indeed, let us hear the holy Doctor himself in his *Letter to Vincentius*:

> “That Cyprian held a different opinion concerning Baptism from the form and custom maintained by the Church is found not in canonical writings but in his own letters and those of a council; but that he corrected this opinion is not found. Nevertheless, it is not unreasonable to suppose of such a man that he did correct it, and that this fact was perhaps suppressed by those who were excessively delighted with that error and did not wish to be deprived of so great a patronage.
>
> “Although there are those who contend that Cyprian did not hold this opinion at all, but that it was fabricated under his name by presumptuous and mendacious men. For the integrity and knowledge of the letters of any single bishop, however illustrious, could not be preserved in the same manner as canonical Scripture is preserved through the writings of so many languages and through the order and succession of ecclesiastical observance. Yet even against canonical Scripture there have not been lacking those who fabricated many things under the names of the Apostles—vainly indeed, because Scripture is so commended, so celebrated, and so well known. Nevertheless, what the audacity of an impious undertaking can accomplish against writings not founded upon canonical authority is demonstrated by the fact that it did not refrain from raising itself even against those writings established by so great a mass of recognition.
>
> “Cyprian, therefore, either did not hold at all the opinion you recount as his; or he afterward corrected it according to the rule of truth; or he covered this blemish, as it were, upon his most candid breast with the abundance of charity, while he both defended most copiously the unity of the Church growing throughout the whole world and most steadfastly maintained the bond of peace. For it is written: ‘Charity covers a multitude of sins.’ [Col. XVI] To this was also added the fact that, as a most fruitful branch, if anything in him required correction, the Father cleansed it with the pruning-hook of martyrdom. For the Lord says: ‘The branch that bears fruit in me, my Father cleanses, so that it may bear more fruit.’ Why was this? Because, clinging to the far-spreading vine, he did not abandon the root of unity. For even if he delivered his body to be burned, but did not have charity, it would profit him nothing.
>
> [Note: [Col. The first and the last arrived at and wrote in support of the same opinion at the same time, without knowledge of one another.

> 3. He finds in these Cyprianic writings principles of doctrine very different from those in his other genuine books.

For he finds that they were highly skilled in calumny, marked by affected sanctity and certain impiety, false doctrine, and slight learning; and that they contrived an error so densely covered with fallacies that it is evident to very few. From this are explained the arrangement of places, the chronological order, the sequence of events, the disposition of persons, and the origin, progress, and outcome of the doctrine. # ARTICLE III. ON THE USE OF ST. CYPRIAN IN MATTERS OF DISCIPLINE

[Col. XVII]

## I

Everyone recognizes what a most abundant supply of useful material the works of St. Cyprian provide, whether they are consulted on exegetical questions, adduced to establish the dogmas of the Church, examined for the vivid and lifelike portrait they present of the morals of his age, or used to summon forth from the tomb, restored to life before us, the chronology of the times, the succession of years, and the personalities of the most illustrious men.

Nevertheless, passing over matters pertaining to Cyprian’s exegetical, dogmatic, moral, or historical use, lest I exceed the limits of a preface too greatly, it seemed that some benefit might be gained by touching in passing upon a few points concerning his use in matters of discipline, almost all taken from D. Lumper’s *Theologico-Critical History of the Fathers*. [Note: [Col. XVII] Vol. XII, pp. 1–112, passim.]

The holy bishop of Carthage and martyr Cyprian was the first of all the holy Fathers to discuss the discipline of the Church at great length. In his works, and especially in his Letters, there are innumerable highly important instructions and judgments from which the ecclesiastical discipline of those times and the Church’s external condition are known more clearly than in the light of midday. For he treats not only discipline in general but also its particular application to the administration of the sacraments, the correction and reconciliation of sinners, the discipline of the ancient Church concerning penance, the use and abuse of indulgences, and other points of ecclesiastical discipline.

## § I.—On Discipline in General

[Col. XVIII]

## II

“In the Holy Scriptures discipline is frequently and everywhere enjoined,” says St. Cyprian in his treatise *On the Dress of Virgins* [Note: [Col. XVIII] St. Cyprian, *On the Discipline and Dress of Virgins*, shortly after the beginning, vol. IV, col. 441 B.], “and the entire foundation of religion and faith proceeds from observance and fear.”

It is readily discerned that nothing is so necessary, especially in the warfare of persecution, as maintaining the due strictness of divine vigor. Whoever relaxes it, says the Roman Clergy [Note: [Col. XVIII] The same, Epistle XXXI, vol. IV, col. 303 A.] in writing to St. Cyprian, must always wander amid the unstable course of affairs, be driven this way and that by the varied and uncertain storms of events, and, as though the helm of counsel had been wrenched from his hands, dash the ship of ecclesiastical salvation against the rocks. Thus it is evident that the Church’s welfare can be secured in no other way than by repelling, like hostile waves, those who act against it, and by always preserving the rule of discipline itself like a saving helm in the storm.

Furthermore, the discipline of the Church ought, as far as possible, to be uniform in the principal matters, such as penance. St. Cyprian indeed gives this admonition to his clergy in his thirty-fourth letter. [Note: [Col. XVIII] Epistle XXVIII, vol. IV, col. 301 A.] He further directs that this discipline should be prescribed by the counsel of all the bishops, as is gathered from several of his other letters. [Note: [Col. XVIII] See Epistles XI, vol. IV, col. 273; *To the People*, vol. IV, col. 256; XIII, *To His Clergy*, vol. IV, col. 260; XIX, *To Caldonius*; XXVIII, *To the Presbyters and Deacons*, vol. IV, col. 300; XXXI, *The Roman Clergy to Cyprian*, col. 307.]

These are St. Cyprian’s statements concerning discipline in general. Let us now see what is found in his writings concerning discipline in particular.

## § II.—On Discipline with Respect to Baptism

## III. The Blessing of the Water to Be Used for Baptism

The first noteworthy point encountered in this ancient Father with respect to Baptism is that in his age the bishop was already bestowing a blessing upon the water of Baptism.

> “The water,” says St. Cyprian in Epistle LXX, addressed to Januarius and the other Numidian bishops, “must therefore first be cleansed and sanctified by the priest, so that by its baptism it may wash away the sins of the person who is baptized. [Note: [Col. XVIII] Cyprian, Epistle LXX, vol. III, col. 1030 A.]”

He confirms this discipline by the following oracle of Ezekiel:

> “I will pour clean water upon you, and you shall be cleansed from all your defilements, and I will cleanse you from all your idols. [Note: [Col. XVIII] Ezekiel 36:25.]”

## IV. Exorcisms in Baptism

The ceremony of Baptism was preceded by exorcisms, as the most celebrated Fathers of the Council of Carthage teach, of which St. Cyprian was and was regarded as the very soul. Read what [Col. XIX] Caecilius of Bilta relates, vol. III, col. 1054; Crescentius of Cirta, col. 1058 of the Oxford edition; and Lucius of Thebeste, all of whom testify to this practice.

It was likewise customary to perform exorcisms over those who were possessed by demons; when adjured in the name of the true God, the demons were compelled to yield and depart from those bodies.

> “Yet when adjured by us through the true God,” says St. Cyprian in his treatise *On the Vanity of Idols* [Note: [Col. XIX] Cyprian, book *On the Vanity of Idols*, vol. IV, col. 577 A.], “they immediately yield and are compelled to depart from the possessed bodies.”

“You may see them,” the holy Doctor adds,

> “at our voice and by the hidden operation of the divine majesty, beaten with scourges and scorched with fire; stretched out as the increasing punishment spreads; wailing, groaning, and pleading; confessing whence they come and when they depart, even while those who worship them are listening. They either leap out immediately or gradually vanish, according as the faith of the sufferer assists or the grace of the healer breathes upon him.”

There were therefore two kinds of exorcisms: some performed over those preparing for Baptism, and others over the possessed. The former were conducted in the church before the faithful. The latter were not ordinarily conducted there because, as we have seen, pagans themselves were sometimes present. Accordingly, since the work so required, the exorcists went to those who needed their ministry.

## V. The Sign of the Cross upon the Forehead of Those Who Were Washed

The sign of the cross upon the forehead of those who were immersed was another ceremony employed in the administration of Baptism. St. Cyprian bears the clearest testimony to this practice:

1. In his treatise *On the Lapsed*. There, speaking of those who had stood as confessors during persecution, he says that their foreheads had been consecrated by the sign of the cross:

   > “The forehead, pure with the sign of God, could not bear the devil’s crown; it reserved itself for the Lord’s crown. [Note: [Col. XIX] Cyprian, book *On the Lapsed*, col. 466 B.]”

2. In his treatise *On the Unity of the Church*, while discussing Uzziah, whose forehead was stained by the mottling of leprosy, he teaches that this is the part of the body upon which those washed with the water of Baptism are signed:

   > “There [upon the forehead] are signed those who merit the Lord. [Note: [Col. XIX] The same, book *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 514 A.]”

3. In his treatise *Against Demetrianus*, he most plainly asserts with Ezekiel [Note: [Col. XIX] Ezekiel 9:6.] that only those who have been reborn and signed with the sign of Christ will be saved. [Note: [Col. XIX] Cyprian, treatise *Against Demetrianus*, vol. IV, col. 526 B; and *Testimonies*, book II, chapter 22, vol. IV, col. 716 A.]

## VI. The Oxford Editor’s Note on This Matter Is Refuted

In the preceding volumes we have already observed from several Fathers, and especially from Tertullian, that the sign of the cross was in extremely frequent use among the ancient faithful. The Oxford editor of St. Cyprian acknowledges this same fact and states in a note upon one of the holy Doctor’s testimonies quoted above:

> “Christians not only [Note: [Col. XIX] Bremen edition of John Fell, Bishop of Oxford, p. 116.] imprinted the sign of the cross upon their foreheads among the solemn rites of Baptism, but at every advance and movement, at every entrance and departure, when dressing and putting on shoes, at the baths and tables, at thresholds, beds, and seats—whatever activity occupied them—they wore down their foreheads with the sign of the Cross.”

Yet this bishop, who holds no insignificant place among Protestants, adds that religious worship was not yet offered to the Cross: “Meanwhile religious worship was absent.” To prove this falsehood he produces a passage from Minucius Felix in which Octavius observes and asserts that Christians by no means adore the Cross: “We neither worship crosses.” I quoted and explained that passage in its proper place, and I refer the reader there. [Note: [Col. XX] D. Lumper, *Theologico-Critical History*, part VII, section 2, chapter 2, article 7, pp. 183–189.]

Here I shall add only one point: namely, that the use of any object in any religious act is the greatest possible sign of religious worship toward that same object. But the Bishop of Oxford, compelled by Tertullian’s authority, acknowledges that Christians did this in the first centuries of the Church. Catholics, therefore, in the worship they offer to the Cross, today follow the practice of those blessed times.

## VII. Salt in the Mouth of Those Who Were Baptized

One of the ceremonies of Baptism is likewise the placing of salt in the mouth or upon the tongue of the person being baptized; and while the minister does this, he pronounces these words: “Receive the salt of wisdom,” etc.

Now I think, says our distinguished Bernard Marechall [Note: [Col. XX] Marechall, *Harmony of the Holy Fathers*, vol. II, p. 115, Latin edition, Venice, 1767.], that this practice is indicated in the celebrated Council of Carthage of which I spoke above. For there Lucius of Castrogalba, in order to prove that schismatics do not baptize lawfully, says that they cannot impart the salt of spiritual wisdom:

> “Schismatics cannot season others with spiritual wisdom, since by withdrawing from the Church they have themselves become insipid. . . . [Note: [Col. XX] Council of Carthage VII, or III *On Baptism*, vol. III, col. 1058 B.]”

In these words he certainly appears to have had in view both the ceremony under discussion and the words: “Receive the salt of wisdom.”

## VIII. Questions in Baptism

Before neophytes were immersed in the water, they were asked, among other things, whether they believed in eternal life and in the forgiveness of sins through the holy Church.

St. Cyprian himself bears the clearest testimony concerning this matter in Epistle LXIX. He employs the force of the aforesaid question to show that it cannot be asked by those who are separated from the Church’s communion:

> “For when they say”—these are St. Cyprian’s very own words, speaking of heretics and schismatics—“‘Do you believe in the forgiveness of sins and eternal life through the holy Church?’ they lie in their question, since they do not possess the Church. [Note: [Col. XX] Cyprian, Epistle LXIX.]”

Elsewhere he recalls this same custom in these words:

> “The very question asked in Baptism is a witness to this truth—that the Church alone forgives sins. For when we say, ‘Do you believe in eternal life and that the forgiveness of sins is given only in the Church?’—yet among heretics, where the Church does not exist, sins cannot be forgiven. [Note: [Col. XXI] Cyprian, Epistle LXX, vol. III, col. 1040 A.]”
