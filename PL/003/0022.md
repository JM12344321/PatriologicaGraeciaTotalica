### III

Nothing in these acts of the martyrs can be observed as proceeding from the school and training of Montanus: nothing superstitious, nothing disorderly, nothing tumultuous, nothing confused, nothing, finally, irregular [Col. 0141B]—all of which, as is evident from Asterius Urbanus, Apollonius, Miltiades, Serapion, Aelius Publius, and other Fathers [Note: [Col. 0141D] In Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book V, ch. 16 and following], are characteristics of the Montanist spirit. On the contrary, throughout there shine forth a marvelous modesty, composure, prudence, piety, reverence, charity, courage, faith, and wisdom; so that there is no other martyrdom of holy women attended by more illustrious ornaments of every virtue.

It seems altogether impossible that a woman driven by madness and frenzy, and impelled by a demonic spirit—such as the Montanist pseudo-prophetesses were—could have guarded herself so carefully in everything and conducted herself in such a way that even the strictest censor could find nothing insolent in her words, nothing arrogant, nothing shameless in her movements, and nothing irreligious in her actions. The Montanists did indeed make a display [Col. 0141C] of a certain outward gravity of conduct; they boasted of the severity of their discipline and greatly vaunted their abstinence, chastity, and endurance of persecutions. Yet those who proclaimed in the most presumptuous language that they had added stricter ordinances to the teachings of the Gospel afterward failed utterly to reflect in their conduct the sanctity of the evangelical law, but departed from it as far as possible. Perpetua and her fellow martyrs, by contrast, reproduce the Gospel in their conduct and express it with the utmost exactness.

There is also something else worthy of observation in the virtues of these martyrs, and especially of Perpetua, by which they can readily be distinguished from the counterfeit virtues of hypocrites and pseudo-prophets: they commend themselves by a certain native simplicity. Just as clear signs of a kind of divine wisdom, of a lofty spirit, and of a mind thoroughly detached from human affairs are discerned in them, so too they please by a certain natural candor [Col. 0141D]; nothing in them is artificial, and nothing is found to have been done through pretense or hypocrisy. This simplicity and the beauty of a candid heart shine even in Perpetua’s own words and manner of writing: she does not conceal her human emotions, as was observed above; she does not exalt herself above others; she does not exaggerate her circumstances; she refers everything to God; in faith, in hope of divine mercy, and in the bosom of Providence she rests with complete serenity; she despises every misfortune, fears nothing, hopes for everything, and dares anything.

Such flowers of virtue assuredly do not grow among the thickets of heresies; a bad tree does not produce such fruits. It can indeed happen that a wicked spirit affects some outward shadow [Col. 0142A] of holiness and eagerly attempts to imitate such acts of virtue; but it cannot present an image of Christian perfection that is complete in every respect and wholly sincere in every part. Hypocrisy cannot guard itself so carefully that nothing slips out by which it betrays itself. Some vice is readily mingled with false virtues, and that spirit of pride is especially recognized when it feigns humility of mind and composes itself into a certain outward appearance of modesty.

But what does Basnage find to censure in matters of conduct in Perpetua and her companions? Absolutely nothing. It can surely be affirmed that, just as no image is more like the discipline of the Gospel, so none is more at variance with heretical training. Let Perpetua and Maximilla be compared with one another: their characters will differ [Col. 0142B] as widely as vice is separated from virtue, and darkness from light. It cannot therefore be that they were impelled by the same spirit of frenzy and madness.

Indeed, if so illustrious a martyrdom, adorned with the embellishments of so many virtues, was or could have been perfected among heretics, then the argument for the Christian religion which arises—and arises most effectively—from the constancy and virtue of the martyrs either perishes altogether or is greatly weakened. For Christian martyrdom is commended not so much by death undertaken for the faith—since many others from every sect have voluntarily surrendered themselves to death for their superstitions—as by the array of the other virtues with which Catholic martyrs adorned their contest: namely faith, hope, charity, reverence, piety, modesty, and the invincible strength of a lofty spirit, all of which, as we have shown, [Col. 0142C] shone marvelously in Perpetua and her fellow martyrs.

### IV

But so that I may neglect nothing, or leave without refutation anything from which some captious faultfinder or shameless slanderer might manufacture ill will against the holy martyrs, I shall examine two passages in the Acts in which they seem to violate the laws of nature. By these laws each person is commanded to preserve his own life, and we are forbidden either to desire death for ourselves or others, or to urge others toward our destruction.

Our martyrs seem to have sinned against these laws of nature, first in chapter V, § 2, where they prayed with common supplications that Felicity might give birth prematurely, “since the day of the spectacle was already at hand,” “lest she be delayed because of her pregnancy, since it is not lawful for pregnant women to be presented for punishment.” More seriously, they seem to have done so in the last section of chapter VI, where Perpetua herself, with her own hands, positioned the instrument of death so as to hasten her martyrdom: “Perpetua, however, so that she might taste something of pain, cried out when she was pierced between the ribs, and herself placed the wandering right hand of the novice gladiator upon her throat.”

No one who has attended to what Daillé poured forth against the martyr Ignatius for an altogether similar reason will be surprised that these things could be censured in Perpetua and her fellow martyrs. Because that most holy martyr had warned the Romans by letter not to delay his desired martyrdom through their prayers to God or their services, and had declared that he would drive even reluctant and resisting beasts to destroy him, Daillé arraigns him as guilty of violating piety and modesty.

But so that it may be understood more clearly how well Ignatius and Perpetua [Col. 0143A] and her fellow martyrs harmonize with one another in such desires, I shall set down Ignatius’s own words here [Note: [Col. 0143D] “I entreat you, do not become an untimely kindness to me. Allow me to be food for the beasts, through whom it is possible to attain to God. I am God’s wheat, and I am ground by the teeth of beasts, so that I may be found pure bread of Christ. Rather entice the beasts, so that they may become my tomb and leave nothing of my body behind, lest, once I have fallen asleep, I become a burden to anyone. . . . Entreat Christ on my behalf, so that through these instruments I may be found a sacrifice.” From the Epistle to the Romans, in Ruinart, *Acts*, at the end, etc.]:

> I beseech you not to show me an untimely kindness. Allow me to be food for the beasts, through whom it is possible to attain to God. I am God’s wheat and am ground by the teeth of beasts, that I may be found pure bread of Christ. Rather entice the beasts, that they may become my tomb and leave nothing of my body behind, lest, after I have fallen asleep, I become a burden to anyone. . . . Entreat Christ on my behalf, that through these instruments I may be found a sacrifice.

What Ignatius wished the Romans to do for him, our martyrs performed as a duty toward Felicity. He wished united prayers to be poured forth to God by the Romans so that he might be ground by the teeth of beasts and through these instruments be made a sacrifice of Christ. This likewise was what Felicity’s companions [Col. 0143B] desired, and in prayers poured forth to God they asked that she might deliver the child prematurely, lest her martyrdom be delayed, but that she might be cast to the beasts together with the others.

Ignatius continues and displays not only his burning desire for martyrdom, but says that he will provoke even unwilling and resisting beasts to kill him [Note: [Col. 0143D] “May I have the enjoyment of the beasts prepared for me; and I pray that they may be found ready for me. I shall even entice them to devour me swiftly, and not, as in the case of some whom they feared and did not touch. But even if, against their will, they should refuse, I shall force them.” Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book V, ch. 16 and following]:

> May I have the enjoyment of the beasts prepared for me, and I pray that they may be found ready for me. I shall even entice them to devour me swiftly, and not, as in the case of some whom they feared and did not touch. But if they should resist and refuse, I shall force them.

It makes no difference whether someone places a gladiator’s right hand upon his own throat, as Perpetua did, or drives beasts to devour him, as Ignatius wrote that he would do. Hence either both are open to the same censures or both must be attended by the same praises. [Col. 0143C] Meanwhile, just as Pearson shows by the example of our martyrs that Ignatius’s desires were not unworthy of a holy martyr, so we shall defend those martyrs’ prayers for Felicity by the example of Ignatius. For why, to use the words of Pontius the deacon, should we condemn ordinary martyrs, newly come from baptism, for that for which we hold the wisest bishop and disciple of the Apostles in honor and admiration?

### V

But since no small number from the Protestant body, including Basnage, reject the epistles of Ignatius as a pseudepigraphal and adulterated work, and among other things which they mark in them as alien to Ignatius’s virtues they censure that burning desire for martyrdom, I shall, with Pearson, defend both the epistolary Ignatius and our martyrs by other examples.

Celerinus the confessor, writing [Col. 0143D] to Lucian the confessor in Epistle 21 among the Cyprianic letters:

> When I wrote these things to you, my lord and brother, I was both joyful and sorrowful: joyful because I had heard that you were arrested for our Lord Jesus Christ, our Savior, [Col. 0144A] and had confessed his name before the magistrates of this world, etc.

And below:

> May he whose name you confessed crown you. . . . Ask that I may be worthy to be crowned among your company.

And again:

> O happy man! You receive your desires, which you always longed for; even while sleeping on the ground you wished to be cast into prison for his name, and this now happens to you, as it is written: “May the Lord grant you according to your heart.”

So wrote Celerinus, commended by Saint Cyprian [Note: [Col. 0144D] Epistle 27, otherwise 23] especially for moderation, humility, fear, caution, and modesty.

Likewise, the martyrs of Lyons—the chief ornament of the Christian religion, distinguished no less by faith and wisdom than by invincible courage—“with abundant tears,” as Eusebius reports [Note: [Col. 0144D] “With tears they besought the brethren, begging that earnest prayers be made that they might be perfected.” Book V, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 2], “besought the brethren that continual prayers be made to God [Col. 0144B] so that they themselves might at last be perfected.”

Pontius, Saint Cyprian’s deacon, writes concerning him:

> Meanwhile, many men of eminent and most distinguished rank, noble also by the nobility of the world, gathered together. Because of their friendship, they repeatedly advised him to withdraw; and lest bare persuasion seem insufficient, they also offered him places to which he might withdraw. But he, with his mind now lifted up to heaven, had disregarded the world and did not assent to their flattering counsels. Perhaps even then he would have done what many faithful persons requested, had he also been commanded by a divine order.

For, as Pontius himself teaches, during the earlier withdrawal, “when the Lord then commanded him to hide, he obeyed.”

But when Saint Cyprian was being led to execution, the faithful people were so far from praying to God that he might be spared, or from impeding his martyrdom in any way, that they desired rather to die together with him [Col. 0144C]:

> Although what their common desires sought could not come to pass—namely that the whole people might suffer together in fellowship of equal glory—whoever, under the eyes of Christ looking on and within the hearing of the bishop, sincerely wished to suffer, sent, through a suitable witness of his desire, what were in a sense letters of embassy to God.

Saint Jerome, *Life of Paul the Hermit*:

> Under the persecutors Decius and Valerian, at the time when Cornelius at Rome and Cyprian at Carthage were condemned to shed their blessed blood, a savage storm devastated many churches throughout Egypt and the Thebaid. The desire of Christians then was to be struck down by the sword for Christ’s name. But the cunning enemy, devising protracted tortures leading to death, wished to slaughter souls, not bodies; and, as Cyprian himself, who suffered from him, says: “Those who wished to die [Col. 0144D] were not permitted to be killed.”

Well known also are the words of the holy martyr Polycarp to the proconsul who was threatening him first with beasts and then with flames: ἀλλὰ τί βραδύνεις; φέρε ὃ βούλει, “But why do you delay? Bring whatever you wish.” Ταῦτα καὶ ἄλλα πλείονα λέγων θάρσους καὶ χαρᾶς ἐνεπίμπλατο, “As he said these and many other things, he was filled with confidence and joy,” as his very ancient Acts testify. And what, I ask, were those “many other things” which he pronounced full of courage and exultation, if they were not like the desires of Ignatius, Perpetua, Felicity, and their fellow martyrs? If they did not accord with that voice which he heard from heaven, Ἴσχυε, καὶ ἀνδρίζου, Πολύκαρπε, “Be strong and act manfully, Polycarp”?

Also very well known are the words of Romanus, the deacon of Caesarea, heard at Antioch: ποῦ μοι τὸ πῦρ; “Where is the fire for me?”

To the examples which Pearson adduces in defense of Ignatius I shall add another, no less remarkable than the preceding ones, from the authentic Acts of Saints Montanus, Lucius, and the others. When the rest had been condemned, Flavian was recalled to prison and his martyrdom was postponed. He endured this no less reluctantly than did his companions. Hence Montanus, when, as the Acts say,

> the executioner was already standing over him and the sword hung poised above his neck, stretched out his hands toward heaven and prayed in a clear voice, so that its sound struck not only the ears of the people but even the pagans themselves, asking and entreating that Flavian, who through the people’s vote had been left behind from their company, might follow on the third day.

The feelings and desires of Flavian’s own mother were no different. The Acts add concerning her:

> At his side clung his incomparable mother, who, besides the faith by which she belonged to the patriarchs, proved herself a daughter of Abraham in this also: that she desired her son to be killed and was deeply grieved that he had meanwhile been left behind.

Flavian himself consoled his afflicted mother with these words:

> You know, mother deservedly most dear, how I have always tried, if the opportunity for confession should arise, to enjoy my martyrdom, to be seen frequently in chains, and repeatedly to be postponed. If, therefore, what I desired has happened, it is a cause for glory rather than sorrow.

Such were the martyrs’ desires. If these are made a charge against them, there will be innumerable martyrs from whom the crown of martyrdom must be taken away.

### VI

Greater ill will might be manufactured against Perpetua on the second ground; but that too, as I said, is dispelled by Ignatius’s example. For Perpetua did nothing other than what Ignatius writes that he would do. But since controversy is raised concerning his Acts and epistles, other examples are not lacking, examples Pearson gathered with a full hand in his defense of the epistles of Ignatius.

In the very ancient letter of the Church of Smyrna, Germanicus is represented as though he were a noble and distinguished combatant against beasts, ἐπισήμως θηριομαχῶν, “fighting the beasts with distinction.” But what, in the judgment of the most ancient Church, is found to be ἐπίσημον—distinguished or noble—in the brief account of his martyrdom? This one thing, from which Perpetua might perhaps be charged with violating the law of nature [Note: [Col. 0145D] “For when the proconsul wished to persuade him and told him [variant: λέγοντος, ‘was telling him’] to have pity upon his age, he forcibly drew the beast toward himself, desiring to be delivered more swiftly from their unjust and lawless life. From this, therefore, the whole multitude, marveling at the courage of the God-loving and God-fearing race of Christians, etc.”]:

> For [Col. 0145D] when the proconsul wished to sway him and told him to have pity upon his own age, he voluntarily drew the beast toward himself and forced it upon himself.

He is said to have done this in order to hasten his martyrdom:

> Desiring to escape more swiftly from the unjust and lawless life of those people. From this it came about that the whole multitude, marveling at the courage of the pious and religious race of Christians, etc.

Germanicus, therefore—whom the ancient and celebrated Church of Smyrna presented as an especially distinguished and noble martyr, and because of whose remarkable deed the pagans themselves admired the nobility and high courage of the Christian people—did toward the beasts what Perpetua did with the gladiator. Burning with desire for martyrdom, he “voluntarily drew the beast toward himself”; while she, burning with the same desire, “herself placed the right hand of the novice gladiator upon her throat.”

Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria, greatly praises the virgin Apollonia because, after all her teeth had been knocked out by blows to her jaws and the flames had been brought near [Note: [Col. 0146D] Συντόνως ἐπεπήδησεν εἰς τὸ πῦρ, καὶ καταπέφλεκται, “She leapt vigorously into the fire and was burned up.” Letter to Fabius of Antioch, in Eusebius, Book VI, ch. 41], “she swiftly leapt into the fire and was consumed.”

Saint Hilary, *Commentary on Psalm 65*:

> Some glory in the chains of prisons; others rejoice at being struck with blows; others submit the necks of their blessed heads to be severed by the power [Col. 0146B] of the irreligious; many run toward the constructed pyres and, while the ministers of punishment tremble, leap into the fire with the devoted haste of their resolve.

Saint John Chrysostom describes this ardor for suffering in the martyrs most excellently in his discourse on Romanus, introducing the devil as speaking thus [Note: [Col. 0146D] “I spread burning coals beneath them, but they ran toward them as toward roses. I kindled fire, but they cast themselves into it as into springs of cold water. . . . And as though dancing in a sacred procession and playing in a green meadow, each seized the punishments for himself—not receiving them as penalties but as spring flowers, being crowned and, by the excess of their own eagerness, forestalling my punishments”]:

> I spread burning coals beneath them, but they ran toward them as toward roses. I kindled fire, but they cast themselves into it as into springs of cold water. . . . And as though dancing in a sacred procession and playing in a green meadow, each seized the torments—not receiving them as punishments but as spring flowers, being crowned and, through the excess of their own eagerness, forestalling my torments.

### VII

Nor is it strange that some provoked beasts already prepared [Col. 0146C] to kill them, or voluntarily employed instruments of cruelty placed at hand in order to hasten martyrdom, since it is most certainly established—something much greater—that very many sought death for Christ’s name when they were not in the power of their enemies, disclosed themselves, and provoked their enemies themselves to inflict punishment.

Lucian says of the Christians of his own time in *Peregrinus* [Note: [Col. 0146D] “For the wretched people have persuaded themselves that they will be altogether immortal and live forever. Therefore they despise death, and many voluntarily surrender themselves”]:

> The wretched people, having persuaded themselves that they will be altogether immortal and live forever, despise death, and many voluntarily surrender themselves.

Justin Martyr acknowledges that he became a Christian because no wicked person seemed to him [Note: [Col. 0146D] Θάνατον ἀσπάζεσθαι, οὐχ’ ὅτι ἑαυτὸν κατήγγελλε φονευθησόμενον] “to welcome death—much less voluntarily to disclose and denounce himself so that he might be killed,” as Christians did at that time.

Tertullian, *To Scapula*, ch. 1:

> We contend against all your savagery, even bursting forth voluntarily, and we rejoice more when condemned [Col. 0147A] than when acquitted.

And chapter 4:

> Take care only lest, by the very fact that we endure such things, we appear to burst forth for this single purpose: to prove precisely that we do not fear these things but voluntarily summon them. When Arrius Antoninus was persistently persecuting in Asia, all the Christians of that city, having formed a band, presented themselves before his tribunal. After ordering a few to be led away, he said to the rest: Ὦ δειλοί, εἰ θέλετε ἀποθνῄσκειν, κρημνοὺς ἢ βρόχους ἔχετε, “O wretches, if you wish to die, you have cliffs or nooses.” If it should please them that the same be done here, what will you do with so many thousands of people—so many men and women of every sex, every age, and every rank—presenting themselves to you?

Concerning the ninth persecution, Sulpicius says:

> People rushed competitively into glorious contests, and martyrdoms by glorious deaths were then sought far more eagerly than bishoprics are now pursued by headlong ambitions.

The same author, Letter XI, concerning Blessed Martin:

> What if it had been granted to him, in the times of Nero and Decius, to fight in the contest that then existed? I call the God of heaven and earth to witness: he would voluntarily have mounted the rack and of his own accord cast himself into the flames. . . . But if, after the example of the teacher of the Gentiles, he had been assigned to the sword and led among other victims, as often occurred, he would have been the first of all—compelling the executioner—to seize the palm of blood.

Saint John Chrysostom observes that under Julian’s rule [Note: [Col. 0147D] Πᾶσαν ἡλικίαν ἁπλῶς καὶ φύσιν ἑκατέραν πηδῶσαν ἐπὶ τὰς ὑπὲρ τῆς εὐσεβείας σφαγάς] “every age without distinction, and both sexes, leapt forward to undergo slaughter for the true religion.” He gives this reason why Julian the Apostate refused to devise an open persecution against the Christians [Note: [Col. 0147D] “For he knew, he knew clearly, that if he dared this, all would surrender their souls for Christ. ‘For all,’ he says, ‘will come like bees to the honeycomb, flying toward martyrdom’”]:

> For he knew, he knew clearly, that if he dared this, all would surrender their souls [Col. 0147C] for Christ. “For all,” he says, “will come like bees to the honeycomb, flying toward martyrdom.”

Nor did the Christians of the ancient Church merely speak courageous words; they also acted and suffered. Especially celebrated was the name of the martyr Phocas. When by chance he had received as guests the officers who said that they had been sent to kill Phocas, he promised that on the next day he would show Phocas to them. After first preparing a place for burial, he stood before them and said, “I am that Phocas.” When they began to hesitate, he encouraged them by exhortation to kill him [Note: [Col. 0147D] Εἶπεν, ἔπεισεν, ἔπαθεν, καὶ, τὴν κεφαλὴν ἀποτμηθείς, ἱερεῖον τῷ θεῷ κεχαρισμένον προσήχθη]:

> He spoke, persuaded them, and suffered; and, having been beheaded, he was offered as a sacrifice pleasing to God,

as Asterius of Amasea, Epiphanius’s contemporary, says.

During Valerian’s persecution [Col. 0147D], Priscus, Malchus, and Alexander mutually accused one another of cowardice and idleness because, although an opportunity had presented itself, they had still held back, μὴ οὐχὶ προαρπάζοντες τὸν τοῦ μαρτυρίου στέφανον, “and had not seized the crown of martyrdom beforehand.” They therefore approached the judge, were condemned to the beasts, and θείῳ κατεκοσμήθησαν μαρτυρίῳ, “were adorned with divine martyrdom,” as Eusebius relates [Col. 0148A] in Book VII, ch. 12.

Romanus, the deacon of Caesarea, seeing many people sacrificing, ζήλῳ θεοσεβείας πρόσεισι, “approached more closely, impelled by zeal for divine worship,” and, rebuking them with a loud voice, forced himself upon the judge. Arrested for his boldness [Note: [Col. 0148D] Γενναιότατος, εἰ καί τις ἄλλος, ἀποδέδεικται μάρτυς τῆς ἀληθείας, “He proved himself, if anyone ever did, a most courageous martyr of the truth.” Eusebius, *The Martyrs of Palestine*, ch. 2], “he was adorned with a courageous martyrdom, if anyone ever was.”

Indeed, when the persecution was raging, Apphianus, without the knowledge of everyone living in the same house and even of the cohort of soldiers, seized the right hand of the governor Urbanus, compelled him to cease sacrificing, and, surrendering himself to most grievous torments, suffered. Eusebius, who knew him personally, judged that he had done this [Note: [Col. 0148D] Τῆς ἐπὶ τοῦτ’ αὐτὸν προαγούσης ἐνθέου δυνάμεως μονονουχὶ βοώσης διὰ τοῦ γενομένου, etc., ibid., ch. 4], “because divine power impelled him to this very act and all but cried aloud through what occurred, etc.” Eusebius also relates that Aedesius attempted to do the same thing his brother had done and suffered in the same way [Col. 0148B].

It would take too long to enumerate all those whom Eusebius and others commemorate as having voluntarily and deliberately presented themselves for martyrdom. These are Pearson’s arguments for Ignatius; no one fails to see how well they apply to our martyrs.

### VIII

But to the many examples he records, I think that the remarkable and daring deed of Andronicus and Tarachus must certainly be added. Of these, one incited a bear and the other a lioness—both rendered tame by divine power—to devour him, as is read in their Acts [Note: [Col. 0148D] “They released a man-killing bear, which, as some said, had killed three men that day. When it had been released and had come near them, it passed by the other bodies and ran toward the holy martyr Andronicus; sitting beside him, it licked the fluid from his wounds. . . . Holy Andronicus placed his head against it and tried forcibly to enrage the beast so that it might seize him and release him from life; but the bear remained beside him and lay down together with the saint. . . . When the lioness saw the holy bodies cast down, she came to blessed Tarachus, bent down, and did homage at his feet. The holy Tarachus stretched out his hand, grasped her mane and ears, and drew her toward himself; but she was like a sheep being led, etc.” In Ruinart, *Authentic Acts of the Early Martyrs*]:

> They released a most savage bear, which they said had killed three men that very day. It approached the bodies of the saints and harmed no body. After passing by, it came to Saint Andronicus and, sitting down, licked his wounds. Saint Andronicus [Col. 0148C] placed his head upon the bear and provoked it to become angry and make him surrender his spirit more quickly. But the bear suddenly became as though tame.

And a little later:

> When the lioness came out, she saw the bodies of the saints lying there and came to blessed Tarachus. She lowered herself before his feet as though adoring him. Saint Tarachus drew her toward himself with his hand so that the beast might become angry and he might be perfected more quickly. But the lioness was like a sheep, etc.

Besides the natural law by which we are forbidden either to seek or to hasten our own death, there seemed also to be present a manifest indication of the divine will, which was taming the most savage beasts so that they would not harm the saints. Yet the holy martyrs, thinking of none of these things and burning only with desire for martyrdom [Col. 0149A], rushed headlong toward death—if it is permissible to speak thus. That they longed for it under the inward inspiration of the divine Spirit is evident even from the miracle. For since no one is permitted to proceed toward his own destruction unless God commands it, God would never have confirmed a rash and daring deed by miracles unless it had been undertaken by his authority.

We must hold it certain that the same Spirit impelled those who voluntarily presented themselves to tyrants or, employing greater freedom than they ought to have done, inflamed them more and more to cruelty, and whose memory the Church afterward always honored among the holy martyrs—unless we wish, at our own discretion, to expunge innumerable martyrs from the sacred calendars. From the beginning, and throughout the subsequently uninterrupted succession [Col. 0149B] of so many centuries, Perpetua held a place among these martyrs. Hence the fact that she presented her throat to the gladiator and guided the wandering sword toward herself must not be counted as a fault in her, but that noble deed must be referred to the divine Spirit by whom she was inwardly impelled.

For it is beyond controversy that Perpetua was impelled by some spirit. The only question is whether she was led by a good or an evil spirit. But since nothing worthy of censure occurs in her actions and, rather, everything was done with wisdom and virtue, it seems there can be no doubt that the Holy Spirit dwelling in her breast was the author of her visions and revelations, and that, under his inward impulse, she therefore hastened toward martyrdom.

### IX

But so that every ground for uncertainty in this matter may be removed [Col. 0149C], I wish us to examine not only the virtues of Perpetua and her fellow martyrs in general, as we have done thus far, but especially those virtues that were the fruits of the spirit dwelling in her and of her visions and revelations. For if a tree’s nature is known from its fruits, then from the fruits we shall certainly discover the nature of the spirit that was in Perpetua.

When she was baptized, she was taught by this spirit’s instruction that a Christian should seek nothing other than patience and endurance of sufferings:

> During the interval of a few days we were baptized; but the Spirit directed me to ask for nothing else in the water except endurance of the flesh.

What surer proof of a good and divine Spirit could there be than this?

Certainly Montanus’s spirit did not teach him this, perhaps even when he was baptized; for Asterius relates that, though newly baptized, Montanus burned with ungoverned ambition and an immoderate desire for first place [Note: [Col. 0149D] “There is said to be a village in Mysia bordering upon Phrygia named Ardabau. There, they say, a certain Montanus, one of those newly enrolled among the faithful, first, under Gratus, proconsul of Asia, being seized by an immoderate desire of soul for preeminence, gave the opposing spirit entrance into himself; he then began to prophesy and, suddenly falling into a kind of possession and ecstasy, began to rave, speak, and utter strange words.” In Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book V, ch. 16]:

> There is said to be a village in Mysia adjoining Phrygia named Ardabau. There, they say, a certain Montanus, one of those recently enrolled among the faithful, under Gratus, proconsul of Asia, was seized by an immoderate desire for first place [Col. 0150A], gave the adversarial spirit entrance into himself, and, filled with a demon, began to be shaken by a sudden frenzy and mental derangement, and to utter certain novel and unheard-of things, etc.

Tertullian does indeed say of his Paraclete [Note: [Col. 0150D] Book *On Flight in Persecution*, at the end] that he was the exhorter “to every kind of endurance.” But here we seek not the leaves of words but the fruits of deeds. How widely these differed from one another among the Montanists is most clearly evident by comparing this passage of Tertullian with another from Apollonius.

Tertullian speaks thus:

> And therefore the Paraclete is necessary, the guide into all truth and exhorter to every kind of endurance. Those who have received him know neither how to flee persecution nor how to buy themselves free, since they possess him who will act on our behalf—speaking during interrogation and assisting during suffering.

[Col. 0150B] But what does Apollonius say about Themison and Alexander, leading disciples of Montanus and, as they boasted, martyrs of that sect? Did he not hand down to memory in Eusebius things wholly contrary to these?

“And a little later,” says Eusebius [Note: [Col. 0150D] “Again, descending to the case of one of those whom they number among their confessors, he says: ‘Moreover, there is Themison, clothed in plausible covetousness, who did not bear the token of confession but cast off his bonds by paying a large sum of money. Although for this reason he ought thereafter to have behaved humbly, he boasted as though he were a martyr, etc. . . . And so that the facts concerning Alexander may be known to those who wish to know them: he was judged by Aemilius Frontinus, proconsul at Ephesus, not on account of the Name but because of the robberies he had dared to commit, being already a transgressor. Then, falsely invoking the Lord’s name, he was released after deceiving the faithful there. His own local church, from which he came, did not receive him because he was a robber; and those who wish to learn the facts concerning him have the public archives of Asia.’ Book V, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 18], “he says the following concerning one of those whom they number among their confessors”:

> Moreover, Themison, clothed in plausible covetousness, did not bear the token of confession but cast off his bonds by paying out a large sum of money. Although for this reason he should thereafter have conducted himself more modestly and humbly, he boasted as though he were a martyr, etc. . . . As for Alexander, so that the truth may become known to all who desire to know it, he was tried by Aemilius Frontinus, proconsul of Asia, not for the name of Christ but for the robberies he had committed, since he was already [Col. 0150C] a deserter from the faith. Then, falsely assuming the name of Christ, he was released after deceiving the faithful of that city. But the church of the place from which he originated did not receive him, because he practiced robbery. If anyone desires to know his entire affair, the public archives of Asia are available.

These are the martyrs and confessors of the Montanist sect; these are the men upon whom the Paraclete had breathed his spirit and whom Tertullian proclaims with puffed-out cheeks as knowing neither how to flee persecution nor how to buy themselves free.

The spirit of Prisca—or Priscilla—and Maximilla was likewise very far from suggesting such endurance of sufferings to his prophetesses. Concerning them we must again hear Apollonius, who, according to Eusebius [Note: [Col. 0150D–0151D] “Proceeding to the prophetesses of Montanus, he writes thus: ‘We therefore demonstrate that these very first prophetesses, from the moment they were filled with the spirit, abandoned their husbands. How greatly, then, did those lie who called Prisca a virgin!’ Then he adds: ‘Does not all Scripture seem to you to forbid a prophet to receive gifts and money? When, therefore, I see that the prophetess has received gold, silver, and costly garments, how can I fail to reject her?’ At the place cited above], speaks of Montanus’s [Col. 0151A] prophetesses in this manner:

> We therefore demonstrate, he says, that these leading prophetesses, as soon as they were filled with the Spirit, abandoned their husbands. How greatly, then, did those who called Prisca a virgin lie!

He then adds these words:

> Does not the whole of Scripture appear to you to forbid a prophet to receive gifts and money? When, therefore, I see that the prophetess has received gold and silver and costly garments, how can I fail to reject her?

These were the actual fruits of the Montanist spirit, not those which Tertullian falsely alleges in ambitious and inflated language.

### X

But we must return to Perpetua and her fellow martyrs. The fruit of the vision by which Perpetua and Saturus understood that death was imminent—and concerning which Basnage [Col. 0151B] cavils so greatly—was that disdain for all human affairs which is so necessary to candidates for martyrdom, so that they may proceed toward God with greater freedom:

> We understood, she says, that suffering was going to occur, and we began thereafter to place no hope in this world.

From the other vision, in which she saw Dinocrates in punishment, this good resulted: endowed with marvelous confidence in God, Perpetua wearied heaven with prayers and tears until she earned the removal of her brother from punishment and his transfer into rest:

> I trusted that my prayer would benefit his suffering, and I prayed for him every day until we were transferred to the military prison. . . . And I prayed for him day and night, groaning and weeping, that he might be granted to me.

Saturus too saw his own vision and committed it to writing [Col. 0151C] with his own hand. This vision likewise was not without fruit and testimonies of the divine Spirit. Through it a reconciliation was arranged between Optatus and Aspasius. Optatus was admonished to correct the people and reform their conduct. Saturus awoke and rejoiced concerning the coming suffering.

When Perpetua was being mauled in the amphitheater by the most savage cow, she was in ecstasy. After awakening, she remembered modesty rather than pain and was more concerned for her companion than for herself:

> When she saw that Felicity had been thrown down, she approached, gave her a hand, and raised her up.

Finally, she addressed her brother with these words, than which none could be more fitting for a most courageous and holy martyr:

> Stand firm in the faith, and all love one another; and do not be scandalized by our sufferings.

If, as has been said repeatedly, we must recognize and judge the Spirit by which a prophet was inspired from the fruit of visions and revelations, just as we recognize the nature of a tree from its fruits [Col. 0151D], who will any longer doubt that Perpetua was filled with the Holy Spirit, and that this divine Spirit was for her the cause and abundant source of heavenly revelations?

For an evil spirit would not have introduced into her such visions as greatly inflamed her to endure martyrdom, to despise the world, and to perform acts of every Christian virtue; visions from which there sprang the most abundant harvest of faith, hope, charity, reverence, modesty, and patience [Col. 0152A]; from which nothing flowed that was not holy, and nothing, finally, that was not altogether joined to the benefit of the Church.

> “Do people gather grapes from thorns, or figs from thistles? Thus every good tree bears good fruit, but a bad tree bears bad fruit. A good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit” (Matthew, ch. VII, v. 16 and following).
