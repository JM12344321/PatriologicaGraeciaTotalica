Yet I pass over the origin of Caelus and Terra. They were unmarried and childless for a long time before they became spouses and parents. They plainly had to grow through a long age to such stature. [Col. 0601C] Finally, at the same time the voice of Caelus began to grow insolent and Terra’s breasts to become stony, they married one another. I suppose either Caelus descended to his bride or Terra ascended to her bridegroom!

Terra nevertheless conceived by Caelus and bore Athos, and Athos bore Saturn—by a marvelous process. Which of his parents did he resemble? Yet she did give birth. Certainly before Saturn they produced no one, except afterward one Ops; then procreation ceased. Saturn, for his part, castrated Caelus while he slept. We read that Caelus was of the masculine gender; otherwise how could he be a father unless he were male? But how was the castration to be performed? He had a sickle: this . . l . . . For Vulcan, the worker in iron, did not yet exist.

Terra, though widowed while still of youthful age, postponed marrying another. Nor did Caelus have . . . . . Yet the Sea embraces her, but smells of brine . . . . accustomed to waters. Thus [Col. 0601D] Saturn was the only male child of Caelus and Terra. But when he reached puberty, he was joined to his sister. There were not yet any laws prohibiting incest or punishing parricide. And so he devoured his male children—better he than wolves, if he had exposed them; plainly he feared that one of them might have learned from his father’s sickle.

When Jupiter was soon born and removed from him, Saturn swallowed a stone pretending to be the infant. Secure for a long time through this device, he was finally overcome and deprived of his kingdom by the son who, having not yet been digested, had grown to maturity in darkness. Caelus and Terra, with the poets acting as midwives, produced this patriarch of your gods for you.

But certain people think themselves clever when, by allegorical argument, they interpret Saturn in terms of natural philosophy: that he is Time, and that Caelus and Terra are therefore his parents, since they themselves have no parents; that he therefore bears a sickle because all things are cut apart by time; and that he therefore devours his own children because he consumes back into himself everything produced from himself. They also summon the testimony of his name: that he is called *Cronius* in Greek, as though *Chronos*. Likewise they derive the Latin word from sowings . . . . so that those who conjecture that he is the progenitor say that through him the seeds of heaven are carried down into the earth. They join Ops to him because seeds bring the aid of seeing and because seeds emerge through work.

What, I ask, do you make of this transference . . . .? Either Saturn existed, or Time. How is Saturn . . . . how Time? For you cannot make him both: corporeal in the one respect . . . . sea. What prevented Time from being worshiped in its own quality? . . . . of a human being, or the fable of a human being, or the servant of a human being in its own form, not in the form of Time . . . . What is the purpose of this interpretation except to color disgraceful materials with counterfeit arguments? Saturn is not therefore . . d . . . Time; nor, when you make him Time, do you now wish him not to have been a human being.

Plainly . . . . Saturn is recorded in the ancient accounts as having lived on earth with human attributes. Anything that never existed at all can be imagined as incorporeal; where there is truth, there is no room for invention. But since it is agreed that Saturn lived, you transform him in vain. You are not permitted to defend as either a god or Time one whom you will not deny to have been a human being.

The record of Saturn survives everywhere in your literature. We read it in Cassius Severus, in Cornelius Nepos and Tacitus, and among the Greeks also in Diodorus and the other collectors of hoary antiquity. No more trustworthy traces . . . . than those marked in Italy itself. For after many lands and Attic sojourns, he settled in Italy—or Oenotria, as it was then called—having been received by Janus, or Janes, as the Salii [Note: “[Col. 0602D] Rigaltius’s edition: ‘as others call him.’ Compare *Apology* 10: ‘as the Salii maintain.’”] call him. The mountain he had inhabited was called Saturnian; [Col. 0602D] the city whose boundaries he had marked out remains Saturnia to this day; finally, all Italy [Note: “[Col. 0602D] *Finally, all Italy.* That Italy was called Saturnia is clear from Ennius:

With such a witness as the land that now rules the world, even if Saturn’s origin is disputed, it is nevertheless agreed from his conduct that he was a human being. Thus, if Saturn was a man, he was undoubtedly born from [a human being]—indeed, precisely because he was a man, he was certainly not born from heaven and earth. [Note: “[Col. 0603D] Thus restored here from *Apology* 10.”] But since his parents were unknown, it was easy for some to call him the son of those whose child everyone may be thought to be. For who, out of reverence, does not call heaven and earth father and mother? Or was it because of the human custom by which unknown persons who appear unexpectedly are said to have come from heaven?

Accordingly . . . . always needy . . . . suddenly [Note: “[Col. 0603D] Havercamp corrects from *Apology* 10: ‘Accordingly, since Saturn was always a stranger and appeared suddenly everywhere,’ etc.”], the custom everywhere arose of calling him heaven-born. For we also commonly proclaim people of uncertain ancestry “sons of the earth.” [Note: “[Col. 0603D] *We proclaim them sons of the earth.* Those whose origin is unknown or uncertain are called both Sons of the Earth and [non-Latin text omitted]. LE PR.”]

I say nothing of the condition of antiquity, in which . . . uncultivated [Note: “[Col. 0603D] Read: ‘in which the eyes and minds of human beings were so uncultivated at that time that’—others read ‘and’—etc.”] were the eyes and minds of human beings at that time that they were moved as though by something divine at the sight of any unfamiliar man, much more of a king, and indeed the first king.

I shall linger still upon Saturn, because I shall thereby prepare a summary treatment of the others, once the discussion of their beginnings has been satisfied. Nor shall I omit the more important testimony of the divine writings, to which greater credence is due because of their superior antiquity. For the Sibyl existed before all literature. I mean that Sibyl [Note: “[Col. 0604D] *I mean that Sibyl.* Long afterward Juvenal and countless others with him said that Sibyl was genuine and truthful:

In a verse of six feet she sets forth, to this effect, the lineage of Saturn and his deeds: “In the tenth generation of humankind after the flood befell those of former times, Saturn, Titan, and Japhet reigned, the most powerful sons of Terra and Caelus.” If therefore any credibility attaches to writings even older than your . . . . authors, and for that very reason closer to . . . . of that age, it attaches to this account.
