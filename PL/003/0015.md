### V

But more compelling than all the arguments we have produced thus far is the one drawn from the dignity and antiquity of the basilica [Col. 0067C] that Perpetua and Felicity possessed at Carthage. For what appears to have been the most celebrated and ancient of all the churches of Carthage—and, as it is now called, its mother church—is designated in the ancient records sometimes as the “restored basilica” [Note: [Col. 0067D] In the Acts of the Second Council of Carthage; in Augustine, *Sermon* 19; and in Victor of Vita, book I, *On the Vandal Persecution*], sometimes as the “basilica of the ancestors” [Note: [Col. 0067D] In Augustine, *On the Proceedings of Pelagius*, chap. 11, and *Sermon* 294], sometimes as the “greater basilica” [Note: [Col. 0067D] In the same author, *Sermon* 258, and in Victor of Vita, at the place cited above], and sometimes as “at the ancestors” [Note: [Col. 0067D] In Augustine, *Sermon* 34]. Since it was the greater church and was frequented by large gatherings of the people of Carthage, many of Augustine’s sermons delivered at Carthage were preached there. In it the Second Council of Carthage was held under Genethlius in the year 390, as were the subsequent synods of Carthage.

I think that St. Optatus of Milevis should be understood as referring to this same basilica when, speaking of the schismatic ordination of Majorinus, he writes [Note: [Col. 0067D] Book I, *Against Parmenian*]:

> Meanwhile [Col. 0067D] none of those mentioned above came to the basilica where the whole city had assembled in great numbers with Caecilian.

And shortly afterward:

> At that time either he—the accused Caecilian—ought to have been expelled, or communion ought to have been maintained with him as innocent. The church was thronged with people; the episcopal chair was occupied; the altar [Col. 0068A] stood in its proper place, upon which the peaceable bishops of earlier times—Cyprian, Lucian, and the others—had made their offerings. Thus they went outside, and altar was erected against altar, etc.

It is of this basilica, I say—whether called “of the ancestors” or “restored”—that I believe St. Optatus is speaking here, because Victor of Vita recorded [Note: [Col. 0068D] Book I, *On the African Persecution*] that the seat and residence of the bishops of Carthage had always been there. Moreover, the highly authoritative Victor of Vita bears witness that the bodies of Perpetua and Felicity had been laid in this church, which we may most justly establish as at once the most celebrated and the most ancient of all the churches of Carthage [Note: [Col. 0068D] Ibid.]:

> The greater basilica, where the bodies of the holy martyrs Perpetua and Felicity are buried . . . the Vandals, with tyrannical license, subjected to their own religion.

Indeed, it may be inferred with no little clarity from the acts of the Second Council of Carthage that this same [Col. 0068B] basilica had been named after Perpetua, since that council is said to have been held “in the restored basilica Perpetua”—perhaps *Perpetuae*, “of Perpetua.” I regard this as the clearest evidence of a most ancient and celebrated cult and of their orthodoxy.

No one with even a passing acquaintance with ecclesiastical history is unaware that it was a most ancient custom in the Church, almost coeval with the Christian religion itself, for the Catholic people to assemble at the memorials of the martyrs, to frequent those places, and to venerate with religious devotion the sites where their sacred remains had been laid. Concerning the Roman monuments in which the sacred bodies of the Apostles had been deposited, this is explicitly attested by Caius, a contemporary of Zephyrinus and therefore also of Tertullian, Perpetua, [Col. 0068C] and Felicity:

> I can show the memorial-trophies of the Apostles. For whether you choose to go to the Vatican or to the Ostian Way, you will encounter the memorial-trophies of those who founded that church.

[Note: [Col. 0068D] Greek: Ἐγὼ δὲ τὰ τρόπαια τῶν ἀποστόλων ἔχω δεῖξαι· ἐὰν γὰρ θελήσῃς ἀπελθεῖν ἐπὶ τὸν βατικανὸν, ἢ ἐπὶ τὴν ὁδὸν, τὴν Ὠστίαν, εὑρήσεις τὰ τρόπαια τῶν ταύτην ἱδρυσαμένων τὴν ἐκκλησίαν. *Disputation against Proclus*, in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, chap. 25.]

It is also established that, when religion subsequently flourished in those same places and the Church obtained respite from persecution, basilicas and sacred buildings dedicated to religious uses were constructed there. Finally, ancient records establish—and reason itself suggests—that the more celebrated and distinguished basilicas were built for the more eminent and illustrious martyrs, or at least that the remains (*λείψανα*) of the more celebrated and distinguished martyrs were transferred to the more celebrated and distinguished basilicas. [Col. 0068D] Above all, bishops took care that the greater churches should be adorned with the relics of the principal martyrs. This was done both for the greater glory of the martyrs themselves, for the splendor of the churches, and, finally, because the Christian people gathered more frequently at the mother churches, so that, attracted by devotion to the principal martyrs, they might assemble there in still greater numbers. Since, therefore, we learn from Victor [Col. 0069A] of Vita that the bodies of Perpetua and Felicity were brought into the greater church of Carthage, we must not doubt that, before being brought there, they had been numbered among the more illustrious martyrs.

But the date of the translation must be ascertained so that the antiquity of their cult may also be established. In the year 390, when the Second Council of Carthage was held, the greater church of Carthage was, as we have seen, designated by Perpetua’s name. We must therefore date the translation of the sacred pledges—their relics—earlier than that year. The Bucherian Calendar was compiled, as we have said, under Liberius. In it, as in all the martyrologies, the feast of Perpetua and Felicity is assigned to the Nones of March, March 7. But the distinguished Pagi proves by the following argument that this was the day not of their martyrdom, but of their translation [Note: [Col. 0069D] Under the year 198, no. V].

The holy martyrs suffered on the day on which the birthday [Col. 0069B] of Geta Caesar was being celebrated:

> We were to fight in the military games. It was then the birthday of Geta Caesar.

And below:

> Why, then, do you not permit the condemned to take refreshment, since they are to fight on the birthday of the Most Noble Caesar?

Yet neither the anniversary of the imperial dignity nor Geta’s actual birthday fell on March 7. For Geta was born, according to Spartianus [Note: [Col. 0069D] *Geta*, chap. 3], on the sixth day before the Kalends of June, May 27; while he was invested with the rank of Caesar in the year 198, no earlier than April 13.

> And the reason is, says Pagi, that, as we shall shortly see, Caracalla obtained the tribunician power and the rank of Augustus from his father after that date; and it is not likely that Geta, the younger son, was styled Augustus by his father before him. Therefore, either both appointments were made at the same time after the month of March, or Caracalla was styled Augustus before Geta [Col. 0069C] was styled Caesar. Consequently, Sts. Perpetua and Felicity are venerated in the martyrologies on a day other than that on which they suffered. We shall observe throughout this work that this happened not infrequently; March 7 is the day on which their relics were subsequently transferred to Carthage, not the day of their death.

Since Liberius began his episcopate around the middle of the fourth century, the translation of their sacred relics had already been performed by that time. We may therefore most justly conclude that this translation was solemnly carried out not long after the beginning of the fourth century, once peace had been restored to the Church by Constantine; and that, when only a century had elapsed since their martyrdom, Perpetua and Felicity were regarded as principal martyrs, while the people of Carthage gathered in very great numbers at their memorials.

[Col. 0069D] We have seen how the celebrity and antiquity of the cult of our martyrs is demonstrated by the translation of their bodies into the greater church of Carthage. It remains to be seen how clearly their orthodoxy is confirmed from the same principle.

As the most illustrious Albaspinaeus notes [Note: [Col. 0069D] In the notes on Optatus, book I, *Against Parmenian*], the bishop’s church, residence, chair, and especially altar were the center of ecclesiastical communion. The Catholic people gathered there in very great numbers; from there the bishop addressed the people; there he received their prayers, offerings, and vows. This was the place of prayer, the place of the sacraments, [Col. 0070A] and, finally, the place of the solemn sacrifice. The other altars, as Albaspinaeus likewise observes, were erected as auxiliaries to the communion whose center, as has been said, was held to be the bishop’s church and altar.

However numerous the Catholics who communicated at this altar, no schismatic or heretic was permitted access to it. Indeed, those who had fallen away from the communion of the Catholic Church were expelled from it even by force, lest, because of the sanctity and grandeur of the place in which the ancients had established the stronghold of Catholic communion, they might be regarded as Catholics. Evidence of this may be found in what Eusebius relates concerning Paul of Samosata: namely, that although this wicked heresiarch had been deprived of authority by the judgment of a synod and deposed from the bishopric of Antioch, he could by no means be driven from the episcopal residence, seat, and church [Col. 0070B] for as long as Zenobia, whose favor and patronage he enjoyed, held power in the East. For he believed that he should be regarded as the legitimate bishop, and that he could retain the Catholic people for himself, for as long as he occupied the residence, chair, basilica, and altar where his predecessors had customarily sat, assembled the congregation, received the people’s offerings and vows, and offered sacrifices to God.

The Catholic bishops, however, strove with every effort and exertion to have this most wicked man expelled and Domnus, who had been appointed in his place, take possession of the episcopal basilica and be installed in the seat and chair of his predecessors. After Zenobia’s defeat, this was accomplished with the aid of Aurelian, who, as Eusebius says [Note: [Col. 0070D] Greek: Βασιλεὺς ἐντευχθεὶς Αὐρηλιανὸς αἰσιώτατα περὶ τοῦ πρακτέου διείληφε· τούτοις νεῖμαι προστάττων τὸν οἶκον, οἷς ἂν οἱ κατὰ τὴν Ἰταλίαν καὶ τὴν Ῥωμαίων πόλιν ἐπίσκοποι τοῦ δόγματος ἐπιστέλλοιεν. “When petitioned, the emperor Aurelian decided most properly what should be done, ordering the building to be assigned to those to whom the bishops in Italy and the city of Rome should write concerning doctrine.” Book VII, *Ecclesiastical History*, chap. 30], when petitioned by the bishops, judged the matter most properly, ordering the residence to be given [Col. 0070C] to those with whom the Christian bishops of Italy and the bishop of Rome corresponded.

Since this is so, everyone, I think, can readily understand from this argument how fixed, constant, and certain was the judgment among the ancients that our martyrs had died in the peace and communion of the Church, since they wished their bodies to be brought to the place where the people of Carthage assembled as though at the center and stronghold of Catholic communion. The bones of the principal martyrs were laid beneath that principal altar of the bishop so that Catholics assembling there might understand that they participated in the communion not only of other believers, but also of the holy martyrs. Conversely, schismatics and heretics, for whom no access to that sanctuary of piety lay open, were to know that they had no communion either with the Catholic Church [Col. 0070D] or with the holy martyrs.

Hence St. Optatus of Milevis denounces the Donatists as schismatics on the ground that they by no means shared communion at the memorials of the holy Apostles in the City of Rome [Note: [Col. 0070D] Book II, *Against Parmenian*]:

> Finally, if Macrobius—the Donatists’ pseudo-bishop in the City—were asked where he sits there, can he possibly say, “In the chair of Peter”? I do not know whether he is even acquainted with it by sight; and he does not approach Peter’s memorial, behaving as a schismatic in opposition to the Apostle, who says [Note: [Col. 0070D] Romans 12:13]: [Col. 0071A] “Sharing communion in the memorials of the saints.” Behold, the memorials of two Apostles are present there. Tell us whether he was able to enter them in such a way as to make an offering there, where it is established that the memorials of the saints are located.

Among the ancients, therefore, it was so well established that Perpetua and Felicity had had nothing in common with heretics while they lived, and that they had consummated their martyrdom in the peace and communion of the Church, that scarcely a century after their martyrdom their sacred relics were brought to the very place from which heretics were most strictly prohibited and where no one communicated unless Catholic.

### VI

Finally, arguments not unlike the preceding ones [Col. 0071B] may be constructed for the antiquity and celebrity of the cult of our martyrs, and for their orthodoxy, from the continually repeated commemoration of them during the daily sacrifices. For in the Gelasian Missal, Gregory’s Sacramentary, and, finally, the most ancient Gallican Sacramentary [Note: [Col. 0071D] In Mabillon, vol. I of the *Museum Italicum*], Perpetua and Felicity are commemorated daily in the canon of the Mass together with a few other martyrs.

Everyone is familiar with the testimonies of the earliest Fathers, particularly Tertullian [Note: [Col. 0071D] *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chap. 3] and Cyprian [Note: [Col. 0071D] Epistle XII, otherwise XXXVII, and Epistle XXXIX, otherwise XXXIV], in which they explicitly testify that the martyrs’ anniversaries were celebrated by a special commemoration of them during the sacrifices. But whether the practice of early times prescribed that, in addition to the annual celebration of the martyrs upon their anniversary, the names either of all of them or of the more eminent ones should be recited from the sacred diptychs during the daily sacrifices is not so well established from ancient testimony.

Nevertheless, St. Cyprian appears to indicate this [Col. 0071C] in Epistle XXXIX to Pam., no. 30. After speaking of Celerinus’s grandmother Celerina, his paternal uncle, and his maternal uncles Laurentinus and Ignatius, who had long since been crowned with martyrdom, he adds:

> We always offer sacrifices for them, as you remember, whenever we celebrate the sufferings and anniversary days of the martyrs with an annual commemoration.

By these words Cyprian plainly indicates that whenever the anniversary solemnity of any martyr recurred, mention of those named above was always renewed during the solemnities of the sacrifices.

It might very well have happened that, when the number of martyrs was not great, just as none of them lacked an annual solemnity—not only the more eminent and those who had undergone more atrocious tortures, but also the more obscure, including those who, having endured no torments, died in prison for confessing the Name, as the same Cyprian prescribed [Note: [Col. 0071D] Epistle XII, otherwise XXXVII]—so at first all of them were commemorated in the daily sacrifices. Later, however, when their number had increased beyond measure, only the principal martyrs, as the ancient calendars teach us, and those whose fame was more illustrious, were retained for the celebration of annual sacrifices and offerings. Likewise, although at first all were commemorated in the daily sacrifices, subsequently, as is established from liturgies that were indeed very ancient, though later than the third century, the names only of the more illustrious were retained in the daily Masses.

But whatever the practice [Col. 0072A] of the more ancient period may have been, no one doubts that those whose memory is renewed daily in the canon of the Mass were, both formerly and now, reckoned among the more illustrious martyrs. Consequently, this proves that Perpetua and Felicity, who received this honor from our ancestors in the most ancient liturgies together with a few others, were numbered not merely among the martyrs, but among the more eminent martyrs.

Nor should it pass without notice here—as was also remarked above—that the liturgies or sacramentaries in which Perpetua and Felicity are commemorated every day belonged not to the Church of Carthage or to other African churches, but to the Roman and Gallican churches. This demonstrates still more forcefully how renowned [Col. 0072B] our martyrs had been from ancient times.

In former times no martyrs were commemorated in the canon of the Roman Mass except Roman martyrs, if we exclude the Apostles and Ignatius, an apostolic man who also suffered at Rome, as well as our martyrs and Cyprian. For Aldhelm writes that Agatha and Lucy, Sicilian martyrs, were added to the canon by Gregory the Great [Note: [Col. 0072D] *On Virginity*, chap. 25]. Cyprian’s distinguished services to the Roman Church, as we say, secured this honor for him. But from what source did our martyrs receive this special prerogative, so that although they were foreigners, strangers, and from a province beyond the sea, their memory was nevertheless celebrated daily at Rome during the sacred mysteries? From what, I ask, did they receive this distinction, unless from that celebrity of fame by which they were always upon people’s lips and were regarded as eminent martyrs? [Col. 0072C] It is certainly inconsistent with such celebrated fame that, within so brief an interval of time, the religion to which they had adhered while alive should have vanished from human memory.

Finally, it must likewise be observed here that this commemoration of the holy martyrs within the sacred synaxis is principally intended to make us understand that we share communion with them in Christ, whose body is offered in sacrifice, as the very words of the canon explicitly attest:

> Sharing communion, and venerating the memory, etc.

Moreover, that commemoration of martyrs from other regions must be understood as intended to make us know that the Catholic Church is everywhere one, and that all churches, however distant in place, possess one communion, since they [Col. 0072D] venerate foreign martyrs no less than their own. As observed above, this clearly and plainly demonstrates that the ancients were so certain of the orthodoxy of our martyrs that they wished their names to serve as a symbol of Catholic communion.

### VII

Basnage thinks he can diminish the authority of all the testimonies we have thus far discussed with this very trifling reply: “The steadfastness of these martyrs, together with the antiquity of the Acts, deceived posterity, so that they came to be held in esteem.” Then, after advancing the conjecture concerning Augustine’s sermon that I refuted above, he adds:

> Let it nevertheless be granted that these are Augustine’s words. The question is one of fact: whether Perpetua [Col. 0073A] was a woman of the Church or of the Montanist sect. Now, in questions of this kind, good men very often go entirely astray, and we have demonstrated in the proper place that heretical martyrs have slipped into the martyrologies.

The Acts and their authority must be discussed below. For the present we must briefly consider whether it is not the height of rashness, on account of the very slight conjectures that I shall hereafter expose, to call into doubt the orthodoxy and sanctity of persons whom, it is established, the universal Church venerated immediately after their passion and thereafter, for fifteen centuries and more, with every mark of religious devotion and reverence—not merely as genuine martyrs, but as among the more celebrated martyrs of Christ. Their memory, indeed, the Church frequented from ancient times; for them it erected altars and basilicas that were centers of Catholic [Col. 0073B] communion; their annual solemnity was celebrated not only by the Church of Carthage but also by the Roman Church in the second century after their passion; their names are recorded among those of the more illustrious martyrs in the most ancient ecclesiastical calendars; their memory, together with that of a few other similarly distinguished martyrs, is renewed daily in the canon of the Mass; and, finally, they are celebrated not merely by one or another private martyrology, but by all of them alike, including those compiled for the use of different churches. In the present chapter I have now sufficiently demonstrated that all these honors together were accorded to our martyrs.

But “the steadfastness of the martyrs and the antiquity of the Acts deceived posterity.” Could they also have deceived writers who were almost contemporary or only slightly later?

But “in such questions of fact good men are often mistaken.” Could the authority of the universal Church also be mistaken? Augustine deferred so greatly to that authority even in questions of this kind that he refused rashly to pass judgment upon women who, in time of persecution, threw themselves into a river that would sweep them away and kill them, in order to escape those who pursued their chastity, and who died in that manner, while the celebrations of their martyrdom are most renowned in the veneration of the Catholic Church:

> Concerning these women, [Col. 0073C] I dare make no rash judgment. For I do not know whether divine authority persuaded the Church through certain trustworthy testimonies that it should honor their memory in this way; and it is possible that this is so. For what if they did this not through human deception or error, but in obedience? Just as we are not permitted to believe anything else concerning Samson.

[Note: [Col. 0073D] Book I, *The City of God*, chap. 26.]

But heretical martyrs have slipped into the martyrologies. Did they also slip into the most ancient calendars? Were sacrifices and Catholic assemblies also celebrated at their monuments? Were altars to God and basilicas consecrated in their memory? Did the ancient Fathers adorn their praises in speeches and public sermons? Finally, have they been commemorated among the more illustrious martyrs in the canon of the Mass for so many years, even to the present day?

Rabanus was the first, in the ninth century, imprudently to introduce Marculus, the Donatists’ pseudo-martyr, into his martyrology [Note: [Col. 0073D] In Mabillon, vol. IV of the *Veteres Analecta*]. From there he slipped into the spurious martyrology of Bede, and from it into several manuscripts and editions of Usuard’s martyrology [Note: [Col. 0074D] In the Bollandist Fathers, vol. VII for the month of June]. But the error was easily detected, and the compilers of the Roman Martyrology substituted Nicomedia for Numidia, Marcellus for Marculus, Constantius for Constans, and Arians for the Macarians.

For, on reading the name of the Nicomedian martyr Marcellus in more ancient martyrologies under approximately the same date, and being ignorant of his acts, they composed for him from the interpolated acts of Marculus this encomium:

> At Nicomedia—instead of “in Numidia”—St. Marcellus—read “Marculus”—a priest who, in the time of Constantius—more correctly, “Constans”—was cast down from a cliff by the Arians—instead of “by the Macarians”—and died as a martyr.

This is the conjecture of the Benedictine Fathers in their notes on Augustine’s book III *Against Cresconius*, chap. 49. On this matter, the distinguished Mabillon should also be consulted in his annotations on the Acts of Marculus, vol. III of the *Veteres Analecta*.

[Col. 0074B] But what does this example—or any other example like it—have in common with our martyrs in any respect? They did not slip into some private martyrology in later centuries through the carelessness of one or another writer. Rather, from the beginning they received a place in the Church’s public and most ancient calendars, in her public prayers through the recitation of their names, and in every form of testimony to religious veneration.

But let us see by what arguments Basnage has attempted to dislodge the holy martyrs from what may be called their perpetual possession of this most sacred reputation and public cult. For the arguments that, after so many centuries since their passion, led him to form this opinion of our martyrs must be exceedingly weighty. [Col. 0074C] Faith in the unanimous testimonies of the ancients and in the Church’s perpetual tradition concerning their orthodoxy and sanctity ought not to have been rejected in a matter of such gravity unless invincible and manifest arguments imposed a different opinion upon an almost unwilling mind. Whether Basnage’s arguments are of that kind we shall discuss in the following chapters.

## CHAPTER II  
## BASNAGE’S EXTERNAL ARGUMENTS AGAINST THE HOLY MARTYRS ARE ANSWERED

I. The compiler of the Acts of Perpetua and Felicity was infected with the heresy of the Montanists. II. Ruinart’s arguments in defense of the compiler of the Acts are refuted, and his Montanism is established by various arguments. [Col. 0074D] III. Dodwell’s argument for the orthodoxy of the same writer. IV. The first foundation of his argument is demolished. V. The second foundation of the same argument is overturned. VI. The Montanists did not regard Catholics as heretics. VII. The Montanists did not consider themselves entirely estranged from the communion of the Catholic churches. VIII. They aspired also to communion with the Catholic martyrs. IX. Tertullian commemorated with praise not only Perpetua, but other Catholic martyrs as well. X. Heretics other than the Montanists published the Acts of Catholic martyrs. XI. From the author of the Acts himself, a Montanist, it is shown that our martyrs were alien to that sect.

### I

[Col. 0075A] Concerning the author—or rather, as he prefers to appear, the compiler—of the Acts of Perpetua and Felicity, scholars do not hold one and the same opinion. Valesius, a man as candid as he was learned, to use Basnage’s words, formerly judged that he belonged to the Montanist party [Note: [Col. 0075D] Preface to the Paris edition of the Acts]. Dodwell, on the contrary, not only removes from him every suspicion of Montanism, but even contends that the man burned with zeal and ardor for the Catholic faith against the Montanists [Note: [Col. 0075D] Fourth Cyprianic Dissertation, *On Visions*, etc., § 8 ff. See the end of this volume]. The distinguished Ruinart can by no means agree with Valesius [Note: [Col. 0075D] In the prefatory notice to the Acts, no. 6]. Basnage, on the other hand, willingly professes his adherence to Valesius’s opinion and derives from it, as we shall see later, his first argument against our martyrs.

The matter plainly demands that we discuss this entire controversy with the greatest possible care, lest, if we embrace Valesius’s opinion, we should be thought to have done so rashly; or, if we reject it, lest that rejection appear to have arisen from the prejudice of some preconceived opinion. Therefore, so that the reader may recognize that I am dealing candidly with him and bring nothing prejudged to this inquiry, I profess that I am strongly inclined toward Valesius’s opinion, although at first sight it is rather inconvenient for my own case.

[Col. 0075C] My evidence is the author’s preface, in which he uses words that, although they might be interpreted in a Catholic sense if they had been written at another time, nevertheless, when the period in which they were written is considered, arouse in us a powerful suspicion of Montanism. He writes as follows:

> But let those see to it who judge the one power of the one Holy Spirit according to the successive ages of time, although all the more recent manifestations, precisely because they are more recent, should be considered the greater, in accordance with the abundance of grace appointed for the final periods of the age [Note: [Col. 0075D] Joel 2:28 and Acts 2:17]. “For in the last days,” says the Lord, “I will pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh, and their sons and daughters shall prophesy; upon my male and female servants I will pour out of my Spirit; the young shall see visions, and the old shall dream dreams.” Therefore we also, who acknowledge and honor the new visions as having been promised, just as the prophecies were, etc.

Who could believe that a Catholic writer could have let such words escape him [Col. 0075D] at a time when the Montanist heresy was raging most violently in the Church—when the heretics so greatly vaunted and extolled the condition of the last times as reserved for their Paraclete because of the abundance of charisms and the overflowing of the Spirit; when the Church professed that it rejected new visions and prophecies, while those who acknowledged and honored them were regarded as heretics?

> Since all the more recent manifestations, precisely because they are more recent, should be considered the greater, in accordance with the abundance of grace appointed for the final periods of the age.

Could any avowed Montanist ever have spoken more bluntly or shamelessly? Is any egg, as the saying goes, more like another egg than these words of the author are like the following words of Tertullian near the end of his book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*?

> But against these dispositions of unbelief and perversity, almighty God, pouring forth by his most provident grace in the last days from his Spirit upon all flesh, upon his male and female servants, both revived the struggling faith in the resurrection of the flesh and purified the ancient instruments from every obscurity of ambiguity by manifest illuminations of words and meanings. [Col. 0076A] For because there had to be heresies, so that all those who are approved might be made manifest, and because these heresies could not have ventured forth without some occasions offered by the Scriptures, the ancient instruments therefore seem to have supplied them with certain materials—although materials capable of refuting them by means of the same writings. [Col. 0076B] But because it was no longer fitting for the Holy Spirit to refrain from overflowing also with such utterances as would scatter no seeds for any subtlety of the heretics, but would even uproot their old clods, he has therefore now dispelled all former ambiguities, and what they wish to call parables, through the open and transparent proclamation of the entire sacrament, by means of the new prophecy overflowing from the Paraclete. If you drink from its fountains, you will be able to thirst for no doctrine; no fever of questions will scorch you; by drinking everywhere from it, you will also cool your thirst concerning the resurrection of the flesh.

He sets the latest things before the ancient: that is, the new revelations of Montanus before the ancient instruments, namely the books of both Testaments—not, as Rhenanus and Pamelius interpret him, the books of the New Covenant before the Mosaic and prophetic writings. [Col. 0076C] For the heretics drew material for their doctrines not only from the Law and the Prophets, but especially from certain obscure passages, ambiguities, and parables of the New Testament.

Tertullian teaches that God in his most provident grace had provided against this by pouring forth from his Spirit upon all flesh in the last days, so that the Spirit might overflow with utterances of a kind that would supply no matter or seeds for any subtlety of the heretics, and might dispel all former ambiguities and parables through an open and transparent proclamation of the whole sacrament. He then adds that this was accomplished “through the new prophecy overflowing from the Paraclete.” Accordingly, just as the books of the New Testament surpass the legal and [Col. 0076D] prophetic writings by a more open and transparent revelation of heavenly realities, through which the ancient figures were explained, so the prophecies of Montanus—which dispelled even the ambiguities and parables of the Gospel itself through an open proclamation of the whole sacrament—were to be preferred even to the books of the New Covenant themselves.

The compiler of the Acts certainly insinuated the same doctrine in fewer, but no less explicit, words when he said that “all the more recent manifestations should be considered the greater, precisely because they are more recent, in accordance with the abundance of grace,” etc.; that is, as Tertullian puts it, in accordance with “the new prophecy overflowing from the Paraclete.”

### II

[Col. 0077A] Ruinart replies that, although these words appear at first sight to savor of the errors of Montanus, if they are examined somewhat more carefully, they contain nothing except what is orthodox and entirely consonant with the Catholic faith. For, he says, this author compares the most recent visions not with the canonical Scriptures, as Valesius supposed, but only with the visions and revelations of the ancient martyrs; and he asserts that the events that occurred in his own time should be held in no less veneration than those that had occurred in earlier times, that is, in the first or second century of the Church. Indeed, he says that these new revelations should in some measure be received with greater zeal, since examples are all the more effective the more recent they are; and furthermore, because these latest visions, which were themselves destined one day to be ancient, needed to be supported by some authority so that posterity might believe them.

Ruinart says that this is evident from the same author’s entire preface, in which there is not even one word comparing Sacred Scripture with the martyrs’ visions:

> If ancient examples [Col. 0077B] of the faith were committed to writing so that, by reading them as though by reenacting the events, God might be honored and man strengthened, why should not new testimonies, equally suited to both purposes, also be set down in writing?

But the author did not advance only the reasons cited by Ruinart for wishing the new revelations and prophecies to be esteemed above the ancient. He advanced especially the one reason that no one but a thoroughgoing Montanist would have produced, and that sufficiently shows that he preferred the latest revelations not only to the revelations of the ancient martyrs, but even to the canonical Scriptures themselves: namely, “because of the abundance of grace appointed for the final periods of the age,” in accordance with Joel’s prophecy.

For who in his right mind ever dreamed that the grace of the Holy Spirit abounded more in these latest days—that is, at the beginning of the third century [Col. 0077C]—than in the first or second century? Who, unless carried away by the spirit of Montanus, would ever have used the words of Joel, by which this future overflowing of the Spirit in the last days was foretold, to demonstrate this claim?

Therefore, this author is not to be blamed only for the reason Ruinart supposed: that he regarded the visions of these martyrs as belonging among those that God had promised through Joel. For I do not deny that the cited words of Joel can refer to all the charisms dispensed by God under our Testament. Rather, he betrays himself as a Montanist because he employed the prophet’s words to demonstrate a greater abundance of heavenly charisms in the latest days than in the preceding centuries of the Church.

[Col. 0077D] Moreover, although—as Ruinart likewise adds—the Church has by no means reckoned the spoken or written words of the faithful among the canonical Scriptures, even when it does not doubt that they proceeded from the one by whom varieties of tongues are given to some, prophecies to others, the word of wisdom to others, and different gifts to still others, gifts that were assuredly foretold and promised in the cited testimony of Joel, it nevertheless does not follow that the same judgment should likewise be made concerning the writer of the Acts. For although neither the Church nor Catholic writers ever doubted that the charisms of the New Testament both proceeded from God and were foretold and promised in the cited testimony of Joel, nevertheless neither the Church nor any Catholic ever used Joel’s words to secure greater veneration and authority for the visions and revelations of the latest times than for those of the preceding centuries [Col. 0078A] of the Church.
