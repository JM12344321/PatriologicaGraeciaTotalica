We turn the horns of the sail-clad yardarms about. Servius on *Aeneid* V: “Horns: the extremities of the yardarms.” RIG.] On a ship’s yardarm, which forms part of a cross, the extremities are called “horns.” [Note: [Col. 0627B] Semler adds *hujus* (“of this”).] But the unicorn is the central stake of the upright. [Note: [Col. 0627C] “But the unicorn is the central stake of the upright.” This is how Pamelius edited the text. Rhenanus had printed, “But the unicorn is a stake in the central upright.” And thus it is written in the Dijon manuscript. But these words make no good sense. Those who inspected the Fulda manuscript attest that they read there, “But the unicorn is the stake secured in the middle.” This offers some appearance of truth: the “horns” would be the extremities of the cross, or of the crossbeam, to which the hands were nailed or bound; while the unicorn would be the stake secured in the middle, or the upright, which in Book I *To the Nations* is called the projecting seat. RIG.] Finally, by this power of the cross, and horned in this manner, he even now winnows all nations through faith, carrying them from earth into heaven; and then, through judgment, he will winnow them by casting them down from heaven to earth. [Note: [Col. 0627B] “Descending,” Oehler? [Obert.] and Semler.]

He will likewise be the bull elsewhere in the same Scripture, when Jacob, in extending his blessing to Simeon and Levi, prophesies concerning the scribes and Pharisees; for their lineage is traced from them. It is interpreted spiritually thus: [Note: [Col. 0627C] “Simeon and Levi accomplished iniquity from their own sect.” He translated literally the Greek ἐξ αἱρέσεως αὐτῶν (“from their own choice/sect”), in place of what the Latin edition of the Septuagint renders “of their own purpose.” PAM.] “Simeon and Levi accomplished iniquity from their own sect,” namely, the sect by which they persecuted Christ. “Let my soul not enter into their council, and let my inward parts not recline in their assembly; [Note: [Col. 0627B] “My viscera,” Obert. and Rhen.] because in their indignation they slew men”—that is, the prophets—“and in their desire they hamstrung the bull” [Col. 0627B] (Gen. 49:6), that is, Christ, whom they killed after the murder of the prophets, and savagely pierced his sinews by driving in nails. Otherwise it would be pointless if, after the homicide they had already committed, he were reproaching them for the butchery of some ox. [Note: [Col. 0627B] Others read “and not to those very men,” Rhen. and Obert.]

Again, why did Moses, precisely at the time when Joshua was fighting against Amalek, pray seated with his hands outstretched (Exod. 17:11)? [Note: [Col. 0627D] “He prayed with outstretched hands.” Ursinus’s exemplar had this reading. What follows demonstrates that it is the truest reading: “For those who would fight against the devil, the posture of the cross was also necessary.” Likewise in the book *On Prayer*: “When we pray, we spread out our hands in a form patterned upon the Lord’s Passion.” RIG.] In circumstances so alarming, surely he ought rather to have commended his prayer with knees bent, hands beating [Col. 0628A] his breast, and face rolled upon the ground—[Note: [Col. 0627D] “With his face rolled upon the ground.” In the Dijon manuscript one reads *voluntate* (“by the will”). Hence I am led to think that the truest reading is the one preserved by the exemplars in Book III *Against Marcion*: “with his face rolling upon the ground.” Thus in the *Apology*: “But we, parched by fasting, rolling in sackcloth and ashes.” RIG.]—unless it was because, there where the name of Joshua/Jesus was fighting, the posture of the cross was also necessary for those who would one day fight against the devil, since through the cross Jesus was to win the victory.

Again, after Moses had forbidden the likeness of every created thing, why did he set up a bronze serpent placed upon wood, in the posture of one hanging, as a saving spectacle for Israel, at the time when they were being destroyed by serpents after their idolatry (Num. 21:9), unless because in this he was foreshadowing the Lord’s cross, by which the serpent, the devil, is made manifest [Note: [Col. 0628B] “Was represented,” Obert. and Semler.], and salvation was obtained for everyone wounded by serpents of that kind—that is, by his angels—when he turned away from the sinfulness of transgressions toward the mysteries of Christ’s cross? For whoever then looked upon it was freed from the serpents’ bite.

Come now: if [Col. 0628B] you have read in the prophet, in the Psalms, “God has reigned from the tree” (Ps. 95:10), [Note: [Col. 0627D] “The Lord has reigned from the tree.” Thus from the manuscripts and printed editions; below, “will reign,” for that is read in Greek and Latin. Likewise the phrase “from the tree” is rightly added by the author both here and again in chapter 13, as well as in Book III *Against Marcion*: not only in the Roman edition, from which that Paschal verse is sung in the Church, but also in the Gallican edition, at least according to the more correct Latin manuscripts, among them the Donatian manuscript of the Bible, which Erasmus calls “golden” because of its golden capital letters. Other ancient writers read the same phrase, specifically, among the Greeks, Justin Martyr against Trypho; and among the Latins, the author of the book *On Mount Sinai and Zion* among the works of blessed Cyprian, Arnobius on the Psalms, and Theodulf (bishop of Orléans—or rather Fortunatus, bishop of Poitiers) in his poem on the Passion, in these words:

> Fulfilled are the things that David sang  
> in faithful song, saying:  
> “Among the nations  
> God has reigned from the tree.”

The manuscript at this place reads “God,” but we have retained “the Lord.” So far am I from being able to agree with Jacques Lefèvre (*Commentary on the Fivefold Psalter*), who thinks that the phrase was added merely as a paraphrase through someone’s private devotion; on the contrary, I consider it the genuine reading, especially since he himself acknowledges that reading in the old edition of the Psalter [Col. 0628D] prior to the age of blessed Jerome. Nor is it an objection that it is not read in the Hebrew manuscripts, since it could have been expunged by the Jews, as were many other things, as the Most Reverend William Lindanus, bishop of Roermond, discusses more fully. PAM.—Pamelius omitted several testimonies of no slight authority, which we have thought it useful to list here. It is therefore established that the following held the same opinion: Augustine (*Expositions on the Psalms*), St. Leo the Great (*Sermon IV on the Lord’s Passion*), St. Gregory the Great (*On Ezekiel*, Book I, Homily VI; *On 1 Kings* V), Cassiodorus (*On the Psalms*), St. Peter Damian (*Dialogue between a Jew and a Christian*), and St. Bernard (*Sermon I on the Resurrection*). The same reading is also found in the ancient Italic version published first by Blessed Cardinal Joseph Tommasi and afterward by Dom Pierre Sabatier, a Benedictine monk of the Congregation of St. Maur; and in the very ancient Psalters of St. Germanus, bishop of Paris, and of the monastery of St. Peter at Chartres. EDD.]

I await what you understand by this, lest perhaps you think that some timber-merchant king is meant rather than Christ, who has reigned from the time of Christ’s Passion onward, death having been overcome. [Note: [Col. 0628B] “Christ” is omitted by the Latin manuscript, or “of the tree” is substituted.]

Accordingly Isaiah too says: “For a child has been born to us, and a son has been given to us” (Isa. 9:6). What would be remarkable, unless he is speaking of the Son of God, in saying: “A son has been born to us, whose dominion [Note: [Col. 0628B] Semler adds “beginning.”] has been placed upon his shoulder” (ibid.)? What king at all carries the emblem of his power upon his shoulder, rather than a diadem upon his head, a scepter in his hand, or sometimes a distinctive mark upon his own garment? [Note: [Col. 0629D] “Having employed some novel distinction,” Rhen. and Obert.] But Christ Jesus alone, the new King of the ages, carried upon his shoulder both his new glory [Note: [Col. 0629D] “A new glory,” the same editors.] and his power and exaltation—the cross, that is—so that, in accordance with the preceding [Note: [Col. 0629D] “Earlier,” the same editors.] prophecy, the Lord might thereafter reign from the tree.

For concerning this tree God also intimates through Jeremiah what you would say: “Come, let us put wood into his bread, and destroy him from the land of the living, and his name shall no longer be remembered” (Jer. 11:19). Wood, of course, was put into his body. For this is how Christ disclosed it, calling his body bread, [Note: [Col. 0629D] “Calling his body bread.” He appears to allude to those words of the Evangelists and blessed Paul: “Jesus took bread,” etc., and said: “Take and eat; this is my body.” Thus this marginal annotation, “he calls his body bread,” would be truer than the one that has crept in from I know not whom, “the body into bread.” PAM.] whose body the prophet formerly represented under the figure of bread. [Note: [Col. 0629D] “Whose body the prophet formerly represented under the figure of bread.” In the book *On Prayer*: “Then also because his body is reckoned in the bread.” The same passage of Jeremiah is treated somewhat more explicitly in Book III *Against Marcion*. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0629D] “Announced,” Obert. and Semler.]

If you still seek a proclamation of the Lord’s cross, the twenty-first Psalm can now sufficiently satisfy you, since it contains the whole Passion of Christ, who already then sang of his glory: “They pierced,” he says, “my hands and my feet” (Ps. 21:17). This is the distinctive cruelty of the cross. And again, when he implored the Father’s help: “Save me,” he says, “from the lion’s mouth”—that is, surely, from death—“and my humility from the horns of unicorns” (Ps. 21:22), namely, [Col. 0629B] from the points of the cross, as we showed above. [Note: [Col. 0629D] “From the points of the cross.” By “points” he means what he called “horns” above. RIG.] Neither David himself nor any king of the Jews suffered this cross, lest you think that the passion of anyone other than him who alone was so conspicuously crucified by the people was being prophesied.

Now, even if the hardness of your heart rejects and mocks all these interpretations, we have proved that the prophesied death of Christ can suffice, so that, from the fact that the character of the death was not specified, [Col. 0629C] [Note: [Col. 0629D] “Specified—what kind of death,” Rhen. and Obert.] it may be understood to have occurred through the cross, and that the suffering of the cross was to be attributed to no other than the one whose death was proclaimed.

For I wish to demonstrate his death, Passion, and burial with a single utterance of Isaiah: “Because of the crimes,” he says, “of my people he was led to death, and I shall give the wicked for his burial, and the rich for his death; because he committed no crime, nor was deceit found in his mouth, and God willed to deliver his soul from death” (Isa. 53:8–10), etc. [Note: [Col. 0629D] “And God willed to deliver,” etc. He rendered this paraphrastically from what follows; for first the Septuagint has in Greek and Latin: “And the Lord wills to cleanse him from the wound”; and shortly afterward, “and the Lord wills to place the suffering of his soul into his hands.” He therefore interprets the wound and the suffering as death. PAM.] He also says further: “His burial was taken away from the midst” (ibid.). For he was not buried unless he was dead, nor was his burial taken away from the midst [Col. 0630A] [Note: [Col. 0629D] “From the midst. In what way unless,” etc., Obert. and Semler.] except through his resurrection. [Note: [Col. 0630D] “His” is absent from Pam., Rhen., and Obert.]

Finally he adds: “Therefore he himself shall have many as an inheritance, and shall divide the spoils of many” (Isa. 53:12). Who else could this be but the one who was born, as we showed above, because his soul was handed over to death? For once the cause of his favor was shown—namely, as repayment for the injury of death—it was shown at the same time that he would obtain these things because of his death, and thus, after death, would attain resurrection. [Note: [Col. 0630D] Obert. and Semler add “through.”]

For what occurred during his Passion, when midday was darkened, the prophet Amos announces, saying: “And it shall be on that day,” says the Lord, “that the sun will set at midday, and the day of light will grow dark over the earth; and I shall turn your feast days into mourning and all your songs into lamentation; and I shall put [Col. 0630B] sackcloth upon your loins and baldness upon every head; and I shall make it like the mourning for a beloved one, [Note: [Col. 0630D] “For a sinful one,” Obert. and Semler.] and those who are with him like a day of mourning” (Amos 8:9–10). [Note: [Col. 0630D] “The sun will set at midday.” He touches upon the prophecy of Amos, chapter 9; he also mentioned this darkening in chapter 21 of the *Apology*, speaking of the death of Christ the Lord: “At that same moment, the day, though the sun was marking the middle of its course, was withdrawn. An eclipse, certainly,” etc. We once used this testimony, under the name of Eusebius of Rome, in our observations, to prove to the Pre-Adamites that an eclipse—that is, an obscuration of the sun—occurred throughout the whole world. LE PR.]

Moses also prophesied at the beginning of the first month that you would do this, when the whole multitude of the children of Israel was to sacrifice the lamb at evening; [Note: [Col. 0630D] “Would sacrifice,” Pam., Rhen., and Obert.] and he foretold that you would eat the solemn observance of that day—that is, the Passover of unleavened bread—with bitterness; and he added that it was “the Lord’s Passover” (Exod. 12), that is, Christ’s Passion. This too was fulfilled in such a manner that you killed Christ on the first day of Unleavened Bread (Matt. 26), and, so that the prophecies might be fulfilled, the day hastened to bring on evening—that is, to produce darkness—which occurred at midday. [Note: [Col. 0630D] “Will prophesy,” Semler.] And thus God turned your feast days into mourning and your songs [Col. 0630C] into lamentation. For after Christ’s Passion, captivity and dispersion also befell you, as had been foretold through the Holy Spirit.

## CHAPTER XI

For on account of these deserts of yours Ezekiel announces your future disaster—not only the disaster in this age, which has already occurred, but also that on the day of retribution, which will follow. From that disaster no one will be delivered unless he has been sealed by the Passion of Christ, whom you reject. For thus it is written:

“And the Lord said to me: ‘Son of man, have you seen what the elders of Israel are doing, each one [Col. 0631A] of them in darkness, each in his hidden chamber? For they have said: “The Lord does not see us; the Lord has abandoned the earth.”’ And he said to me: ‘Turn again, and you will see still greater crimes that these men commit.’ And he brought me to the threshold of the door of the Lord’s house that faces north, and behold, women were sitting there [Note: [Col. 0631D] “And mourning for Tammuz.” So it also stands in Greek, for which the standard Latin translator from the Hebrew renders “Adonis.” He appears, however, to have read the phrase twice, although in the Greek exemplars today it occurs only once. Then, both here and shortly afterward, where one reads, “Is it a small thing” (or, as he says presently, “a slight thing,” Greek μὴ μικρά) “for the house of Judah,” since the Greek reads “house,” I preferred to follow the manuscript reading. PAM.] and mourning for Tammuz. And the Lord said to me: ‘Son of man, have you seen? Is it a small thing for the house of Judah to commit the crimes they have committed? Yet you will see still greater inclinations on their part.’ And he brought me into the inner sanctuary of the Lord’s house, and behold, at the thresholds of the Lord’s temple, midway between the porch and the altar, there were about twenty-five [Note: [Col. 0631D] “Five” is absent from Rhen. and Obert.] men. They had turned their backs toward the Lord’s temple and their faces toward the east; these men were worshiping the sun. And he said to me: ‘Do you see, son of man? [Col. 0631B] Is it a slight thing for the house of Judah to commit the crimes they have committed here? [Note: [Col. 0631D] “These men,” Rhen. and Obert.] For they have filled up their impieties, and behold, they are as though jeering. I shall act in my indignation; my eye shall not spare, nor shall I have mercy. They will cry into my ears with a loud voice, and I shall not hear them, nor shall I have mercy’” (Ezek. 8:12–18).

[Note: [Col. 0631D] “And he cried into my ears.” This is indeed a continuation of the prophet Ezekiel’s words, but the beginning of chapter 9. Moreover, the author more correctly translates πέλυξ as “double axe”; but the Greek today does not contain “of dispersion,” but rather what the Latin Septuagint translator renders “of extermination,” and Jerome, translating from the Hebrew, “of slaughter.” PAM.]

“And he cried into my ears with a loud voice, saying: ‘The vengeance upon this city has drawn near, and each man had the instruments of extermination in his hand.’ And behold, six men were coming by the way of the upper gate, which faced north, and each man’s double axe of dispersion was in his hand. And one man in their midst was clothed in a full-length robe, with a sapphire belt upon his loins. And they entered and stood near the bronze altar; and the glory of the God of Israel rose from the cherubim upon which it had been, [Note: [Col. 0631D] “They had been,” the same editors.] [Col. 0631C] into the open court of the house.” [Note: [Col. 0631D] “Into the open court of the house.” *Subdival* stands for *subdivale*; it is the same as *subdivum*. For the glosses give *subdivum* as ὑπαίθριον (“open to the sky”) and *subdival* as αἴθριον (“open court”), while those Greek words signify the same thing, namely, something bright and open. The *subdivum* of a house is the open space outside the roof. Hesychius says, αἴθρον ὑπὸ τὸν ἀέρα ποιμανεῖ (“in the open air he will pasture beneath the sky” [wording uncertain]). LE PR.]

“And he called the man who was clothed in the full-length robe, who had the belt upon his loins, and the Lord said to him: [Note: [Col. 0632D] ‘Pass through the midst of Jerusalem.’ He also cites this scriptural passage concerning the sign Tau below, in Book III *Against Marcion*. LE PR.] ‘Pass through the midst of Jerusalem and write the sign Tau upon the foreheads of the men who groan and grieve over all the crimes being committed in their midst.’ And to the others, within hearing, he said: ‘Go after him into the city and cut them down; do not let your eyes spare, and have no pity on old man or virgin; kill all the little children and women, so that they may be utterly destroyed. But do not approach any upon whom is the sign Tau. And begin from my holy places’” (Ezek. 9:1–6).

[Col. 0632A] The mystery of this sign was proclaimed in various ways: in it life was being prepared in advance for human beings, yet the Jews would not believe in it. [Note: [Col. 0632D] “As Moses foretold in Exodus.” Since the words of Scripture he cites occur not in Exodus but in Deuteronomy 28, Tertullian’s memory has failed him here too, unless, against the authority of all the manuscripts, we should read “in Deuteronomy.” LE PR.] Thus Moses foretold in Exodus, saying: “You will be cast out of the land into which you are entering, and among those nations you will have no rest. There will be no firm standing for the sole of your foot; and God will give you a weary heart, a wasting soul, and failing eyes, so that they do not see; and your life will hang upon wood before your eyes, and you will not believe your life” (Deut. 28:64–65).

Therefore, since the prophecy was fulfilled through his advent—that is, through the birth we mentioned above—and through the Passion we have clearly declared, for that reason Daniel also said that vision and prophet were to be sealed, because Christ is the seal of all the prophets, fulfilling everything that had formerly been announced concerning him. For after his advent and his Passion there is now neither vision nor prophet. Hence he says with the utmost firmness that his advent seals vision and prophecy.

Accordingly, by showing both the number of years and the completed period of sixty-two and one-half weeks, we have proved that Christ then came—that is, was born; and within the seven and one-half weeks [Note: [Col. 0632D] “And seven and one-half weeks”—namely, the period. This reading is necessary, not “and four,” as the text was most corruptly read; this is more than sufficiently clear above in chapter 8, and not only once. PAM.] that are separated off after the cutting-off of the former weeks, we have shown that Christ suffered. Thus, when the seventy weeks had been completed, the city was destroyed, and sacrifice and anointing thereafter ceased.

For the present it is sufficient that our exposition of Christ has proceeded thus far, proving him to be such as he was announced to be, also from this agreement of the Scriptures, which [Note: [Col. 0632D] “Which,” Pam., Rhen., and Obert.] we have set forth against the Jews on the basis of the judgment of the greater part. For they cannot call into doubt or deny the texts we produce; so that from this too they cannot deny that later events correspond to the divine Scriptures, since the things foretold as future after Christ are recognized as fulfilled. For the divine arrangement would not have been found carried out [Note: [Col. 0632D] “For the divine arrangement would not have been found carried out” is absent from Semler and Obert.] unless he had come after whom the things announced were to be carried out—the same things that are proved to have been completed. [Note: [Col. 0632D] “Have been,” Obert. and Semler.]

## CHAPTER XII

Look upon all the nations, thereafter emerging from the abyss of human error toward the Lord God, the Creator, [Note: [Col. 0631D] “It,” the same editors.] [Col. 0633A] and toward his Christ, who is God; [Note: [Col. 0633D] “And toward his Christ, who is God.” Thus from the frequently cited Book III *Against Marcion*, in place of which the text previously had only “and his Christ.” Moreover, whether Christ was God will provide an occasion for fuller treatment, either there or elsewhere, against certain sectarians of our own age who are not afraid to deny it. PAM.] [Note: [Col. 0633C] “God” is absent from Semler and Obert.] and, if you dare, deny that the prophet spoke truly. Immediately the Father’s promise confronts you in the Psalms, saying: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you. Ask of me, and I shall give you the nations as your inheritance and the ends of the earth as your possession” (Ps. 2:7–8).

You will not be able to call him the son of David rather than the Christ, nor to say that the ends of the earth were promised to David—who reigned within Judea alone—rather than to Christ, who has already taken the whole world by faith in his Gospel. As he says through Isaiah: “Behold, I have given you as a covenant for my people, as a light to the nations, to open the eyes of the blind”—that is, of those in error—“to release the bound from their chains,” that is, to free them from transgressions, “and those sitting in darkness”—namely, the darkness of ignorance—“from the prison-house,” that is, from death (Isa. 42:6–7). If these things come about through Christ, they will not have been prophesied concerning any other than the one through whom [Note: [Col. 0633D] “Anyone,” Semler.] we see them accomplished.
