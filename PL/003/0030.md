That method, moreover, can readily be shown to possess many great advantages, and also a very considerable measure of refined enjoyment. First of all, the fact that the restoration of Greek and Latin letters contributed to restoring the purity and integrity of sacred doctrine—a fact proclaimed with one unanimous voice by scholars and learned theologians alike—came about chiefly because the study of those languages [Col. 0221A] was joined first with the reading and interpretation of the divine books, and afterward with that of the ancient books of the Church as well. For although those languages could sharpen minds to a sense of elegance and kindle a love of it, nevertheless, if their students had confined themselves to them—that is, to reading pagan books—they could have acquired nothing but contempt for those who transmitted knowledge of divine matters, because their teaching was contaminated and obscured by the filth of barbarism.

As for the report handed down concerning certain scholars of that period, that they recoiled from religion and despised and ridiculed divine things—if this is true, rather than a fabrication devised at the time by barbarous men for the purpose of accusation and odium, because they wished to avenge [Col. 0221B] the contempt shown them, as certain very learned men believe—I wonder whether this befell them because they rested content with reading and treating the books of the pagans, and were so captivated by their elegance and charm that they wished, or could bear, to touch nothing else. In such circumstances it could easily happen that they judged the discipline of religion from the dullness and folly either of the men who then professed it or of the books about religion that were in people’s hands. But when some men imbued with these languages devoted themselves to understanding the ancient Greek and Latin books of the Doctors of the Church—a capacity for understanding which their knowledge of those languages had given them—then they learned to understand the sacred books themselves more correctly, and realized that whatever folly and ignorance the ordinary discipline [Col. 0221C] of theology contained had been added to it through the fault of unlearned and barbarous men. By this their minds were kindled to the effort of freeing divine doctrine from the filth of scholastic errors and absurdities beneath which it had lain contaminated and oppressed for so many centuries.

And just as humane letters benefited religion in this way, so religion in turn, and the union of those letters with the reading of ecclesiastical writers, benefited them very greatly. For the barbarous Sophists and Theologasters had previously cried out that those letters were injurious to religion, or at least were wholly occupied with words, fables, and empty histories; that they contributed nothing to the higher disciplines; that they were devoid of matters great and useful for religion; and that for this reason they were useless and unworthy of the study [Col. 0221D] of youth. It is reported that this boast was made even in this Academy when Rhagius Aesticampianus began to teach them, Andreas Delitianus calling him “a fifth wheel on a wagon.” But their mouths were stopped when those languages were applied to the understanding of the sacred books and the Fathers of the Church, and it was understood that through them access was given to the true interpretation of the sacred books and to the true knowledge of theology.

Accordingly, when Peter Mosellanus began to interpret the Epistles of Paul—in which undertaking, especially given the circumstances of the time, he was assisted by reading the Greek and Latin Fathers—such crowds of young men gathered both for those very lectures and for his other lectures that those who had previously opposed humane letters now lay defeated. [Col. 0222A] And I wonder whether even now the excellent wares of humane letters would not be more marketable in the universities if their teachers—who in most places sit almost alone in their classrooms—were to unite their instruction with sacred studies in this manner. For the discipline of the humanities would both become more useful in itself, and young men would readily understand that these languages would benefit them not merely, as is now commonly believed, by giving them some ability to write Latin—an ability very many do not even seek, because they foresee little or no use for it under the plan of life they have adopted—but also, and much more, by furnishing an abundance of useful learning. They would gradually be attracted to desire that very learning and be led to acquire it.

For it certainly cannot truthfully be said that the books of the Doctors of the ancient Church [Col. 0222B] contain nothing from which we may derive assistance in every kind of learning that has some connection either with religion or with the proper treatment of sacred scholarship. It would take too long to demonstrate what assistance may be sought from them, and how, for theology, the interpretation of Holy Scripture, sacred and civil history, both branches of geography, and other arts and disciplines. This subject would require an entire commentary, which we are not now writing.

As for the eloquence appropriate for use in churches, how great a wealth of subject matter, arguments—especially arguments suited to the people—and excellent, even pointed and graceful, maxims will a properly directed reading of these works supply, provided that a teacher experienced in this class of books indicates [Col. 0222C] what is useful for this purpose in each one. In this class there are certainly many excellent and abundant writers. To say nothing of Chrysostom and other Greeks renowned for eloquence, there are Tertullian, Cyprian, Augustine, and especially Ambrose, together with Salvian, Peter Chrysologus, and Bernard. So far as concerns an abundance of good, pointed, and graceful maxims useful in ecclesiastical eloquence, Bernard is chief of them all; it is for no other reason that he was called “honey-flowing.”

Students will draw this abundance of maxims from such writers all the more beneficially because their works were written about true religion; by reading them, moreover, their sense of piety will be awakened, nourished, and strengthened. This is what Georg Fabricius chiefly sought in reading the ancient Christians, including their poets:

> “For,” he says (*Preface to the Christian Poets*, p. 4), “the sole end of study and learning is not that you should possess a polished and fluent tongue, but that the mind should be cultivated and abound in knowledge of great and useful things. Yet there are assuredly no subjects greater, more fruitful, or worthier than the knowledge of piety and religion that may be sought from these writers. Even if other qualities do not excel in them, they can nevertheless send their reader away a better and holier person.”

But in truth, the ignorance and indolence of our times—which reigns far and wide in this respect even among theologians—holds far too low an opinion of this entire class of writers, although it either does not know it at all or has scarcely ever glanced at it on a few slight and rare occasions [Col. 0223A]—that is, for the purpose of citing a testimony, and generally upon the indication of more recent writers.

To confine myself to the part most closely related to the study of eloquence: take one of Cyprian’s longer and more carefully written letters, such as the second; or Ambrose’s letter replying to Symmachus, who had requested in a most eloquent epistle that the emperors preserve the pagan religion; or Bernard’s letter to William, Abbot of Saint-Thierry, before whom he had been accused of zealously disparaging the Cluniac monks, in which he so clears himself that he portrays every one of their vices. These letters, to say nothing of other similar works, I would far prefer to all the Latin panegyrics appended to Pliny’s; indeed, I come very close to preferring them even to the ambiguous subtleties and learned flatteries of Pliny himself, on account of which [Col. 0223B] his *Panegyric* is admired.

While mentioning Bernard, I am reminded of his books *On Consideration*, written to Pope Eugene III, which I recently recommended to students in my lectures on ecclesiastical history. Those books, like the other writings of that man and of almost all the Fathers of the Church, are—as Luis Vives judged with perfect truth—not water that is clear, but water somewhat turbid; that is, their Latin and style are not entirely pure and elegant. Yet they were written, I think, in imitation of Cicero’s most famous letter to his brother Quintus on the proper administration of a province, and even contain certain thoughts from it, though dressed in different clothing. In them Bernard handles Eugene with no less skill and no less delicate a touch than Marcus handles his brother: he exhorts him by praising him, and so prescribes the proper manner of administering the pontificate that he gives the clearest description of all the vices of the pontiffs as they existed in those times, of Eugene himself, of the papal court, and of the whole clergy, everywhere observing propriety and avoiding offense. [Col. 0223C] Accordingly, when I first began to read those books, although accustomed to purer and more elegant authors, they nevertheless held me so firmly that I did not leave them until I had read them to the end. If I myself could be held by reading such works—although I was treating the chief masters of Greek and Latin eloquence with such zeal and, I think, not without a sense of elegance—and could derive from them a profit in learning that seemed by no means contemptible to me, should I suppose that others, less devoted to those humanistic studies and less advanced in them, could neither enjoy nor be assisted by them in their learning?

[Col. 0223D] But just as teachers of the humanities customarily prescribe a certain order, suited to the age and progress of students, among the older Greek and Latin authors whom we call classical, so also some such method must be employed in this class of writings. For not everything is suited to everyone, or to every period and every course of study. Youth requires one sort of work when it is chiefly occupied with Greek and Latin letters, another when engaged in the higher studies of theology; one sort at the beginning, and another when it has advanced farther in them and aspires to a certain perfection and abundance of learning.

My present purpose is not to discuss the entire method and complete order in which the Fathers should be read; it will be enough to offer some advice about the beginnings of that study. In these beginnings it seems to me most correct [Col. 0224A] that those books should be presented and recommended which have the closest connection with the other studies of that age, whether in subject matter or in style. There can be no doubt that the books especially belonging to this class are those which demonstrate the truth of the Christian religion and attack pagan superstition. For, first, a large part of their subject matter is either already known to that age from reading pagan writers, especially poets—which makes those books easier to understand—or in turn sheds light upon the books with whose study the student ought then chiefly to be occupied. This class of writings is also very closely connected in date with the classical authors, since the greater part of it comes from the period when pagan superstition was dominant. The authors of these books themselves, even those who lived later, such [Col. 0224B] as Augustine, Orosius, and others, were either teachers of eloquence—as is reported of most of them—or particularly devoted to eloquence and to humane letters of every kind. Accordingly, attention to diction and skill in speaking and argumentation generally appear in these more than in the others.

For if some contain a certain impurity of vocabulary, or are harsh and rough in style, as is Arnobius, they generally derived this from their native country. Young people may be protected from acquiring anything of the kind if such writers are deferred until the time when they have acquired some firmness in the art of writing well, at least with respect to purity of vocabulary.

My own reading of such books began with Minucius’ *Octavius*, which I first encountered at the school of Pforta. At that time some attention was given there to this reading, upon the recommendation of the teachers; and it was not easy to find one among the more advanced students [Col. 0224C] who did not possess one, and then another, such writer—even several Christian poets—to read on Sundays and other feast days. From this there was first instilled in me a love of ecclesiastical writers. It was afterward further confirmed when I saw that the greatest scholars had valued them highly, and when I first heard from my teachers, and then saw for myself, that the foremost theologians, from Luther’s time onward, had devoted very great effort to understanding the writings of the Fathers.

I therefore approved all the more highly the plan of the most learned Johann Gottlieb Lindner, most worthy vice-rector of the school at Salza in Thuringia, to publish Minucius once again and to elucidate him with such observations both by other learned men and by himself as would make the work easier and more profitable for school pupils to read. Upon examining the man’s notes themselves, I saw not only that many things had been well explained concerning words and subject matter, but also that he had employed a modest and exact criticism, occupied more with defending and explaining the received reading than with exercising conjecture, of which Minucius has experienced far too much. [Col. 0224D] Accordingly, I am confident that this little book will in many respects be useful and suitable for young people; and I earnestly hope that the most learned man, in addition to receiving other rewards for this learned labor and for his scholarship generally, may receive this above all: the knowledge that by it he has rendered useful service to Christian youth. I am confident that this very aim was foremost in his undertaking of this labor.

Written at Leipzig, on the fourth day before the Kalends of March.

# JOHANN GOTTLIEB LINDNER’S LOGICAL ANALYSIS OF THE DIALOGUE

> **Note:** [Col. 0225B] Abraham Scultetus has already provided an analysis of this dialogue [Col. 0225C] in *The Marrow of the Theology of the Fathers*, Part I, Book X, chapter 9, p. 357. Various reasons, however, persuaded me not to retain it. I could never bring myself to believe that Caecilius was so changeable and so little consistent with himself that he advanced arguments which had little coherence and could not be reduced to some definite proposition. I therefore considered reducing Caecilius’ arguments to syllogistic forms. This was not a task of great labor once I reflected that almost two things were required of him: first, that he should relinquish the superstition in which he had been reared; second, that he should submit himself for initiation into the rites of the Christians. [Col. 0225D]
>
> He endeavored to reject the Christian religion by the following reasoning: Whatever religion is uncertain and less advantageous is certainly not to be embraced. But the Christian religion is neither certain nor advantageous. Therefore the Christian religion is not to be embraced.
>
> In favor of retaining his native religion, he formed this syllogism: Whatever religion is certain and useful must certainly be retained. But the religion received by the Romans is certain and useful. Therefore one must remain in the religion handed down by tradition.
>
> Caecilius’ entire disputation is contained in these two arguments.

## ΠΑΡΑΣΚΕΥΗ — PREPARATION

### THE WORK CONSISTS OF:

[Col. 0225A]

1. **What precedes**, where the author digresses into praise of Octavius the Christian. Caecilius, a worshipper of the gods who had come to Rome to visit Minucius, was recalled to the true religion by Octavius while they were walking. Chapters I–II.

2. **The accompanying circumstances**, where he explains what occurred on the way and how Octavius admonished Minucius not to permit his intimate friend to remain in error any longer. Chapters II–III in their entirety.

3. **What follows**, which explains the occasion for the disputation about religion arising from the rebuke just mentioned. Chapter IV.

## ΑΓΩΝ — THE CONTEST

The **TRANSITION** contains Caecilius’ admonition to Marcus [Col. 0225B] Minucius, the presiding judge, that he duly observe the obligations of his office. Chapter V.

> **Note:** [Col. 0225B] In Migne, the sections headed *Thesis* and *Antithesis* are printed in parallel.

# THESIS

## I

**Christians cannot possibly advance anything certain when introducing a new religion.**

### I.—Not in the article concerning God. This is proved:

a. From the condition of truth, which is uncertain, chapter V.

[Col. 0226A]

b. From the condition of the subject, which is ill-suited to investigating that truth, chapter V; for it lacks:

1. The causative means: instruction and learning;

2. The means of rank: wealth and resources.

c. From uselessness. For knowledge of oneself is sufficient for a blessed life, chapter V.

d. From the natural condition of man, who is lowly and cast down toward the earth, [Col. 0226B] ibid.

### II.—Nor in the article concerning creation,

which Christians wrongly attribute to God, because all things have been assembled by fortune and a certain chance in the form in which they are now seen, chapter V.

### III.—Nor in the article concerning Providence:

a. Because things once begun are continued by the law of necessity; and thus everything may more suitably be derived from the mechanical structure of bodies, chapter V.

[Col. 0226C]

b. Because of the absence of order:

1. In natural things, [Col. 0226D] chapter V;

2. In moral things, ibid.

## II

[Col. 0227A]

**One must remain in the religion handed down by one’s ancestors,** [Col. 0227B]

### I.—Because it is certain:

a. On account of the uncertainty of truth, which is evident from the preceding arguments, chapter VI;

b. On account of its antiquity: for we possess religions received through tradition, ibid.;

c. On account of the trust owed to our ancestors: for the nearer anyone approaches to the age of the gods, the greater the trust that must be placed in him, ibid.

[Col. 0227D]

### II.—Because it is useful.

For the Romans owed the greatness of their empire to the gods.

a. That the Romans were religious is evident from their reverence for:

1. Their native gods, chapter VI;

2. Foreign gods, ibid.;

3. Their institution of chaste virgins and priestly offices, ibid.

[Col. 0228A]

b. That this religion secured empire for them [Col. 0228B] is evident:

1. From its institution, because the Roman rites were instituted upon an excellent plan, chapter VII:

   a. To repay divine favor, ibid.;

   b. To avert wrath, ibid.;

2. From the outcome, because auspices neglected were followed by disaster, and those observed by success, ibid.;

3. From oracles and prophecies, not only of the most ancient times [Col. 0228C] but also of more recent times, ibid.

## III

[Col. 0228D]

**The Christian religion is not useful:**

1. Because of the utterly wicked morals of Christians. For if it were useful, it would surely display its first usefulness in correcting morals, chapter VII. But in fact Christians are:

[Col. 0229A]

a. From the lowest dregs of the populace, chapter VII;

b. Seditious, ibid.;

c. Silent in public, ibid.;

d. Increasing in number daily, chapter IX, like darnel;

e. Recognizable to one another by secret signs, ibid.;

[Col. 0229B]

f. Committing incest through the appellation “brother,” ibid.

2. Because of their far worse rites; for they:

a. Worship the head of an ass, chapter IX;

b. Adore the genitals of their priest, ibid.;

c. Supplicate a man hanging from a cross, ibid.;

d. Are initiated through the killing of an infant, ibid.;

e. Celebrate incestuous banquets, ibid.;

f. Conceal their rites, chapter X.

[Col. 0229C]

3. Because of several other monstrous beliefs:

### A.—In the article concerning God; for they worship:

a. A solitary God, and one who is also powerless, as is evident from the example of the Jews, chapter X;

b. An invisible God, which is absurd, ibid.;

c. An omniscient God, which is impious, ibid.

### B.—Concerning the end of the world.

They maintain that the world will someday perish, an opinion contrary to the law [Col. 0229D] of nature once established and eternal, chapter X.

### C.—Concerning the resurrection of the dead,

which he attacks with arguments:

1. **Κατ’ ἄνθρωπον — *ad hominem***: because, to establish the resurrection, many other absurdities must be assumed at the same time; for example:

a. That burial by fire must be rejected, lest the body intended for resurrection perish, chapter XI;

[Col. 0230A]

b. That punishments and rewards after death must be posited, because one error readily engenders another, ibid.;

c. That Christians themselves deserve punishment, since they are wicked, ibid.;

d. That God is unjust because he punishes a person’s lot, although all things are subject to fate, ibid.

2. **Κατ’ ἀλήθειαν — according to the truth**, by demonstrating its impossibility:

a. Through a dilemma, ibid.;

b. Through the absence of an example, ibid.

[Col. 0230B]

3. Because of the many evils to which it exposes its worshippers. For the worship of one God does not lead to happiness:

a. Because his worshippers are poor, chapter XII;

b. Because they are weak and afflicted by calamities, ibid.;

[Col. 0230C]

c. Because they are exposed to tortures inflicted by others, ibid.;

d. Because they are forsaken by God, ibid.;

e. Others are happy without God, ibid.;

f. It calls them away from honorable pleasures, such as:

1. Processions and spectacles, ibid.;

2. Foods tasted beforehand and drinks poured as libations, ibid.;

g. It condemns the use of lawful things, such as flowers, [Col. 0230D] ibid.;

h. It deprives the dead even of the honor owed them by forbidding them to be crowned, ibid.;

i. Nor does it compensate for the loss of the present life with future happiness, ibid.

## IV

**Summary of the entire disputation.**

1. One must abstain from investigating divine matters:

a. Because truth is above us, chapter XII, ibid.;

b. Because of the indolence and rudeness of those who devote themselves to this [Col. 0231A] study, chapter XII;

c. From the examples of Socrates, Arcesilaus, Carneades, the Academics, and Simonides the lyric poet, chapter XIII.

2. Matters that are doubtful should therefore rightly be left alone, ibid.

## V. EPILOGUE

It contains:

1. Caecilius’ challenge [Col. 0232A] to Octavius, inviting him to reply, chapter XIV;

2. Minucius’ address to Caecilius, warning him not to exult before both sides have concluded their speeches, ibid.;

3. Caecilius’ reply, in which he reproaches Minucius for interrupting him with a most weighty address, and promises that he will be an attentive hearer of Januarius, chapter XV in its entirety.

# ANTITHESIS

[Col. 0225B]

## I

**The path to attaining certainty is readily accessible, because:**

### I.—That uncertainty does not lie in truth,

but in the judgments of men investigating truth; and it arises from an obscurity that is not objective but subjective, chapter XVI.

[Col. 0226A]

b. The external condition of human beings in no way impedes truth, chapter XVI:

1. We can acquire certain truths even without learning;

2. Wealth draws people away from wisdom more than poverty does.

c. I grant that knowledge of oneself is salutary, but without investigation of the universe it makes less progress, chapter XVII.

d. Man’s natural condition, since he stands erect, commands him rather to look toward heaven.

[Col. 0226B]

### II.—God is the creator of this universe.

This is evident from the order of things established toward a definite end, which assuredly cannot be the product of fortuitous chance, chapter XVII.

### III.—Providence exists. This is proved:

a. From perfection in every respect, chapters XVII–XVIII, which is observed:

1. In the universe, for example:

   α. In the four seasons of the year;

   β. In the waters;

   γ. On dry land; [Col. 0226C]

   δ. In living creatures;

   ε. In man;

   ζ. In the manner of birth.

2. In the individual parts of the universe, § 3:

   α. Britain;

   β. Egypt;

   γ. Mesopotamia;

   δ. The East.

b. From the analogy of a well-built house, chapter XVIII.

[Col. 0226D]

## DIGRESSION I

### On the Unity of God

This is proved:

1. By Socratic induction, with examples drawn:

a. From earthly governments, chapter XVIII;

[Col. 0227A]

b. From social animals, ibid.;

2. From God’s eternity, ibid.;

3. From his omnipotence, ibid.;

4. From his other perfections, which belong to one, not to many, ibid.;

5. From the absence of a name for him, ibid.;

6. From the testimony of the common people, ibid.; of the poets, chapter XIX, ibid.; and also of the philosophers, ibid.

## II

**It is foolish to wish to remain in a religion that is indeed false, merely because it was handed down by one’s ancestors.** [Col. 0227B]

This is proved:

a. By denying that all things are uncertain; Providence is proved, chapter XX;

b. By distinguishing between a fabulous antiquity and one that loves truth: the latter, not the former, must be followed, ibid.;

c. From the doubtful credibility of the ancestors, ibid.

## DIGRESSION II

### That Those Whom the Pagans Worshipped as Gods Were Human Beings

[Col. 0227C]

This is proved:

1. By showing how this came about, tracing the origin of the gods farther back from historical accounts, chapter XX;

2. From the testimonies of philosophers and historians, chapters XXI–XXII;

3. From human passions, chapter XXI;

4. From the testimonies of poets, chapter XXII;

5. From their birth and death, chapter XXIII.

## DIGRESSION III

### On the Origin and Vanity of Idols

[Col. 0227D]

1. Their origin, chapter XXIII;

2. Their vanity, chapter XXIV.

### II.—It is utterly false that the Romans owed their empire to the gods, chapter XXV.

a. Because the Romans were not so much religious as sacrilegious with impunity.

This is proved indirectly, by a disjunctive argument. If the Romans received and possessed their kingdoms from the gods, they had them:

1. Either from foreign gods;

2. Or from their native gods;

3. Or because of their more religious worship.

[Col. 0228A] But not from foreign gods, who would rather have protected their ancient worshippers if they possessed any power, chapter XXV;

Nor from their native gods, who are diseases and obscenities rather than gods, ibid.;

Nor because of purer worship, since for virgins unchastity proved more fortunate than chastity was secure, ibid.

Therefore the Romans in no way owe their empire to the gods; rather, it is the spoil of audacity, ibid.

b. Foreign nations possessed empires [Col. 0228B] even without Roman superstition, ibid.

2. Auspices failed many times; therefore the fallacy of treating a non-cause as a cause is committed, chapter XXVI.

3. Oracles very often lied; therefore the outcome must be attributed not to design [Col. 0228C] but to chance, ibid.

## DIGRESSION IV

### On the Existence and Machinations of Demons

Here are described:

1. Their existence, chapter XXVI:

a. By description, ibid.;

b. By testimonies, ibid.

2. Their machinations, chapter XXVII in its entirety.

## DIGRESSION V

### On the Perverse Judicial Examination of Christians

This examination, instigated by demons, is perverse because it is conducted:

1. Concerning things unknown and uninvestigated, chapter XXVIII;

2. Because it does not admit a defense, [Col. 0228D] ibid.;

3. Because it compels the accused not to confess but to deny, ibid.

## III

1. **The reports circulated about the crimes of Christians are partly manifestly false and disseminated by certain malicious persons at the instigation of demons; others are indeed partly true, but corrupted by hostile interpretation and by the intermixture of falsehoods.**

[Col. 0229A]

a. He denies that this follows from the fact that Christians refuse the honors and purple robes of the pagans, chapter XXXI;

b. He denies it by explaining that Christians gathered together behave with the same quietness as they do individually, ibid.;

c. He concedes it, but points out that this happens because the pagans would be ashamed to hear them publicly, ibid.;

d. He concedes it, but teaches that this is grounds for praise, not accusation, ibid.;

e. He concedes it, but removes the charge because they recognize one another not by a mark on the body but by a sign of innocence, ibid.;

[Col. 0229B]

f. He denies it and defends the innocence of the fraternal appellation, ibid.

2. **Most of the things you spread abroad about our rites are false; not a few are distortions.**

a. He denies and turns the charge back upon them, chapter XXVIII;

b. He denies it and returns the insult, chapters XXVIII–XXIX;

c. He denies and turns the charge back upon them, ibid.;

d. He denies and turns the charge back upon them, chapter XXX in its entirety;

e. He denies and turns the charge back upon them, chapter XXXI;

f. He concedes it, but points out that it is done justly, chapter XXX.

[Col. 0229C]

3. **The teaching of the Christians is entirely sound. Hence:**

### A.—In the article concerning God:

a. He advises that a distinction be made between the ancient Jews, obedient to the divine law, and the more recent, disobedient Jews, chapter XXXIII;

b. He teaches that God’s invisibility is rightly affirmed, chapter XXXII;

c. He asserts that God’s omniscience rests upon the strongest arguments, chapters XXXII–XXXIII.

### B.—The doctrine concerning the end of the world

is contrary neither to the law of nature nor to the opinions of the philosophers, chapter XXXIV.

[Col. 0229D]

### C.—A reply is given to the objections concerning the resurrection of the dead.

a. Burial by fire is rejected not to facilitate resurrection, but so that the ancient custom of burying bodies may be followed, chapter XXXIV;

[Col. 0230A]

b. The pagans also posit punishments after death, but dread those same punishments, ibid.; chapter XXXIV;

c. Christians are nevertheless much better than pagans, ibid.;

d. He concedes that fate exists, but that it is free, because of God’s omniscience, by which he foreknows future events, chapter XXXVI.

2. **The possibility of resurrection is affirmed:**

a. Both by the opinions of philosophers and by arguments drawn from reason, chapter XXXIV;

b. To wish to see the example of a man who has risen from the dead [Col. 0230B] is just as absurd as it would be to demand that the sun rise at nightfall and that trees blossom in winter. Examples of those rising will certainly appear, but in their proper time, ibid.

3. **Christians and pagans do not agree about what is good or evil.**

a. This poverty is not a matter of disgrace but of glory; it is not forced but voluntary, chapter XXXVI;

b. Courage is strengthened by infirmities, virtue by calamities, [Col. 0230C] § 8;

c. We glory when subjected to punishments, trusting in God’s assistance, chapter XXXVII;

d. We are not abandoned by God but tested, ibid.;

e. Happiness without God cannot be secure, ibid.;

f. The pleasures from which we abstain are not honorable but corrupt and unlawful, ibid.; this is proved there with regard to:

1. Processions and spectacles, §§ 12–13;

2. The remnants of sacrifices, which are offered as libations to demons, chapter XXXVIII;

g. Not the use but the abuse of flowers is condemned, [Col. 0230D] ibid.;

h. The custom of crowning the dead is contrary to reason, ibid.;

i. Christians not only live in tranquillity but are also blessed in the hope of future happiness, ibid.

## IV

1. **No one should be deterred from investigating divine matters, because:**

a. Truth is not above us but in our possession, ibid.;

b. We display wisdom not by our garb but by our mind, [Col. 0231A] chapter XXXVIII;

c. The examples of the philosophers teach us nothing, ibid.

2. Things that were once doubtful are no longer doubtful, since the truth of the divinity has now come to maturity.

## V. EPILOGUE

It shows:

1. What impulses Octavius’ speech [Col. 0232A] left in the minds of his hearers, chapter XXXIX in its entirety;

# MARCUS MINUCIUS FELIX’S *OCTAVIUS*

> **Note:** [Col. 0231C]
>
> **M. Minucius Felix.** The Minucian clan possessed several surnames: Rufus, Thermus, Faustus, Macer, Fundanus, and among them also Felix. An ancient Roman inscription in Gruter, p. 918, reads:
>
> **SACRED TO THE DIVINE SPIRITS OF THE DEAD. QUINTUS MINUCIUS FELIX.**
>
> It is written with a *c*, because it is not derived from *minutus* (“diminished”); the short quantity of the syllable also requires this. Silius Italicus VII.523:
>
> > Without delay, once the rampart was demolished, Minucius’ madness  
> > Burned with the desire at once to destroy and to perish.
>
> **CELLARIUS.**
>
> **Octavius.** This is the title of the dialogue, taken from its principal interlocutor, just as Cicero entitled his dialogues *Cato*, *Lucullus*, *Brutus*, and *Laelius*, in imitation of Plato, whose *Phaedo*, *Alcibiades*, *Socrates*, and other books bear the names of their principal speakers. Who Octavius was may be learned from chapters I and II, to the extent that information about him has been handed down to us. Below, [Col. 0232C] in chapter XV, he is called by his full name, Januarius Octavius. The other interlocutors are Caecilius Natalis, still a pagan at the time but a Christian after the conversation, and our own Marcus Minucius Felix, who in this dialogue is sometimes called merely by his praenomen, Marcus. See chapters III and V. **CELLARIUS.**

(C)

# PROEM

[Col. 0231B] In this dialogue Caecilius is introduced as a man devoted to pagan superstitions. By various arguments he attempts to prove that no one should abandon the ancestral worship of the gods; that no one should make an anxious inquiry into religion, but that the ancient religion should be retained and the new rejected. Octavius, on the other hand, had rejected that impious worship and, together with Minucius Felix, enrolled himself in the Christian profession. He so completely overturns every argument of Caecilius, and demonstrates the truth of the Christian religion with arguments of such weight, that Caecilius, broken and wholly convinced by the most serious disputation, embraces the true religion with a joyful and grateful heart. The author first presents Caecilius’ speech in defense of the worship of the gods. [Col. 0232B] He then supplies Octavius’ reply to each individual part of Caecilius’ discourse.

# CHAPTER ONE

> **Note:** [Col. 0232C]
>
> **Most faithful companion.** Buzelius interprets this in a military sense, namely, that our Minucius, in accordance with Roman custom, had as a young man shared Caecilius’ military quarters. It is incumbent upon him to prove whether that custom remained so consistently in use in later centuries; for we impose upon no one this burden of proof concerning Minucius himself. It is better to understand the expression generally as a companion in labor and study. **LINDNER.**
>
> **So great a sweetness.** Wopkenius thinks the text should be emended to “only sweetness,” but with insufficient justification. **LINDNER.**
>
> [Col. 0233A] **Departing.** From the entire context of the discourse it is perfectly clear that our Felix committed this dialogue to writing after Octavius’ death; therefore the reading should be *decedens*, “dying.” [Col. 0233B] Cicero and other excellent writers use the word in this way very frequently. Phaedrus, Book IV, fable 4: “A certain man, when dying, left three daughters.” Sulpicius Severus, *Sacred History*, Book I, chapter 7: “When Sarah was one hundred and twenty-seven years old, she died.” The same historian uses it everywhere else in the same way.
>
> Claudian, however, writes in *Against Rufinus*, Book II.10:
>
> > As consolation for death,  
> > A common destruction will suffice; nor shall I depart in terror beforehand:  
> > Together with life, power must be relinquished.
>
> But in that passage certain manuscripts read “I shall die”; correctly, indeed. **DAVIS.**
>
> —If you listen to Gruner in the *Transactions of the Latin Society of Jena*, vol. IV, p. 209, the editors’ reading must be retained in the same sense. But the learned man appears to establish this with examples that are not sufficiently apposite, such as “departing from life,” “I depart from life,” and “the departure from the body.” Passages should have been produced in which the verb *discedo* (“depart”) is used by itself and is understood absolutely and beyond dispute of death, as is certainly established for the verb *decedo* (“die”). [Col. 0233C] **GALLAND.**
>
> —There is nothing that compels us to think of death, as Gronovius does, merely because of a slight imitation of Cicero, who says at the beginning of the *Brutus*: “Because that excellent man was cut off at a most unfavorable time for the republic, and left us with a sorrowful longing for both his authority and his wisdom.” For:
>
> 1. Minucius could have written these words just as readily after Octavius’ departure from Rome as after his death.
>
> 2. There is remembrance of the absent no less than of the dead.
>
> 3. Longing for an absent friend is immeasurable; longing for a dead friend is sorrowful and not easily endured.
>
> 4. Octavius was still young at the time, since his children were infants.
>
> 5. I doubt whether *discedere* by itself is used to mean “to die.”
>
> **LINDNER.**
>
> **An extraordinary and holy man.** Even pagans called a man or woman “holy” when distinguished in morals and virtue. Among Christians, one who kept the faith pledged to Christ and preserved his Baptism inviolate was called holy. This designation denotes both religion and morals. But if anyone was marked by wicked conduct, he then ceased both to be and to be regarded [Col. 0233D] as a Christian. **RIGALTIUS.**
