Hence rose the proud altar of Jupiter Feretrius.  
(Propertius, Book IV.)

Some, however, think that in this text of Minucius—“Feretrius is not heard”—the final word, *auditur* (“is heard”), is corrupt, and that *aditur* (“is approached”) should be read. For after the death of Romulus, Numa Pompilius ordered the *spolia opima* (spoils of honor) taken from enemies [Col. 0582A] to be carried to Mars or Quirinus, not to Jupiter Feretrius. Moreover, what can the phrase “is not heard” mean? That Jupiter Feretrius is mute? But was not Jupiter Latiaris equally mute?

Others nevertheless judge that the reading in our manuscript codex, *non auditur* (“is not heard”), should be retained, and that the adverb *non* should there be taken for the word *nomen* (“name”), just as if Minucius had said: “The name of Feretrius, of whom there was no image, is no longer heard.” They attempt to prove this by certain examples, but these carry little weight. What, then, if you take the words *non auditur* in their native sense, as meaning “is left unheeded, spurned, disregarded”? Will not Minucius’ meaning be sufficiently open and clear: that Jupiter Latiaris was still being drenched with blood in his own [Col. 0582B] day, but Feretrius was no longer heeded? In any case, whether one reads *auditur* or *aditur*, neither is their meaning very different nor does either appear very foreign to the thought of Minucius.

Finally, Minucius continues: “There are as many monstrosities of Jupiter as there are names.” And therefore Theophilus, in his book *To Autolycus*, said:

> I should like to ask you, O man, just how many Jupiters are to be found. Ζεὺς μὲν γὰρ ἐν πρώτοις προσαγορεύεται Ὀλύμπιος, καὶ Ζεὺς Λατεάριος, καὶ Ζεὺς Κάσσιος, καὶ Ζεὺς Κεραύνιος, καὶ Ζεὺς Προπάτωρ, καὶ Ζεὺς Παννύχιος, καὶ Ζεὺς Πολιοῦχος, καὶ Ζεὺς Καπετώλιος—“First Jupiter is called Olympian; then Jupiter Latiaris, Cassius, Thunderer, Forefather, All-Night Watcher, Defender of Cities, and Capitoline. And indeed Jupiter, the son of Saturn and king of the Cretans, was buried [Col. 0582C] in Crete.”

(Theophilus, *To Autolycus*, Book II, p. 76.) He considers the other Jupiters, however, unworthy of any burial.

Moreover, concerning the virtually infinite crowd and multitude of those Jupiters—whom some imagined to number three hundred—or, as our author rightly called them, monstrosities and portents, see Lilius Giraldus and, if you please and have the leisure, what we have discussed elsewhere (Giraldus, *History of the Gods*, Syntagma 2, p. 72 ff.; Vol. I, *Apparatus*, Book III, Dissertation 1, ch. 9, p. 752).

## ARTICLE III

### Another argument of the same force is explained, which Minucius drew from the shameful and utterly disgraceful deeds of the gods: Erigone said to have hanged herself with a noose; Castor and Pollux alternately living and dying; Aesculapius struck by lightning; and Hercules burned in the fires of Oeta

[Col. 0582D]

From the utterly absurd and plainly ridiculous forms of the gods, Minucius passes to their most shameful and abominable deeds, by which he demonstrates with the greatest clarity that they are wholly unworthy not only of the name of gods but even of human beings endowed with reason. To prove this, he enumerates several of them individually, and we shall follow him in this argument just as we did in the preceding one.

He begins his discussion with Erigone: “She who,” as he says, “was suspended from a noose, so that, as a maiden, she might be made fiery among the stars.” There are two opinions—or rather fables—among pagan writers [Col. 0583A] concerning this Erigone. Some say that she was the daughter of the Athenian Icarius. When drunken peasants killed him, she was alerted to her father’s murder by a dog; and, upon seeing his corpse, she hanged herself with a noose. After her death, the maiden herself and the dog that had alerted her are imagined to have obtained, under their own names, a place among the stars.

Others relate that Erigone was the daughter of Aegisthus and Clytemnestra. She went to Athens with her grandfather Tyndareus to accuse Orestes; but when she there learned that he had been acquitted, she took her own life with a noose.

Minucius, however, seems rather to have had in view the first opinion, according to which they babbled that Erigone and that dog were numbered among the stars, as Statius [Col. 0583B] had sung:

> The stars lend force to my undertaking, and the heat-bearing  
> Dog of my Erigone foams.

(Statius, *Thebaid*, Book IV, v. 538.) If you desire more, you may consult Nonnus, Servius, and others (Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*, Book XLVII, v. 600 ff.; Servius on Virgil, *Georgics*, Book II, v. 388). For this reason Tatian had objected to the pagans: Κύων ὁ τῆς Ἠριγόνης ἐν οὐρανῷ δείκνυται—“Erigone’s dog is shown in heaven” (Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, p. 149).

It is worth observing here that our manuscript codex reads *ut in astra ignata sit* (“that she might be ‘ignated’ among the stars”), undoubtedly through a copyist’s error. The printed editions accordingly have *ignita sit* (“that she might be made fiery”), that is, that Erigone might be placed among the fiery stars. [Col. 0583C] This correction is not altogether to be rejected, since it can be supported not inaptly by the cited words of Statius and Tatian. It did not please Gronovius, however, who preferred to correct the text to *signata sit* (“that she might be marked out”), that is, “placed.” But he ought first to have shown where Minucius uses this manner of expression. In any event, whatever the genuine reading may be, Minucius very plainly means that the pagans’ opinion concerning their gods is altogether absurd, since they solemnly proclaimed that Erigone, after ending her life with a noose, was both a goddess and placed among the stars.

Minucius then says—and Cyprian, his follower, uses the same words: “The Castors,” that is, Castor and Pollux, “die by turns so that they may live” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 11). Lactantius [Col. 0583D] explains the matter somewhat more fully:

> Castor and Pollux, while abducting other men’s betrothed brides, ceased to be twins. For Idas, aroused by grief at the wrong, ran one of them through with his sword; and the poets relate that the same pair live by turns and die by turns.

(Lactantius, Book I, ch. 10, p. 150.) For they imagined that Castor and Pollux fell in love with Phoebe and Talaïta, the daughters of Leucippus, who had been betrothed to Lynceus and Idas. Castor was therefore killed by Lynceus, as some suppose, or by Idas, Meleager, or Policine [name uncertain], as others maintain. Pollux then asked his father Jupiter to bestow immortality upon Castor. When he could not obtain this, he secured instead that half of immortality should be allotted to the one and half to the other. They were therefore said to live on alternate [Col. 0584A] days or, as Pliny says, to be “living and dying on alternate days” (Pliny, *Natural History*, Book II, ch. 7, p. 143). On this matter we have the following verses of Homer, cited by Origen:

> Ἄλλοτε μὲν ζώουσ᾽, ἑτερήμεροι, ἄλλοτε δ᾽ αὖτε  
> Τετνᾶσιν, τιμὴν δὲ λελόγχασιν ἶσα θεοῖσι.
>
> At times they live, on alternate days, and then again  
> At times they are dead; yet they have received honor equal to the gods.

(Origen, *Against Celsus*, Book III, p. 123; Homer, *Odyssey*, Book XI, v. 331 ff.) Hence Tatian, following the same Homer, calls them ἑτερόημεροι Διόσκουροι, “the Dioscuri who live on alternate days” (Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, p. 150). Finally, to omit many others, Virgil sings of them:

[Col. 0584B]

> If Pollux redeemed his brother by taking death in turn,  
> And so many times goes and returns along the path—

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book VI, v. 121.) Elsewhere, however, we shall see that this was not accepted by everyone.

Minucius next says—and again Cyprian in precisely the same words: “Aesculapius is struck by lightning so that he may rise to become a god” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 11). If you ask Cicero who this man was, you will receive this answer from him: “The second Aesculapius was the brother of the second Mercury; he was struck by lightning and is said to have been buried at Cynosurae” (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book III, p. 248).

[Col. 0584C]

But if you ask again why he was struck by lightning, Diodorus Siculus replies:

> Pluto lodged a complaint against Aesculapius before Jupiter, on the ground that the number of the dead was continually diminishing through his medical treatment, and accused him of reducing the power of the kingdom below. Angered by this, Jupiter killed Aesculapius with a thunderbolt: Παραξυνθέντα, καὶ κεραυνώσαντα Ἀσκληπιὸν διαφθεῖραι—“Being provoked, he destroyed Aesculapius by striking him with lightning.”

(Diodorus Siculus, *Library*, Book IV, p. 190.) Plato, however, writes that he was struck by lightning because, corrupted by avarice and gold, he “treated a certain wealthy man who was suffering from a fatal illness and at the point of death” (Plato, *Republic*, Book III, Vol. II, p. 408). Or, as Virgil sang, it was because he had restored Hippolytus to life after Hippolytus had been killed through the contrivance of his stepmother (Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book VII, v. 765 ff.). [Col. 0584D] The same was said by Pindar, as cited by Tertullian, Arnobius, and Athenagoras; the last called Hesiod as a second witness, though Hesiod gives the name of the man raised from the dead (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 14, and *To the Nations*, Book II, ch. 14; Arnobius, Book IV, p. 143; Athenagoras, *Plea for the Christians*, p. 33; Pindar, Ode 3). Read also Lucian, Origen, Arnobius, Lactantius, Theodoret, and—if it is not burdensome—what we noted in the preceding volume (Lucian, *Dialogues of the Gods*, p. 78; Origen, *Against Celsus*, Book III, p. 123; Arnobius, Book IV, p. 143; Lactantius, *Institutes*, Book I, ch. 17, p. 84; Theodoret, Discourse 8, *On the Martyrs*, p. 595; Vol. I, *Apparatus*, Book III, Dissertation I, ch. 9, art. 1, p. 754). Plato, Tertullian, and Lactantius therefore rightly [Col. 0585A] conclude from this, together with our author—as we shall see in the proper place—that Aesculapius cannot be called the son of God.

What more need be said? “Hercules,” to quote the exact words of our author and of Cyprian, “is consumed in the fires of Oeta, so that he may cast off the man” (the Royal manuscript has *Henneis*, but wrongly). The whole matter, or fable, is related by Diodorus Siculus—whom Servius follows—in the following way. After Hercules put on the tunic smeared by Deianira with the Centaur’s potion, which had been prepared for his destruction, he immediately fell into a fatal—or, as Arnobius relates, epileptic—illness. Then, as the illness grew worse and every hope of recovering his health was abandoned, he mounted a funeral pyre in obedience to the Delphic oracle. Philoctetes set fire to it, and soon, after being touched by thunderbolts, it was reduced to ashes together with Hercules’ body. [Col. 0585B] Later, when Iolaus had searched in vain for his bones, the report spread that Hercules had flown away to the gods.

Augustine, moreover, indicates where and when this occurred and who that Hercules was. Then, in close accord with the thought of our author, he argues from it as follows: “As matters now stand, I do not see how that Hercules for whom torches were brought on Mount Oeta, as Accius says, passed from that burning into the eternal home of his father Jupiter.” Consult Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, Book IX, fable 3; Sophocles, *Women of Trachis*, Vol. II of the Greek Poets; Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations*, Book II, p. 138; Seneca, *Hercules on Oeta*; Justin Martyr, *First Apology*, p. 76; Athenagoras, *Plea for the Christians*, p. 53; Theodoret, Discourse 3, *On Angels*, p. 510, and more fully Discourse [Col. 0585C] 8, *On the Martyrs*, p. 594 ff.; Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, Book I, ch. 9; and Hyginus and Apollodorus, who committed to writing much about these and the other aforementioned opinions of the pagans.

## ARTICLE IV

### Why and how Minucius shows that the gods of the pagans are false from the books of Homer, read by everyone for instruction in life and morals, in which are celebrated Venus wounded; Mars bound and wounded; Jupiter rescued by Briareus, mourning Sarpedon, and enticed by Venus’ girdle; Hercules clearing away dung; Apollo tending Admetus’ cattle; Neptune rebuilding Laomedon’s walls; Jupiter’s thunderbolt fashioned together with the arms of Aeneas; the adultery of Mars and Venus; Ganymede abducted by Jupiter; and how deservedly Homer was expelled by Plato from his city

[Col. 0585D]

Minucius deplores the utterly wretched and unhappy condition of the pagans, who had been taught old wives’ tales about their gods by their parents and had almost imbibed them with their mothers’ milk. Indeed, when they grew older, they learned those tales “through their very studies and disciplines,” in the poems of the poets and especially of Homer. For while drinking poison in what seemed delightful to them, they corrupted their morals by hearing of the disgraceful deeds of their gods. What would they not have done when they heard that Homer—“renowned, praised, and crowned,” as Minucius says, by all peoples—who “in his account of the Trojan War, although he makes sport of the gods, nevertheless mingles them in human affairs and actions? There [Col. 0586A] he matched them in pairs, wounded Venus, bound, wounded, and put Mars to flight. He relates that Jupiter was rescued by Briareus lest he be bound by the other gods; that Jupiter shed bloody showers of tears for his son Sarpedon because he could not save him from death; and that, enticed by Venus’ girdle, he lay with his wife Juno more ardently than he was accustomed to lie with his adulterous partners.”

Tertullian had written precisely the same things, though in different words:

> But when I turn to your literature, by which you are shaped for wisdom and the liberal duties, how much mockery do I find? The gods engaged one another in battle over the Trojans and Achaeans, like matched pairs of gladiators; Venus wounded by a human arrow; Mars nearly wasted away through thirteen months in chains; Jupiter rescued through the agency of a certain monster lest he suffer the same violence from the other heavenly beings; [Col. 0586B] and Jupiter now weeping over Sarpedon’s fate, now foully rutting with his sister while recalling mistresses who had long since ceased to be so beloved.

(Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 14.) We have quoted the complete passages from our Minucius and from Tertullian together, both because they mutually illuminate one another and because they are so similar that Minucius appears to have borrowed his material from Tertullian.

What Tertullian drew only in general terms from pagan literature, our Minucius declares to have been taken from Homer. From this we understand why Minucius and the other defenders of the Christian religion raised these utterly absurd fables—scarcely credible to any person of sound mind—against all the pagans in order to overthrow their false religions. For Homer was held in such honor among them that they taught children the gods’ mockeries celebrated by him [Col. 0586C], while others were shaped by his poems “for wisdom,” as Tertullian says, “and the liberal duties.” Accordingly, imbued with these stories from their earliest years and regarding it as impious to withhold credence from so great a man, as they supposed him to be, they readily believed their gods to be such as he and the other poets had portrayed them, and such as they were later publicly represented in theaters and mysteries, as we shall show below. For this reason Minucius says: “By these and similar inventions and more seductive falsehoods, the minds of children are corrupted; and as those same fables cling to them, they grow old in them even to the full strength of mature age, although truth is readily available—but only to those who seek it.” Moreover, although Homer and the other poets composed such abominable and shameful verses about the gods, they were themselves nevertheless called the theologians of the pagans, as Augustine says [Col. 0586D] (Augustine, *City of God*, Book XVIII, ch. 14, p. 498). It is therefore no wonder that our Minucius and the other most ancient Fathers of the Church repeatedly and seriously assailed this authority of Homer and the other poets, even though it rested upon such ridiculous fables.

Since Minucius and Tertullian cited neither the books nor the passages of Homer in which the things they recount were written, we have therefore considered it our duty to indicate them individually and to cite others by whom the same things are similarly mentioned or confirmed. As for Venus’ being wounded—by Diomedes, of course—which Minucius mentions first, we read of it in Homer’s fifth book of the *Iliad*, v. 335 ff.; it was also cited by Plato, *Symposium Questions*, Book IX, Question 4, p. 739; Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book II, v. 277; Justin Martyr, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, p. 3; Athenagoras, *Plea for the Christians*, p. 21; Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, p. 23; and Athanasius, *Oration Against the Greeks*, § 12, p. 112.

What Minucius also related concerning Mars being bound and wounded you will find celebrated in the same book of Homer’s *Iliad*, v. 385 ff. On this see also Clement of Alexandria and Athanasius in the passages just cited, as well as Gregory of Nazianzus, Oration 3, *Against Julian*, p. 258, and Oration 4, p. 308.

Homer also sang—as Minucius next observes—that Jupiter was rescued by Briareus, *Iliad*, Book I, v. 399 ff.; on this see later Justin Martyr, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, p. 3, and Servius on *Aeneid*, Book VI, p. 415.

Next, in the same Homer, *Iliad*, Book XVI, [Col. 0587B] v. 667 ff., you will find what is related concerning the same Jupiter and Sarpedon not only by Minucius but also by Athenagoras, *Plea for the Christians*, p. 21, and Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, both of whom transcribed two verses of Homer.

Finally, concerning the strap or girdle by which Venus, as our author says, enticed Jupiter, you may hear Homer sing in *Iliad*, Book XIV, v. 204 ff. Gregory of Nazianzus, like Minucius, turned this against the pagans in Oration 3, *Against Julian*, p. 258. See there the notes of Billius, who rendered into Latin verse the poet’s lines about that girdle of Venus.

Come now, and let us examine other things no less absurd, likewise doubtless described in other verses of Homer, though Minucius did not identify them: “Hercules,” he says, [Col. 0587C] “carried away dung; Apollo tended cattle for Admetus; and Neptune erected walls for Laomedon.” Diodorus Siculus reports that the fifth labor of Hercules was the one in which he cleared the stable of King Augeas of dung. He explains this at greater length, as do Pausanias, Natalis Comes, and very many others (Diodorus Siculus, *Library*, Book IV, p. 154 ff.; Pausanias, Book V, p. 148). Consider, however, whether our author intended to aim at Homer, *Iliad*, Book II, v. 695 ff.

Homer sang far more clearly of those mentioned by our author: Apollo, who tended Admetus’ cattle, and Neptune, who built walls for the Trojan Laomedon. The first, indeed, occurs in the second book of the *Iliad*, v. 780 ff.; the second in Book XXI, v. 443, passages that Pausanias is recorded as citing (Pausanias, Book VII, p. [Col. 0587D] 226). On both Apollo and Neptune see Lucian, *On Sacrifices*, p. 188; Arnobius, *Against the Pagans*, Book IV, p. 144; Lactantius, Book I, ch. 10; Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 14; Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 11; Augustine, *City of God*, Book III, ch. 2, and Book XVIII, ch. 13; Servius on Virgil, *Georgics*, Book III, v. 2, and on *Aeneid*, Book I, v. 554; Vossius, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, Book I, ch. 15, p. 16, and Book II, ch. 12, p. 187. Finally, on Apollo see Callimachus, Ode 2, *To Apollo*; Euripides, the prologue to *Alcestis*; Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*, Book I, v. 323; Ovid, *Art of Love*, Book II, p. 447; Athenagoras, *Plea for the Christians*, p. 22; Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, p. 159; Eusebius, *Chronicle*, Book I, p. 19. And [Col. 0588A] from the passage of Augustine already cited and from another passage of his, some light may be cast upon what we noted elsewhere as having been committed to writing by Clement of Alexandria concerning the same servitude of Apollo (Vol. I, *Apparatus*, Book III, Dissertation 2, ch. 14, art. 1, p. 1090).

Minucius continues and reproaches the pagans with “Jupiter’s thunderbolt fashioned on the anvil together with Aeneas’ arms.” He is undoubtedly speaking of Vulcan’s anvil. Homer, however, relates how on that anvil Vulcan fashioned the arms not of Aeneas but of Achilles (Homer, *Iliad*, Book XVIII, v. 454 ff.). Consider, therefore, whether our author there had in view not only Homer but also Virgil, who sang that Vulcan ordered the Cyclopes, who were fashioning thunderbolts for Jupiter, to leave them aside and make for Aeneas the arms requested by Venus [Col. 0588B] (Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book VIII, v. 422 ff.).

But the adultery of Mars and Venus, “discovered by Vulcan,” as our Minucius continues, had been sung by Homer and Ovid (Homer, *Odyssey*, Book VIII, v. 267; Ovid, *Art of Love*, Book II, near the end). If we believe Lucian, moreover, a certain dancer represented by himself, without percussion or flutes, but by dance and gesture alone, that abominable adultery of the gods (Lucian, *On Dancing*, p. 515). Hence Clement of Alexandria said: Ἀφροδίτη ἐπ᾽ Ἄρει κατῃσχυμμένη—“Aphrodite was put to shame and disgrace on account of Ares.” Arnobius and Lactantius also discuss it, as we shall note below (Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Pagans*, p. 21; Dissertation on Arnobius, ch. 10, art. 1; and on Lactantius, ch. 31, art. 1).

[Col. 0588C]

Finally, what Minucius says is found in Homer: “Jupiter’s violation of Ganymede, consecrated in heaven.” Pausanias indeed says:

> Ἔστι δὲ Ὁμήρῳ πεποιημένα, ὡς ἁρπασθείη τε ὑπὸ θεῶν Γανυμήδης οἰνοχοεῖν Διί—“Homer recorded in his verses that Ganymede was abducted by the gods to serve cups to Jupiter, and that in return horses were given to Tros,” that is, to Ganymede’s father.

(Minucius, p. 208; Homer, *Iliad*, Book XX, v. 231 ff., and *Hymn to Venus*; Pausanias, Book V, p. 173.) But if you please, you may consult the Greek itself in the poet at the passage cited by us, as well as Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, Book X, fable 5; Theocritus, Idyll 12, v. 35 ff.; Apollonius, *Argonautica*, Book III, v. 114 ff.; and Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*, Book I, v. 316.

From this entire argument of our author, [Col. 0588D] you can indeed see how deservedly he approves the judgment of Plato, who expelled Homer—“renowned, praised, and crowned,” as we have said, a word also used by Tertullian—from his city on account of those execrable and detestable inventions, as a teacher of licentiousness and impiety, according to Theodoret, or as a plague upon youth, as Origen rightly argues. But Plato himself, and indeed all pagans without exception, ought to have expelled these criminal gods celebrated by Homer, together with the other gods of the pagans, not merely from one city but from every city and every part of the world (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, Book II, ch. 7; Theodoret, Discourse 2, *On the First Principle*, p. 486; Origen, *Against Celsus*, Book IV, p. 186).

# CHAPTER XVIII

## CAECILIUS’ ARGUMENT, BY WHICH HE ATTEMPTS TO PROVE THAT THE GODS OF THE PAGANS EXIST BECAUSE THE ROMANS, THROUGH THEIR DEVOUT WORSHIP OF THEM, MERITED THE EMPIRE OF THE WHOLE WORLD, IS EXAMINED

[Col. 0589A]

## ARTICLE I

### Caecilius’ argument is explained

Caecilius contends that the Roman Empire was extended “beyond the courses of the sun and the very bounds of Ocean” because the Romans had always worshiped, with devout observance and true religion, not merely some gods but all the gods and every divine power. Nor was Caecilius alone in asserting and vaunting this; very many other pagans did the same, and Valerius Maximus above all. For these are his very words:

[Col. 0589B]

> It is no wonder if, for the enlargement and preservation of that empire, the steadfast favor of the gods has always kept watch over a state which appears to scrutinize with such scrupulous care even the smallest details of religion; for our commonwealth must be judged never to have turned its eyes away from the most exact observance of the rites.

(Valerius Maximus, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings*, ch. 1, § 8, p. 8.) Yet the Romans could not have received so great a reward for their piety toward the gods unless those gods truly existed and possessed supreme power.

But what, you will ask, did Caecilius mean when, in so extravagantly proclaiming the various offices of that Roman piety, he confidently asserted that the Romans obtained that greatest of benefits from their gods not by their piety alone but also by military courage? You will undoubtedly understand [Col. 0589C] his thought and meaning without great difficulty if you will first observe that, “during assaults” upon cities, as Pliny writes after Verrius Flaccus, “it was customary for Roman priests to summon away the god under whose protection the town stood, and to promise him the same or a greater worship among the Romans. And,” he continues, “this rite endures in the pontifical discipline; and it is agreed that for this reason the identity of the deity under whose protection Rome stood was concealed, lest any enemy act in the same way” (Pliny, *Natural History*, Book XXVIII, ch. 2, p. 559). Macrobius relates the same thing and supplies the formula by which the Romans, during sieges of cities, were accustomed to summon away their guardian gods and vow temples and games to them (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, Book III, ch. 9). It is therefore undoubtedly on account of this military custom that Caecilius asserts that the Romans [Col. 0589D] obtained dominion over the whole world from the gods.

Let us consider, if you please, how he attempted to prove it. First, he says, “while they exercise religious valor in arms,” that is, by devoutly summoning the gods away, as we have said, and by making solemn vows, prayers, and sacrifices. Second, “while they fortify the city of Rome with sacred religious observances,” both their own and those of other peoples whom they had conquered. For after vanquishing those peoples, they carried to Rome both their gods and their sacred—or rather profane and abominable—worship. For the same reason Caecilius added: “While they seek gods as guests from every quarter and make them their own.”

He continues and says that the Romans fortified their city not only with these pious and sacred rites, [Col. 0590A] but also “with chaste virgins,” namely, the Vestals, “and with the many honors and titles of priests,” that is, with stipends, revenues, exemptions, and privileges. That this is indeed our author’s meaning, which some have wholly failed to perceive, can be confirmed by the authority of Symmachus, likewise a pagan, and of Ambrose, who answered him. For in his *Memorial* to the emperor Valentinian, Symmachus addresses him in these words: “The law of our ancestors had honored the Vestal Virgins and the ministers of the gods with a modest maintenance and rightful privileges” (Symmachus, *Memorial*, in Ambrose, § 14, p. 831). Ambrose replies:

> They complain that public maintenance is not owed to their priests and ministers. . . . Yet no one has denied votive gifts to the temples or legacies to the haruspices. Only their estates have been taken away, [Col. 0590B] because they did not use religiously the possessions that they defended by the right of religion.

(Ambrose, Epistle 18, §§ 13–16.)

Caecilius further boldly declares that the gods brought the Romans the most certain assistance:

> While under siege and captured except for the Capitol, they worship the gods whom another would already have spurned as angry; and through the battle lines of the Gauls, who marvel at the boldness of their superstition, they advance unarmed with weapons but armed with the observance of religion; while, after the enemy walls have been captured and victory is still raging fiercely, they venerate the divine powers they have vanquished.

He is undoubtedly speaking of the Senones, who, after taking the city of Rome, were besieging the Capitol. Nor did the dull and obtuse Symmachus hesitate to hurl that blunted weapon once more against the Christians, in these fewer words: “These sacred rites repelled Hannibal from the walls and the Senones from the Capitol” (Symmachus, *Memorial*, § 9, p. 830; Ambrose, Epistle [Col. 0590C] 18, § 4, p. 834).

But Gronovius, following some others, conjectures that in our author’s text, and contrary to the evidence of our manuscript codex, *iratus* (“angry,” singular) should be read instead of *iratos* (“angry,” plural). “For the Romans,” he says, “did not despise the angry gods by whom they felt themselves being punished.” He therefore thinks Minucius’ meaning is this: the Romans worship their gods, whom another person, “angry” because they denied him their aid, would already have spurned. Yet the sense will undoubtedly be better if you read: “whom another would already have spurned as angry,” because they therefore brought him no assistance. For what if the term *iratos* (“angry”) is there opposed to “propitious gods,” on whom much may be read in Brissonius? (Brissonius, *On Formulas*, Book I, p. 120 ff.) What Caecilius relates concerning the Capitol besieged by the Senones and the Romans’ religious devotion toward the gods [Col. 0590D] you will find explained by Livy, Valerius Maximus, Florus, and others (Livy, Book V, § 46 ff.; Valerius Maximus, Book I, ch. 1, §§ 10–11; Florus, Book I, ch. 13; ibid., p. 51).

Besides these things, Caecilius regards as another certain proof of Roman religion the fact that “they erect altars to unknown divine powers and to the Manes.” Concerning the former, these are Tertullian’s words:

> I indeed find altars exposed for unknown gods. But that is Attic idolatry. There are likewise altars to uncertain gods, but that is Roman superstition. Now uncertain gods are less known because they are less certain, and consequently unknown because they are less certain.

(Tertullian, *Against Marcion*, Book I, ch. 9.) If you wish to know the words by which those unknown gods were invoked, Gellius, Macrobius, and Brissonius will teach you (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, [Col. 0591A] Book II, ch. 28; Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, Book III, ch. 9; Brissonius, *On Formulas*, Book I, p. 120).

You may perhaps be astonished that it occurred to some to suppose that Caecilius’ word *Manibus* (“to the Manes”) had been corrupted and that *inanibus* (“to empty gods”) should be written in its place. Nothing could have been invented more contrary to the authority of the Royal manuscript codex and to the thought of the author. For who, except in a dream, could have suspected that Caecilius—who there plays the part of a pagan against a Christian—seriously said that the Romans had erected altars to “empty” gods?

But to what purpose, they ask, are the Manes mentioned there, or to whom could they have been unknown? What, then, if the adjective “unknown” refers only to the substantive “divine powers”? Caecilius mentions the Manes there because the pagans frequently invoked them and [Col. 0591B] offered them sacrifices. Nor were they, as these critics maintain, so well known to everyone. For, as Servius observes, some thought them heavenly gods, others gods below, others nocturnal gods wandering between heaven and earth. Servius and Lilius Giraldus say more about them (Servius on Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book III, p. 266; Giraldus, *History of the Gods*, Syntagma 7, p. 205). Nothing, therefore, should be changed in our author’s text, whether you call the Manes known or unknown.

Finally, Caecilius concludes: “Thus, while the Romans embrace the sacred rites of all nations, they have also merited kingdoms.” It is indeed true that the Romans embraced the rites of every nation. For Ovid sang it in this single verse:

> Rome is a place worthy for every god to enter.

[Col. 0591C] (Ovid, *Fasti*, Book I, § 3.) But we shall have another occasion to discuss these matters.

Caecilius and the other pagans thought that from what has thus far been said it could be concluded that their gods truly existed, because the Romans, “while embracing,” as he babbled, “the sacred rites of all nations, also merited kingdoms.” It cannot be denied that this argument would indeed carry no slight weight if it rested upon as much truth as it does falsehood. Tertullian fully perceived its force and set it forth without any concealment whatever in these words:

> I shall not omit the engagement provoked by the presumption of those who say that the Romans, as the reward of their most diligent religious devotion, were raised to so great a height that they took possession of the world; and that they are therefore worthy to flourish beyond all others because they perform their duty toward the gods [Col. 0591D] beyond all others.

(Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25.) But that most learned African writer demonstrates, as we shall see in the proper place, that this claim is false—indeed, utterly false. We must now examine how this very argument is refuted by our Minucius.

## ARTICLE II

### How Minucius refutes Caecilius’ argument and demonstrates that the Roman Empire was founded not at all upon the Romans’ religion toward the gods, but upon impiety and crimes; that the gods brought the Romans no assistance in extending the boundaries of their realm—not even, first, the foreign and immigrant gods: Thracian Mars, Cretan Jupiter, Argive, Samian, and Punic Juno, Tauric Diana, or the Idaean Mother

[Col. 0592A]

Minucius suppresses the ridiculous boasting about the Romans’ piety toward their gods as easily as they rashly boasted that, through their singular and most chaste worship of those gods, they had merited dominion over the whole world. For whether one considers the beginnings or the advancement of any people, none exhibited greater impiety than the Romans. [Col. 0592B] Everyone certainly knows that their first origin was derived from nothing other than a multitude of depraved men—criminals, incestuous offenders, murderers, and brigands—gathered into an asylum, and that Romulus laid the foundations of his city in fratricide (Augustine, *City of God*, Book III, ch. 6, p. 62; Livy and others). Soon afterward he “carried off other men’s maidens without precedent,” as Minucius says—that is, without any example or good custom. By this form of expression he alludes to Virgil’s line:

> . . . And the Sabine women carried off without precedent.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book VIII, v. 655.)

Romulus then waged war upon “their parents, that is, upon his own fathers-in-law.” These words are indeed read in the Royal codex. We therefore do not see why some perversely conjecture [Col. 0592C] that they were inserted into the text. Add that all these points thus far mentioned are confirmed by the testimony of Cyprian, who excerpted them from this very passage of our author (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 16). Since no one disputes that these things are most certain, Minucius rightly concludes against Caecilius that nothing could be “more irreligious,” criminal, and impious than these beginnings of the Romans.

He continues and shows that the Romans extended their empire with the same impiety with which they had begun it: namely, by overthrowing neighboring cities together with their temples and altars; by compelling captured enemies to become partners in their crimes; and by “constructing all their temples from spoils of war—that is, from the ruins of cities, the plunder of gods, and the slaughter of priests,” to use Minucius’ words. Therefore, [Col. 0592D] as he further says, “the Romans committed an act of impiety as often as they celebrated a triumph. They took as many spoils from gods as trophies from nations.” From this he draws the most fully justified conclusion: “The Romans are therefore not so great because they are religious, but because they are sacrilegious with impunity.”

I should like you to consider whether Minucius, when writing this, had not looked to Tertullian’s *Apology*. For in that book, after assailing and crushing the same boast of the pagans, Tertullian completed his argument with this similar conclusion:

> The Romans, therefore, were not religious before they were great; consequently, they were not great because they were religious. Indeed, how could they be great because of religion when their greatness arose from irreligion? For unless I am mistaken, every kingdom or empire [Col. 0593A] is acquired through wars and extended through victories. Wars and victories, moreover, ordinarily depend upon the capture and overthrow of cities. That business cannot be conducted without injury to the gods. The destruction of walls and temples is the same; the slaughter of citizens and priests is equal; nor is the plunder of sacred and profane wealth dissimilar. The Romans therefore have as many acts of sacrilege as trophies, as many triumphs over gods as over nations, and as many spoils of war as there still remain images of captive gods.

(Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 25, p. 28).
