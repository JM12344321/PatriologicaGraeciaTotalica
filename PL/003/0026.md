[Note: [Col. 0181D] “But the Epistle of Paul—which ones, from what place, or for what reason they were sent—he himself makes clear to those wishing to understand.” Freindaller corrects this, as I later saw, to “the epistles,” “sent,” “to those wishing,” and “they themselves.” I myself would prefer to restore “he makes clear” in place of “they make clear,” on account of the verb “forbidding” in what follows.

“A schism of heresy.” A rather unusual verbal expression. [Col. 0182B] In Greek, σχίσμα τῆς αἱρέσεως, “a schism of the heresy.” Shortly afterward, Freindaller restored “Galatians,” which is indeed read below, in place of “Callactians.”

“In the order of the Scriptures.” That is, unless I am mistaken, he interpreted the Scriptures of the Old Testament in their order, one after another. Freindaller says: “The words ‘in the order of the Scriptures’ do not appear to suggest anything other than that Paul here advanced his arguments from the old Law in order to establish the new, and subordinated the former to the latter in due order.”

“Beginning.” That is, ἡ ἀρχή, “the beginning, foundation.”

“Following the order of his predecessor John.” This is ineptly written, if the author means—as is nevertheless far from certain—that Paul imitated the Apocalypse; for, as Stosch plausibly determined in his *Commentary on the Canon of the Books of the New Testament*, chapter V, §62, the Apocalypse was published under Domitian and must not be carried back to the time of Claudius or [Col. 0182C] Nero—although, as I have said, some distinguished men have indeed maintained this. Freindaller further says that he believes John was called Paul’s predecessor by the author “because he preceded him in his calling to the apostolate; and that this designation corresponds in meaning to the other term, ‘Elder,’ which John himself employed in his epistles. That interpretation, certainly not discordant with the truth, plainly restores the order of the whole passage, since the comparison between the Pauline and Apocalyptic epistles now concerns not the date of each group, but only the number seven.”

“First . . . second,” etc., etc. Perhaps one should read “firstly . . . secondly,” and so thereafter.

*Correbtione*. Thus in the old manner for *correptione*, “correction.”

“Nevertheless, it is recognized that there is one Church throughout the whole world.” Tertullian, *On the Prescription of Heretics*, chapter 20: “Every kind must necessarily be classified according to its origin. Accordingly, so many and such great churches are one: that first Church from the Apostles, from which all derive. [Col. 0182D] Thus all are first, and all are apostolic, while they are one; all demonstrate unity.”

“Although he writes to seven churches,” etc. Stosch, in the work cited a little above, §59: “I seem to myself to have found in this yet another trace of the second century, or of the beginning of the third: the author of the fragment, in imitation of John’s epistles to the seven churches of Asia, similarly reduces to seven the number of churches to which the apostle Paul wrote epistles. For one may see from Cyprian, book I of the *Testimonies against the Jews*, chapter 20, that at the beginning of the third century there were people who sought some mystery in this. Likewise, Cyprian writes, in the first book of *Kings*: ‘The barren woman bore seven, and she who had very many children grew weak. The seven sons, however, are the seven churches. Hence Paul also wrote to seven churches, and the Apocalypse sets forth seven churches, so that the number seven might be preserved: like the seven days in which God made the world, and like the seven angels [Col. 0183A] who stand before the face of God.’” See also the same Cyprian’s *Exhortation to Martyrdom*, p. 270, Benedictine edition. “Nor perhaps will the thought be inept if we say that the reason why doubts raised against the Epistle to the Hebrews gained weight among some of the ancients was this alleged mystery of the number seven, which they wished by every means to preserve intact with respect to the churches to which Paul [Col. 0183B] wrote. Hence also others, who did not doubt the Epistle to the Hebrews, preferred to say that Paul wrote twice seven epistles rather than that he wrote fourteen.” Thus Stosch.

Freindaller says that “perhaps the Epistle to the Hebrews is found to have been omitted for this reason, lest the excess offend against that number. He could, moreover, rightly leave it unmentioned here even if he attributed it to Paul, because the Hebrews, or Jewish Christians, to whom it was sent were indeed intermingled with the individual communities, but did not constitute a distinct community.” Thus he argued, and plausibly.

But more on this Epistle below. Freindaller also rightly observed that by the second century the Apocalyptic epistles were already regarded as catholic—that is, as epistles directed to the universal (ὅλην, “whole”) Church. The Church is called “sevenfold” by Optatus in book II of *On the Donatist Schism*, chapter 3, p. 93, Dupin’s edition. But, to omit the passages of other ancient writers [Col. 0183C] who, with Augustine in book VII of *The City of God*, chapter 4, maintain that the perfection of the universal Church was signified by the number seven, I shall note only this: Jesus Christ, who undoubtedly abides perpetually in the midst of all the churches, was seen by John in the Apocalypse, chapter 1, verse 13, in the midst of seven lampstands—that is, churches.

I must now present words of Victorinus very closely related to this passage of our fragment, which I have taken from the commentary on the Apocalypse attributed to him: “Paul taught that throughout the whole world all seven churches—and the seven that are named—are one catholic Church. And first, indeed, so that he too might preserve the pattern of seven churches, he did not exceed the number. But he wrote to the Romans, to the Corinthians, to the Galatians, to the Ephesians, to the Thessalonians, to the Philippians, and to the Colossians. Afterward he wrote to individual persons, so that he would not exceed the measure of seven churches. And, condensing his preaching into a brief statement, he says thus to Timothy: ‘That you may know how you ought to conduct yourself in the Church of the living God’ (1 Tim. 3:15).” Thus [Col. 0183D] Victorinus, chapter 1.

Finally, since this number seven was regarded among the ancient Pythagoreans as both perfect and especially suited to religion—whence the *Etymologicum Magnum*: Ἑπτὰς, ἀπὸ τοῦ σέβω, σεπτάς τις οὖσα, ὡς θεία, καὶ ἀμήτωρ, καὶ πάρθενος, “*Heptas* (‘seven’), from *sebō* (‘I revere’), being something august, as divine, motherless, and virgin”—Glycas, pursuing similar ideas in his *Annals*, maintains that the Holy Spirit descended from heaven on the tenth day after Christ had risen because the number ten is also perfect; part 3, p. 225, Labbe’s edition.]

“They have been sanctified in the ordering of ecclesiastical discipline.” Tertullian says at the end of the last book *Against Marcion*: “This Epistle alone—namely, that to the Philippians—was aided by its own brevity, so that it escaped Marcion’s falsifying hands. Yet I marvel that he accepted a letter written to one man, Philemon, but rejected the two to Timothy and the one to Titus, composed concerning ecclesiastical order.” Thus he. Note that at least one of these epistles was sent to several people.

Moreover, to speak of all the Pauline epistles: since all his epistles were either written or signed by Paul’s own hand (see Gal. 6:11 and 2 Thess. 3:18), and most of them were sent to very populous churches that continued flourishing for a long time afterward, in which it was customary for those very epistles to be read aloud (compare Col. 4:16 with the fragments of Dionysius of Corinth, p. 168, vol. I of the *Reliquiae*), [Col. 0184A] and since Paul [Col. 0184B] himself later visited most of those same churches, there can be no doubt that no other author—not even among more recent writers who have already died—can have his own work attributed to him on weightier and more certain evidence. Add that, from apostolic times onward, all the Fathers who survive have cited these thirteen epistles as witnesses and a rule of faith and religion. The matter of Paul’s Epistle to the Hebrews, which the author of the fragment omits, must be discussed presently.

“To the Laodiceans, another to the Alexandrians.” Perhaps it should be written: “To the Laodiceans one, another to the Alexandrians.”

Stosch writes as follows in *On the Canon of the Books of the New Testament*, §56: “First, then, there is mentioned a certain epistle of Paul to the Laodiceans, which pertains to the heresy of Marcion. Now it is established from Tertullian, *Against Marcion*, book V, chapters 11 and 17, and Epiphanius, *Heresy* XLII, that Marcion, who disseminated his heresy in the middle of the second century, had in his canon some epistle to the Laodiceans. And although it should almost be inferred from Tertullian’s words that it differed from the Epistle to the Ephesians only [Col. 0184C] in title, nevertheless, from the testimony of Epiphanius and from the fact that it was almost invariably Marcion’s custom to corrupt the genuine writings of the Apostles, there can scarcely be any doubt that Marcion’s epistle to the Laodiceans differed from Paul’s genuine Epistle to the Ephesians, and that it is the one intended by the author of the fragment.

“Joined to this Epistle to the Laodiceans is another of Paul to the Alexandrians, of which, says the celebrated Muratori, I do not know whether anyone else has made mention. Nor have I been able to find any mention of it among the ancients. It has therefore either completely perished, or perhaps was mentioned by the ancients under another title, since it is certain that several titles were often given to one and the same apocryphal book. See the celebrated Beausobre’s *Dissertation on the Apocryphal Books of the New Testament*, §10; or perhaps it still lies somewhere unpublished. Kirsten certainly testifies that several epistles of Paul, still unknown to us, are found among the Arabs. See Fabricius, *Apocryphal Codex of the New Testament*, part II, p. 920.” Thus Stosch.

Jerome says at the end of chapter 5 of *On Illustrious Men*: “Some also read the Epistle to the Laodiceans, but it is rejected by everyone.” [Col. 0184D] Philastrius, however, has material concerning this epistle worthy of being presented here, in his *Heresy* LXXXIX: “Others, however, say that the Epistle to the Hebrews is by Luke the Evangelist; and that an Epistle to the Laodiceans was also written. And because some people with unsound opinions added certain things to it, it is therefore not read in the Church. And although it is read by some, nevertheless only thirteen Epistles of Paul himself are read publicly in the Church, and occasionally the Epistle to the Hebrews”; p. 169, Fabricius’s edition.

Meanwhile, a singular instance occurs in Abbot Ælfric’s treatise on the Old and New Testaments, composed in the time of Edgar, king of England: after Ælfric had placed each and every book of the New Testament in the canon of Scripture, he added to them only Paul’s Epistle to the Laodiceans. See p. 28, Lisle’s edition, 1623.

Finally, there exists a certain very brief epistle called Paul’s Epistle to the Laodiceans, but it differs from the ancient work and is pieced together from the other Pauline epistles; it was reprinted by Fabricius in the *Apocryphal Codex of the New Testament*, vol. II, p. 873.

“According to the heresy of Marcion.” In Greek, κατὰ τὴν αἵρεσιν τοῦ Μαρκίωνος, “according to the heresy of Marcion.” Ancient Glosses: “according to,” “toward,” κατά. [Col. 0185A]

“Here, moreover,” says Freindaller, “there occurs another spurious Epistle of Paul bearing the superscription ‘To the Alexandrians’; what judgment should be made concerning it was indeed unknown to Muratori himself, nor do I remember ever having encountered anything about a similar Epistle. Our author further records that these letters, both to the Laodiceans and to the Alexandrians, were fabricated for the sake of confirming the heresy of the Marcionites, with the result that a definite date can be assigned to their origin. What is more, Epiphanius’s [Col. 0185B] testimony also agrees with this, since in *Heresy* XLII he writes expressly that Marcion borrowed support from the Epistle to the Laodiceans in order to reinforce his errors.”]

But one Epistle to Philemon, one to Titus, and two to Timothy [Note: [Col. 0183A] “Two.” Freindaller.], written from affection and love, have nevertheless been sanctified in honor of the catholic Church, for the ordering of ecclesiastical discipline. Routh.] of Marcion, as well as several others, which cannot be received into [Col. 0185A] the catholic Church. For it is not fitting that gall be mixed with honey.

[Note: [Col. 0185B] “Cannot be received.” According to the form of the Greek expression, παραλαμβάνεσθαι οὐ δυνατόν ἐστι, “it is not possible for it to be received.” Freindaller wishes to restore “they cannot” in place of “it cannot.”

“For it is not fitting that gall be mixed with honey.” The elder mentioned by Irenaeus, p. 42, vol. I of the *Reliquiae*, employed a similar metaphor: “Plaster is badly mixed with the milk of God.”

“And two of the above-mentioned John.” Read “two,” as above at line 8.

Stosch writes as follows in *On the Canon of the Books of the New Testament*, §53: “I infer that the author of this fragment should be assigned to the end of the second century or the beginning of the third, because in it there occur quite a few statements concerning the books of the New Testament that conform perfectly to the opinions of certain Christians of that period. Namely:

“[Col. 0185C] I. In this canon of the sacred books, the Epistle to the Hebrews and the Epistle of James are omitted, and only two Epistles of the apostle John are mentioned. Now the clearest testimonies establish that, toward the end of the second century, both the Epistle to the Hebrews and that of James, and even John’s third Epistle, were not included in the canon by some. For, as regards the Epistle to the Hebrews, Stephen Gobarus, in Photius, *Library*, codex 232, testifies that Irenaeus and his disciple Hippolytus did not regard it as a genuine work of Paul: Ὅτι Ἱππόλυτος καὶ Εἰρηναιοῖς τὴν πρὸς Ἑβραῖους ἐπιστολὴν Παύλου, οὐκ ἐκείνου εἶναι φασίν, ‘Hippolytus and Irenaeus say that Paul’s Epistle to the Hebrews is not his.’ In the same work, codex 121, from Hippolytus’s book *Against Heresies*: Λέγει δὲ ἀλλά τέ τινα τῆς ἀκριβείας λειπόμενα, καὶ ὅτι ἡ πρὸς Ἑβραίους ἐπιστολὴ οὐκ ἔστι τοῦ ἀποστόλου Παύλου, ‘He also says certain other things deficient in accuracy, and that the Epistle to the Hebrews is not by the apostle Paul.’

“Concerning Gaius, the Roman presbyter, as Muratori already observed, Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VI, chapter 20, [Col. 0185D] likewise reports that, in his disputation against Proclus, a defender of the Cataphrygian heresy, he mentions only thirteen Epistles of the divine Apostle, not numbering among the rest the one inscribed to the Hebrews. What Origen thought of it, see above, §48”—where Stosch shows that Origen regarded this Epistle as a little work conveying Paul’s thought.

“That the Epistle of James, moreover, was not included in the canon by everyone at about the same time is attested by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, chapter 23, at the end. Certainly very few of the earlier writers made mention either of this Epistle or of that of Jude, which is itself numbered among the seven catholic epistles. As for John’s third Epistle, it is established that it too was regarded as doubtful by some. Irenaeus certainly cites John’s first and second Epistles, but makes no mention of the third. See Lardner, *Credibility of the Gospel History*, part II, vol. I, chapter 17, pp. 301–302.” Thus Stosch.

[Col. 0186A] I. Concerning the Epistle to the Hebrews, however, I should like to add the following: Tertullian attributed it to Barnabas, not Paul, in *On Modesty*, chapter 20; Jerome also records this concerning Tertullian in *On Illustrious Men*, chapter 5. Nor had that Epistle been received into the Latin canon even in Jerome’s time. Among other passages, his Epistle to Dardanus, p. 69, Froben’s edition, should be consulted.

Nevertheless, it is not established that Irenaeus or his disciple Hippolytus, whom Stosch names above, cast this Epistle out of the number of sacred books, since, according to the testimony of [Col. 0186B] Eusebius, book V of the *History*, chapter 26, that same Irenaeus cited certain passages from it in his work *Various Discussions*. Origen too wrote commentaries on this Epistle.

Moreover, in the passage just cited, Jerome shows Dardanus that it was accepted as the Epistle of the apostle Paul by the churches of the East and by all writers in the Greek language. During my reading, I have certainly seen this done clearly and openly by the authors whom I shall name here, all of whom, except St. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, and Eusebius, lived before the end of the third century: namely, Clement of Alexandria—although the Epistle was also cited by Clement of Rome, who lived in the first century, in chapters 17 and 36; furthermore, by the ancient presbyter in the same Clement of Alexandria, whom I placed at p. 472, vol. I of the *Reliquiae*; by Origen, both elsewhere and in his *Epistle to Africanus*, chapter 9; by Dionysius of Alexandria; by the bishops of Antioch in their Epistle to Paul of Samosata, placed at pp. 473–474 of vol. II; by Peter of Alexandria [Col. 0186C] in his ninth Canon; and finally by Eusebius himself in *The Martyrs of Palestine*, chapter 11.

To Clement of Rome, who did not append Paul’s own name, should be added Pinytus in his Epistle to Dionysius of Corinth, p. 132, vol. I of the *Reliquiae*; Theognostus, head of the catechetical school in the city of Alexandria; St. Methodius, in the *Banquet of the Virgins*, pp. 90 and 96, Combefis’s edition; and Manes and Archelaus in the *Disputation*, chapters 5, 34, 41, and 53.

II. As regards the Epistle of James—which Origen, like that of Jude, omitted at least from his catalogue of the sacred books in Eusebius, book VI, chapter 25—I must point out that the words of Eusebius mentioned by Stosch are, in Greek, οὐ πολλοὶ τῶν παλαίων αὐτῆς ἐμνημόνευσαν, “not many of the ancients made mention of it.” Valesius translated them “very few of the earlier writers,” but scarcely accurately, although perhaps one might have spoken in that way even in Eusebius’s time. For, if there are any besides Irenaeus, book IV, chapter 16 or 30, there are few surviving writers earlier than Eusebius who expressly cited this Epistle; [Col. 0186D] nevertheless, some other surviving writers among them may allude to it, especially where passages of the Old Testament are quoted in it.

Cosmas Indicopleustes certainly reports, in *On the World*, book VII, p. 292, Montfaucon’s edition, that the Epistle of James was received by the Syrians; and that same Epistle always existed in the Syriac version, as did one other of the seven ἀντιλεγόμεναι γραφαί, “disputed Scriptures,” namely the Epistle to the Hebrews.

III. Finally, concerning the Third Epistle of St. John, I shall add the following. Clement did indeed comment on the first two Epistles of this apostle, as Cassiodorus testifies in book I of the *Divine Readings*, chapter 8; indeed, the *Adumbrations* on these two Epistles attributed to Clement of Alexandria still survive, but not on the third. Nevertheless, Eusebius, book VI of the *History*, chapter 14, reports that in the books of the Ὑποτυπώσεις, or *Outlines*, Clement composed a concise exposition of all the Scriptures of both Testaments, not even omitting those writings concerning which many were in doubt: the Epistle of Jude, that of Barnabas, and the remaining catholic Epistles. [Col. 0187A] Therefore Clement, if Eusebius is an accurate witness, wrote commentaries on John’s third Epistle just as he did on the Epistle of James.

However the matter stands, this at least must certainly be inferred from the fact that the same Clement, in book II of the *Stromata*, chapter 15, when citing John’s first Epistle—which everyone had received from the time of Polycarp onward—described it as ἐν τῇ μείζονι ἐπιστολῇ, “in the longer Epistle”: [Col. 0187B] at least that second Epistle, in Clement’s judgment, must be attributed to John the apostle, not to the presbyter, to whom many in antiquity, according to Jerome, *On Illustrious Men*, chapter 9, and Eusebius, book III of the *History*, chapter 35, were accustomed to assign the two latter Epistles. Jerome himself, however, did not agree; for, as is clear from his Epistles to Paulinus, p. 9 of Froben’s edition, and to Evagrius, p. 334, he attributed both to John the apostle.

Meanwhile, I showed above, at the Seventh Council of Carthage, p. 166, vol. III of the *Reliquiae*, which writers before Cyprian’s time accepted the second Epistle. To these I shall now add Alexander of Alexandria, an author before the Council of Nicaea, who wrote in his Epistle preserved by Socrates, book I of the *History*, chapter 6.

It must, however, be admitted that I have encountered no pre-Nicene writer—perhaps because of the Epistle’s brevity—who cited the third Epistle, except one who, after mentioning it, also added either his own doubt or at least that of others concerning the γνησιότης, “genuineness,” of both this and the second Epistle. But enough of this.

[Col. 0187C] Meanwhile, how does it happen—and this is more surprising—that the author of this fragment is silent concerning the First Epistle of St. Peter? According to Eusebius, and according to Origen himself as quoted by Eusebius, it is reckoned among the ὁμολογούμεναι γραφαί, “acknowledged Scriptures”; that is, it was received by everyone, always, and everywhere. So many Fathers, from the time of Polycarp and Papias onward, cited it in their works. Both Stosch and Muratori pass over the matter in silence, leaving it entirely untouched.

I can scarcely think anyone would say that the Church in which this anonymous writer on the canon lived did not yet know St. Peter’s Epistle, for he flourished in the middle of the second century. The celebrated Freindaller does indeed say, as I now find: “The two Epistles of Peter are passed over here, although the first is already found to have been generally received in the author’s own time; likewise the Epistle of James, which could not have been unknown to an author living at Rome; and finally John’s third Epistle is left entirely unmentioned. But, on the one hand, it must be considered that we are dealing here with a fragment which in a great many places lacks completeness of sense, [Col. 0187D] and that for this very reason the question is still unresolved whether these portions of the Bible were mentioned in a preceding or following part of the text, and whether mention of them is missing today solely because of the mutilated condition of the work. On the other hand, it must not be concealed that not all the divine books were received in all churches at the same time, and that Eusebius himself individually numbers the Epistle of James, the Second of Peter, and the Third of John among the disputed writings.”

Thus he. Here I shall note only that Eusebius, who more than once calls the aforesaid books of Holy Scripture τοὺς ἀντιλεγομένους, “the disputed books,” adds γνωρίμους δ` οὖν ὅμως τοῖς πολλοῖς, “yet nevertheless known to most,” and ὅμως δὲ ἐν πλείσταις ἐκκλησίαις παρὰ πολλοῖς δεδημοσιευμένους, “nevertheless publicly circulated by many in most churches.”

However the matter stands, and for whatever reason St. Peter’s Epistle was omitted, the author certainly mentioned the Epistle of Jude, although everyone knows that Eusebius counted it among the ἀντιλεγόμεναι γραφαί, “disputed Scriptures.” In book II of the *History*, chapter 23, in the passage cited above by Stosch, Eusebius also says that not many of the earlier writers made mention of this Epistle.

[Col. 0188A] Among those few I have seen Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen cite it as a book of Holy Scripture; perhaps also the Council of Antioch held against Paul of Samosata, and the author against Novatian, who at least repeats statements that Jude himself had made. To these writers must now at last be added the author of this fragment, who also confirms by his own approval, as you have seen, two further books among those same seven disputed books of Holy Scripture: the Apocalypse and the Second Epistle [Col. 0188B] of John.

Moreover—and this is more important—as I have already observed, this same writer has intruded no ὑποβολιμαῖον, “supposititious,” work into the canon of the New Testament except the Revelation of Peter, while at the same time recording the mark of νοθεία, “spuriousness,” which others had affixed to that book. Furthermore, this writer of ours has enumerated all the ὁμολογούμεναι γραφαί, “acknowledged Scriptures,” with only one omitted, namely that Epistle of St. Peter.]

[Col. 0186A] Certainly the Epistle of Jude and two of the above-mentioned John [Note: [Col. 0185A] “Two.” Freindaller.], [Col. 0187A] cannot be received into the catholic Church.

[Note: [Col. 0188B] “In the catholic.” In Greek, τῇ καθολικῇ, “in the catholic,” with the word ἐκκλησίᾳ, “Church,” understood, as sometimes happens. Examples occur both in Tertullian’s *On the Prescription of Heretics*, chapter 30, and frequently elsewhere in writers later than Tertullian, such as Optatus and Augustine. See also the notes on the fragments of St. Cornelius, p. 30, vol. III.

In the Theodosian Code too, book XVI, title II, *On Bishops*, law 4, the emperor Constantine has the expression “the most holy council of the catholic.” Likewise, in the *Code of the African Canons*, no. 109, the following occurs: “Likewise, if a bishop has been converted from the party of Donatus to the catholic,” etc.; the Greek translation reads: Ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ ἐὰν ἐπίσκοπος ἐκ τῶν τοῦ Δονάτου μερῶν πρὸς τὴν καθολικὴν ἐπιστρέψῃ, “Likewise, if a bishop from the party of Donatus should turn to the catholic,” [Col. 0188C] etc.

“Wisdom, written by the friends of Solomon . . . .” Since this work was included in the Version of the Seventy [the Septuagint], and was designated by the name *Wisdom* not only by Methodius—cited among the fragments of Melito, p. 128, vol. I—but also by Clement of Alexandria and others more ancient than Methodius, the earliest Christians, like our author, cited the same book at least as ecclesiastical and useful, although of uncertain origin and unknown to the Jewish canon.

Yet the statement of the fragment’s author that the book was composed by the friends of Solomon is certainly somewhat improbable, novel, and unheard of; according to Jerome’s testimony in the Preface to Proverbs, this book is nowhere found among the Hebrews; p. 26.

Meanwhile Freindaller observed: “By what reasoning the Book of Wisdom—unless perhaps a different work is meant here—obtained a place among the Scriptures of the New Covenant escapes the acuity of criticism. But the Psalms too are mentioned a little below in this fragment, although touched upon only in the most cursory fashion.”

“Apocalypse.” Perhaps *Apocalypses*, but Freindaller prefers to rewrite it as *Apocalypsim*.

“And only that of Peter do we receive, which some of our people do not wish to be read in the Church.” “These words,” says Muratori in the passage cited above, p. 853, “fit well with the time of Gaius. For Eusebius, book III, chapter 25, numbers the Apocalypse of Peter among the disputed books, but does not reject it as the offspring of heretics. According to the same witness, book VI, chapter 14, Clement of Alexandria also used this Apocalypse, just as he did the Epistle of Barnabas and the catholic Epistles. Sozomen likewise informs us, book VII, chapter 19, that this Apocalypse was still read once every year in certain churches of Palestine.”

Thus Muratori. In a certain catalogue of Holy Scriptures, first published by Cotelier in his *Judgment on the Epistle of St. Barnabas*, I see four other books mentioned outside the canonical books of the New Testament: the Epistle of Barnabas, the *Shepherd*, the *Acts of Paul*, and the *Revelation of Peter*. Eusebius, in the passage previously cited by Muratori, placed these same works—[Col. 0189A] with the addition of the book entitled *The Teaching of the Apostles*—after the acknowledged and disputed Scriptures, as books indeed νόθοι, “spurious,” while wholly omitting that immense heap of ψευδεπίγραφα, “falsely attributed writings,” composed by heretics.

Nevertheless, the brief passages translated from the *Apocalypse of Peter* in the *Eclogues* attributed to Clement of Alexandria are too frivolous for the book to possess even the slightest claim to reverence. See §§49–50, p. 207, Combefis’s *Latest Supplement to the Fathers*.

Perhaps, meanwhile, some may think that the relative *quas*, “which” in the plural, should be restored here in place of *quam*, “which” in the singular, so that the Apocalypse of John might also be included. It is well known that at that time there were some people unfavorably disposed toward this book. [Col. 0189B] The words concerning the *Shepherd* of Hermas that immediately follow persuade me that the subject here is reception by the Church among the divine Scriptures, not merely reading in sacred assemblies, as in the final canon of the Council of Laodicea, where the Apocalypse is omitted. Compare throughout the passage of Philastrius that I cited above.

I stated earlier, in connection with the fragments of Gaius, p. 16, vol. II, how many writers either older than Gaius the presbyter or at least of his own time accepted the book of the Apocalypse; I shall now append their names. They are Papias, Justin Martyr, the brethren of Vienne and Lyons together with Irenaeus, Melito, Theophilus of Antioch, Apollonius, Hippolytus, Tertullian, and Clement of Alexandria.

It is fitting to show from Clement’s little work entitled *Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?* that this last Father attributed the Apocalypse to John the apostle. For in its final section it is reported that John the apostle, after the tyrant’s death, returned from the island of Patmos to Ephesus; and John, the author of the Apocalypse, himself mentions this very exile in chapter 1, verse 9.

[Col. 0189C] I omit the other writers down to Dionysius of Alexandria, who indeed attributed the book to John the presbyter rather than to the apostle, yet by no means excluded this divine work from the sacred books; see Eusebius, *History*, book VI, chapter 22. Indeed, in the fragment of his Epistle to Hermammon preserved by the same Eusebius, book VII, chapter 20, Dionysius cited words from it as from that prophetic and Θεόπνευστος, “divinely inspired,” book. Καὶ τοι Ἰωάννης, he says, ὁμοίως ἀποκαλύπτεται, etc., “And indeed John is similarly given a revelation,” etc.

“The *Shepherd*, however, was written very recently in our own times,” etc. Against the opinion of those who would have Hermas, the author of the book called the *Shepherd*, be the Hermas whom St. Paul mentions at the end of the Epistle to the Romans, chapter 16, verse 14—or at least an exceedingly ancient writer who lived before the destruction of Jerusalem—the celebrated Mosheim, in *On the Affairs of the Christians before Constantine the Great*, century I, §54, adduces an argument from the very ancient author of the poem *Against Marcion*, which is attributed to Tertullian. Near the end of book III are these lines:

> And now, in the ninth place, Hyginus received the chair;
>
> [Col. 0189D] Then after him came Pius, to whom Hermas was a brother by birth.
