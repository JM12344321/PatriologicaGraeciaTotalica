Addition to the law. For, to the sixth commandment of the Decalogue, by which adultery and so forth are forbidden, he also added a new commandment, by which even the inward will, although it has not been completed in deed, is made subject to guilt.

At close quarters. Or, in the act itself.

Would have defiled. “Everyone who looks at a woman in order to lust after her has already committed adultery with her in his heart,” says Christ, Matthew 5:28.

To administer, i.e., to act, or in any manner whatsoever to contribute to the action.

Cancels out [*expungit*]. This word, which our author uses frequently, must be examined somewhat more carefully, since, unless it is properly understood, it can sometimes create great obscurity. First of all, it should be noted that creditors formerly wrote the names of their debtors upon wax tablets with a stylus. But when those debts had been discharged and the creditor’s claim thereby dissolved, they were accustomed either to pierce those names with points or to erase them with the reversed stylus, and thus to cancel them out [*expungere*]. Hence it came about [Col. 1215B] that this word, by a metaphor derived from that practice, was used to mean “to fulfill” and “to complete.”

But let us now produce examples from our Tertullian himself, and first of all the passage that he has in Book I *Against the Jews*, chapter 11, which is clearer than the rest: “Nor should they call into doubt or deny the things that we bring forward as written—so that those things foretold as destined to occur after Christ may be recognized as fulfilled. For,” he continues, “the dispensation would not be found completed [*expuncta*] unless he had come, after whom the things proclaimed were to be completed, and thereby proved to have been fulfilled.” So too in the same work, chapter 4, he has: “The abolition of fleshly circumcision and of the Old Law is shown to have been completed”—fulfilled and consummated—“in its own times.” Again, chapter 9: “The prophecy is understood to have been proclaimed just as it is believed to have been completed,” i.e., fulfilled. Likewise, chapter 12: “They will not have been prophesied concerning anyone other than him through whom we perceive them to have been completed.”

The word also recurs several times in the *Apology Against the Gentiles*, as in chapter 2: “The guilty man must discharge the debt of punishment”—i.e., that debt must be fulfilled by him [Col. 1215C], or the sentence pronounced against him must be carried into execution—“not be exempted from it.” And in chapter 15 he says that lust is “discharged,” that is, satisfied or completed. Likewise, chapter 20: “Prophecy, when foretelling the future among men, has but one tense, if perhaps it is distinguished while it is being fulfilled,” etc. And in chapter 21 he says of Christ, “who has now fulfilled [what was foretold] in the lowliness of the human condition.” Also in chapter 35: “For why do we, chaste, sober, and upright men, fulfill the vows and rejoicings of the Caesars?” Finally, chapter 48: “Then the whole human race will be restored, to discharge what it has deserved, whether good or evil, in this age.”

But let us examine the rest of his books as well. Thus in the book *On Patience*, chapter 14, he has: “He fulfilled every form of patience against every power of the devil.” And in the book *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 1: “The soldiers crowned with laurel were checked off in the camp.” For when they passed by in numerical order to receive the donative, after receiving it the names of the individuals were either marked with a point or wholly crossed out, as persons for whom that distribution had now been concluded and completed. In the book *Exhortation to Martyrdom*, chapter 6: “How many have been killed by robbers [Col. 1215D] with the sword, by enemies even upon the cross, first tortured, and indeed subjected to every indignity?” That is, with every indignity as it were consummated upon them. And in Book II *Against Marcion*, chapter 20: “He satisfied his people, in the straits of their expedition, with some consolation of tacit compensation,” or “fulfilled their need.” Thus in the book *On Prayer*, chapter 1, he calls the Gospel “the fulfiller of all former antiquity,” since in it, through Christ, all the things prefigured in the Old Law were fulfilled and consummated. Finally, in the book *On Idolatry*, chapter 16, he says: “If neither in the invocation nor in the sacrifice there is any designation of duty and fulfillment of my service”—that is, its performance and the fulfillment of the duty—“what then?” etc. Here you see that almost all these passages return to the same meaning. Therefore, by the phrase “the will fulfills the effect,” he meant that the very will to bring something about is already regarded as the deed itself fulfilled, as the context itself demonstrates. See also *On Prescription*, chapter 10.

[Col. 1216A] Satisfying. In the sense of filling out and completing.

May count as the deed, i.e., may be regarded as the deed.

## Notes on Chapter IV

Has pledged. Therefore, no kind of sin, however grave, is excluded from the hope of pardon and the fruit of repentance. This is again a clear indication that, when Tertullian wrote these words, he had not yet been corrupted by Montanus. For here he makes no distinction, as he does in the book *On Modesty*, chapter 2—when he was already an open Montanist—between remissible and irremissible sins. “According to this difference among offenses,” he continues there, “the condition of repentance is likewise distinguished. One kind will be able to obtain pardon, namely for a remissible offense; another will in no way be able to obtain it, namely for an irremissible offense.” He then proceeds to examine the various kinds of sins. But perhaps we shall treat this matter more fully elsewhere.

Repent, i.e., perform repentance; for in accordance with [Col. 1216B] this idiom our Vulgate translator twice uses *poeniteor* in the sense of a deponent verb: namely, Mark 1:15, where Christ says, “Repent” (the Greek has μετανοεῖτε, “repent”), “and believe the Gospel”; likewise Acts 3:19, where St. Peter says to the people, “Repent” (μετανοήσατε, “repent”) “and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out.” The Latins use the verb *poenitet* as an impersonal, more rarely as a personal verb, and scarcely ever as a deponent.

As I live. A formula by which God frequently swears by himself.

Preeminence. He uses this word in a bad sense for excellence, in the sense in which St. Paul, 1 Timothy 1:15, called himself the foremost among sinners, as though surpassing all others in the gravity of his sins.

I acknowledge. This was the voice of Tertullian while he still thought humbly of himself, not that of the proud Montanist insolently rising against the Church herself.

Faith, or faithfulness in saving. And hence [Col. 1216C] repentance was afterward called “the second plank after shipwreck.” St. Ambrose, or another author, beautifully imitated this passage in the *Epistle to a Fallen Virgin*, addressing her thus: “But you, who have now entered the contest of repentance, persevere bravely, wretched woman; cling to the plank like a SHIPWRECKED PERSON.” St. Jerome likewise, in Epistle VIII to Demetrias, speaking in the same way about repentance, says: “That plank, as it were, the SECOND AFTER SHIPWRECK for the wretched,” etc. And thereafter other holy Fathers adopted this phrase.

Will raise completely [*perlevabit*]. Some manuscripts read *perlavabit*, “will wash completely”; but unless I am mistaken, absurdly. For is someone who has sunk beneath the waves to be washed still further, or rather to be raised up and drawn out?

Will draw forward [*protelabit*]. *Protelare* means to advance or propel something *protelo*, that is, in a continuous draft, as oxen bound to the same yoke are accustomed to proceed with an equal and even impulse. Hence that long strap which binds them to the yoke was formerly called a *protelum*. From this comes Cato’s statement in *On Agriculture*: “Three oxen [Col. 1216D] will draw one plow in continuous draft.” There is no doubt that this word is of Greek origin and, as Donatus maintains, derived from πρό, “before,” and ἑλεῖν, “to draw.” Others derive it from τῆλε or τηλόθι, “far,” because in a *protelum* a rather lengthy drawing takes place. The word also has various figurative senses, concerning which the grammarians may be consulted, if one has leisure.

Unexpected. For the plank is sought in vain by a shipwrecked person unless it suddenly offers itself when hope is already at its last gasp.

Of minds, i.e., of wills. For will anyone value a servant’s obedience if it disagrees with his master’s will?

Through this, i.e., for this reason. Other manuscripts have “in proportion to this,” that is, “to so great a breadth of the subject.”

Constraints. Those, namely, of ability, time, or a treatment circumscribed in any other way.

It is good. And useful to us.

To listen. He takes it here in the sense of obeying. But I warn the [Col. 1217A] reader here to no purpose.

The advantage of the servant. Advantage, therefore, must be subordinated to the command. Yet this very thing is itself far more full of infinite advantage and reward: to have obeyed GOD when he commands, even without regard to advantage.

You turn it over. Or, you deliberate too anxiously within yourself; for that is what scrupulous people are accustomed to do.

He commands. What can be more powerful than these two little words, or more effective in thoroughly subjecting us so that we fulfill his commands?

He exhorts. But is “to exhort”—so familiar a word—greater than TO COMMAND? Indeed it is; for one who commands acts by his authority, but one who exhorts, even while commanding, is at the same time gracious: he presses his command, yet at the same time accommodates that very command to our weakness, and simultaneously shows that, in the very thing he has commanded, he is ready to help us fulfill it.

Salvation. This indeed can be wholly DIVINE and proper to the MERCY OF GOD alone.

[Col. 1217B] Saying, “As I live.” Passages of this kind are innumerable, especially in the Prophets.

By an oath. For “with a sworn oath.”

By an asseveration, i.e., by a promise, or perhaps better, by an assurance of fulfillment.

## Notes on Chapter V

To be unsealed. That is, after that repentance had been ratified and sealed by God through his grace, that seal must never again be broken by new sins, through expelling that grace once more.

Pretext. He also uses *praetextum* in the neuter gender in the book *On the Dress of Women*, chapter 9, where he says to the same women: “Do not claim unbridled license under the pretext of necessity,” etc. And in the book *Against the Valentinians*, chapter 9, he says: “under the pretext of affection for the Father,” etc.

You are separated, i.e., you withdraw from it, namely toward the knowledge of God.

You are glued to it. Made the more obstinate, the less you know.

[Col. 1217C] To fear. For just as the fear of the Lord is the beginning of WISDOM (Psalm 110:10), so too it is the beginning of repentance. Thus the Council of Trent, Session VI, *On Justification*, chapter 6, says concerning this: “They are disposed toward repentance when, understanding that they are sinners, they turn from the fear of DIVINE JUSTICE, by which they are profitably shaken, to consider the MERCY OF GOD,” etc. And again in Session XIV, chapter 4: “Attrition, conceived from fear of punishment, is an impulse of the HOLY SPIRIT by whose assistance the penitent prepares a way for himself toward justice.”

Obstinacy. Here he calls perseverance in evil “obstinacy,” by which the heart gradually hardens and wholly banishes the fear of God. For, as Ecclesiasticus 27:4 says, “Unless you hold yourself steadfastly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown.” Would that it could be restored from those most wretched ruins with equal ease and speed.

It is not permissible to remain ignorant. You will read nothing more elegant upon this subject than our Tertullian’s little book *On the Testimony of the Soul*, a work indeed weighty with substantial thoughts.

[Col. 1217D] By God’s gift. He refers again to what he had said above in chapter 1: “REASON IS A THING OF GOD.”

His rival. He meant the unhappy and excessively impudent ape of God. In the book *On Prescription*, chapter 40, you will read how this plotter against the human race long ago attempted to imitate the mysteries of God.

By returning. Or, by relapsing into former sins.

The Evil One. The devil, so called by way of eminence, as among the Greeks he is called ὁ Πονηρός, “the Evil One.” So too in the book *On Patience*, chapter 11, he says: “Joyful and widespread is the activity of the Evil One”; the context indicates that the devil is meant. And in the same work, chapter 14, he again says concerning him: “The Evil One was being torn apart, whom Job . . . was rending with great equanimity.” So too elsewhere. No less in the Lord’s Prayer, where in Matthew 6:13 Christ himself taught us, among other things, to pray, “but deliver us from evil,” the Greek text has ἀλλὰ ῥῦσαι ἡμᾶς ἀπὸ τοῦ πονηροῦ, “but deliver us from the Evil One”; by this expression most Latin as well as Greek Fathers understand the devil.

It is dangerous. As something scarcely to be set forth without [Col. 1218A] offending and scandalizing readers.

Edification. This is opposed to scandal: what scandal destroys, edification repairs.

After judging. This seems to mean the same as “with judgment,” which ought to be done only after serious deliberation and after the matter has been weighed on both sides.

Repentance. By which he now repents of his former repentance itself.

In act. The outward act, namely, as above, chapter 3, note “It is performed.”

In good faith, i.e., with the fear of God and faithfulness preserved unimpaired, and without prejudice to them.

With fear preserved. Bitter mockery!

It is a defense. He continues that sarcasm to this point.

Of hypocrites. Ὑπόκρισις otherwise means an actor’s impersonation, imitating the gestures of another person; hence an actor of this sort is also called a ὑποκριτής, “hypocrite” or “actor” [the printed reading *ὑποκρίσις* here is likely erroneous]. But in the idiom of Holy Scripture a hypocrite is one who outwardly displays uprightness in his conduct, but inwardly and beneath the skin, as they say, is wicked—[Col. 1218B] an unscrupulous pretender. Of such people the Poet says:

> Those who feign the Curii and live like Bacchanals.

Such above all were those against whom Christ warned us in Matthew 7:15: “Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep’s clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves.” The Evangelists say more about them.

## Notes on Chapter VI

Mediocrity, i.e., our modest ability, as those who think humbly of themselves are accustomed to say. See also above, chapter 3.

That every. Junius wishes *omnis* here to be understood as *omneis*, as the ancients used to say for *omnes*. But those who think *omnis* should be referred to τὸ salutis, “the matter of salvation,” defend themselves by another passage of Tertullian, in the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 8: “EVERYTHING is whole and complete, lacking no part of itself.”

To novices. Here he means catechumens recently admitted to the first rank and therefore still [Col. 1218C] weak in faith.

Hangs over these people, or is appointed and directed toward them.

To water their ears. A rather florid metaphor concerning those who were admitted to hear the word of God in the Church, being placed among the catechumens in the rank of Hearers, of whom a little below.

Whelps. A *catulus* properly means a young dog or puppy, although I find it rightly applied to all brute animals—but only viviparous ones—while they are still young.

Perfect, i.e., with sight not yet perfected, as in the proverb: “A hurrying bitch gives birth to blind puppies.”

They crawl. This is a Hellenism, as from the Greek ἄδηλα ἕρπουσι, “they crawl uncertainly” or “into uncertainty,” nowhere planting sure and firm steps. Similar is the expression in the book *On Patience*, chapter 1: “those who live blind things,” for “those who live blindly.”

Indeed. Either *se*, “themselves,” must be understood, or it must certainly be inserted here.

[Col. 1218D] To enclose, i.e., to confine and, as it were, secure. For dogs endeavor to keep their whelps within their lairs so that, if they thoughtlessly stray, they do not in their blindness run into some obstacle and thereby suffer injury.

Of desiring. Because they are shortly to put an end to their evil desires, renouncing them in Baptism and being transformed from the old person into the new.

To grow old. An apt and elegant comparison for repentance.

They flatter, i.e., they flatter the beauty of the fruits, which they have still retained in part, by taking pleasure in it. See the book *On Baptism*, chapter 3, note “But grace,” 1147 B, and chapter 9, “The grace of water,” 1171 D.

Of the immersion, i.e., of Baptism, as frequently elsewhere.

The intervening time. Or intermediate time, namely between their conversion and Baptism itself, while they are still among the catechumens.

Furlough [*commeatum*]. A military term signifying permission granted to soldiers whereby, for reasons of health or other business, they may be absent from camp for a time. It also sometimes denotes the very period during which a soldier, having gone elsewhere, is absent from camp; indeed it is occasionally used for the provisions given as traveling supplies. Numerous examples occur in our author, as in the *Apology*, chapter 32: “We know that the overwhelming force threatening the whole world . . . is delayed by the furlough”—the period that God, as supreme Commander, will grant it—“of the Roman Empire.” It is used in the same sense in the book *Against Praxeas*, chapter 1: “But it too will again be rooted out during this reprieve,” etc. Thus in the *Apology*, chapter 46, he says that “the opportunity for deliberation” is often frustrated. And in the book *On Flight in Persecution*, chapter 9, he wishes us “to gain a reprieve.” Again, in Book II *Against Marcion*, chapter 10, he says: “By measuring out an interval for his operations, he acted according to the rationale of his goodness.” Finally, in the book *On the Soul*, chapter 30: “Because this span of life would not have equaled”—that is, would not have been equal and duly proportionate to—“a millennium,” being, of course, a far shorter time. For the rest, after *commeatum* understand “more” or “rather”; perhaps [Col. 1219B] this word has fallen out of the comparison, or is absent by an ellipsis frequent among the Greeks.

Than repentance. The particle *quam*, “than,” appears to require deletion; it was perhaps the work of a drowsy copyist.

To await. It seems to be used for “to wait for,” as in the book *Against the Jews*, chapter 6, where he says of Christ’s future coming: “Since he has not yet come, he must be awaited, until his coming is manifest.”

To emancipate. This example—continued still further here—could readily occur to Tertullian, since he had formerly been an advocate and was thoroughly practiced in legal affairs.

To adjudge. In the sense of “to give.” This is again a term proper to the forum. Thus Samuel Pitiscus, in his *Lexicon*, under the solemn formula of praetorian law, “I GIVE, I DECLARE, I ADJUDGE” (*DO, DICO, ADDICO*), cites a certain law from the ancients: “Whichever magistrate shall preside over the administration of justice in that colony, to that magistrate shall belong jurisdiction concerning that matter, the appointment of a judge, and ADJUDICATION.” Otherwise, “to adjudge” [Col. 1219C] properly belongs to a seller.

Institutes. For “establishes.”

By compensation. Indeed, through repentance the divine right violated by sin is repaired, and the injury inflicted upon it is compensated, insofar as the act of repentance has Christ’s satisfaction for us and his merits intimately joined to itself. Many of the Scholastics subsequently taught—and some still teach today—that what they call the formal character of repentance consists in the compensation of the aforesaid injury.

Lest an adulterer. Something must certainly be supplied by ellipsis.

The truth. As something to be examined more fully below.

We are absolved. This expression is ironic, as though absolution itself were proof either that true repentance had already been performed or that it was to be performed.

While pending, i.e., while still suspended.

Is foreseen. As though still impending.

Has been changed. Has now been transferred into another status. In this sense our author says in the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 57: “Tell me, if you have changed your servant by granting him freedom,” etc.

[Col. 1219D] He charges it to himself. As recognizing himself still guilty of these things and therefore intending to perform repentance. But what was formerly more common among that thieving and fugitive class of men?

Discharged. Soldiers, after completing their lawful term of service—a specified number of years—were sent from camp or discharged from military service, thereafter released from their military oath. This was an honorable discharge; another kind, occasioned by illness or some other bodily defect, was called a medical discharge and was equivalent to the honorable one.

For marks, that is, military offenses by which he had been badly marked. If you prefer, you may take “marks” here as the punctures or brands with which recruits newly enrolled in military service were marked, so that, if they fled, they could be recognized by them and brought back beneath their standards and banners, to be subjected under military law to military punishment.

Vegetius, Book II, chapter 5, speaks thus of these marks: “Soldiers are inscribed with enduring [Col. 1220A] points in the skin”—points that will last and no longer be erased—“entered upon the rolls, and accustomed to swear the oath.” And Aëtius, a Greek physician, quoted by Lipsius in *On the Roman Military*, Book I, Dialogue 9, speaks as follows: Στίγματα καλοῦσι τὰ ἐπὶ τοῦ προσώπου, ἢ ἄλλου τινὸς μέρους τοῦ σώματος, ἐπιγραφόμενα, οἷα τῶν στρατευομένων ἐν τοῖς χερσί. That is: “They call ‘stigmata’ the marks inscribed upon the face or some other part of the body, such as those upon soldiers’ hands.” By these marks, therefore, soldiers were continually reminded of their duties for as long as they had to serve, and transgressors of those duties certainly had the gravest punishments to fear.

For those about to enter the water, or to be baptized. At that time Baptism was commonly performed by immersion, as our dogmatic theologians discuss at length. Thus the water was not then merely to be approached, but actually entered.

Safe. Secure and certain.

A sprinkling. Some prefer *asparginem*. We have said more about this word in the book *On Baptism*, chapter 5. For the rest, Tertullian seems here to allude to Baptism, [Col. 1220B] as it was formerly administered by sprinkling, especially to the clinically ill or bedridden; on this matter, consult again the dogmatic theologians treating the proximate matter of Baptism. It may also be understood thus: “even a single sprinkling”—that is, water sprinkled drop by drop—“not to speak of a whole abundance of water,” etc.

Will furnish. In the sense of “will impart.” It is not used in the legal sense in which someone lends his property to another for a time, to be returned afterward, but insofar as it signifies rendering to another an advantageous or useful service.

By theft. For “stealthily and secretly.”

To be circumvented. Or to be covered over with deceptions and cunning; in this sense someone is also said to be outwitted or circumvented by another. For, as he says in the book *On Baptism*, chapter 18, “Every petition is capable both of deceiving and of being deceived.”

God is light. An elegant statement indeed!

A symbol of death, i.e., Baptism, instituted by Christ as a commemorative sign of his Death and burial, [Col. 1220C] and indeed of his Resurrection as well, so that we too, having died in Adam and been buried with Christ through Baptism, might be raised from the death of sin to the LIFE OF GRACE. See the book *On Baptism*, chapter 11, note “Without his resurrection,” and chapter 16, note “Might be glorified.”

Permits. Junius rightly reads “promises,” although I think that “permits,” which is in my manuscript, can also be tolerated.

They fall away. From the grace obtained in Baptism, namely; we also know that many, forestalled by death, were not rarely prevented and disappointed even from obtaining it. See the book *On Baptism*, chapter 18, note “It is more advantageous.”

Having undertaken. That is, acting with faithless and inconstant, and therefore neither serious nor sincere, repentance.

About to fall. Such a person, namely, will be like the foolish man who builds his house upon sand, etc., Matthew 7:26.

Of the Hearers. This is a specific rank of catechumens, concerning which see above and perhaps elsewhere at greater length. Yet I think the word should be taken here simply for catechumens, [Col. 1220D] since he repeats it so often in the same sense.

For that reason. Or, for that cause.

As soon as. In the sense of “at the very first moment.”

As soon as you have examined him. Or, immediately when you have come to know him more closely and thoroughly.

You lie upon it. And continue to press upon it, as though from an excessively tenacious habit and custom. See the book *On Baptism*, chapter 11, note “Resurrection.”

Ignorant, i.e., while you did not yet know God.

Separates. Pamelius, according to Junius, reads from three manuscripts: “from the perfect word of God.” But what would that be? Is it not better to retain the reading of my manuscript, which may be explained quite suitably as follows: “What separates, distinguishes, and differentiates you from a servant of God already perfected through Baptism?” Nor does a catechumen possess anything exceptional in comparison with the baptized, as though the former could rush immediately into Baptism [Col. 1221A] without repentance for his own sins; indeed, he requires at least prior attrition in order to receive it fruitfully.

In good faith, i.e., with faithful and true repentance, not as in the preceding chapter, note “Of hypocrites.”

We have been washed. Inwardly, namely; although this is not always a perfect washing, but generally only one begun. For that repentance arising from fear which we call attrition does not of itself justify before Baptism is actually received; only perfect contrition arising from love of God above all things does so. Nevertheless, in the very long repentance that catechumens formerly had to perform before Baptism, it could scarcely happen that attrition did not at some point pass, as though through certain stages, into perfect contrition, so that someone might already be inwardly justified with his sins erased before actually receiving Baptism itself. Here one may consult what the Council of Trent, cited above, clearly explains in Session VI, *On Justification*, chapter 6.

Whole. Sincere and perfect; [Col. 1221B] this he calls the first—i.e., initial and begun—immersion or baptism, as we said immediately above.

As soon as. In the sense of “immediately when,” as above.

You have perceived, or have recognized.

From the waters, i.e., after Baptism, or after we have again emerged from the waters.

We have put on. For this is a necessary effect of Baptism and, as we call it, an effect *ex opere operato*; the Sacraments, like natural causes, necessarily operate in a properly disposed subject.

Obstructed, i.e., hindered on every side.

Bound. To Christ’s New Law, namely, after the baptized person’s renunciation of the devil and all his pomps.

For he who desires. Truly remarkable antitheses follow.

More fearful. Somewhat more shaken by the fear of GOD. See above.

Would promise in return. Simply for “would promise,” as our author [Col. 1221C] frequently uses similar compounds.

About to fulfill. Entirely correctly for the alternative form *praestiturus*, since *praestare* is taken to mean “to fulfill” or “to complete.”

## Notes on Chapter VII

To the Hearers. The author indicates clearly enough that what he has set forth thus far concerns the repentance to be performed before Baptism by Hearers or catechumens. Now, however, he turns to that kind of repentance which remains to be embraced by those who relapse after Baptism, as the “second plank after shipwreck,” as we said above.

Nothing now. Either “nothing now” concerns those who, after Baptism, are no longer conscious of anything worthy of repentance; or it should be referred to infants, who are incapable of actual sin and therefore of repentance.

Treating, that is, discussing. I am now weary of noting this so often.

To demonstrate, i.e., as though to point it out to persons invited to go on [Col. 1221D] sinning.

To that point. Or, to such an extent, or for that reason.

Let there now be open. Understand “a space”—namely, of time—or “a door,” or something similar.

Will have a way of escaping. The way of escape having, of course, been closed.

Will not have. The person himself, namely, who does not put an end to his offending.

Thus far. Thus far, or to this point. Or perhaps: Shall we Christians wait for the Gentiles themselves to set their god Terminus—faugh! a name not to be uttered without impiety—as a boundary to our sins?

To dangerous things. Others read “to dangers.” That is indeed plainer, but not so compelling that I must therefore abandon my manuscript.

They blame the sea. “He who suffers shipwreck twice wickedly accuses Neptune,” says Publilius, the writer of mimes.

They honor them by remembrance of the danger. A statement most elegantly expressed!

[Col. 1222A] To be a burden. An emphatic phrase, that is, well suited to arousing emotion.

To trample upon. To tread into it. But why multiply words? I take “trample upon” here simply for “tread upon,” or insult with one’s heels in utter contempt—and who will object?

A limit, i.e., restraint or restriction.

While it is extinguished. A most beautiful antithesis.

Works of death. Of that death, namely, which among all forms of death is the most terrible: the death of the soul.

Formerly his own. Previously his own, that is, the sinner’s.

About to judge. For St. Paul says in 1 Corinthians 6:3: “Do you not know that we shall judge angels?” Interpreters commonly understand this of evil or condemned spirits. “These,” our author says in the book *On the Apparel of Women*, chapter 2, “are those whom we shall judge; these are the angels whom we renounce in the laver.” And in the book *On Flight in Persecution*, chapter 10, he speaks thus: “Christian, you fear a man, though you ought to be feared by angels—since you are to judge angels—and ought to be feared by demons,” etc. Tertullian therefore speaks of these angels here as well.

[Col. 1222B] By deviations. Others read “by conditions,” which I cannot approve; Junius reads “by leadings astray,” a word our author uses in the book *On Prescription*, chapter 22. Indeed, this reading seems more consonant with the context, for we properly speak of leading someone from the road or straight path into trackless places, etc.

The door barred by a bolt. The word “barred” should not be referred to the bolt, but to the door, so that the sense is: “the door barred by the bolt of immersion or Baptism.” This is rightly stated because Baptism cannot be repeated; consequently, once someone has been baptized, this door no longer lies open to him for the pardon and remission of sins, as a door through which he might enter twice.

In vain. This expression is rather harsh, as though even repentance could not be repeated by the baptized after repeated relapses and consequently every hope of obtaining pardon anew were thereafter closed. But perhaps Tertullian meant to say no more [Col. 1222C] than that the more frequently someone has sinned, the more difficult sincere repentance becomes, and repentance worthy of pardon so many times. Indeed, immediately afterward he speaks more gently. But see rather below, chapter 9, “The defenses of nature,” 1169 D.

What you had received. A weighty statement indeed, to be deeply impressed upon the mind.

Lends. In the sense of gives and grants for your use.

You may restore, or recover; or reestablish.

Much more when enlarged. Here *nedum* must be explained as “much more.” I know that other meanings, and sometimes a negative force, are assigned to this otherwise sufficiently ambiguous word. But let these matters belong to the grammarians. Anyone will readily take “enlarged” here to mean “increased” or “extended.” Why say more, then? Here *nedum* will mean the same to me as “not to say when enlarged.”

Than to give. Because he who restores gives twice, by once again placing another in the same condition from which he had fallen.

## Notes on Chapter VIII

That. In the sense of “concerning that.”

Things sacrificed to idols [*idolothyta*] are the flesh of victims offered to idols, from εἴδωλον, “idol,” and θύω, “I offer” or “sacrifice.” Whoever ate of these offerings was therefore considered a participant in that sacrifice and polluted by it, as though sacrilegiously sharing the very religion of the Gentiles. For this reason, among other things, the eating of food sacrificed to idols was forbidden at the Council of Jerusalem, Acts 15, [Col. 1223A] because at that time it could scarcely take place without scandal. But once that circumstance had changed, eating such foods privately was by no means forbidden, since they could never in reality be polluted by idols or contaminated with impurity. And this is the very teaching of St. Paul in 1 Corinthians 10:27ff.:

> If any of the unbelievers invites you—to a meal, namely—and you are willing to go, eat whatever is set before you, asking no questions for conscience’s sake. But if someone says, “This has been offered to idols,” do not eat it, for the sake of the one who informed you—lest, namely, he think it eaten in honor of his idols—and for conscience’s sake. I mean, however, not your own conscience but the other person’s, lest you become a scandal to another and injure his conscience.

Doubtful. I supply, “it would be,” or “it might appear.” For in former times the Apocalypse of St. John had not yet been universally received in the Church among the canonical books, that is, books of undoubted authority. Even Eusebius himself, as late as the fourth century, had doubts about it in Book III of his *Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 33, believing with certain others that the author of this book [Col. 1223B] was not John the Evangelist but another John surnamed the Theologian. Since, therefore, it was not sufficiently established who this person was, or whether he had been inspired by the Holy Spirit, most Churches long hesitated to number the Apocalypse among the divine Scriptures. Indeed, the Council of Laodicea, held about A.D. 372, had not yet ventured to receive it into its canon. This was first done by the Council of Carthage in the year 397, and afterward Pope Innocent I finally proposed the complete canon of Holy Scripture.

Had been wearied by wandering. The little sheep, namely, exhausted from wandering about.

Relieves, i.e., diminishes.

Concealment. This seems to be used here for silence.

Aggravates. In the sense of increases.

## Notes on Chapter IX

Of one. Immediately before this, however, in chapter 8—and indeed already in chapter 5—Tertullian mentions only one repentance granted by the Church after Baptism, strictly excluding a further one. [Col. 1223C] Since he again insists upon this here, the matter seems to require somewhat more exact examination, though within the limits of our undertaking.

No one should suppose that this severity of discipline prevailed only in Tertullian’s time or only in the African Churches. First of all, ST. AUGUSTINE, that great LIGHT not merely of the African Church but of the WHOLE CHURCH, says in Epistle 54 to Macedonius:

> Although no place for the most humble repentance is granted in the Church to them—

he is speaking of those who, as he says shortly before, even after performing repentance and after reconciliation at the Altar, commit similar or graver offenses—

> nevertheless God does not forget his patience toward them.

And again, a little below:

> Although it has been cautiously and salutarily provided that a place for that most humble repentance should be granted ONCE in the Church, lest the medicine, if held cheap, should be less beneficial to the sick—a medicine that will have been all the less contemptible, etc.

Therefore, even in the fourth and fifth centuries, not only in the Church of Hippo but also in the other [Col. 1223D] Churches of Africa, such rigor prevailed that no one who relapsed into grave sins was admitted to a second *exomologesis*—that is, to be performed publicly, in the sight of the Church, and in the manner that our author subsequently describes in this very chapter and that St. Augustine above called “most humble.”

But what of CLEMENT regarding his Church of Alexandria and the other Churches subject to it? For in Book II of the *Stromata* he says:

> God, since he is rich in mercy, gave even to those who, after faith—

that is, after the faith received in Baptism—

> fall into some sin, μετάνοιαν δευτέραν, A SECOND REPENTANCE. If anyone, after his calling, has been tempted, driven by a certain violence, and cunningly circumvented, he will receive μίαν ἔτι μετάνοιαν ἀμετανόητον λάβῃ, ONE further REPENTANCE not to be repented of—

after which repentance, if he sins again, he will thereafter no longer be able to repent.

Origen himself, Clement’s disciple, likewise allowed no place for a second repentance after Baptism, as Dupin [Col. 1224A] testifies in the *Bibliothèque des auteurs ecclésiastiques*, vol. I, p. 216 of my edition.
