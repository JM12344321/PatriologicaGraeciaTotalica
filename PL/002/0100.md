We also read him writing to the Romans: “The woman who is under a husband is bound to her husband while he lives; but if he dies, she is released from the law of her husband. Accordingly, then, while her husband lives she will be considered an adulteress if she is joined to another man. But if her husband dies, she is freed from the law, so that she is not an adulteress if she becomes another man’s wife” [Col. 0949A] (Rom. 7:2–3). But consider also what follows, so that this meaning which flatters you may vanish: “Therefore, my brothers,” he says, “you also were put to death to the law through the body of Christ, so that you might belong to another, namely to him who rose from the dead, in order that we might bear fruit for God. For when we were in the flesh, the passions of sins, which were aroused through the law, were at work in our members to bear fruit for death. But now we have been released from the law, having died to that in which we were held, so that we may serve God in the newness of the Spirit and not in the oldness of the letter.”

Therefore, if he commands us to be put to death to the law through the body of Christ—that is, the Church, which consists in the Spirit’s newness, not in the letter’s oldness, that is, the law—then, by removing you from the law, which does not prevent a wife whose husband has died from becoming another man’s wife, he brings you under the opposite condition: that, [Col. 0949B] having lost your husband, you are not to marry. And to the same degree that you would not be reckoned an adulteress for becoming another man’s wife after your husband’s death, if you were still obliged to conduct yourself under the law, so by the difference of your condition he adjudges you guilty of adultery if you marry another after your husband’s death. For you have now been put to death to the law: something cannot be lawful for you after you have withdrawn from that law under which it was lawful for you.

## CHAPTER XIV

Now even if the Apostle had unconditionally permitted a believer who had lost a marriage partner to marry, he would have acted in the same way as in those other matters which, contrary to the form of his own rule, he carried out because of the circumstances of the times: circumcising Timothy because of false brethren who had been brought in, [Note: [Col. 0949D] Rhenanus: “brought in.”] and bringing certain men who had shaved themselves into the temple because of Jewish observance (Acts 16:3; 21:24)—the very man [Col. 0949C] who chastises the Galatians for wishing to conduct themselves under the law. But circumstances required [Note: [Col. 0949D] Others: “required” in the singular.] him to become all things to all people, so that he might gain them all, laboring to give birth to them until Christ should be formed in them, and warming the little ones in faith like a nurse (1 Cor. 9:20; Gal. 4:19), teaching certain things by permission, not by command—for it is one thing to grant indulgence, another to give an order. In the same way, he permitted for a time the license to marry anew because of the weakness of the flesh, just as Moses permitted divorce because of hardness of heart (Deut. 24:1).

Here too, therefore, we shall supply what completes this line of thought. If Christ abolished what Moses prescribed because “from the beginning it was not so” (Matt. 19:8), and Christ will not on that account be considered to have come from another power, why should the Paraclete not also have abolished what Paul allowed? For [Col. 0949D] a second marriage likewise did not exist from the beginning; nor, on that account, ought the Paraclete to be held suspect as though he were an alien spirit, provided only that what is subsequently introduced [Note: [Col. 0949D] Others: “subsequently declared.”] is worthy of God and Christ. If [Note: [Col. 0949D] Semler and Oberthür: “But if.”] it was worthy of God and Christ to restrain hardness of heart when its time had run its course, why should it not be still worthier [Col. 0950A] of God and Christ to shake off weakness of the flesh now that time has been drawn nearer to its consummation?

If it is right that a marriage not be dissolved, surely it is also honorable that it not be repeated. Indeed, in the secular world both are counted part of good discipline: the one under the name of concord, the other under that of chastity. Hardness of heart reigned until Christ; let weakness of the flesh likewise have reigned until the Paraclete. The new law abolished divorce; it had something to abolish. The new prophecy abolishes second marriage, which is no less a divorce from the first. Yet hardness of heart yielded to Christ more readily than weakness of the flesh does. The latter defends itself by Paul more than the former did by Moses; nevertheless, it defends him when it seizes upon him as granting indulgence, but rejects him when he lays down precepts. It evades his weightier pronouncements and abiding intentions; it does not permit us to render to this Apostle [Col. 0950B] what he prefers.

And how long will this most shameless weakness persist in assailing what is better? Its time lasted until the Paraclete should begin his work; to him were deferred by the Lord those things which could not then be borne, [Note: [Col. 0950D] Rhenanus: “were prolonged.”] [Note: [Col. 0950D] François: “to bear.”] things which it now befits no one to be unable to bear, because he through whom the ability to bear is given is not absent. But he first said, “The Spirit is willing,” so that the Spirit might conquer the flesh and what is weak might yield to the stronger.

For he also says, “He who can receive it, let him receive it” (Matt. 19:12); that is, he who cannot, let him depart. That rich man too departed because he had not received the command to distribute his property to the needy, and he was dismissed by the Lord to the judgment of his own choice (Matt. 19:16–22). Nor on that account will hardness be imputed [Col. 0950C] to Christ because of the fault of each person’s free choice. ## CHAPTER XV

What, then, is our hardness here, if we renounce those who do not perform the will of God? What heresy is there if we judge second marriages to be unlawful, alongside adultery? For what is adultery but an unlawful marriage? The Apostle censures those who forbade marriage altogether and who also prohibited foods which God created (1 Tim. 4:3). But we no more abolish marriages by refusing second marriages [Col. 0950D] than we condemn foods by fasting more frequently. It is one thing to abolish, another to moderate; one thing to impose a law against marrying, another [Note: [Col. 0950D] Rhenanus repeats “is.”] to establish a limit on marrying.

Certainly, those who reproach us with hardness or heresy in this matter, [Note: [Col. 0950D] Others add “suppose.”] if they cherish the weakness of the flesh so much [Col. 0951A] that they think it must repeatedly be supported in marrying, why do they neither support it in another matter nor cherish it with pardon when it has been overcome by torments into denial? Surely that weakness is more entitled to excuse which fell in battle than that which fell in the bedroom; which succumbed on the rack rather than on the bed; which yielded in the matter of faith rather than to lust; which was conquered while groaning rather than while willing. Yet the former they drive from communion because it did not endure to the end, while the latter they receive as though it too had endured to the end. Set forth what each failed to endure to the end, and you will find the cause of the one who could not endure cruelty more honorable than that of the one who could not endure chastity. And yet weakness of the flesh does not excuse even a bloody apostasy, much less an unchaste one.

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN: ON FASTING

[Note: [Col. 0953C] In the editions of Gelenius, Semler, and others, this book is entitled *On Fasting, Against the Psychics*.] (C)

## ARGUMENT

[Col. 0953B]

The orthodox, whom he here calls “Psychics,” rejected Montanus’s teachings concerning fasts. Having therefore become a follower of Montanus, he defends the Montanist fasts, xerophagies, and stations.

## CHAPTER I

I would be surprised if these Psychics [Note: [Col. 0953C] François adds “these.”] were strained only by the luxury through which they marry repeatedly, and were not also torn apart by the gluttony [Note: [Col. 0953D] “Torn apart by gluttony”: “burst.” RIG.] through which they hate fasts. [Note: [Col. 0953C] Rhenanus and Leopold: “were held”; Semler and Leopold: “were led”; Ursinus’s codex: “were loosened.”] For lust without gluttony would surely be regarded as a monster, since these two are so united and fused together that, if they could ever have been separated, the genitals themselves would not in the first place be attached to the belly. Look at the body: it is a single region.

Indeed, [Note: [Col. 0953D] “According to the arrangement of the members is the order of the vices.” Hence Jerome’s statement in Letter 147 to Amasis: “According to the order of the members is the order of the vices.” RIG.] according to the arrangement of the members is the order of the vices: first the belly, and immediately thereafter lust lies beneath the fattening. Sexual heat passes through [Col. 0953C] voracity.

I therefore recognize [Note: [Col. 0953D] “Animal faith”: psychic faith. RIG. (See above, note (a). EDD.] the animal [Note: [Col. 0953C] Ψυχικήν, “psychic.”] faith by its devotion to the flesh, of which it wholly consists, inclined equally to many meals and many marriages. Thus it deservedly accuses the spiritual discipline, its rival in substance, in [Col. 0954B] this kind of continence also, since that discipline puts a bridle upon gluttony by sometimes admitting no food, or only late or dry food, just as it puts a bridle upon lust through a single marriage.

> And the red stake projecting from his obscene groin.

Concerning the measure of [Col. 0954C] marrying, we have already published our defense of monogamy. Now, concerning the chastisement of diet, comes the second—or rather the first—[Note: [Col. 0954C] Semler and Leopold: “of the second, or rather the first.”] contest of continence.

## CHAPTER II
