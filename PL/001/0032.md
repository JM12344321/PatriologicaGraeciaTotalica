Since it is therefore established that it was not Domitian, that savage and bloodthirsty man, but the most gentle old man Nerva Cocceius who recalled the banished, the great Scaliger, in his notes on Eusebius, p. 205, not undeservedly marks this as an σφάλμα μνημονικόν, “a lapse of memory” of Tertullian or of those whom he follows [Col. 0294C]. You will free the most learned Father from this fault if, with the Vatican codex, you delete this entire passage.

If my own judgment is to receive any consideration, however, the author wrote *restituistis*, “you restored,” rather than *restitutis*, “having restored”; the following words confirm this: “those condemned by whom you have been accustomed to restore.” Thus he would be saying that Domitian did not continue persecuting Christians with equal severity to the very end; indeed, that the Roman people afterward permitted the exiles to return.

The matter would certainly be clear if the manuscripts read as follows: “But the good Nerva readily suppressed what had been begun, even restoring those who had been banished.” He was assuredly a most highly praised prince, who changed all things for the better and was called “peaceful and most gentle” by Martial, book VIII, epigram 70:

> As great as peaceful Nerva’s repose, so great is his eloquence;

and XII.6:

> Nerva, gentlest of its princes, has fallen to the Ausonian court;  
> Now it may enjoy the whole of Helicon.

Nerva is expressly called “good” by Symmachus I.13. The same poet aspires in his thoughts to those happy times when, in the episode of the dove that had flown down into Aretulla’s lap, he plays among other things with these lines, book VIII.32:

> If it is right for a devoted sister to hope for better things,  
> And if prayers have power to bend the lord of the world,
