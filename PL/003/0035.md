This, so far as I know, etc. He speaks thus because he could conceive in his mind nothing that was not embodied. Velleius speaks in the same way in Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I.12: “As for his claim that God is without any body—ἀσώματον (*asōmaton*, ‘incorporeal’), as the Greeks say—it is impossible to understand what such a being could be; for he must necessarily lack sensation, and also lack intelligence and pleasure.” And in chapter 11: “But when he likewise maintains that God is without a body, he deprives him of every sensation, including intelligence.” Yet the sounder philosophers also held that inferior natures endowed with reason were furnished with bodies; Origen and others among the Fathers shared this opinion, as P. *And yet so great an age has passed . . . countless ages have flowed by.* Heumann confidently rewrites this: “But since so great an age *has passed* . . . and countless ages *have flowed by*.” Yet there is no need. So chapter 36: “And yet, if we thought resources useful, we would ask them from God”; chapter 37: “And yet even an emperor does not give what he does not possess”; chapter 36: “And yet who can be poor?” And: “Yet these things were born for us.” LINDNER.

*What single person whatsoever from the dead.* Lactantius takes this from Minucius in *Divine Institutes* VII.22, omitting the word *ullus* (“whatsoever”). But it is read in the manuscript, despite the contrary assertion of those who peddle their excerpts to the excessively credulous. RIGALTIUS.—If what Wower testifies were true—namely, that the manuscript does not recognize the word *ullus*—everything would be straightforward and would agree exactly with the passage of Lactantius. But Heraldus and Rigaltius are so far from agreeing with him that Heraldus instead supposes a defect here in Lactantius and says that the word *ullus*, omitted in Lactantius, is found in the Royal Paris manuscript. Yet since Bonemann, a most diligent investigator of books and manuscripts, declares that he found nothing of the sort, we have reason to doubt. The expression *quis unus* (“what single person”) is frequent in Livy and Cicero. (Wower is mistaken in his customary fashion: Her[aldus] and Rigal[tius] correctly testify that *ullus* is present in the manuscript. EDD.) On this pagan argument against belief in the resurrection, see, besides Lactantius VII.22.10, also Theophilus, *To Autolycus* I.18: φὴς γὰρ δεῖξόν μοι κἂν ἕνα ἐγερθέντα ἐκ νεκρῶν, ἵνα ἰδὼν πιστεύσω, “For you say: ‘Show me even one person raised from the dead, so that upon seeing him I may believe.’” Compare Luke 16:27 ff. LINDNER.

*At least of hours.* Broukhusius, on Propertius I, Elegy XIX.10, thought that something had fallen out and emended the text to: “with a leave of absence permitted for at least a few hours.” Davis, however, proposed “at least three hours,” as reported by Hyginus, *Fables* CIII; he proves that this corruption could easily have arisen from the resemblance of the numeral sign to the preceding letter. Gronovius nevertheless ably defends the received reading. Here are his very words:

“Although everyone now understands the very pleasing transferred sense of the word *commeatus* (‘leave of absence’), drawn from either a military camp or the imperial court, nevertheless the genitive *horarum* (‘of hours’), standing by itself, expresses the fullest completeness. In Roman calculations of time, the number of hours did not extend beyond twelve; hence, to designate merely a small portion of time—one that did not extend beyond a single day and indeed did not fill even one day—the author rightly calls for *hours*, not days, weeks, or months. [Col. 0270D] Lucian plainly exhibits this ancient meaning in the *Downward Journey*, where Megapenthes says, οὐ πολὺν χρόνον, ὦ Μοῖρα, αἰτῶ, ‘I do not ask for much time, O Fate’; and the scholiast of our Academic Library aptly explains, εἰς Πρωτεσίλαον ἀποσκώπτει, ‘he is jesting at Protesilaus.’ Thus what the Greeks commonly called ‘a leave of no great duration’ is described by Minucius as a leave ‘of hours’—a wholly sound expression, especially since military leaves of this kind could also be specified in hours according to the distance involved, and not only in days or weeks. The distinguished Price likewise observed that those very words were perfectly complete, in his illustration of Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* II, p. 114. To be sure, when the Latins say simply *horarum*, according to the grace of Latin idiom, it is not the word ‘all’ that is understood, but ‘some’ . . . . . Not that they did not sometimes choose to express this in full—for thus Cicero, *On the Orator* II.18: ‘The eloquent man is said to have spoken for some hours’; Suetonius, *Augustus* 56: ‘He sat on the benches for some hours,’ and the reason for the difference is sufficiently evident—or that they were not permitted to define them by adding a specific number within which they were comprised. But they preferred to do this with intervals of time whose exact duration it mattered to know, as the same Suetonius does in *Nero* 33 concerning a kid, after Nero prolonged the affair for five hours. This cannot be applied to the present story, in which, as commonly happens, the accounts vary.”

Our author likewise disliked an exact determination of time below, chapter 18: “I omit the Persians. But who has not eagerly desired and exulted to celebrate those seven Persians . . .?” DAVIS cites Justin II.14: “the news of the victory was reported within so brief a span of hours.”

*In sweetness.* *Into sweetness* would be preferable; and I do not believe that the Latins, especially the more elegant writers, used the ablative in place of the accusative as often as these cases were confused by copyists. Thus Justin IV.1: “This was composed by the ancients to add sweetness to the tale.” WOPKENS.

*By you, namely the credulous.* Thus the manuscript and the Roman editions, Rigaltius, Gronovius, and Davis. But Gelenius and Cellarius read “the excessively credulous,” which Heraldus thought the sense required. Punctuate: “by you—namely, the credulous—they were shamefully remade with reference to your God.” Nothing then remains that could give offense. GRUNER.

**SUMMARY.—They also assert that the whole world will one day be consumed by fire; and after the resurrection of our bodies they promise the righteous an eternity of most blessed life, but the unrighteous an eternity of the greatest punishments.**

Not content even with this frenzied opinion, they construct and append old women’s tales. They maintain that after death, ashes, and cinders they are born again; and with some inexplicable confidence they mutually believe one another’s lies—you would think they had already come back to life. A twofold evil and a double madness! They announce destruction for heaven and the stars, which we leave exactly as we found them; but for themselves, when dead and extinguished—for we perish just as we are born—they promise eternity in return. That, of course, is why they curse funeral pyres and condemn burial by fire, as though every body, even if withdrawn from the flames, were not nevertheless dissolved into earth by years and ages; and as though it made any difference whether beasts tear it apart, the seas consume it, the soil covers it, or flame carries it away. For if corpses possess sensation, every burial is punishment; if they do not, the very speed of their destruction is a remedy.

Deceived by this error, they promise themselves, as good people, a blessed and everlasting life after death, but all others, as unrighteous, eternal punishment. Many replies to this occur to me, were my speech not hastening onward. That they themselves are the unrighteous is a point on which I need not labor further: I have already proved it. Yet even if I granted that they were righteous, nevertheless guilt—or innocence—is attributed to fate in the opinions of very many, and this is also your own position. For whatever we do, just as others assign it to fate, so you assign it [Col. 0269A] to God: thus you maintain that the members of your sect do not desire it of their own accord, but are chosen. You therefore imagine an unjust judge, who punishes fortune in human beings rather than will.

Yet I should like to ask whether resurrection occurs with bodies—and with what bodies: the same or renewed ones? Without a body? Without a body, so far as I know, there is neither mind, soul, nor life. With the same body? But it has already dissolved. With another body? Then a new person is born; the former person is not restored. And yet so great an age has passed, countless centuries have flowed by: what single person whatsoever has returned from the dead—even by the fortune of Protesilaus—with leave for at least a few hours, so that we might at least believe on the strength of an example? All these fabrications of a diseased opinion and foolish consolations, playfully fashioned [Col. 0271A] by deceitful poets to add sweetness to their verse, have been shamefully remade by you—credulous people indeed—with reference to your God.

> **CHAPTER XII.—*Judge*.** Cellarius wrongly printed *aestimatis* (“you judge”). Ouzelius, Davis, and Gronovius correctly give *aestimate* (“judge”), which is also the reading of the Roman edition. HEUMANN.—I add that the manuscript has it as well. LINDNER.
>
> *You are in need, you are cold,* etc. The manuscript does indeed have *opere* as a single word, but it must be pronounced separately, as most editors have printed it. Moreover, the passage is perfectly sound and clear. James 2:15–16 also joins being in need with being cold. *Ope laborare* means to lack assistance, or, as Cicero expresses it, *egere ope*, “to be in need of aid.” *Re laborare* means to lack property. Columella has *fame laborare*, “to suffer from hunger,” in his first preface. The pagan Caecilius intensifies the Christians’ poverty through *exergasia*, or elaboration. [Col. 0271D] I am astonished that learned men have tampered with this passage: Davis rewrites it, “you are sick, you are cold, you suffer from poverty and hunger”; Wopkens reads, “you are in need, you are cold, you labor at a futile work”; Heumann restores the passage thus: “you suffer in body and mind. While God tolerates and disregards this, he either does not wish or is unable to aid his own.” LINDNER.
>
> *Does not wish or cannot.* For “or does not wish.” See what we note above on chapter 1, and likewise chapter 34. LINDNER.
>
> *When you are shaken by danger.* Heraldus, Rigaltius, the Baluze manuscript, Ursinus, Wower, and Lindner read *cum querquero quateris*, “when you are shaken by a feverish chill,” that is, by cold and trembling. Pompeius Festus, or his interpolator Paulus, says: “It is certain that *querquera*, a chill accompanied by trembling, is so called from the Greek κάρκηρα.” He adds from Lucilius: “A shivering fever follows, and pains of the head.” Gellius XX.1: “a serious illness with a raging and shivering fever.” Apuleius also has *querquerum* in the *Apology*. Fr. Balduinus, Fulvius Ursinus, and Joannes Wowerus accordingly read *querquero* in Minucius; by contrast, Heraldus and Rigaltius read *periculo* (“danger”) from ancient books. I would follow them if “to be shaken by danger” seemed a convenient expression, especially in this passage and in such a sequence of words. CELLARIUS.—On this disease consult Keuchenius on Serenus Sammonicus, p. 269; he nevertheless supports Ludovicus Carrio, *Emendations* II.18, who rewrites our Minucius as *querquera*, when he maintains that the form is *querqueram*, not *querquerum*. HEUMANN.
>
> *And now no longer.* Some editions wrongly have “also not to be worshipped.” *Jam* (“now”) thus frequently intensifies the force of the negative *non*. Below, chapter 21: “These are now no longer sacred rites; they are tortures.” Lactantius, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors* 19: “now no longer a shepherd of cattle, but of soldiers.” Salvian, *On the Governance of God* I, p. 17, Bremen edition: “For in Egypt at that time, no longer merely a single but a manifold . . .” I would read there with the common text, “for at that time not . . .” Paulinus, *Letter to Aper*: “Nor now in scholastic rather than divine speech.” Justin, *Preface*: “Which I sent to you, no longer so much for the sake of learning as of correcting.” WOPKENS.
>
> *You deny even garlands to tombs.* The earliest Christians abstained from this rite so as to have nothing in common with the nations. Pagans decorated tombs with wreaths. Propertius III, Elegy 16:
>
> > She will bring perfumes here for me, and adorn my tomb  
> > With a wreath, sitting as guardian beside my ashes.
>
> And the ancient poet in *The Barmaid*:
>
> > Why preserve fragrant wreaths for ungrateful ashes?  
> > Do you wish them to cover a stone crowned with flowers?
>
> Hence, among the charges brought against Christians, Justin Martyr reports in *Apology* II, p. 68: μὴ τοῖς ἀποθανοῦσι χοὰς καὶ κνίσας, καὶ ἐν ταφαῖς (as Meursius emends it) στεφάνους καὶ θυσίας φέρειν, “that they do not bring libations and the savor of burnt offerings to the dead, nor garlands and sacrifices at burials.” Later Christians, however, departed from this simplicity. Prudentius, *Cathemerinon*, Hymn X, at the end of the funeral hymn:
>
> > We shall cherish the covered bones  
> > With violets and abundant foliage,  
> > And sprinkle the inscription and cold stones  
> > With liquid perfume. CELLARIUS.
>
> *Worthy of pity, but of our gods.* In place of *sed* (“but”), J. F. Gronovius, *Observations on Ecclesiastical Writers* 7, p. 75, rewrote *et* (“and”), while Davis proposed *vel* (“or”). Yet the manuscript reading is sound. If anyone is offended by the word order, let him read: “worthy of the gods’ pity, but of our gods’.” Thus below, chapter 26: “angels, that is, servants and messengers of God—but of the true God,” which could also be phrased: “messengers—but of the true God.” Florus I.1.6: “The former first sees six vultures; the latter afterward—but twelve.” Pliny 33.3: “Among us Antonius, as an insult to nature, made cheapness worthy of proscription by means of gold—but the cheapness of Spartacus.” LINDNER.
>
> [Col. 0273A] *Before your feet.* That is, the things that lie before your feet. Cicero, *On Divination* II.13: “No one observes what is before his feet; they scrutinize the regions of the sky.” [Col. 0273B] LINDNER.
>
> *To discuss divine things.* That is, “to discuss concerning divine things.” Thus Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* III.40: “I have preferred to discuss rather than judge the matters I have debated”; Sulpicius Severus, *Sacred History* II.28: “As for the remainder concerning him, it is uncertain whether one should feel more regret or shame in discussing it”; Jerome, *Against Jovinian* I, col. 147, Benedictine edition: “My teacher Gregory of Nazianzus, discussing virginity and marriage, explained them in Greek verses.” The same author, *Letter XXXI to Pammachius*: “You are compelled to discuss that passage [περιοχήν, *periochēn*] of the Apostle differently.” Tacitus and other very celebrated writers use the construction likewise. DAVIS.

**SUMMARY.—What is destined to happen to the Christians themselves after death may be inferred from the fact that they are now deprived of every resource and oppressed by the greatest calamities and miseries.**

Yet you do not draw a lesson even from present circumstances about how the empty hopes of a futile promise deceive you. While you still live, wretches, judge what threatens you after death. Behold: a part of you—and the greater and better part, as you say—are needy and cold; you suffer from lack of aid and from hunger. Yet God permits it and disregards it: he either does not wish or [Col. 0271B] is unable to help his own. Thus he is either powerless or unjust.

You who dream of immortality after death: when danger shakes you, when fevers burn you, when pain tears you apart, do you not then perceive your condition? [Col. 0272A] Do you not then acknowledge your frailty? Against your will, wretch, you are convicted of weakness, yet you will not admit it. But I pass over what is common to all. Behold what awaits you: threats, punishments, tortures, and crosses now no longer to be worshipped but to be endured; fires as well, which you both foretell and fear. Where is that God who can assist people when they come back to life, but cannot assist them while they are alive?

Do not the Romans rule, reign, enjoy the whole world, and exercise dominion over you without your God? Meanwhile, you live in suspense and anxiety, abstaining from respectable pleasures. You do not attend public shows or take part in processions; public banquets go on without you. You abhor sacred contests, foods from which a portion has first been taken, and drinks first poured in libation upon altars. Thus you dread the gods whose existence you deny. You do not bind your heads with flowers or honor your bodies with perfumes; you reserve ointments for funerals and deny even garlands to tombs—pale and trembling, worthy of pity, but of our gods’ pity. Thus, wretches, you neither rise again nor live in the meantime.

(IX) Accordingly, if you possess any wisdom or modesty, cease examining the regions of heaven, the destinies of the world, and its secrets. It is enough to look at what lies before your feet—especially for the unlearned, uncultivated, ignorant, and rustic. Since it has not been granted to them to understand civil matters, much more has it been denied them to discuss divine things.

> *Which oracle he himself perceived, and therefore to have been placed before all.* We have presented the exact words of the Royal manuscript and understand them in this way: “The oracle—namely, Socrates himself—perceived, therefore,” etc. It is not so absurd for a wise man to be called an oracle, since Cicero himself, *On the Orator* I.45, called the jurist’s house “the oracle of the entire state.” Before the infinitive *esse* (“to be”), *se* (“himself”) must be supplied, as also commonly happens in Latin usage. Let us now examine the commentators’ conjectures about this passage. Rigaltius: “What is above us is nothing to us. Deservedly. He therefore earned from the oracle testimony to his singular wisdom—which oracle he himself perceived—and was therefore placed before all, not because,” [Col. 0274B] etc. J. F. Gronovius, *Observations on Ecclesiastical Writers* 6, p. 76: “He himself perceived what the oracle meant; for that reason he was placed before all,” etc. The younger Gronovius: “What of the oracle? He himself perceived it, therefore,” etc. Ouzelius: “By this oracle he himself perceived that he was therefore placed before all, not because,” etc. Wopkens: “Therefore he himself was placed before all,” and explains it thus: “He himself rightly understood the meaning of that oracle—namely, that by it he was placed before all for this reason, not because,” etc. Davis: “Through this oracle he himself thoroughly perceived that he was therefore placed before all,” etc. Gruner: “Which oracle he himself perceived, therefore,” etc.; he adds that Minucius wished to remove every trace of dissimulation from Socrates, as Justin also did in the words cited by Heraldus. Heumann deletes the words “which oracle . . . placed before all” as a gloss and wishes the text to read: “Deservedly . . . singular wisdom: not because . . . he had learned,” etc. LINDNER.

> [Col. 0274C] *Of very many Academics* means numerous and diverse Academics. Pliny 9.12: “Aquatic creatures have numerous coverings.” And the Academic school was indeed numerous: the Old, Middle, New, Fourth, and Fifth Academies are counted. These classes, as though separate heads, likewise included several persons. Davis conjectured: “of Academics and Pyrrhonists in the highest,” etc.; yet, as Wopkens correctly observes, the Pyrrhonists may readily be understood as included among the Academics. Heumann preferred to rewrite: “of the younger Academics.” Is this meant in apposition? But Arcesilaus was head of the Middle Academy. Or are younger Academics other than the two leading figures named by Caecilius being mentioned? But Carneades was a follower of the New Academy. And did not the Old Academy equally approve of doubting individual matters, just as the New or Younger Academy did? LINDNER.

> *Simonides of Meles.* It is unclear how every editor without contradiction, down to the most recent, came to read “Simonides the lyric poet.” Nor did Lindner himself write more soundly, although he has these remarks in his Notes. “Simonides of Meles” is the reading of the Royal manuscript. Minucius certainly likes to repeat expressions in this manner, as below in chapter 19: “Let Thales of Miletus be first of all to have debated celestial matters. This same Thales of Miletus said that water was the beginning of things.” Thus Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* I: “Thessaly—for there the foundations of our maternal ancestry, transmitted by the celebrated Plutarch and shortly afterward by his grandson Sextus the philosopher, bring us glory—that Thessaly I was making for on business.” I remember having observed such repetitions quite frequently even in Caesar. These spirits, therefore, after,” etc.; chapter 27: “These unclean spirits, therefore”; chapter III.7: “to pick up from the shore a rounded potsherd smoothed by the tossing of the waves, and to hold that potsherd with its flat side between the fingers,” etc.; Cornelius Nepos, *Eumenes* III.3: “Since Eumenes had neither large nor reliable forces.—Eumenes therefore understood.” On that passage see Boeclerus. LINDNER.
>
> *What and of what kind.* Thus above, chapter 17: “A human being ought to know himself and look about him: what he is and why he exists.” LINDNER.
>
> *Toward the other side.* There is no reason to substitute *alterutram* (“either of the two”) for *alteram* (“the other”), as Davis did; the latter is very frequently used by the Latins for the former. Above, chapter 5: “So that you may hold the balance of a perfectly impartial judge and not lean with partiality toward either side.” Cicero, *Academics* II.43: “Therefore both cannot be wise . . . but one or the other.” Many more examples may be drawn from Faber’s *Thesaurus*, Tursellinus *On Particles*, Freinsheim’s index to Florus, and elsewhere. WOPKENS.
>
> *A man of Plautine lineage.* Caecilius’ mind is still rankled by Octavius’ jest that in the clearest daylight he had stumbled against stones, because on the shore at Ostia he had worshipped a stone that the pagans had anointed and fashioned into an image. Caecilius therefore rubs down his Octavius with a different salt and calls him “a man of Plautine lineage.” At first sight this might seem to refer only to Plautine jokes. But immediately afterward he drives that very Plautine man into a mill, where Varro and most other authorities report that Marcus Accius Plautus wrote three plays. Caecilius turns the disgrace of this service in the mill against Octavius because of the simplicity of the Christian religion, a great part of whose adherents were uneducated laborers, such as bakers, shoemakers, and others of that kind. He therefore says that Octavius is preeminent among Christian bakers, but last among philosophers. RIGALTIUS.—The following expressions, frequent enough in St. Jerome, attest that this proverbial formula was once well worn: “members of the Plautine family have been discovered,” Letter XXVII . . . “the pillar of the Plautine family,” Letter XLVII. EDD.
>
> *Refrain from applauding against him.* Minucius now strikes Caecilius back with the phrase “Plautine lineage.” “Refrain,” he says, “from applauding against him”—as though he said: “Do not bark at our Octavius with that Plautine, or barking, lineage of yours.” The jest is double, for after a comedy is completed the actors customarily ask for applause. Here Caecilius applauds himself to Octavius’ disparagement and disgrace. Applause is given by striking or clapping the hands together; shells suited particularly well for clapping are called *plautiores*, and dogs whose hanging, flaccid, and rather broad ears flap against their heads are called *plauti*. RIGALTIUS.

**SUMMARY.—Caecilius finally concludes that the new religion must be rejected and that one must not pronounce rashly upon doubtful matters.**

Yet if you have a desire to philosophize, let whoever among you is so great imitate Socrates, the foremost figure in wisdom, if he can. Whenever that man was asked about heavenly matters, his well-known response was: **WHAT IS ABOVE US IS NOTHING TO US.** Deservedly, therefore, did he earn from the oracle testimony to his singular wisdom. That oracle he himself understood to mean that he had been placed before everyone for this reason: not because he had discovered everything, but because he had learned that he knew nothing. Thus confession of ignorance is the highest wisdom.

From this source flowed the cautious doubt of Arcesilaus, and much later of Carneades, and of very many Academics in the highest questions—a mode of philosophizing in which the unlearned can engage cautiously and the learned gloriously.

(X) What then? Is not the hesitation of Simonides of Meles something for everyone to admire and all to follow? When the tyrant Hiero asked Simonides what he thought the gods were and of what nature, Simonides first requested one day for deliberation; on the following day he extended it to two days; soon afterward, when reminded, he added as much again. Finally, when the tyrant asked the reason for so great a delay, Simonides replied that the longer his inquiry continued, the more obscure the truth became to him.

In my opinion also, matters that are doubtful should be left as they are. While so many eminent men remain in deliberation, judgment should not rashly and boldly be delivered in favor of either side, lest either an old woman’s superstition be introduced or all religion be destroyed.

## CHAPTER XIV

> **Note: [Col. 0275D]**
>
> **CHAPTER XIV.—*More fully*.** Gronovius wishes to read *penitus* (“thoroughly”), but without reason. The Latins call that *plenum* (“full”) which lacks nothing; concerning this meaning of the word, we give further material in our note on Aurelius Victor, *Origin of the Roman People* XII.12. Hence *plene* means “entirely.” Cicero, *Letter to Quintus* I.1.13: “You cannot entirely avoid this”; Justin XI.11.9. Hence in Minucius, “until the case has been argued more fully on both sides,” means “entirely.” As for the comparative, it can be explained as having the force of the positive. More exacting scholars, however, maintain that one or more words must always be understood. See Sanctius and Perizonius on *Minerva* II.10, p. 219 of my edition. In Minucius’ words one could understand “than hitherto.” GRUNER.
>
> *Not on praise and truth,* Royal manuscript. From this Heraldus produced “on praise, but on truth.” Others read, “by praise, but by truth.” EDD. Heumann reads “on applause,” because shortly before Minucius had said, “Refrain from applauding against him.” But applause is a sign of praise. LINDNER.
>
> *Your speech has delighted me with its subtle variety.* Minucius calls Caecilius’ variety “subtle”; shortly afterward Octavius will call it a “subtle variation and wavering,” which comes very close to sophistry and cunning. RIGALTIUS.
>
> [Col. 0276C] *According to the abilities of those debating.* I write “according to the powers of debate,” because “the power of eloquence” follows. HEUMANN. According to the abilities of the debaters, a good case is sometimes defended badly and a bad case well. All this force resides in the weight of arguments and opinions. Yet arguments neither alone nor everywhere exercise their force unless elegance of language is also joined to them. Eloquence has such power that it moves the hearer’s mind and, through a splendid style of speaking, readily sweeps it away—uplifted and struck with astonishment—into admiration and assent. LINDNER.
>
> *Without discrimination.* Thus the Royal manuscript and the Roman edition. Others read a variant spelling, *dilectu*. LINDNER.
>
> *From what is right.* I consider the word *rectis* (“right things”) sound, contrary to the judgment of Jacobus Gronovius when he emends it to *certis* (“certain things”). For statements are called “right” when they are true and sincere, containing nothing fabricated, and thus opposed to falsehoods. So below, chapter XVI.9: “Speech . . . is not painted with the pomp of eloquence, but is sustained, just as it is, by the rule of what is right.” Plautus, chapter 5: “Speak right and true things.” WOPKENS. Cicero, *On Friendship* 10: “to ask of friends what is not right”; and chapter 11: “if we have determined that it is right to grant friends whatever they wish.” The same author, *On Duties* III.11: “It is right”—that is, “it is true”—“that this is not permitted.” Ovid, *Metamorphoses* VII.72: “right, piety, and shame.” It is evident from this that both what is morally honorable and what is true are called *rectum*. Indeed, does not our own Minucius say at the conclusion of this chapter that we ought to select and approve those things that are right? HEUMANN.
>
> *Within the incredible.* The manuscript reads “and incredible.” But the author’s meaning requires us to understand that the two syllables were contracted into one in the copyist’s manner noted above. Just as Minucius said that falsehood is present “within the probable,” he surely said that truth is present “within the incredible.” It is incredible that God became man and was crucified. It is incredible that the bodies of the dead, whether cremated or buried, will return to life even after many centuries. Yet these things, although incredible, are true. Conversely, it appears probable that there is no god; or that he takes no care for human affairs; or that, if he did, he would be polluted by so sorrowful and manifold a ministry. Likewise, it appears probable that death ends all human things. Yet although these claims are probable, they are utterly false. RIGALTIUS.
>
> *They transfer the judge’s fault to a complaint about uncertainty.* This reading is unsound, for Minucius is speaking of many judges. Wower therefore restores “the fault of the judgment.” Meursius and Cellarius emend: “the judges transfer the fault to a complaint about uncertainty.” The sentence seems feeble to me unless the pronoun is added, so that it reads: “The judges transfer **their own** fault to a complaint about uncertainty.” Ausonius, *The Crucifixion of Cupid*, verse 64:
>
> > So they transfer their own faults to another’s crimes.
>
> Sallust, *Jugurtha* 1: “The authors themselves transfer **their own** fault to their occupations.” The same author likewise said in chapter 34, “to transfer fear from his own crime to your cowardice.” DAVIS.—[Col. 0277C] Heumann explains it thus: “Although they ought to accuse their own rashness in judging, from the fact that they are so often mistaken they conclude that nothing is right, but that everything must perpetually remain in doubt.” Yet the manuscript has *judicis* (“of the judge”), which is sound, as Gronovius explains on this passage. For it is “the judge’s fault” that the judge commits when his attention slackens during the hearing, or when in listening his mind is disengaged or becomes more disengaged from the pleader’s case, to use Terence’s expression as noted by Gronovius’ father in *Observations* III.7. Consequently, instead of avoiding the fault committed solely in the judge’s own person, they transfer it to the subject matter, which they complain was full of darkness, obscurity, and uncertainty, although the cause of the difficulty or darkness lies with the judge himself. It is precisely the same expression that Sallust uses in the passage cited. LINDNER.
>
> *So that, after condemning everything.* I read, “so that, after adjourning everything.” MEURSIUS. But “adjourning” means the same as “suspending,” whereas that participle ought to have had some force different from the meaning of the verb *suspendere*. Indeed, why would the author wish to be so foolishly redundant as to say, “after adjourning everything, they prefer to suspend everything”—that is, “they prefer to adjourn everything,” or “they prefer to suspend everything”? “Condemning,” however, means rejecting, alienating, and setting aside because of the difficulty of penetrating the matters, not for a time but permanently. GRONOVIUS.—If anything must nevertheless be changed, I would prefer: “after condemning opinions.” For opinions are uncertain and fallacious, and they are properly opposed to certainty. LINDNER.
>
> *Let us labor; so that,* etc. I read and punctuate: “let us labor in such a way that, as an object of execration,” etc. Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* IV.8: “But as for what I wrote above concerning what Fabricius said about Cornelius Rufinus, he said it in the form in which it is recorded in most historical works.” And IX.14: “I found it written in rather numerous books in the form stated by Caesellius.” Our author speaks in the same way below. DAVIS.
>
> *Are made savage.* Davis changes this to “are carried forth.” Wrongly; for the verb is *efferare* (“to make savage”), not *efferre* (“to carry forth”). Here is a parallel passage from Livy V.27: “made savage by hatred and anger.” Had Wopkens noticed this, he would not have wasted so many words in refuting Davis and showing that indirect discourse, which properly ought to be direct, is not infrequently shifted in this way into the subjunctive after an infinitive. HEUMANN.
>
> *For incautiously credulous.* Heumann has no doubt that Minucius wrote: “For **while** incautiously,” etc. This emendation is not particularly necessary; at most the particle “while” can be understood. LINDNER.
>
> *Anxious.* Since this word is foreign to the present context, I write “accustomed”; and because an infinitive verb is now required, I read: “We, accordingly, are accustomed to hear.” The sense is: “We who plead cases in the forum are accustomed to hear that on both sides,” etc. HEUMANN. The manuscript’s received reading is sound, for *sollicitus* means doubtful or attentive. Cicero, *For His House* 96: “the whole community being in suspense and anxiety.” The same author, *On Friendship* 52: “Everything in the lives of tyrants is always suspect and anxious.” Livy V.47: “dogs, an animal alert to noises in the night.” LINDNER.
>
> *From the other side.* Thus the manuscript. The Roman edition has “may lie concealed.” Supply “may be.” “From the other side, there may be marvelous subtlety.” Heumann, though hesitantly, writes “may shine forth.” The emendation of Fulvius Ursinus, which appears in the Roman edition, is, I confess, highly pleasing to me. LINDNER.
>
> *To look up to.* Perhaps “to undertake.” Thus chapter 6.7: “While they undertake the rites of all nations.” Yet the progression is sufficiently sound even as it stands, for “to look up to” is more than “to approve.” But I now see that Heraldus printed it thus from the Royal manuscript, so that I do not know where Cellarius obtained the verb “to look up to”; it must be entirely removed. LINDNER.

**SUMMARY.—With the disdain of a proud spirit, Caecilius challenges Octavius to answer his arguments. Minucius replies modestly that Caecilius should not exult over his own by no means inconsiderable eloquence or over the harmonious variety of his speech.**
