### Argument.—

He shows that the true knowledge of this God, handed down in writings, is older than all secular literature—indeed, older than all the pagans’ gods themselves, since they were only subsequently enrolled among the gods. For since Moses was approximately contemporary with Inachus himself, he therefore leaves Danaus, the Trojan War, Homer, and anything else reputed ancient far behind him. He sufficiently, and more than sufficiently, shows that, if he wished, he could confirm this from the chronologists.

Thus the highest antiquity vindicates for these documents [Note: “These” is absent from the Paris edition.] their primary authority [Note: *Vindicat*, Junius]. Among you [Col. 0382A] also, it is virtually a religious principle to establish credibility [Note: *Finem*, Fran.] by chronology.

Other editions have different readings: the Gelenian, “most of the numerous things are dead”; the Aldine, “generally of populous mountains”; Barraeus, “generally of seething mountains”; so too Rhenanus in his second edition, though in the first he printed “of populous mountains.” If that reading seems plausible to anyone, I would prefer to correct it thus: “and generally the seethings of mountains.” Certainly in Chapter XL, where he treats all these matters more fully: “But neither Etruria nor Campania was then complaining about the Christians, when fire poured down upon Volsinii from heaven and upon Pompeii from its own mountain.” Accordingly, I myself do not strongly oppose this reading; but my reason for preferring the other is that in the conclusion he says, “They were written down by providence.” He thus intimates that all those events which occur today, and which he now enumerates, were foretold beforehand in the sacred writings. But where are we to look for burning mountains belching out God’s avenging flame? ### Argument.—

Leaving aside antiquity, which can be subject to error, he shows the divinity of Sacred Scripture from its confirmation by the outcome of events. For whatever happens is found to have already been foretold in it, so that it is therefore necessary to believe the things that still remain in the future, since they too are contained and foretold in it.

We now offer more in compensation for that postponement: the majesty [Col. 0389A] of the Scriptures, if not their antiquity [Note: “By antiquity,” Paris.]. We prove them divine, if their ancientness [Note: “Antiquity,” Paris.] is doubted. Nor [Note: “Not,” Paris.] must this be learned more slowly or from some other source. The things that will teach it are before your eyes: the world, the age, and events. Whatever is happening was foretold; whatever is seen was once heard.
