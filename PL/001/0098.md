Accordingly, since Christians had multiplied to such an extent, there is certainly no room for doubt that, mingled with the pagans, they shared with them a common manner of life. For, as Tertullian says, they lived among them with the same food and manner of dress—that is, food and clothing—the same equipment, [Col. 0911C] and the same necessities of life (Idem, *Apologet.*, chap. 42, p. 490). But did he not express it better, someone will say, in the book *To the Nations*, by the word *apparatus*, that is, style and adornment (Idem, book I *ad Nation.*, chap. 10)? Yet if this is the meaning of the noun *apparatus*, and if *instructus* is taken by him, as it is by Cicero (Cicero, book III, *de Orat.*, p. 181, line 37), to mean adornment, how could he have asserted that the Christians’ equipment was the same? Had he not, in the same *Apologeticum*, when censuring the luxury of the pagans, said: “In dress, food, equipment, wealth, you have renounced your ancestors” (Idem, *Apologet.*, chap. 6, p. 305)? It is established, however, by his own testimony, that the Christians’ frugality in all these matters was far greater. We shall therefore have little difficulty in being persuaded that, in the former passage, he was speaking of the things necessary for sustaining life, and which Christians, [Col. 0911D] like common people or the more modest pagans, used.

Yet certainly, although the Christians and pagans had the same food and clothing, and the same public manner of living and acting, Christians were nevertheless distinguished from them and could readily be recognized by their exceptional modesty, patience, and correction of their former vices. Nor was this unknown to the pagans, whom our author therefore addresses in these words: “It can be sufficiently evident to you that we act according to that discipline of divine patience, since so great a multitude of people—almost the greater part of every city—conduct ourselves in silence and modesty, perhaps better known individually than collectively, and [Col. 0912A] recognizable by nothing more than by the correction of our vices” (Tertull., book *ad Scapul.*, chap. 2).

Such, then, were the Christians at that time, whose number was greater than that of the pagans. Nor indeed is it surprising that, though mingled with the pagans, they pursued so pious and holy a manner of life, since they held nothing more venerable or dearer than obedience to those most holy laws which they knew had been given to them by God and Christ the Lord, and which were far superior to all human laws. It now falls to us to prove this point together with our author.

## ARTICLE II. How greatly the laws of the Christians surpassed all laws enacted by the pagans in their perfection and authority; with what diligence those laws were observed by those same Christians, who for that reason refused, even at the peril of their lives, to eat sausages swollen with blood that were offered to them by the pagans

[Col. 0912B]

Since the Christian religion was founded and established by the true God and his Son, all its laws must undoubtedly be most perfect. Therefore, in Tertullian’s time, Christians obeyed them with all the greater care because they knew with all the greater certainty that they had been enacted by the all-seeing and omnipotent God, who therefore can neither be deceived by anyone at all nor treated with contempt with impunity. Accordingly, our author openly declares to everyone: “Having been taught innocence by God, we know it in its perfection, as revealed by a perfect teacher, and we faithfully preserve it, as commanded by a judge who cannot be treated with contempt” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 45, p. 498).

[Col. 0912C] But the laws of the pagans, enacted by human beings alone, are manifestly imperfect and can be scorned, undermined, and violated by everyone with impunity. “For how great,” Tertullian continues—or, as our manuscripts read, “so great,” that is, so small and slight—“is the authority of a human being to exact” (Ibid.) faithful observance of the laws enacted by himself, when he can as easily be deceived as he can be held in contempt by everyone? For such is the weakness of human intelligence that no human being can foresee all those things which will always be good or evil for everyone else. And such is the malice of the wicked that very many of them have little difficulty in escaping the penalties imposed upon transgressors by any human law whatsoever.

Tertullian shows by several further examples that the laws of the Christians are far more perfect than those of the pagans. First, he says: “Which is more comprehensive” (Ibid.), [Col. 0912D] “to say, ‘You shall not kill,’ or to teach, ‘You shall not even become angry’?” (Matt. 5:21ff.). So it stands in all our manuscripts and early editions; but Rigaltius disliked the former words and ordered the text to be written: “Which was more comprehensively said?” He undoubtedly wished to make Tertullian speak more purely and correctly. But if human law, says Tertullian, prohibits adultery, divine law restrains even the solitary lust of the eyes (Ibid.). He said “solitary,” that is, alone, secret, and hidden. Thus Cicero asserts that virtue cannot be solitary, and that nature loves nothing solitary (Cicero, book *de Amicit.*, p. 43, lines 7 and 42). Divine law, therefore, prohibits not only adultery, as human law does, but also the secret [Col. 0913A] and hidden lust of the eyes.

Moreover, human law indeed prohibits wrongdoing but permits vengeance for an injury; divine law, however, forbids even abusive speech itself and the repaying of injury in kind (Tertullian, passage cited). How greatly that law excels human law has already been noted by us elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Minut.*, chap. 13, art. IV, and *Dissertat. in Lactant.*, chap. 26, art. 1ff.). Tertullian nevertheless does not deny that certain human laws are good, but contends that these were taken from the divine books of Moses and the other prophets, whose antiquity, as we saw above, he had previously demonstrated (Above, chap. 4, art. 4).

Not only is the perfection of divine law greater than [Col. 0913B] that of human law, but so too is its authority. For absolutely nothing is hidden from God; and, as we taught a little above, he chastises those guilty of violating his law with the eternal punishments they deserve (Ibid.). But it happens that the person who acts against human laws, as our author says, “usually remains concealed in his offenses, or scorns those laws because of his will or the necessity of offending” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 45, p. 497). Rigaltius, however, altered these words of our manuscripts and printed editions as follows: “For one offending through unwillingness or necessity.” But without indicating any variant reading from any manuscript, he merely observes that “through unwillingness” is the same as “by accident.” What, then, if Tertullian sets the words “through his will” in opposition to “or through necessity” (Ibid.)? Could not his meaning here be: A guilty person usually remains concealed when he sins either [Col. 0913C] through his own will or under some necessity?

Furthermore, he says, human laws are brought to ruin when people reflect upon the brevity of a punishment that cannot be inflicted by human beings beyond death. For this reason, he continues, Epicurus depreciates every torture and pain, declaring a moderate pain contemptible, but a great one not long-lasting (Idem, book I *ad Nation.*, chap. 10; book II *ad Uxor.*, chap. 3; book I *adv. Marcion.*, chap. 6; book *de Spectac.*, chap. 22; book *de Anim.*, chap. 24; book *de Fuga*, chap. 10). He frequently uses the verb *depretiat*, by which he meant nothing other than that one diminishes the value or magnitude of something. Cicero explains Epicurus’ maxim more clearly: “Let them bring out,” he says, “those Epicurean remedies for pain as though from a medicine chest: if it is severe, it is brief; if prolonged, it is slight.” Elsewhere [Col. 0913D] he says: “He consoles us for the magnitude of pain by its brevity, and for its duration by its slightness.” If you desire more, you may consult Laertius, Plutarch, and others (Cicero, book II *de Finib.*, p. 55, line 10, and book IV *Tuscul.*, p. 190, line 49; Laert., book 10 *de Vita Epicuri*, § 140; Plutarch, book *de Poet. audien.*, near the end, p. 36).

Since Christians therefore thoroughly understood the supreme perfection and authority of their laws, they obeyed them with such diligence that they preferred even to place their lives in danger rather than do anything that appeared contrary to any law. From the almost infinite number of examples by which this can be most clearly proved, we shall now take one that our Tertullian records (Vol. I, *Apparat.*, p. 680; *Dissertat.* [Col. 0914A] *in Minut.*, chap. 9, art. 1). The pagans had discovered that among those same Christians there was in force a law by which, as we have observed elsewhere, they were forbidden the blood of animals, meat from strangled animals, and carrion. Therefore, with the utmost pagan malice, “among their trials of Christians,” says Tertullian—that is, in order to test their faith and religion—“they offered sausages swollen with blood” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 9, p. 314). A *botulus*, says Festus, is a kind of sausage which, because of its connection, is so called from *boli* (Festus, book II *de Verbor. Signific.*). Martial also has these verses about it:

> The sausage that came to me in the midst of winter  
> Had arrived seven days before Saturn’s feast.

(Martial, book XIV, epigram 72.)

Accordingly, by offering these sausages, made from pieces of meat mixed with [Col. 0914B] animal blood, the pagans ascertained that those by whom they were rejected were truly followers of the Christian religion. By refusing them, the Christians made it plain to everyone that they preferred to undergo the peril of death rather than ever oppose their laws. Nor did they obey with the same diligence only this and the other laws of God and Christ, but also most of his counsels, although they were not bound by them. These matters, however, will become clearer to everyone from what must now be explained concerning their morals as reported by Tertullian.

## ARTICLE III. That the morals of the Christians were as chaste as those of the pagans were depraved; how great was the Christians’ love and charity toward all; why they called one another by the name of brothers and had all things except wives in common; and how, by contrast, the pagans violated the most sacred rights of brotherhood with impunity

[Col. 0914C]

To demonstrate the character and the piety and holiness of the Christians’ manner of life, our Tertullian compares their morals with those of the pagans and clearly shows that the former were as holy and chaste as the latter were depraved and corrupt. First, although Christians lived together with the pagans in the manner we have described, they were nevertheless joined by so strong a bond of charity and mutual love that even the pagans, their most bitter enemies, could not help admiring it. For why indeed would they not have admired what they recognized as excellent but were unable to imitate? [Col. 0914D] Therefore, by introducing them as speaking in the following manner, Tertullian reveals their mutual hatred just as he reveals the love of the Christians. He writes: “‘See,’ they say, ‘how they love one another’”—for the pagans themselves hate one another—“and how those same Christians are prepared to die for one another.” For the pagans themselves are more prepared to kill one another (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 39, p. 467). How greatly the pagans hated one another, and how prepared they were to kill one another, will become evident to everyone from what will be said below.

So great indeed was the Christians’ charity that, as we have already observed elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Minut.*, chap. 13, art. 4), they called one another brothers not only by the right of nature itself, by which they were also brothers of the pagans, [Col. 0915A] but by four further titles. First, because they acknowledged one God as Father, unknown to the pagans or repudiated by them. Second, because, imbued with the religion of Christ, they “drank one spirit of holiness” (Tertullian, passage cited). Third, because from the one womb of ignorance—in which, before recognizing the true God, they had lain so long in the foul darkness of idolatry—they started at the one light of truth. For they were astounded that they had not previously recognized their utterly absurd errors or the supreme God, whom, as we showed above (Above, chap. 5, art. 1), all were compelled, even against their will, to acknowledge by the light and prompting of their conscience alone.

Fourth and finally, they addressed one another by the name of brothers because, our author further says, “we are brothers in our family property” (Tertullian, Ibid.). [Col. 0915B] For they were accustomed to share this among themselves far more extensively than natural brothers did. In a word, says Tertullian (Ibid.), all things among them were entirely undivided and common.

At that time, therefore, they diligently observed—at least for the most part—what we read was handed down concerning the first Christians in the Acts of the Apostles: “The multitude of believers had one heart and one soul; nor did anyone say that anything he possessed was his own, but all things were common to them” (Acts 4:32). Irenaeus also wrote in this manner concerning the Christians of his own age: “They, the Jews, held the tithes of their possessions as consecrated; but those who have received liberty—that is, Christians—assign all that belongs to them [Col. 0915C] to the Lord’s uses, giving their possessions cheerfully and freely—not lesser things, since they have the hope of greater things—just as that poor widow cast her entire livelihood into the treasury of God” (Irenaeus, book IV *contr. Haeres.*, chap. 18, p. 250, new ed.).

Cyprian, however, Tertullian’s disciple, complains in the following words about that charity of the Christians which had by then diminished—a matter to be discussed again below: “At that time they sold houses and estates, and, laying up treasures for themselves in heaven, offered the proceeds to the apostles to be distributed for the uses of the needy. But now we do not give even a tithe from our patrimony; and though the Lord commands us to sell, we rather buy and increase our possessions. Thus has the vigor of faith withered within us; thus has the strength of believers grown feeble” (Cyprian, book *de Unit. Eccles.*, p. 119ff.; Below, chap. 15, art. 2). In another book, [Col. 0915D] after again proposing the example of the first Christians, he does not ask his own people to sell their patrimonies and estates, but says: “The person who, though a possessor on earth, shares his revenues and produce with the brotherhood, being equitable and just in his gratuitous gifts, is an imitator of God the Father” (Idem, book *de Oper. et Eleemosyn.*, p. 208). Does not our Tertullian, then, likewise appear to be speaking of those same revenues from goods and estates, which, if not all, at least very many Christians shared with others with the utmost liberality?

The pagans were as far removed as possible from this liberality, since on account of their paternal property they broke every bond of brotherhood. For that reason, they greatly marveled that, as Tertullian again says, “no tragedy cries out concerning our brotherhood” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 39, p. 431). [Col. 0916A] For what is more frequent in tragedies than lamentations over brotherhood shattered by pagans? These are found especially, beyond the others, in the tragedy Seneca composed concerning Atreus and Thyestes. For who does not know that it represents the story, often celebrated by Horace, Ovid, and others, in which they imagined that Thyestes was deprived of his kingdom by his brother Atreus—whose wife he had violated—and was then served his own sons to eat at a banquet? Horace sang of this horrible tragedy:

> Let not Medea slaughter her children before the people,  
> Nor wicked Atreus openly cook human entrails.

(Horace, *de Art. poet.*, v. 185.)

Yet the pagans, more savage than the beasts themselves, watched this tragedy with the greatest [Col. 0916B] pleasure.

Christians, however, so abhorred spectacles of this kind, crimes, and greed for the possessions of others that, as we have said, they held absolutely nothing as separate except their wives. “For in this one matter,” says Tertullian, “we dissolve partnership, in which alone other people practice partnership” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 39, p. 431), namely by handing their own wives over to others or corrupting the wives of others. But we shall examine these matters in the following articles.

## ARTICLE IV. It is shown how great was the chastity of Christian spouses, and how certain it is that many persevered in the state of virginity until death; how far the pagans were removed from these morals, among whom Cato and Socrates gave their wives to their friends in marriage

[Col. 0916C]

The more stringent the law by which Christians knew that adultery had been forbidden them, the greater was the care with which they preserved the fidelity pledged in marriage. Therefore, “the most diligent and faithful chastity,” says Tertullian, “guarded all married Christians against illicit intercourse and against every transgression after marriage” (Ibid., chap. 9, p. 314). Nor need this now be proved by us at greater length, since no one can doubt it in view of what has been said elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Lactant.*, chap. 27, arts. 5 and 6) and what will be said below.

Yet not a few, desiring to do something beyond that divine commandment, were accustomed to remain in celibacy and virginity throughout the entire course of their lives. [Col. 0916D] But listen, I pray, to our author narrating these matters in his own words: “Some, far more secure, repel the whole force of this error against the rights of marriage by virginal continence—children grown old” (Tertullian, passage cited); that is, from the earliest age of childhood to extreme old age and the final breath of life, they preserved their virginity inviolate. For followers of true philosophy, says Eusebius (Eusebius, *Orat. de Laud. Constantin.*, chap. 17, p. 661), men who worshiped God, sacred women, and choirs of virgins had learned from Christ to devote themselves to perpetual chastity of mind and body even amid the dreadful storms of persecution. We have discussed this perpetual chastity and virginity of very many Christians in our preceding dissertations [Col. 0917A] (*Dissertat. in Minut.*, chap. 10, art. 1; *Dissertat. in Lactant.*, chap. 27, art. 6).

The pagans, however, were exceedingly far removed from this bodily chastity. “For they not only usurp the marriages of their friends,” says Tertullian, “but most patiently make their own wives available to their friends” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 39, p. 467). There he notes two outrages against chastity committed by the pagans. The first is that of adulterers, who defiled the marriage beds of friends and others. But that crime was extremely frequent, and Juvenal and other pagan poets inveigh against it as often as it deserves. Hence Jerome says: “Whatever causes tragedies to swell and overthrows households, cities, and kingdoms is the rivalry between wives and mistresses” (Jerome, book II *adv. Jovinian.*, p. 191).

[Col. 0917B] Their other crime, no less grave than the first, was that of those who prostituted the chastity of their own wives to their friends. Tertullian proves this by two examples, the Greek Socrates and the Roman Cato, who were nevertheless regarded as the wisest men among those nations: “For these men,” he says, “shared with their friends the wives whom they had married for the sake of children, so that children might be begotten elsewhere”—that is, in a second but altogether unlawful and infamous marriage (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 39, p. 431).

Plutarch, in the *Life of Cato the Younger*, relates that he had received from Thrasea, and Thrasea from Munatius, Cato’s companion, the report that Hortensius, who was bound to Cato by friendship beyond all others, asked him to give him in marriage his wife Marcia, the daughter of Philippus, by whom Cato had already had several sons and who was then still pregnant (Plutarch, *Vit. Caton. minor.*, p. 770ff.). [Col. 0917C] Easily overcome by his friend’s entreaties, Cato granted her to him in the presence and with the approval of her father Philippus. But after Hortensius’ subsequent death, Cato took back his Marcia, whom Hortensius had left heir to his property. Therefore Caesar, as Plutarch expresses it, φιλοπλουτίαν προσφέρει καὶ μισθαρνίαν ἐπι τῷ γάμῳ “reproached him with avarice and with having received payment in the marriage” (Ibid., p. 784).

Appian reported the matter more briefly in these words: “When Cato had married Marcia, the daughter of Philippus, while she was a virgin, and she had become deeply beloved and already the mother of children they had in common, he yielded her to his friend Hortensius, who desired children and a fertile wife, things he had hitherto lacked, until, after she had become a mother by him as well, Cato received her back into his home as though she had been lent for use” (Appian, [Col. 0917D] book II *de Bell. civil.*, after the middle, p. 266).

We likewise read that Strabo wrote that among the Tapyri a law had been accepted whereby men, after having two or three children, gave their wives to others in marriage: καθάπερ καὶ Κάτων Ὁρτησίῳ δεηθέντι ἐξέδωκε τῆν Μαρκίαν ἐφ` ἡμῶν κατὰ παλαιὸν Ῥωμαίων ἔθος “Just as Cato also in our own age handed over his Marcia to Hortensius at his request, following the ancient custom of the Romans” (Strabo, book XI *Geogr.*, p. 265). Nor were there lacking poets who celebrated this deed of Cato in their verses, especially Lucan:

> Meanwhile, as Phoebus dispelled the icy darkness,  
> The doors sounded beneath a knocking; these doors holy Marcia,  
> Mourning, burst through after leaving Hortensius’ tomb,  
> Once a maiden joined to the bed of a better husband. [Col. 0918A]  
> Soon, when the price and payment of marriage had been discharged,  
> Already fruitful with three children, she was given to fill  
> Another household and to mingle two homes  
> Through the blood of their mother.

(Lucan, book II, v. 326ff.)

He then describes how Cato, after Hortensius’ death, had her again in a second marriage:

> No customary jests played, nor in Sabine fashion  
> Did the grave husband receive the festive gathering.  
> No pledges adorned the house; no relatives assembled.  
> They are joined in silence, content with Brutus as witness.  
> He did not remove the bristling hair from his solemn face,  
> Nor admit joy to his stern countenance.

(Ibid., v. 367ff.)

Finally, to pass over Augustine and others, Jerome argues as follows against Jovinian: “Cato married Marcia as his wife, but not as a virgin; rather, Marcia passed back and forth between Hortensius and Cato, and Marcia was able to live without Cato.” And again in the following book: “The nation of the Scots,” he says, “does not have wives of its own; and, as though it had read Plato’s *Republic* and followed Cato’s example, no woman among them is the exclusive wife of anyone, but, like cattle, they act wantonly as each pleases” (Augustine, book *de Fide et Operib.*, chap. 6, and book *de Bon. conjug.*, chap. 18; Jerome, book I *adv. Jovin.*, p. 188; Ibid., book II, p. 201).

Although there were several men distinguished by the name Cato, whose genealogy is presented by Aulus Gellius (Gellius, book XIII, *Noct. Att.*, chap. 19), it is nevertheless established from the authors cited that the Cato with whom we are dealing was Cato of Utica, who is therefore called the Younger because, as the same Gellius says, [Col. 0918C] he was the grandson, through his son, of Cato the Censor. Why then, someone will ask, is he called “Censor” by our Tertullian? For in that very passage he expressly wrote—our manuscripts reading *Lenones*, “panders”—“The pander is Socrates the philosopher, and the censor Cato” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 39, p. 467). Is there, then, in that passage a ἀμάρτημα lapse of his memory and an error?

It may be answered that Tertullian calls Cato a censor not by office, but in disposition and severity of morals. For Lucan, already cited by us, sang:

> These were the morals, this the unchanging school  
> Of stern Cato,

(Lucan, book II, v. 379), namely the Stoic school. The noun “censor” is also taken in this sense by Horace:

> But he who desires to have produced a lawful poem  
> Will assume, along with his tablets, the mind of an honorable censor.

[Col. 0918D] (Horace, book II, epistle 2, v. 379.)

And elsewhere, in the *Art of Poetry*:

> A censor and corrector of the younger men.

(*de Art. poet.*, v. 174.)

Moreover, that Cato, as Appian further testifies, was regarded as the gravest of all men and the most steadfast in his purpose, since he measured what was just and honorable not by the morals of the crowd but by upright and noble principles; or, as Lucan again sang:

> A worshiper of justice, a guardian of strict honor.

(Lucan, book II, v. 388.)

Yet that man, although a preacher of stricter discipline, nevertheless placed his own wife with his friend Hortensius in shameful procuration, [Col. 0919A] contrary to the rights and fidelity of marriage.

Our author accuses Socrates of the same crime. It is indeed certain that he had two wives simultaneously, namely Xanthippe and Myrrho, the daughter of a certain Aristides, or his granddaughter through a daughter. Yet the more this is demonstrated by the testimony of Athenaeus, Laertius, Plutarch, Jerome, Cyril of Alexandria, and Theodoret, the more difficult it is to prove whether Socrates, no differently from Cato, truly shared his wife with his friends (Athenaeus, book XIII, beginning of the *Deipnosoph.*, p. 555; Laertius, book XII, *Vit. Socrat.*, § 26; Plutarch, end of *Vit. Aristid.*, p. 335; Jerome, book II *adv. Jovinian.*, p. 190; Cyril, book VI *advers. Julian.*, p. 186, and book VII, p. 226; Theodoret, [Col. 0919B] vol. IV, sermon 12, *de Virtut. act.*, p. 673). Consider, however, whether the knot of this difficulty may be untied sufficiently by what Cyril of Alexandria and Theodoret have related (Cyril and Theodoret, passages cited): that Xanthippe, married by Socrates, was κοινωτέραν πως at one time rather common, and that by secretly having intercourse with her he fathered his son Lamprocles.

Moreover, concerning both Socrates and Cato, Tertullian added: “I do not indeed know whether they gave their unwilling wives to their friends in marriage.” For how could it have happened that these two wives were unwillingly given by their husbands to marry other men? Does not our author therefore seem to allude to Lucan’s words in which Marcia is represented, as we said, as addressing her husband Cato as follows?

[Col. 0919C]

> While blood and maternal strength remained in me,  
> I carried out your commands, Cato, and, fruitful, received two husbands.  
> With womb exhausted and worn out by childbirth, I return,  
> No longer to be handed over to any man. Grant me the bond  
> Of my former marriage inviolate; grant only the empty name  
> Of wedlock. Let it be permitted that upon my tomb be written,  
> “Marcia, wife of Cato,” lest in a distant age it be asked in doubt  
> Whether I exchanged my first marriage torches because I was cast out  
> Or because I was handed over.

(Lucan, book II, v. 234.)

For there she is said to have requested that, after Hortensius’ death, she be received by Cato into her former marriage. But she offers this reason for her request: that posterity might know she had not been rejected and repudiated by Cato himself, by whom she had been handed over to Hortensius. Yet she could have been handed over by him without being unwilling or resistant. Since, however, she had previously said, “I carried out your commands,” it can be inferred that against her own will she first left her husband and [Col. 0919D] accepted a second. Tertullian therefore does not improperly admit that the matter was uncertain to him. But however the matter may stand, Christians were far removed from this corruption of morals, since they always preserved intact, with the utmost zeal, the sacred rights of marriage established by God. But, if you please, we shall examine in the following article how he confirms this.

## ARTICLE V. How Tertullian demonstrates that the chastity of the Christians was very great and the incontinence of the pagans equally great: that Christians did not change into the female sex, whereas young men were corrupted by Socrates; that Christians preserved the fidelity pledged to their wives, whereas Aristippus desperately loved the prostitute Phryne and Speusippus perished in adultery; that Christians, with sound eyes but a blind mind, saw women, whereas Democritus blinded himself so that he would not desire them; and that the pagans, by condemning a Christian woman to a procurer rather than a lion, betrayed both their own intemperance and the chastity of Christians

[Col. 0919D]

[Col. 0920A] Let us follow our author and see how he continues the comparison he has begun between the chastity of Christians and that of pagans: “A Christian,” he says, “does not change even into the sex of a woman” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 46, p. 500). So indeed it stands in Rigaltius’ edition, and he observes that the words “into the sex of a woman” mean the same as “he changes into the female sex.” But neither he nor the others who followed him have indicated to us whence he took this reading. In our manuscripts, however, and in all the other printed editions, we read: “A Christian does not change even into the female sex.” [Col. 0920B] That is, to use the Apostle’s words, he is not “one who lies with males” (Paul, Second Epistle to the Corinthians, chap. 6, v. 10, and First Epistle to Timothy, chap. 1, v. 10).

For there our author sets Christians in opposition to Socrates, concerning whom he writes: “I read part of the Attic sentence against Socrates: he is pronounced a corrupter of young men.” But here again Tertullian’s text has been altered by Rigaltius; in our manuscripts and all other printed editions it stands as follows: “I read . . . that Socrates was pronounced a corrupter of young men” (Tertullian, passage cited). Why, then, should anything be altered there without the testimony of any manuscript whatsoever and contrary to the unanimous authority of all the books? Does not this form of expression convey clearly enough, and in good Latin, Tertullian’s true meaning: that the sentence of death passed by the Athenians upon Socrates was based in part on the fact that he was accused not only of denying the gods [Col. 0920C] but also of corrupting young men?

Certainly he is charged more than once with this unspeakable crime by Lucian, who in one place introduces him confessing his guilt (Lucian, *Vitar. Auctio.*, p. 193), and elsewhere accuses him of perjury, since he had sworn that he associated innocently with young men (Idem, book II, *Verar. histor.*, p. 397), although Hyacinthus and Narcissus openly testified that they had been shamefully loved by him. Athenaeus also charges him with an utterly disgraceful love for Alcibiades, as Cyril of Alexandria and Theodoret later did (Athenaeus, book V, *Deipnosoph.*, chap. 19, p. 219; Cyril, book VI *in Julian.*, p. 137; Theodoret, sermon 12, *de Virt. Act.*, p. 672). Indeed, these two later writers affirm that he burned with detestable lusts.

But by whose testimony, you will ask, can it be proved that [Col. 0920D] he was condemned by the Athenians not only for scorning and rejecting the gods of the pagans, but also for this love of young men, which can never be sufficiently execrated? First, you have Plato, who recorded that Socrates was accused by Meletus of unlawfully and impiously corrupting the youth and, after denying the gods, introducing new ones. Laertius repeats the same charges. Xenophon, nevertheless, attempted to acquit him of both crimes, and especially the first (Xenophon, book I, *Memorabil.*, p. 711ff.), both because he cultivated continence with the utmost zeal and because he continually taught this virtue to his disciples. He does not deny, however, that Alcibiades and Critias, after being placed under his instruction, became corrupt [Col. 0921A] and utterly dissolute young men; but not, he says, until after they had departed from him.

Yet Xenophon may have written this with greater partiality toward Socrates than zeal for the truth. For if Socrates believed the gods of the pagans to be real and worshiped them, why was he afraid to imitate their deeds and crimes? Furthermore, although he may have preached continence in the public schools, were not pagan philosophers, as we have said elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Lactant.*, chap. 35, art. 4), accustomed to do privately and at home the very things they publicly condemned in their schools? Finally, Xenophon himself could not deny that Socrates was accused of a most shameful love for young men, and very many, as we have shown, were persuaded that he was in fact guilty of it. Who, then, will not readily persuade himself that mention of this crime also formed part of the sentence [Col. 0921B] passed against him?

Furthermore, our author says: “A Christian is born a man for his wife alone” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 46, p. 500). For he greatly abhorred every forbidden kind of immodesty and, as we have seen, always preserved fidelity toward his wife. By contrast, Tertullian says of the pagans: “I know Phryne the prostitute, in heat for the passion of Diogenes reclining above her.” In editions earlier than Rigaltius: “in heat for the passion of him reclining above her”; but in our manuscripts: “in heat for the passion of the one reclining above her.” There he censures the wholly ungovernable lust of Diogenes, who desperately loved Phryne. We read in Athenaeus, however, that Aristippus was rebuked by his slave because he gave an immense sum of money to Phryne, who rolled about with Diogenes [Col. 0921C] the Cynic for nothing (Athenaeus, book XIII, *Deipnosoph.*, p. 588). We have said something elsewhere, and more than once, about that Phryne, the most famous of prostitutes (Vol. I, *Apparat.*, p. 744; *Dissertat. in Arnob.*, chap. 20, art. 3).

Tertullian then says: “I also hear that a certain Speusippus of Plato’s school perished in adultery” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, chap. 46, p. 500). We have observed elsewhere that this Speusippus was, as Laertius says, the son of Plato’s sister Potone, and Plato’s successor in the Academy (Vol. I, *Apparat.*, pp. 1131 and 1134; Laertius, book IV, *Vit. Speus.*, § 1). Yet according to the same Laertius he is said to have been ὀργίλος, καὶ ἡδονῶν ἥττων ἦν “irascible and devoted to pleasures” (Idem, Ibid.). Athenaeus likewise testified that he was not far removed from the pleasure-seeking Epicureans [Col. 0921D] and Cyrenaics, and that Dionysius the tyrant reproached him for his love of the prostitute Lasthenia (book XII, chap. 12, p. 546). Φιλήδονος ἦν καὶ Σπεύσιππος, ὁ Πλάτωνος συγγενὴς, καὶ διαδοχος τῆς σχολῆς Διονύσιος γοῦν, ὁ της Σικελίας τύραννος, ἐν τῇ πρὸς αὐτὸν ἐπιστολῇ κατὰ τῆς φιληδονίας αὐτοῦ εἰπὼν, καὶ φιλαργυρίαν αὐτῷ ὀνειδίζει, καὶ τὸν Λασθενείας τῆς Ἀρκαδικῆς ἔρωτα, ἥτις Πλάτωνος ἠκηκόει “Speusippus, Plato’s successor and kinsman, was also devoted to pleasure. Accordingly, Dionysius, tyrant of Sicily, in his letter to him censures his pursuit of pleasure, reproaches him with avarice, and condemns his love for Lasthenia of Arcadia, who attended Plato’s lectures.” Yet this Lasthenia is earlier called by him σαρδικὴ, “of Sardis,” and by Laertius μαντινικὴ, “of Mantinea” (Laertius, book [Col. 0922A] *Vit. Platon.*, § 45, and book IV, *Vit. Speus.*, § 2).

Laertius reports two opinions concerning Speusippus’ death (Ibid., §§ 3 and 4). According to the first, worn out by paralysis, grief, and old age, he voluntarily brought death upon himself; according to the second, his life was taken from him by a violent eruption of lice. Therefore, when Tertullian says that he perished in adultery, he does not appear to mean, as some think, that he breathed his last when caught in adultery, but that he wallowed in adultery down to the time of his death.
