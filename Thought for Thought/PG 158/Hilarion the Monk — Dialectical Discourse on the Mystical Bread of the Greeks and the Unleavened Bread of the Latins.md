In a discussion about the Greeks’ mystical bread and the Latins’ unleavened bread, I was shown a treatise—an exposition by Nicetas, a very learned Greek father. It argues at length against the Latins over the unleavened bread at Christ’s supper. Nicetas says that there could have been no unleavened bread on that Thursday, because the moon was only on its thirteenth day and unleavened bread was customarily prepared on the fifteenth. He therefore takes the evangelists’ “he took bread, gave thanks, and broke it” to mean ordinary bread, not unleavened bread, because the season for unleavened bread had not yet begun. The exact words in this clause are corrupt, but the contrast is fixed by Nicetas’s claim and Hilarion’s reply. Elsewhere he asks: if Christ was sacrificed and crucified on the fourteenth day, Friday, while the feast of Unleavened Bread fell on Saturday, when did Christ eat unleavened bread and institute its use for the apostles?

I am astonished that an educated man should say this. Nicetas plainly contradicts both the Gospels and the Law, both about eating the lamb and about the date of Passover: he puts it on Saturday rather than Friday. The OCR then breaks down in a short phrase, but the author rejects the claim. To say that Christ ate the Paschal lamb without unleavened bread is self-contradictory. The Law never commands the Paschal lamb separately from unleavened bread: God tells Moses and Aaron that every household is to take a perfect year-old male lamb or kid, slaughter it, eat it roasted by fire that night, and eat it with unleavened bread and bitter herbs.

The author continues with the Law: from the fourteenth evening until the twenty-first evening the Israelites must eat unleavened bread; no leaven may be found in their homes for seven days; whoever eats leavened food is cut off from Israel. If Christ ate the lamb in obedience to that Law, he must also have eaten unleavened bread.

He then adduces Matthew, Mark, and Luke: the disciples ask where they are to prepare the Passover; Mark calls it the first day of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover was sacrificed; and Luke says that the day arrived on which the Passover had to be sacrificed. He invokes Theophylact, whose see is garbled in the OCR, to say that the evangelist calls the day before the Preparation the first of Unleavened Bread, because unleavened bread was eaten that evening. “Beloved Greeks,” he says, the old Law and New Testament make it perfectly clear: Christ ate both the lamb and unleavened bread. Jewish custom too, he claims, was to eat unleavened bread on the evening of the fourteenth lunar day.

The argument then turns philosophical. “Bread” includes both unleavened and leavened bread. Some people foolishly deny that unleavened bread is bread, deriving *artos* from an alleged relation to leaven’s “raising.” The author dismisses this as empty etymology. Bread is the genus; unleavened and leavened bread are its species, like man and horse under the genus animal. Both kinds share the name and definition of bread, just as man and horse share the name and definition of animal. Leaven may even be called the completion of bread, yet it corrupts it; Paul’s maxim is then quoted: a little leaven corrupts the whole lump. The source ends the locus in garbled OCR while returning to Mosaic legislation.

## Source locus PG158.527

If Passover fell on Saturday, the author asks, why did Christ say on Wednesday at Bethany that in two days the Passover would come and the Son of Man [OCR-normalized from a damaged phrase] would be handed over for crucifixion—that is, on Friday? On Thursday evening, which all the evangelists call the first day of Unleavened Bread, Christ ate the lamb with his disciples. By the Law, the lamb is sacrificed on the fourteenth lunar day and Passover with the feast of Unleavened Bread is on the fifteenth.

Why then claim that Christ moved the observance forward one day because he would be crucified on Friday? Luke says that the day came “on which it was necessary” to sacrifice the Passover. “Necessary,” the author insists, means the compulsion of the Law, not a free choice by Christ to alter the time.

The author concludes, following learned Latin theologians, that Christ was crucified on the actual day of Passover: Friday, the Preparation. He willingly suffered and offered himself as the true Lamb at the time when the lamb that prefigured him was sacrificed [OCR: `τοπικοῦ`, probably `τυπικοῦ`]; astronomers, he says, place the moon on its fifteenth day after the vernal equinox on that Friday. The association of Passover with Saturday, he argues, developed from an old Jewish practice of combining two feasts into one Sabbath for reasons concerning food; this explains John’s “great Sabbath,” as a double feast. He says that Jews never celebrate Passover on Friday.

He closes by addressing the Greeks affectionately. He is not attacking their bread—he worships it just as he worships “our unleavened bread,” as the author calls it. What is wrong and unchristian, he says, is insulting and dishonoring Latin unleavened bread. The true Christ is present in both. Having received this loving admonition, they should abandon their obstinacy and abusive speech.
