GRAVITY. I, 1325 A, “With regard to the gravity that must be obeyed.” Gravity (*gravitas*), σεμνότης (“dignity”), is the quality of men that must be obeyed, that is, to which women ought to submit because of the fear owed to the Lord. II, 550 A, “Lest they be worshipped with gravity.” See WORSHIP.

[Col. 1310B] RUDDER. II, 22 C, “To contend with the rudder of interpretation,” that is, with reason, with the discipline of reason, which ought to govern every interpretation and always reign within it.

THROAT. II, 133 B, “For man has been torn from the devil’s throat through faith.” II, 958 B, “He sold salvation to his throat.” II, 977 A, “That man, in short, sacrifices his throat to an idol.” II, 281 A, “They commit an offense against the throat,” that is, they harm the very person who has overwhelmed his throat with delicacies. II, 800 A, “And the particular voracity of those very times.” Rhenanus thinks that Tertullian is alluding to the gluttonous cruelty of Vedius Pollio, who used to plunge condemned slaves into tanks of lampreys, not only so that he might watch an entire human being torn apart at once, as Pliny reports, but also so that he might feed upon human flesh in the fish. Compare II, 1048 B ff. It may, however, also be understood of time itself, for time devours all things. I, 632 C, “And the throat toward the offense of the throat,” that is, toward intemperance of appetite. II, 965 C, “It is not credible that anyone should sacrifice half his throat [Col. 1310C] to God, sober in drink but drunk on food.”

GYMNOSOPHIST. I, 490 B, “For we are neither Brahmins nor Indian gymnosophists, forest-dwellers and exiles from life.” The Brahmins were philosophers of the Indians; they were also called gymnosophists because their bodies were naked except for those parts which nature willed to be concealed, although some assert that the gymnosophists were distinct from the Brahmins. They are called forest-dwellers either because they lived in forests, like the Druids among the ancient Gauls, or because they were estranged from the possessions and mode of life enjoyed by ordinary human society. II, 260 C, “The gymnosophists of the Indians.”

# H

TO HAVE is the Greek ἔχειν, in both senses, so that it can mean both “to be able” and “to be obliged.” For example, I, 409 A, “They have, by way of dwelling in the air,” that is, they are able to dwell and have an abode in the air. II, 834 A, “We can also allegorize the mention of garments in the Scriptures as referring to the hope [Col. 1310D] of the flesh.” II, 710 A, “But that the soul is held to be recalled so many times,” that is, ought to be recalled. II, 1044 B, “On which they were held,” that is, on which they were seated. Latinus proposes “were carried.” II, 206 B, “And thus he is now held by that which is permitted,” etc., that is, he is guilty in respect of what is permitted, as in the Greek ἔχεται, ἐνοχός ἐστι (“he is held; he is liable”). II, 564 A, “While things stand thus,” that is, while his affairs are in that condition, οὕτω τῶν πραγμάτων ἔχουσα (“with affairs being thus”). I, 1283 A, “For that blindness was then situated far from the boundaries of the age.”

CAPABLE. II, 224 B, “By making it visible and capable,” that is, capable of perfection—not perfect in every respect, but fitted to receive perfection. I, 1222 B, “Every time is suitable for baptism.”

GARB; CONDITION. II, 82 C, “Your substance was given to you by God, its garb by the age,” that is, that manner of wearing flowers, whatever precisely it may be, comes from the world. I, 452 B, “To efface the city by giving it the condition of a tavern,” that is, to exchange civic order and respectability for the confusion of a tavern. I, [Col. 1311A] 453 A, “To clothe your house in the appearance of some new brothel.” II, 648 A, “So that it may swallow even death, not from a cup, but under the guise of pleasure.”

HITHERTO. II, 229 A, “Even the sea only until then,” that is, it will exist up to that time and no further; thereafter it will not exist. II, 1034 B, “Thus far Sodom, and no Gomorrah.” I, 1240 C, “Only thus far shall we expose ourselves to dangers,” that is, never hereafter. II, 1001 A, “So that those who have sinned hitherto.”

ADHERENCE. II, 420 B, “As a testimony and an adherence of their land.” He appears to have said “adherence of the land” in order to explain what Luke intended to be understood by the word “dust.”

TO HESITATE. II, 557 A, “To hesitate concerning the explanation of the occurrence,” that is, to be uncertain about the cause of the event that happened.

HOOKED. II, 122 A, “A hooked dart,” namely one barbed at the tip, whose series of knots constricts the wound while it is being inflicted and, as it is withdrawn, pours its venom from a little channel into the sore.

SAND. II, 1034 A, “Samos of the sand”; others read “of sand.” Junius reads *Arene*. He understands Samos to mean the Achaean islands. Strabo, book VIII, III.45. Or, “by the atrocities of the arena”; see ARENA. II, 700 A, “The sands are sown.”

AUCTION-REGISTER, a sheet on which items for sale and items sold were recorded. Compare *To the Nations*, I, 345 A; 573 A, “Whom you hold as taxable entries in the auction-register.”

HEBREW. II, 160 A, “They say, indeed, that Genesis also begins thus in the Hebrew: ‘In the beginning God made a Son for himself.’”

HEBDOMAD. II, 375 B, “So that it might receive the expiation of the whole hebdomad,” that is, of the seven stains discussed immediately before.

HELUCUS; others read *elucus*, although Gellius noted that an *h* was prefixed for euphony, book XVI, chapter 12. Festus says: “*Elucus* signifies one who is languid and half-asleep, or, as others maintain, a hallucinator and lover of trifles; or a *halo*, that is, one languishing from yesterday’s wine, whom the Greeks call ἕωλον.” Gellius likewise correctly explains it as slowness of mind and stupor, which commonly [Col. 1311C] befalls those who hallucinate. II, 85 B, “Because it is ivy’s nature to protect the brain from a *helucus*.”

YESTERDAY, meaning very recently. I, 287 A, “Only yesterday Severus, the most resolute of emperors, excluded them.”

HERMAPHRODITE. II, 590 A, “Lest he suppose that a Hermaphrodite exists only among the people of Luna.” Hermaphrodites are human beings of both sexes. They were formerly called Androgynes, as Pliny reports, *Natural History*, book VII, chapter 3, and book XI, chapter 49. Hence, by catachresis, cheeses too were called “Hermaphrodites” when made from two adjoining regions, as Fenestella appears to have written in his *Annals* concerning the cheeses of Luna, which were once highly esteemed. See Pliny, *Natural History*, book XI, chapter 42.

HERMOTIMUS. II, 649 B, “Like Hermotimus, to whom the people of Clazomenae dedicated a temple after his death.” Pliny, book VII, chapter 52, calls him Hermotimus of Clazomenae; Plutarch, however, substitutes Hermodorus in his book *On the Daimonion of Socrates*, where he says that it is false that the soul of Hermodorus of Clazomenae used to leave his body, wander through many places by day and night, and return again to the body. [Col. 1311D] That city, Clazomenae, which was in Ionia, was formerly called Gryna; there was a temple of Apollo there, very widely renowned for its oracle, whence the name Apollo Grynaeus.

RECENT, new or recent. II, 157 A, “Even before Praxeas, a man of yesterday.”

HIERAN ANAPHEN. I, 480 A. Others read *Hieranapen*. Pamelius reads “Hierapolis” and says, on the authority of Eusebius and Orosius, that three cities fell during Nero’s reign: Hierapolis, Laodicea, and Colossae.

HIEROPHANT. II, 260 C, “And the hierophants of the Egyptians.” They were called ἱεροφάνται (“revealers of sacred things”) because they displayed or performed the sacred rites; they were a class of priests among the Egyptians.

JERUSALEM. II, 20 B, “What, then, has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” that is, what have philosophers to do with Christians?

HENCE, meaning “concerning this.” II, 246 B, “As for this, let him see to it,” a Tertullianic phrase meaning: I do not care; it is of no concern to me; I do not wish it to be regarded as my responsibility to make good.

HIPPONA. Turnebus and Scaliger judge that this should be written [Col. 1312A] *Epona*. Plutarch, in the *Parallels*: Φούλβιος στέλλος μισῶν γυναῖκας ἵππῳ συνεμίγγετο, ἡ δὲ κατὰ χρόνον ἔτεκε κόρην σύμμορφον, καὶ ὠνόμασεν Ἔποναν, ἔστι δὲ θεὸς πρόνοιαν ποιουμένη ἵππων: “Fulvius Stellus, hating women, coupled with a mare; in due course she bore a girl resembling herself, and he named her Epona; she is a goddess who exercises providential care over horses.” Her image was generally erected in stables and adorned with garlands of roses. At I, 365 A, and I, 577 C, the reading is also *Epona*.

HISTRICULUS, δασύπρωκτος (“hairy-buttocked”), one free from abominable and unnatural sexual intercourse. II, 1041 A. Mercerus reads *ustriculas*, that is, “little fires and sparks of lust.” Turnebus proposes *striculis*, that is, “a somewhat older boy.”

TODAY. II, 274 A, “Those who today know from admitted facts that a murderer is homicide.” Heumann reads: “When the murderer’s guilt is admitted, knowing what homicide is.”

HOMERIC EYES, that is, blind eyes. I, 1033 A.

HOMEROCENTONES, those who are so called. II, 53 A.

MAN. II, 87 A, “Certainly not otherwise when he has completed the human lot,” τὸν ἄνθρωπον τετεληκότι, that is, τὰ [Col. 1312B] ἀνθρώπινα (“human affairs”). II, 94 B, “Nor to render man to God,” for man is the coin and image of God; so Tertullian often writes.

COMELINESS. II, 772 A, “Thus his body possessed not even human comeliness,” that is, form and beauty.

COMELY. II, 329 C, “Not comely even in appearance,” that is, beautiful. II, 639 A.

HONOR. I, 1312 A, “Likewise the honor derived from the colors of garments,” that is, the most highly esteemed garments. II, 810 A, “The honor of the world is darkened,” that is, the sun. I, 1265 A, “But what honor shall we sacrifice to the Lord God?” II, 651 B, “The honor of Plato,” that is, Plato, the most highly honored of philosophers, δι’ ὑπαλλαγῆς (“by hypallage”), as with everything that follows. For Labeo also thought Plato should be numbered among the demigods; after his death the Magi sacrificed to him as to a god, according to Cicero in the *Tusculan Disputations*.

HONORARY. I, 470 A, “Not concerning the honorary payment,” that is, the sum given for admission into an association, as though its honors had been purchased. Havercamp [Col. 1312C] reads it as one word, *dehonorario*, meaning “disgraceful, dishonorable.”

HONOR-BEARING. II, 903 A, “The honor-bearing mark of her virginity.”

HOROSCOPE-TAKER, one who examines the hour of a person’s birth and predicts the future from it. II, 1039 A, “Which, by dissolving through the air, stretches out positions suitable for the horoscope-takers among spiders.” Others read: “It stretches itself out, after the manner of spiders, with suitable brushes.” The *scopi* or *scorpiones* are the fibers of leaves by which it fastens and stretches itself out.

STRANGE. II, 249 A, “He readily argued that a new and strange divinity had been revealed in Christ,” that is, a foreign divinity, a newcomer.

HUMANITY, meaning a human being or human nature. I, 335 A, “The reason for choosing humanity for divinity.” II, 750 B, “To which all humanity is drawn,” that is, all human beings.

HUMILIATION. II, 908 A, “Whose proof consists in every humiliation.” II, 733 A, “But in order to win God over through duties of humiliation.”

TO HUMBLE. I, 1243 B, “Accordingly, confession is a discipline for prostrating and humbling a human being.”

HUMILITY. I, 1326 B, “For how shall we be able to fulfill the humility which we Christians profess?” II, 98 A, “It bears the burden of its humility.”

HYDROPHOBIC, ὑδροφόβος, one who fears water. I, 1205 A.

A SUBMERGED ABYSS, ὑποβρύχιον, in water or the sea, equivalent to a chasm on land, which devours and swallows up people and ships by submerging them. I, 695 A, “Idolatry is a breathless submerged abyss for those whom it has devoured.”

EXALTATION. I, 701 B, “Placed in its own exaltation and house.” He employs the vocabulary of astrologers while speaking of an eclipse [Col. 1313A] of the sun that was not ordinary but prodigious. Astrologers today commonly call these “altitude” and “house.”

# I

IAO. II, 565 A, “So that he even shouted ‘Iao!’ at her.” It is the cry of one who, by exclamation, halts another and urges him to desist, as is shown by the following formulas, in the first of which the Quirites were invoked, and in the second Caesar, so that the immediate assault might be checked by their majesty and authority. Ibid., “Hence *Iao* is found in the writings,” namely those of the heretics.

STROKE. II, 131 A, “Let us consider another stroke with regard to the quality of the will,” a metaphor taken from gladiators. Cicero, *First Catilinarian*: “How many attacks of yours have I escaped by a slight deflection and, as they say, by a movement of the body, though they were so aimed that it did not appear possible to avoid them!” The meaning is: once one argument has been refuted, to investigate how another may be weakened and to reckon within oneself by what reasoning it can be disproved.

UNLEARNED PERSON. II, 164 A, “Every unlearned or perverse person has misunderstood this saying.” II, 157 C, “For all the simple—so as not to call them imprudent and unlearned—who always constitute the greater part of believers.” II, 1012 D, “But this is customary among perverse and unlearned heretics.” Rigault reads “among the unlearned and heretics.”

ABLE is the Greek ἱκανός (“sufficient, competent”). II, 809 C, “And surely he who made it is able to restore it.” II, 597 B, “Although we have God himself as an able promisor.” II, 991 A, “And the cast lots [more correctly, as Ursinus already saw, ‘filth’] cannot be washed away”; see FILTH. II, 1039 A, “Which, by dissolving through the air, stretches out positions suitable for the horoscope-takers among spiders.”

TO GROW SLUGGISH. II, 724 A, “Nor does it become wholly sluggish.”

FIERY. II, 19 B, “A fiery god,” that is, fire, which Heraclitus of Ephesus made a god, or at least the first principle of everything.

FIRE. II, 13 B, “Bringing upon themselves the heat of a greater fire,” that is, a divine fire, greater than any human or worldly fire. II, 27 A, “For the profane to be judged by perpetual fire.” II, 276 [Col. 1313C] C, “For whom no fire is kindled in Gehenna.” I, 1284 A, “At Rome, indeed, those women who tend an image of that unquenchable fire”—a periphrasis for the Vestal Virgins.

FORGIVENESS. I, 1241 B, “Although the gate of forgiveness is closed.”

ALLURER. I, 1244 A, “Enjoining a way of life that allures mercy,” that is, induces it, from *illicere*, “to allure.” I, 488 A, “You who are always enticers of evils.”

CHILDLESS, ἄπαις. II, 443 C, “As when a childless brother has died.”

TO DRIVE IN. II, 126 B, “There let the scorpion drive its curse into the sandal”; this is equivalent to what he said above, “to press upon the beast’s heel.”

TO ILLUMINATE. II, 386 A, “While he illuminates the difference between works.” II, 847 A–B, “He likewise illuminated what is beneficial to whom.” I, 286 A, “Do not daily experiences illuminate for you the darkness of antiquity?” etc.

ILLUMINATOR. II, 401 B, “The illuminator of a new and so great religion.” I, 395 A, “The illuminator and guide of the human race.” II, 85 A, “The authors or illuminators of the matter.”

[Col. 1313D] TO SACRIFICE. I, 1327 A, “But they sacrifice the humility of their soul to God also through chastisement of their diet.” II, 968 A, “She who, fasting until evening, sacrifices a richer prayer to God.” II, 999 B, “If he has sacrificed repentance to God.” II, 707 B, “To whom the people sacrifice with their votes.”

UNCLEAN, used substantivally. I, 1309 A, “The adornment that ought to be called feminine uncleanness,” namely the care of the hair and skin.

UNTOUCHED. II, 1040 A, “And only the tip of the knife remained untouched.”

UNMUSICAL, ἄμουσος, one ignorant of music. I, 265 A.

UNABLE TO ENDURE. I, 1257 B, “He is unable even to endure keeping silent.”

TO BE EXPENDED. I, 496 A, “Since so many righteous persons among us are expended,” concerning Christians who are handed over to death and removed from the midst of society. I, 651 B, “Because his equal, a human being, has become so guilty that he should be expended so cruelly.”

COMMAND. I, 426 A, “He likewise entrusted firm commands.” [Col. 1314A] Just as in the Eleusinian mysteries there was an ἄρχων (“ruler”), who was the king or superintendent of the mysteries. In Hesychius: βασιλεύς, ἄρχων τις Ἀθήνησι μυστηρίων προνοῶν, “king: a certain magistrate at Athens who has charge of the mysteries.” Subordinate to him and attached to his command were οἱ ἐπιμελῆται (“the overseers”) and ἱεροποιοί (“performers of sacred rites”), concerning whom see Pollux, VIII.8.3 and 26. Meursius also writes admirably in *The Eleusinian Mysteries*, chapter 15: “Thus here too, in the service of Cybele, such a king of the Galli, or Archigallus, is indicated, who has his own petty sacrificing priests and lesser Galli, whom he likewise commanded to make vows and cut their arms for the safety of Aurelius.”

SKIN ERUPTION, a disfigurement of the skin that spreads with itching. II, 1048 A, “I spare no lethargic disorders, no skin eruption.”

TO STRIKE AGAINST. I, 280 B, “Through the blindness of hatred they stumble into voting approval,” that is, with blind hatred they praise, as though by their vote, the uprightness of these people. I, 661 B, “If anyone has lustfully struck against an eye.”

TO PLACE UPON, meaning to found or construct. II, 1031 A, “Where Statilius Taurus erected walls.”

TO BRING UPON. I, 1318 A, “Why do we bring lust upon another?”

[Col. 1314B] UNTIMELINESS. II, 927 C, “The untimeliness of children.” Junius interprets this as offspring inopportunely imposed upon free persons. This is unconvincing. *Importunitas* is that which possesses neither a convenient time nor place. He spoke of “the untimeliness of children” because parents had to undergo greater calamities and more numerous hardships on account of their children. II, 103 A, “According to place and time, and according to the unsuitability of certain persons.”

INCONVENIENT; BANEFUL. Servius, on Virgil, *Aeneid* XI.305, explains *importunus* as a place where there is no refuge, because it lacks a harbor, that is, rest. I, 488 A, “You, therefore, are baneful to human affairs,” that is, calamitous and troublesome, because on your account the world lacks public peace. II, 133 A, “He would refrain from only one little tree, which the physician himself knew to be unsuitable for the time being.”

EMPHATICALLY. II, 332 B, “Which the Apostle enjoins so emphatically.” I, 377 B, “But in a fuller and more emphatic manner,” that is, more clearly.

TO IMPRESS, to promulgate by a law enacted within one’s dominion. [Col. 1314C] I, 297 A, “No Verus promulgated it.” The expression is taken from military affairs, where cohorts are said “to make an impression” when they charge the enemy’s battle line and throw it into confusion.

LACK OF FORESIGHT. II, 469 A, “As though I considered it a lack of foresight.”

SHAMELESSNESS. II, 412 B, “He would make satisfaction to God for the shamelessness of idolatry through the shamelessness of faith.” The shamelessness of idolatry consisted in feeling no shame at worshipping stones and pieces of wood; the shamelessness of faith consists in feeling no shame at believing that God was born and crucified.

UNCHASTITY. I, 397 A, “The Son of God has no mother arising from unchastity.” Rigault, Rhenanus, and Havercamp read “arising from chastity,” so that the sense would be: the Son of God was not merely not born from impure and illicit intercourse, but was not even conceived within a pure and lawful marriage.

IN. II, 1031 A, “In the bite of the clasp,” that is, through the clasp’s bite. II, 123 A, “Into the wind,” that is, with the wind carrying him, [Col. 1314D] before a favorable wind, as opportunity may offer or recommend. II, 484 A, “If it is accepted in reference to the Creator”; supply “interpretation,” namely, if it is understood as referring to the Creator. I, 1262 A, “When they commit robbery on the road after the manners of beasts,” that is, in the manner of beasts, as beasts do. Instead of *per viam* (“on the road”), Rigault reads *per invia* (“through pathless places”). Thus in *On the Soul*: “Because it has also exposed to beasts those whom it slaughtered in forests and trackless places.”

UNACCUSED. I, 529 A, “Unaccused and unpunished.”

TO MAKE ONE WITH ANOTHER. II, 893 C, “Therefore, those whom he did not divide, he made one with another by his silence.” This should be read instead of *in altera iniit*. *Inalteravit* means “he placed in another, joined, blended,” so that the two are now one and mutually contain each other. He himself writes shortly afterward: “He did not separate him from the woman, and by not separating him he joined him to her from whom he did not separate him.”

INANIMATE THINGS, things that do not move of themselves. II, 230 C; 236 C, “Inanimate and incorporeal things sing praises.” He alludes to the hymn of the three youths in Daniel III, according to Theodotion’s edition; Jerome correctly noted that it is [Col. 1315A] not found in the Hebrew. Cyprian also cites the same hymn, under the title *Confession*, in his books *On the Lapsed* and *On the Lord’s Prayer*.

INCOMPREHENSIBLE. II, 236 A.

LACK OF GOODNESS. I, 624 A, “Where every hardness, lack of goodness, and unpleasantness resides.”

UNCERTAIN. II, 970 B, “Uncertain martyrs,” that is, those whose faith has been less thoroughly observed and not sufficiently tested.

TO ENGRAVE. I, 429 A, “Before those gods were engraved,” that is, cut and carved, for the gods were identified by their inscriptions. II, 255 C, “Which title of the two shall we engrave for Marcion’s god?”

TO ENCLOSE. I, 1236 B, “But they neglect to enclose it”—that is, repentance—“by baptism,” which is also called a sealing. He himself writes shortly afterward: “The bolt of intention shut fast.” Repentance and faith are therefore enclosed when they are brought to baptism, just as Gaius called legal proceedings “enclosed by a trial” when they had been brought to trial. Furthermore, those who renounce their former ways only reluctantly neglect to enclose repentance, as though the full sum [Col. 1315B] of the misdeeds that they intend to have enclosed by baptism were not yet complete.

DWELLING, abode or residence. This is a word employed also by Varro, whom Tertullian greatly imitates. I, 409 A, “They have it by way of dwelling in the air.” II, 833 A, “Truly holy through the indwelling of the Holy Spirit.” II, 747 C, “It promises that it will summon her from her dwelling in the underworld.” II, 96 A, “We are removed from dwelling within that Babylon.”

TO INDWELL. I, 643 B, “Who inhabit the names of their originators,” that is, in honor of the demons who dwell in the images of the dead as though they were tenants, and who misuse the names of those who handed down those arts: by synecdoche, “to indwell,” that is, to reside as a tenant.

INADMISSIBLE. II, 979 A, “Or that adultery and fornication, their equals, are likewise reserved as inadmissible.”

NOT SHARED, particular. II, 1039 B, “Some of which are inhabited by heathens but are not shared with the rest.”

INACCESSIBLE. II, 315 B, “Therefore, whatever qualities [Col. 1315C] worthy of God you require will be found in the invisible and inaccessible Father.”

INCONGRUITY. II, 654 A, “For this purpose we shall first marvel at the incongruity of the definition that summons us to things incompatible with the soul.”

LACK OF CONSIDERATION. II, 868 B, “Indeed, in order to convict the Apostle himself of the greatest lack of consideration.”

INCONTROVERTIBLE. II, 456 B, “For what is wiser and more incontrovertible?”

UNCHANGING. II, 757 A, concerning the nature of God.

DEVOID OF RESOURCES. II, 960 A, “But immediately scandalized by the prospect of a solitude devoid of resources.”

INCORPOREAL THINGS. II, 230 C, “If any incorporeal things are present among them,” that is, non-bodies, with an indefinite meaning.

INCORPOREAL. II, 229 C, “At first sight matter appears to us to be incorporeal.”

INCORPOREALITY. II, 657 A, “Incorporeality is free from every kind of confinement, exempt from punishment and from cherishing care.”

REBUKE. II, 573 C, “And he, [Col. 1315D] expelling them by command and rebuke,” namely, the demons.

TO BROOD OVER. I, 1266 A, “But if I brood over patience,” that is, sleep upon it—a metaphor taken from precious possessions, which, lest we lose them, we place beneath ourselves for safekeeping even when we sleep. I, 658 B, “And the very air that broods above.”

NIGHTMARE. II, 724 C, “It was a somewhat heavier kind of sleep, as is commonly supposed concerning the nightmare-demon.”

TO TRAMPLE. I, 1296 B, “They do endure them, but only to trample them underfoot”—that is, they crush their wives and make a mockery of them; see SECRET.

TO LIE UPON. II, 1032 B, “The world upon which we lie,” that is, in which we live. I, 653 B, “And from above he gazes down with the most unflinching eyes upon bodies befouled in their own blood.” I, 340 A, “But axes and planes fall more forcefully upon your gods throughout all their limbs.”

TO ASSAIL. II, 996 C, “For it makes no difference whether someone assails another man’s wife or a widow.”

[Col. 1316A] INCURSION. II, 1020 A, “Because there are certain sins of daily incursion.”

ONSLAUGHT. II, 782 A, “And yet, lest the onslaught of the name toward that same Christ be unavailable to me,” etc.—that is, lest I allow the opportunity of arguing from the name Adam to escape. He calls the force of the argument its “onslaught.” II, 87 A, “While I shake off the onslaught of the question,” that is, its force.

TO REGISTER. II, 267 C, “By which, finally, they are initiated and registered into this heresy”; that is, they are enrolled and entered, as it were, upon the register of their order.

INDICATION. I, 259 A, “If, finally—as recently happened—by domestic indications”; this should read “by domestic judgments.” Domestic judgments are preconceived opinions.

NATIVE. I, 429 A, “But afterward at Rome, together with his own native deities.” “Native deities” should be referred not to the Romans but to Sterculius. They are those gods who, together with Sterculius, were indigenous and peculiar to the Romans, such as Mutunus, Tutunus, and the like.

TO INVOKE. II, 976 C, “They invoke a prayer,” that is, they pray a mystical prayer that cannot [Col. 1316B] be uttered in public. For Festus explains *indigitare* as “to invoke by prayer,” and specifically by a secret prayer not intended to pass among the general public.

IRASCIBLE. II, 673 A, “The irascible element, which they call θυμικόν, and the concupiscent element, which they call ἐπιθυμητικόν.”

WITHOUT DISTINCTION. I, 472 A, “Everything among us is held without distinction, except our wives.”

INDIVISIBLE. II, 161 B, “Having within himself his indivisible companions,” namely reason and wisdom.

INDUBITABLE. II, 579 A, “Indubitable destruction.” Irenaeus: κατ’ ἀνάγκην ἀπολλύσθαι λέγεται, “it is said necessarily to perish.”

TO ABOLISH; TO INTRODUCE. II, 29 A, “I should have abolished it,” that is, erased it, removed it, and refrained from adducing it. II, 352 A, “The Apostles likewise abolished that faith,” that is, effaced it through the promulgation of the Gospel. II, 107 A, “We can now abolish your question,” that is, remove the uncertainty from your inquiry. He explains himself when he adds, “and determine it.” II, 680 B, “For in this way they prepare a road for subsequently abolishing it,” that is, erasing and obliterating it. II, 38 B, “Nor could they have induced the heathen,” [Col. 1316C] that is, brought or impelled them to believe.

TO PUT ON; TO ASSUME. II, 35 C, “Those who had put on the faith from the Apostles.” Junius suspects the reading *imbuerant*, “had been imbued with.” II, 935 B, “Thus the Lord assumed for himself the two letters of Greece, the first and the last.” Similarly Tacitus: “He had even assumed hostile intentions against us”; likewise, “to have assumed a great spirit.” II, 103 B, “Therefore, we have also imposed order upon your inquiry.” II, 734 B, “Whoever has put on his baptism.” II, 779 A, “Why are you clothed by a naked man, if you have put on Christ?” II, 112 D, “You have put on Christ, since you were baptized into Christ.”

UNATTAINABLE. II, 818 B, “Not because it is unattainable even by the substance itself,” that is, not because the flesh cannot reach or obtain it; for *mereri* means “to obtain.”

TO COME INTO BEING. II, 652 B, “Understand, therefore, that ‘a creature too is said to be generated’ is put for ‘to come into being.’” From the proper force of its composition, the verb *inesse* signifies not only “to be in something” but also “to be generated and brought into existence,” προβάλλεσθαι εἰς τὸ εἶναι (“to be projected into being”).

INEXPERIENCE. II, 683 C.

INEXTRICABLE. I, 695 A, “An inextricable shipwreck awaits those who are dashed [Col. 1316D] against it.” The Greeks say ναυάγιον ἄφυκτον, “an inescapable shipwreck,” one that cannot be avoided. Those who strike reefs and wreck their ship cannot extricate or free themselves from danger. Virgil says of the Labyrinth: “an inextricable wandering.”

TO BEFOUL. II, 741 A, “And it befouls it with the concretion of flesh.”

TO DISGRACE. I, 411 A, “And they now disgrace the souls of the dead.” To disgrace the souls of the dead is for the shades of brave and eminent men to be summoned—or pretended to be summoned—at the whim of an utterly impure sorceress, although such things are nothing but sheer trifles and deceptions. II, 997 A, “And although they disgrace the office of mourning through the ceremony and adornment of grief.” II, 450 D, “Lest you disgrace his patience.” I, 699 B, “Thus we are also defamed in matters concerning the emperor’s majesty.” II, 84 A, “Thus, therefore, the condition is also disgraced with respect to the pleasures of the spectacles.” II, 97 B, “But a Christian will not disgrace even his doorway with laurels.”

CHILD. II, 544 A, “Children consecrated the testimony of Christ with their blood.” “Children” may mean either children in age or those childlike in the simplicity [Col. 1317A] of their faith and conduct. The former interpretation is maintained by those who think this is said of Herod’s slaughter of the infants, from Matthew II; the latter by those who understand it of the apostles, disciples, and martyrs of Christ, who were children not in age but in the disposition of their faith and life, from Matthew XVIII. The words that follow confirm the latter interpretation, for the phrase “those who proclaim the cross” is not spoken of Jews, but of those who glory in the cross of Christ. I, 312 A, “Come, plunge your blade into a child who is no one’s enemy, guilty before no one, and the son of all.” II, 422 B, “And children in shamelessness.”

TO NURSE AS AN INFANT. II, 262 A, “With which she nurses her own as infants,” that is, feeds them with infant food.

CHILD-SLAYER. I, 561 A, “The child-slayers, cooks, and the very dogs that served as bridal attendants should be brought forward.” They were, namely, accused of three crimes: impiety, Thyestean banquets, and incestuous intercourse. The heathen alleged that when Christians assembled, they customarily slaughtered an infant, ate its flesh and drank its blood, and thereby entered into a compact with one another; after the banquet they then coupled indiscriminately with any women whatsoever. They further alleged that the Christians customarily employed dogs tied to lampstands, which would overturn the lampstands when morsels were thrown to them; once the lights had been extinguished, those most shameful acts of intercourse were then said to occur.

INFANCY. II, 681 A, “And indeed from an infancy of their own,” concerning trees. II, 944 C, “So much so that, on account of that infancy of faith, they were still ignorant.”

UNMADE, not made. I, 333 A, “A body unborn and unmade.”

THE UNDERWORLD, every place or condition of the dead. I, 680 A, “Thus Lazarus too obtained refreshment in Abraham’s bosom in the underworld.” This derives from Christ’s words to the thief, “Today you will be with me in paradise”; from Abraham’s words concerning Lazarus to the rich man clothed in purple; from John’s vision of the souls of the martyrs beneath the altar; and from Paul’s words to the Thessalonians, First Epistle, chapter 4. Most of the ancients held the view that there were receptacles in the underworld for the souls of the righteous and the impious while they awaited the day of divine judgment. Meanwhile, however, they believed that the righteous enjoyed peace, rest, and refreshment, while [Col. 1317C] the impious, by contrast, were tormented in prison, fire, and Gehenna. They placed the receptacles of the righteous in a subterranean recess, but one built far above the lower abysses of the impious. Between the two, moreover, lay an impassable chasm of immense depth. Since Tertullian held this view, there is no reason to be surprised that both Lazarus and Abraham’s bosom are here located in the underworld. For in the same sense, in *On the Soul*, he says that Christ’s soul, after departing from his crucified body, descended to the underworld so that he might make the patriarchs participants in himself.

Others, moreover, holding the same faith, believed that paradise also existed in the underworld—the paradise which Christ promised to the thief. For they maintained that the word “paradise” signified a garden or meadow, or any other pleasant place of refreshment, where the patriarchs, Abraham, Jacob, Lazarus, the faithful thief, and also the souls of the martyrs might rest peacefully. Concerning the martyrs, indeed, our Septimius appears to have held this view in the *Scorpiace*, when he says that their souls beneath the altar feed upon patience and, clothed in the white robe of glory, enjoy its use. It is as though that abode [Col. 1317D] “beneath the altar,” ὑποκάτω θυσιαστηρίου, revealed to John, signifies by the word “altar” the earth upon which the sacrificed bodies of the martyrs lay as though upon an altar; from there their souls were carried by angels to subterranean places of delight, where they await the resurrection and God’s kingdom. A delay so slight, although lasting many years, would be no burden to the saints because of their singular confidence in eternal glory.

Nevertheless, what Tertullian taught in *On the Resurrection* proves that he was not entirely consistent in this opinion. For after establishing that every soul is sequestered in the underworld until the day of the Lord, he immediately exempts the souls of the martyrs from that arrangement, saying that they will lodge in paradise, not in the underworld. By these words he certainly denies that paradise is in the underworld, although in *On the Soul*, following Irenaeus’s view, he had said that Christ himself underwent the form of human death in the underworld; and he judged excessively proud those who did not consider the souls of the faithful [Col. 1318A] worthy of the underworld. “Servants above their lord,” he says, “and disciples above their master, disdainful of receiving, if it should be so, the consolation of awaiting the resurrection in Abraham’s bosom.”

From these passages, therefore, we gather that people in that age were still very uncertain and doubtful about these matters. The following age imagined that it saw what its predecessors had not seen. Yet it found Augustine equally uncertain and doubtful. Indeed, the ambiguity in the meaning of the term *inferna* or *inferi* (“the underworld”) even put the rule of faith to the test: some shrank from saying that Christ descended into the underworld, so that they included both burial and descent within the profession of his Passion; others, having said that he was buried, did not additionally say that he descended, because they wished the statement that he descended to be understood as referring to burial. Rufinus testifies that this had long been the practice in the churches of the East. Irenaeus’s view is found at the end of book V; Justin’s in Questions LXXV and LXXVI; Tertullian’s at II, 656 B; 742 B ff.; 747 C ff.; 831 A–B. See Augustine, Epistle LVII to Dardanus and XCIX to Evodius; *On the Literal Interpretation of Genesis*, book VII, chapters 32, 33, and 34; and *Questions on Genesis*, book I, chapter 25.

[Col. 1318B] FUNERAL OFFERINGS, sacrifices that people offered to the divine spirits of the dead, as Festus says. I, 645 A, “Soon afterward, on the appointed day of the funeral offerings,” that is, on the day of the funeral offerings publicly designated and proclaimed.

TO FAN. II, 577 A, “The fire was fanned.”

TO INFLAME. I, 1314 A, “Thus he also inflamed the prices of commodities, so that he too might be set on fire.” Pliny likewise used the phrase “to kindle a price,” *Natural History*, book XVIII.

PRIESTLY FILLET. II, 947 B, “We lay aside the fillets and become unequal,” that is, when we laymen are summoned to equality with the discipline of the priesthood, we lay aside the priestly character and then say that laymen are one thing and priests another.

TO DARKEN; TO STAIN. II, 205 C; 206 A, “To stain the Spirit of God by sin,” τοῦ σπιλῶσαι (“to defile”), as it is explained in the *Glossary*; that is, to blemish and corrupt it with a stain. II, 308 C, “The quantity and quality of the gift will obscure the rich man.” II, 808 A, “You possess the Scriptures by which the flesh is stained.” II, 652 A, “If any things, therefore, darken the bright and pure air of truth.”

DEVICE; CAPACITY. A thing fashioned and invented by ingenuity, εὕρημα (“invention”). II, [Col. 1318C] 1032 A, “The Carthaginians were immediately amazed at it as a new and foreign device.” I, 528 B, “And ingenious devices,” that is, things ingeniously invented. II, 889 B, “Toward the devices of iniquity,” εὑρήματα (“inventions”). I, 1306 B, “Without devices of adornment.” I, 1319 C, “Because glory is a device of exaltation.” I, 625 A, “And every device of the executioner in tortures.” II, 668 B, “For they are not members of the animal substance, but capacities,” that is, powers, efficacies, and operations, as he explained above. II, 986 B, “But let the extraordinary talent see to itself.” He calls the actor’s talent “extraordinary” because it is entirely inappropriate for a Christian and lies outside the order of Christian discipline. The following words confirm this.

PUTTING ON. II, 855 A, “Surely not by the putting on of immortality,” that is, by its super-garment.

INGRATITUDE. II, 311 B, “Sometimes also from ingratitude for some good work.” I, 1230 A, “Let human ingratitude, therefore, see to that.”

AGAINST ONE’S WILL, ἀκουσίως, as though under compulsion. I, 284 B, “Or let the necessity of obedience against one’s will be preferred to truth.” I, 525 A, “Against your will, you will experience the law of your condition,” that is, [Col. 1318D] whether you will it or not, you must both die and rise again. II, 214 A, “He attempts to support his suspicions against his will.” II, 143 B, “Or luminous against his will,” that is, disagreeably and malignantly, concerning the prison.

ENTRY. II, 714 A, “The lawful entry into birth occurs at about the tenth month,” that is, when the child in the womb enters its tenth month.

TO BE INHABITED, meaning to be clothed or to serve as clothing. II, 1039 B, “Parts of which are inhabited by heathen peoples.” II, 223 A, “But to be inhabited.” He translates literally the words of Isaiah XLV, Ἀλλὰ καὶ οἰκεῖσθαι, “but also to be inhabited.”

TO BREATHE UPON. I, 413 A, “Those who, breathing upon the altars, conceive the divine presence from the sacrificial odor.” Sacred history provides an example of such divination in Balaam, Numbers 23:1–3. Indeed, Apollo himself denies that he can prophesy without incense.

[Col. 1319A] UGLINESS. II, 331 B; 641 A, “He was celebrated in song as prostrated by every ugliness,” that is, deformity.
