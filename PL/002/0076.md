Let us first discuss sleep; afterward, how the soul passes through death.

[Note: [Col. 0721D] Sleep is certainly not contrary to nature, as some philosophers maintain. Here he proves from the various doctrines of the philosophers that sleep accords with nature. There are, however, various opinions about the cause of sleep. See Galen in *History of Philosophy* and Plutarch, books 24 and 25 of *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*. LE PR.]

Sleep is certainly not contrary to nature, as some philosophers maintain, although they assign it to causes that seem to be regarded as contrary to nature. The Stoics affirm that sleep is a relaxation of sensory vigor; the Epicureans, a diminution of the animal spirit;

[Note: [Col. 0721D] Anaxagoras, together with Xenophanes, calls it weariness. Plutarch, book 5, chapter 25 of *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*: “Anaxagoras thinks that sleep occurs in the bodily functions; for it is an affection not of the [Col. 0722C] soul but of the body, while death is [Col. 0722D] the soul’s separation. Leucippus holds that sleep belongs to the body alone; and when, because of the fineness of its particles, more than the proper amount of animate heat is discharged, death thereby comes upon the body. These are affections of the body, not of the soul. Empedocles holds that death is the separation of the fiery nature from those elements from which the human being was compounded; and therefore death is common to soul and body, while sleep occurs through a separation of the fiery nature.” Aristotle refutes this opinion in his book *On Sleep and Waking*, chapter 3. LAC.]

Anaxagoras, together with Xenophanes, calls it weariness; Empedocles and Parmenides, cooling;

[Note: [Col. 0722D] Strato calls it a separation of the associated spirit, etc. Plutarch does not mention these men; but their opinion appears akin to that of Epicurus and to the one Plutarch attributes to Empedocles—namely, that sleep occurs through a separation of the fiery nature. Plutarch nevertheless mentions others, such as Alcmaeon and Diogenes. LAC.]

Strato [Col. 0721C] calls it a separation of the associated spirit; Democritus, a deficiency of spirit; Aristotle, a languishing of the heat surrounding the heart. I presume that I have never slept in such a way as to recognize any of these things in myself.

For sleep must not be believed to be [Col. 0722A] weariness; it is, rather, the opposite of weariness, which it removes. Indeed, a person is refreshed by sleep more than he is fatigued by it. Moreover, sleep does not always arise from fatigue; and yet, when it does arise from fatigue, the fatigue is then no longer present.

Nor shall I admit either cooling or any languishing of the heat, since bodies grow so warm in sleep that the processing of food would not readily proceed during sleep—with heat hastening it and cold retarding it—if sleep made us cold. More than this, even perspiration

[Note: [Col. 0722C] “More than this: even perspiration,” Rigault, Venetian edition.]

is an indication of active digestion. Finally, we speak of “digesting,” which is an operation of heat, not of cold.

Likewise, the soul’s immortality does not permit us to believe in a diminution of the animal spirit, a deficiency of spirit, or a separation of the associated spirit.

[Note: [Col. 0722D] The soul perishes if it is diminished. Some have said that the associated spirit is the soul; consequently, if that spirit is diminished, the soul will be diminished, and would therefore be mortal. LAC.]

The soul perishes if it is diminished.

It remains, perhaps, for us to define sleep with the Stoics as a relaxation of sensory vigor, because it provides rest for the body alone, not for the soul also. For the soul, as something always in motion [Col. 0722B] and always active, never gives way

[Note: [Col. 0722C] “Succeeds to,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.]

to rest, which is plainly foreign to the condition of immortality. For nothing immortal admits an end to its activity, whereas sleep is an end to activity. Finally, rest, as an end to activity, indulges only the body, to which mortality belongs.

Whoever, therefore, doubts that sleep is natural has, to be sure, the dialecticians, who call into doubt the entire distinction between natural and contrary-to-natural things. From them he may learn that the things he has thought to be outside nature can be claimed for nature, from which they received precisely such a condition that they appear to be regarded as outside it; and thus either everything is nature or nothing is nature.

Among us, however, heed may be given to what contemplation of God suggests—God being the author of all the things under inquiry. For we believe

[Note: [Col. 0722D] If nature is anything, it is some rational work, etc. Lactantius, [Col. 0723C] *Divine Institutes*, book 2, chapter 9, testifies in the following words that this is Seneca’s opinion: “Better, therefore, is Seneca, the most acute of all the Stoics, who saw that nature is nothing other than God. Shall we therefore, he says, not praise God, for whom virtue is natural? For he did not learn it from anyone. [Col. 0723D] Indeed, we shall praise him; for although it is natural to him, he gave it to himself, since God himself is nature.” Since it is not unusual for Tertullian to cite Seneca, I would readily think that he received this from Seneca; and I would judge the same concerning Lactantius. It was evidently taken from chapter 7 of book IV of *On Benefits*: “For what else is nature but God and divine reason?” LE PR.]

that, if nature is anything, it is some rational work of God. Moreover, reason precedes sleep, which is so fitting, so useful, [Col. 0722C] so necessary that no soul can sustain itself without it: it refreshes bodies, restores strength, confirms

[Note: [Col. 0722C] “Prolongs,” or “extends,” Latin edition.]

health, pacifies activity, and heals toil. To allow its lawful enjoyment, the day gives way and the night imposes its law, even removing the color of things. [Col. 0723A]

But if sleep is life-giving, healthful, and helpful, nothing of this sort is irrational, and nothing is unnatural.

[Note: [Col. 0723C] “Nothing unnatural” is absent from the Paris edition and others.]

Thus physicians also relegate everything opposed to life, health, and aid beyond the bounds of nature. For by judging the conditions that rival sleep—delirium and cardiac disease—to be contrary to nature, they have already pronounced sleep natural. Even when they characterize sleep in lethargy as unnatural, they answer to natural sleep’s testimony when it exists in its proper measure. For every nature is destroyed either by deprivation or by excess, but is preserved by the propriety of due measure. Thus something will be natural in its normal condition, although it can be made unnatural by deficiency or excess.

What if you also remove eating and drinking from the allotments of nature? For in these too there is much preparation

[Note: [Col. 0723C] “Provision,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.]

for sleep. Certainly, the human being was imbued with these things from the beginning of his nature. [Col. 0723B] If you learn from God—

[Note: [Col. 0723D] If you learn from God—that is, from the sacred writings among the Jews or Christians. LE PR.]

Adam, the fountainhead of the human race, drank deeply of slumber before he thirsted for rest; he slept before he labored—indeed, before he even ate, and indeed before he even spoke—so that people may see in sleep a natural token more primordial than all other natural things.

From this we are led also to recognize that even then he displayed an image of death. For if Adam furnished a figure of Christ—

[Note: [Col. 0723C] “Prefigured,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.]

Adam’s sleep was the death of Christ, who was to sleep in death, so that from the wound in his side the Church, the true mother of the living, might likewise be prefigured. Therefore sleep, so healthful and so rational, is also fashioned as a representation of the death that is now public and common to all.

For God, who in other respects also undertook nothing in his ordering without models, willed—

[Note: [Col. 0723D] More fully than by a Platonic paradigm. This again comes from Plato’s *Phaedo*, where Socrates, through examples of contraries succeeding one another, proves that life will succeed death. RIG.—Pamelius admits that he does not know to what Platonic paradigm Tertullian alludes. Junius, no more learned but shrewdly diverting the matter elsewhere, says that “more fully than by a Platonic paradigm” was expressed by inversion—that is, more fully than Plato did by any mathematical demonstration or paradigm. I am surprised that they did not attain the more straightforward and obvious sense concerning Platonic ideas. [Col. 0724C] They were misled by a less common word. Yet even the divine Justin, in his *Exhortation to the Greeks*, called τὸ ἑκάστου τῶν γινομένων παράδειγμα, “the paradigm of each individual thing that is created.” Similarly, St. Dionysius, *On the Divine Names*, chapter 5, calls the same things [Col. 0724D] τῶν ὄντων παραδείγματα, “paradigms of existing things.” The same Dionysius cites Clement the philosopher, whom I think to have been St. Clement of Rome, who also called them παραδείγματα, “paradigms.” Consequently, Tertullian’s meaning is that God undertakes nothing without models, and this not in the Platonic sense, but more fully and perfectly than Plato supposed: God undertakes all things according to his own ideas and models. That Plato also compared sleep and waking to life and death in the *Phaedo* is of little relevance to the present matter. LAC.]

more fully than by a Platonic paradigm—to enact with us daily the outlines especially of the human beginning and end, [Col. 0723C] extending a hand to faith so that it might more easily be assisted through images and parables of things just as of words.

He therefore presents to you a body overcome by the friendly force of slumber, laid low by the gentle necessity of rest, motionless in its position, just as it lay before life and as it will lie [Col. 0724A] after life, as a testimony to its fashioning and burial, awaiting the soul as though it had not yet been bestowed and as though it had already been taken away.

But the soul too undergoes this in such a way that it seems to act elsewhere, learning its future absence through the concealment of its presence. Yet meanwhile it dreams and does not rest; it is not altogether idle, nor does it subject its immortal nature as a slave to sleep.

[Note: [Col. 0723C] “Of slumber,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.]

It proves itself always in motion: it travels over land and sea, conducts business, is agitated, labors, plays, grieves, rejoices, and pursues lawful and unlawful things. It shows both that it can do very much even without the body and that it is furnished with limbs of its own, but nevertheless has need of the body once again in order to set it in motion.

Thus, when the body has awakened and been restored to its functions, it affirms to you the resurrection of the dead. This will be both sleep’s natural rationale and its rational nature. Even through an image of death you are initiated into faith, rehearse hope, learn to die and to live, and learn to be awake while you sleep.

Next, concerning Hermotimus. As they say, he was deprived of his soul in sleep, because it departed from his body as though taking advantage of an opportunity afforded by the man’s coming idleness. His wife betrayed this. His enemies found him asleep and cremated him as though he were dead. When the soul returned too late, it imputed the murder, I suppose, to itself. The citizens of Clazomenae console Hermotimus with a temple. No woman enters it, because of the disgrace attached to his wife.

## CHAPTER XLV

[Col. 0725B]

At this point we are obliged also to set forth the Christian position concerning dreams, as incidents

[Note: [Col. 0726C] “Accents,” Rhenanus, Semler.]

of sleep and as no slight agitations of the soul, which we have declared to be busy and always active because of the perpetuity of its motion—the rationale of its divinity and immortality.

Accordingly, when rest comes to bodies, for which it is a peculiar solace, the soul, being without this alien solace, does not rest; and if it lacks the agency of bodily limbs, it employs its own. Imagine

[Note: [Col. 0726C] “Observe,” Fran.]

a gladiator without weapons or a charioteer without chariots, gesticulating every posture and exertion of his craft. There is fighting and competing, but the agitation is empty. Nevertheless, the things that do not appear actually to occur still appear to occur: in action they do occur, but in result

[Note: [Col. 0726C] “In feeling,” Junius.]

they do not.

This force [Col. 0725C]

[Note: [Col. 0726D] We call ecstasy a departure from sense, etc. This is the distinction between sleep and ecstasy: through sleep the functions of mind and sensation are bound, so that the soul’s vegetative powers are then freer and stronger; in ecstasy, however, the opposite occurs, for the nutritive and sensory activities are impeded, while the functions of the mind are then especially vigorous. Finally, sensation and ecstasy agree in this: in both, the senses grow torpid—or, rather, are bound. As far as Tertullian is concerned, he sometimes takes “ecstasy” in an unfavorable sense and said that it was a kind of madness, as in the book *On Ecstasy*, which he wrote against the Church; there, following Montanus’s position, he took away freedom and the cognition belonging to prophecy itself. Above, however, in chapter 21, he explained madness in ecstasy in a sounder sense. LAC.]

we call *ecstasy* (*ecstasis*), a departure from sense and a likeness of madness. Thus also in the beginning sleep was inaugurated together with ecstasy (Gen. II): “And God sent an ecstasy upon Adam, and he fell asleep.” For sleep came upon the body for rest; ecstasy came upon the soul in opposition to rest. From that point onward there was already the established form, mixing sleep with ecstasy, and nature proceeded from the form. [Col. 0726A]

Finally, in dreams we are delighted, saddened, and terrified—with what feeling, anxiety, and susceptibility to suffering! Yet we would be moved by nothing at all, namely by empty images, if we dreamed while in possession of ourselves. Finally, good deeds are unrewarded in dreams and offenses go unpunished; for we shall no more be condemned because of a vision of fornication than crowned because of one of martyrdom.

“And how,” you ask, “does the soul remember dreams, if it is not permitted to be in possession of itself?” This will be a property of this madness, because it does not arise from the corruption of good health but from the rational order of nature. For it does not banish the mind but summons it away. It is one thing to shatter, another to move; one thing to overthrow, another to agitate.

Accordingly, the fact that memory remains available is evidence of the mind’s soundness; the fact that soundness [Col. 0726B] of mind, with memory preserved, nevertheless stands stupefied is a kind of madness. Therefore we are said not to be insane but to dream.

[Note: [Col. 0726D] Therefore even prudent people dream, if ever we are prudent. Therefore, he says, even we prudent people dream—if, nevertheless, we can ever be called prudent, since it is our characteristic to err and stumble. RIG.]

Therefore even prudent people dream, if ever we are prudent; for although our wisdom may be overshadowed, it is not extinguished. Except that even wisdom itself may then seem to be idle; while ecstasy also operates from its own peculiar power, so as to bring before us images of wisdom just as it does images of error.
