Thus, says that great man, “so many and such great churches are that one first Church from the Apostles, from which they all come” (*Prescription* 20).—“We have the Lord’s Apostles as our authorities. They themselves chose nothing by their own judgment to introduce, but faithfully assigned to the nations the teaching received from Christ” (ibid. 6).—“No nation is now [Col. 0019D] foreign to God, since the Gospel flashes over all the earth and to the ends of the world” (*On the Soul* 49). “Taught innocence by God, we know it perfectly, as revealed by a perfect teacher, and guard it faithfully, as commanded by a judge who cannot be despised” (*Apology* 45). Yet this sect will not fail; know that it is built up more when it seems to be cut down (*To Scapula* 5). “We become more numerous whenever you mow us down: the blood of Christians is seed” (*Apology* 49, vol. I, col. 535A).

But remember that here the Lord left his keys to Peter and, through him, to the Church (*Scorpiace* 10). “You have Rome, from which authority is ready for us too. How blessed is that Church, upon which the Apostles poured out their whole doctrine together with their blood!” (*Prescription* 36, vol. II, col. 49).

[Col. 0020A] This one, apostolic, catholic, holy, immortal, and visible Church—that is, the Roman Church—to which belongs the right to teach all nations, receives her doctrine from one source, namely the word of God, whether written or handed down. By her own right it belongs to her both to discern, confirm, and sanction traditions and to establish the Scriptures, their inspiration, interpretation, and canon. “For where it shall appear that the truth of Christian teaching and faith resides, there will also be the truth of the Scriptures and their expositions and of all Christian traditions” (*Prescription* 19, vol. II, col. 31).

Little need be added to what we said earlier from Tertullian about the inspiration of Scripture, its adorable fullness, its apostolic interpretation, and the legitimate canon. We note only that sacred books [Col. 0020B] must be used so that heretics, abusing parables, do not fashion from them the very substance of doctrines; one should not therefore appeal to them or establish the contest in matters where victory is either nonexistent, uncertain, or no better than uncertain (*Prescription* 19, vol. II, col. 31). One must first examine to whom the Scriptures belong, by whom, through whom, when, and to whom was delivered the teaching by which men become Christians. God permitted—indeed willed—the Scriptures to be arranged so as to supply material to heretics, who cannot exist without Scriptures (ibid. 39, col. 53). Also notable and very famous in Tertullian is the passage about the authentic writings of the Apostles, on which highly learned men have offered various conjectures (ibid. 36, col. 49) [Note: [Col. 0020D] See Pamelius’s notes at the cited passage; Hugo Grotius, *On the Truth of the Christian Religion* III, notes; Dodwell, dissertation, vol. I, §41; Richard Simon, *Critical History of the New Testament*, ch. 4, p. 40; Nathaniel Lardner, *Credibility of the Gospel History*, part II, ch. 27, §8, pp. 501–508; Lumper, “Life and Writings of Tertullian,” in *Historia theologico-critico-literaria SS. Patrum*, vol. VII, pp. 257–262]. Nor should we omit what he discusses in many places concerning both the translation of the Seventy [Note: [Col. 0020D] Cf. Christoph Cellarius, Dissertation XIII on the Seventy Translators; Simone de Magistris, dissertations appended to the edition of the Septuagint version of Daniel from the Chigi codex; Lumper, cited work, p. 666 ff.; consult below Dom Le Nourry’s dissertation, ch. 6, arts. 2–3, p. 787 ff.] and the most ancient Latin translations of the Bible, already current in the West [Col. 0020C] and received with greater or equal authority than the Greek codices.

Having laid these foundations of the Church, Tertullian then incidentally overturns the perishable structures of the heretics: now reproaching their crimes against God’s word and their slaughter of the Scriptures; now showing that heresy, idolatry, and profane philosophy differ little from one another; now describing marks of heresy utterly alien to the true Church (*Against Marcion* V.17; IV.19; *Prescription* 7, 17, 37, 40, 41, 44). By the single argument from prescription he pierces and crushes with an incurable wound all heresies, present or future, including Montanism and Tertullianism themselves.

[Col. 0020D] This mode of argument is invincibly valid not only against the Gnostics, as Neander wished in a recent work [Note: [Col. 0020D] *Antignostikus: The Spirit of Tertullian*, etc.], [Col. 0021A] but even against all heretics, without excepting the newest Protestant sects. Modern and ancient heretics display the same chief errors: they desert the unity and authority of the apostolic Church; despising tradition, they appeal to Scripture alone; they interpret and break up the canon at will. The leaders of new sects display no more lawful signs of divine authority than Marcion or Valentinus once did; before or after them they have not a trace or jot of apostolic succession. Moreover their doctrines are rejected alike by the Roman Church and by all churches throughout the world, whether Greek or Asian [Note: [Col. 0021D] See J. A. Möhler, *Patrology, or History of Christian Literature*, vol. II, p. 354, French trans., Paris, 1843].

Next, to return to Tertullian, one of the three formulas of faith Septimius inserted in his books would have to be recited, or at least compared with the creeds published by Irenaeus, Gregory Thaumaturgus, and others, were we not constrained by the limits of a preface.

[Col. 0021B] We shall deal briefly with Septimius on God and his attributes, since we give below Dom Le Nourry’s very rich and extensive dissertation. Go there, therefore, and see by what sixfold argument Tertullian proves that God exists (ch. 7, art. 1, col. 804 ff.); in what sense he says God is known to men although invisible, incomprehensible, and beyond estimation; what he says of this true and living God’s knowledge, wisdom, and eternity (col. 809 ff.); and what Septimius means by his often repeated phrase that God is a body, though God is spirit (col. 811 ff.) [Note: [Col. 0021D] Cf. Noël Alexandre, *Ecclesiastical History*, second century, art. VIII, vol. III; Cantova, dissertation on the anthropomorphism of Septimius and Epiphanius, Milan, 1773, octavo]. Tertullian likewise declares clearly that God is nothing if not good (*Scorpiace* 5). Against Marcion he defends God’s justice as well as his goodness (*Against Marcion* II.11), though he calls goodness innate in God and justice accidental—that is, adapted to the creature in its effect and subsequent to sin (ibid.). Hence he argues splendidly against Hermogenes that God created everything from nothing and that matter is not eternal (*Against Hermogenes* 19 ff.).

[Col. 0021C] Tertullian speaks so clearly and exactly of the Trinity of divine Persons and their consubstantiality that nothing further could be desired. As clearly as the theologians and doctors of our age, he distinguishes three divine persons and predicates actual existence of each in the unity of one and the same nature. From these points the Catholic Church’s doctrine concerning this mystery is surely constituted.

[Col. 0021D] (Cf. *Against Hermogenes* 45, vol. II, col. 237; *Against Praxeas* 2, col. 156; 3, col. 157; 4, col. 159; 8, col. 159; 11, col. 166; 12, col. 167; 13, col. 168; 26, col. 188; 30, col. 195; 31, col. 196.)

For the Son’s divinity, generation, consubstantiality, sonship, and eternity, and for Christ as God and man foretold by the prophets, clothed in flesh and dwelling in Judea, dead, restored to life, and assumed into heaven, we again refer [Col. 0022A] to Dom Le Nourry’s excellent work (below, ch. 9, cols. 853–867), which splendidly crushes the obstinate inventions of Marcion, Apelles, and the other Gnostics.

The same author explains with precision what Tertullian says about angels and demons, ibid., ch. 10, cols. 868–891.

Discussing man in various writings, Tertullian first teaches that man is the handiwork of God the creator; consists of two substances, body and soul; was made as a work in the likeness of the Son and Spirit and as God’s image; is possessor of the whole universe; and was constructed in it by God so that he might be lord while made by the Lord (*Against Marcion* IV.37; V.17; *Against Praxeas* 12; *On the Shows* 2; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 5–6).

[Col. 0022B] Although to some who taste his writings only in fragments Tertullian seems to philosophize about man and the soul in a rather coarse and material way, anyone who looks deeply into his thought will rightly judge that his acute gaze penetrated the difficult and most subtle mysteries of anthropology and psychology [Note: [Col. 0022D] See this lucidly explained in J. A. Möhler’s already praised work, *Patrology*, vol. II, p. 364, French trans.].

Like Plato, he distinguishes a twofold part of the soul, one intelligent and the seat of wisdom, the other blind and devoid of understanding ([non-Latin text omitted] and [non-Latin text omitted]). But he denies, in the Gnostic sense, that soul and spirit are distinct: “If you separate spirit and soul, separate their works too; let both perform something apart, the soul by itself and the spirit by itself; let the soul live without the spirit and the spirit breathe without the soul.… Therefore they will not be two things that cannot be divided” (*On the Soul* 10, vol. II, col. 663).

[Col. 0022C] Since soul is the same as spirit, it necessarily follows that the soul’s properties are the same as the spirit’s. Hence the soul is simple and separable from the body: they are two things and diverse. One should therefore not stumble over the fact that he also calls it corporeal and shaped—that is, subsisting in form and reality. Elsewhere he confesses that it was born from God’s breath, Christian by nature, simple in substance, endowed with free will, rational, and immortal—not by grace, as almost all the ancients held, but by its own nature—and that after this present life it either suffers in the underworld or rejoices in heaven (*On the Soul* 58). Yet elsewhere he teaches that before the universal resurrection the soul is neither punished nor rejoices, and further that certain sins must be expiated by us immediately after death while awaiting the resurrection.

[Col. 0022D] Tertullian wonderfully glorifies man’s flesh itself, and perhaps no Father can be found who said more remarkable things about it. “Whatever shape the clay was taking, Christ was being contemplated as the man who was to be.… Thus that clay, already then putting on the image of Christ who was to come in the flesh, was not only God’s work but also his pledge” (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 6, vol. II, col. 802).

[Col. 0023A]

Again he sketches in these outlines the same flesh recreated from sin and rising again with the revivified soul: “When the soul is joined to God, it [the flesh] is what makes it possible for the soul to be joined. The flesh is washed so that the soul may be cleansed; the flesh is anointed so that the soul may be consecrated; the flesh is signed so that the soul too may be fortified; the flesh is overshadowed by the laying on of hands so that the soul too may be illuminated by the Spirit; the flesh feeds upon Christ’s body and blood so that the soul too may be fattened upon God” (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 8). “When the soul marries the Spirit, the flesh follows as part of the dowry, and is now no longer the soul’s servant but the Spirit’s. O blessed marriage, if it admits no adultery!” (*On the Soul* 41). Finally, he splendidly describes the triumph of the flesh exulting with Christ: “He [Jesus Christ] … keeps the deposit of the flesh also in himself, the earnest of the entire sum. For as he left us the earnest of the Spirit, so he received from us the earnest of the flesh and carried into heaven the pledge of the whole sum that is one day to be brought there. Be confident, flesh and blood: in Christ you have taken possession of heaven and the kingdom of God” (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 51, vol. II, col. 869).

[Col. 0023B] He is no less successful in describing the soul in its fallen and stained state than in its whole and innocent state. He admits original sin and teaches admirably how widely the primal stain crept and infected all: “The human soul, like a shoot derived for propagation from the rootstock Adam and entrusted to the woman’s generative furrows, will sprout with all its equipment,” etc. (*On the Soul* 19). “You pronounce Satan … by whom man was deceived from the beginning into transgressing God’s command and therefore delivered to death, and who thereafter made the entire race, infected from his seed, the transmitter also of his condemnation” (*On the Testimony of the Soul* 3, vol. I, col. 618).

[Col. 0023C] Let us now hear him sing, as if in a new song, of Christ’s birth on earth: “The inaugurator of a new birth had to be born in a new way.… This is the new birth, when man is born in God—in that man in whom God was born—having taken flesh of the ancient seed without the ancient seed, so that he might reform it by new seed, that is spiritually, purified by the exclusion of its ancient stains” (*On the Flesh of Christ* 17, vol. II, col. 781).

[Col. 0023D]

To Tertullian the humanity and kindness of the Savior appeared in flesh, the Word made flesh, as the beginning and end of all mysteries of faith, the summit and foundation of the new creation, the unique center of the dawning world, from which every good flows to us and to which all things that bind God and men return and are joined. Hence Septimius’s vehement and ardent impulse, by which he is swept into wonder at the incarnate Word and attacks and repels the impious attempts of anyone who assails even lightly this great mystery of godliness; through excessive amazement at Christ born and suffering he seems to go mad. “But now,” he says to Marcion, “answer here, murderer of truth: was God not truly crucified? Did he not truly die, since he was truly crucified? Was he not truly raised?… Spare the one hope of the whole world. Why destroy the shame necessary to faith? Whatever is unworthy of God profits me. I am safe if I am not ashamed of my Lord. ‘Whoever is ashamed of me,’ he says, ‘of him shall I also be ashamed.’ I find no other occasions of shame that, through contempt for shame, prove me rightly shameless and happily foolish. The Son of God was born: it causes no shame, because it is shameful. And the Son of God died: it is wholly credible because it is absurd; and, buried, he rose again: it is certain because it is impossible” (*On the Flesh of Christ* 5, vol. II, col. 763).

[Col. 0024A] Let this suffice to make known how greatly Tertullian can aid the exposition of dogmatic faith. There are very many other dogmas which he asserted with equal abundance and the very learned Dom Le Nourry commented upon more abundantly; you will find them brought to light again in the course of this volume.

Nothing shows us better how admirably and correctly Septimius understood the true and sincere notion of our active, practical faith than the extensive description of liturgical discipline and dense abundance of ancient rites scattered throughout his writings. Since passages displaying the discipline and customs of the first centuries are of the greatest value, we shall present as accurately as possible each liturgical document mentioned in Septimius’s writings.

[Col. 0024B] To begin with baptism, this ancient doctor first teaches the laborious works to be undertaken before baptism: frequent prayers, fasts, kneelings, and vigils, with at least private confession of all past sins. Next come the things belonging to the conferral of baptism: triple immersion and invocation of the three persons of the most holy Trinity. Last come those that follow: anointing after the washing, laying on of hands and, as many have rightly thought, the conferral of the Holy Spirit through the sacrament of confirmation, and even participation in the Eucharist [Col. 0024C] (cf. *On Baptism*).

Tertullian composed a particular treatise *On Repentance*, so beautiful and elegant that some have wrongly dared deny it to him. In it he admirably describes all the rites and dispositions of penance, public or private, performed before or after baptism, as may be seen to some extent below, where he speaks of *exomologesis*, which “when it casts man down, raises him all the more; when it makes him squalid, renders him the more cleansed; when it accuses, excuses; when it condemns, absolves” (*On Repentance* 9, vol. I, col. 1244).

Discussing the Eucharist, Tertullian teaches that Christians customarily assembled very early in the morning to celebrate this mystery; that laypeople received the sacrament from the hand of those presiding; that they received it into their own hands and carried it into their homes so as to partake fasting under one species; and that the newly baptized, whether infants or adults, were granted the same benefit.

[Col. 0024D] Tertullian professes everywhere, especially in that golden book *On Prescriptions*—which he perhaps wrote while still Catholic but published when already a Montanist—that the ecclesiastical hierarchy is distinct from the laity, furnished with various orders having their own duties and proper names, disrupted or utterly extinguished among heretics, but preserved unshaken among Catholics in perpetual succession from the Apostles to the latest times. There he censures the heretics of his time because “their ordinations are rash, frivolous, and inconstant: one man is bishop today and another tomorrow; today a deacon is tomorrow a reader; today a presbyter is tomorrow a layman” (ibid. 41, vol. II, cols. 56–57).

[Col. 0025A] With the Apostle he calls marriage a great sacrament; but he teaches that secret unions, neither first professed before the Church nor requested from a monogamous bishop, priests, and deacons, are to be reckoned alongside adultery and fornication (*On Modesty* 4, vol. II, col. 987). Everyone knows this passage: “How shall we suffice to recount the happiness of that marriage which the Church joins, the offering confirms, the blessing seals, angels announce, and the Father ratifies?” (*To His Wife* II.9, vol. I, col. 1302).

Finally, Tertullian records the following about prayer, private and public, sacred assemblies, and the Lord’s Supper. Christians prayed looking toward heaven, [Col. 0025B] with hands extended in the image and form of Christ fixed to the cross, head uncovered, face and voice lowered, turned toward the east. Before prayer they commonly washed their hands and removed their cloak. Some sat while praying; others, in the sight of God and while the angel of prayer still stood, thought it more reverent to stand themselves. They refreshed one another with alternating psalms and hymns and did not withhold the kiss of peace after prayer. They prayed without a prompter leading aloud, from the breast and the heart’s inward affection. They gathered with joy on Sunday, then attended to readings from the sacred books, reciting both writings of the Prophets and memoirs of the Apostles, and heard the president’s admonition. Then they received corrections and divine censure, offenders being banished from participation in prayer [Col. 0025C] and assembly and every sacred fellowship. Bishops presided over these assemblies and, under their authority, approved and elected presbyters and deacons, called elders. From these men and no others they received, at mealtime and even in gatherings before dawn, the sacrament of the Eucharist, either to consume or to reserve for consumption at home. Besides Sunday there were days of fasting and stations, on which they prayed longer, bending their knees with all other signs of humility and remaining longer at the altar after receiving the Lord’s body. There were also more solemn hours of the day—the third, sixth, and ninth—besides the appointed prayers owed at the coming of light and night; these customarily preceded food and bathing and interrupted the night watches. Otherwise nothing at all is prescribed, [Col. 0025D] except prayer at every time and place. Nothing, however, is more famous than the supper called the *Agape*. They do not recline until prayer to God has first been tasted. They eat as much as the hungry and temperate take; they drink as much as benefits the chaste. After supper water is offered for washing hands and lamps are lit until daylight. Then, the table removed, each person is invited to stand in the midst of the whole gathering and circle and sing a hymn, whether drawn from Holy Scripture or elicited from his own talent. Prayers then return thanks to God, and they depart with the same care for modesty and chastity, as men who have eaten discipline rather than merely supper. Christians offered this victim—devoted from the heart, fed by faith, tended by truth, whole in innocence, pure in chastity, crowned with agape, led to God’s altar with the procession of good works amid psalms and hymns—one that would obtain all things from God.

[Col. 0026A] We proceed more closely and rapidly, as promised, through Tertullian’s use in moral and ascetical matters. First one should investigate what the Christians’ morals were. “That we live by the discipline of divine patience,” says our author, “can be sufficiently plain to you, since so great a multitude of men—almost the greater part of every city—conduct ourselves in silence and modesty, perhaps better known individually than collectively, recognizable from nothing but the correction of our vices” (*To Scapula* 2, vol. I, col. 700).

[Col. 0026B] How great was Christian charity, which swept even pagans into admiration: “‘See,’ they say, ‘how they love one another, and how ready they are to die for each other!’ For the pagans themselves are readier to kill each other” (*Apology* 39, vol. I, col. 471). How great the chastity of Christian spouses, the integrity of many celibates, and the innocence of virgins! “For what distinction do we display except the first wisdom, by which we do not adore the frivolous works of human hands; temperance, by which we abstain from another’s property; chastity, which we do not defile even with our eyes; mercy, by which we bend over the needy; truth itself, by which we give offense; and liberty itself, for which we know how to die?” (*To the Nations* I.4, vol. I, col. 961). Who has not heard how invincibly Tertullian compares the finest examples of philosophers’ modesty, equanimity, fidelity, and simplicity [Col. 0026C] with Christian conduct (*Apology* 46)? Among the other Christian virtues there shone especially the observance of fasts, as diligent as it was severe, by which Christians differed from pagans no less than by their other virtues. It did not satisfy them to placate God’s wrath by such stern and austere fasting only in public calamities, but “rolling even in sackcloth and ashes,” says Tertullian, “we assail heaven with our importunity” (*Apology* 40). Here should be mentioned the beautiful things our author composed in special books about prayer, the virtue of patience, women’s vain dress and adornment, unlawful attendance at shows, idolatry and its various forms, and the magnanimity of martyrdom, surpassing all others.

[Col. 0026D] As a crown, let selected ascetical teachings from Tertullian’s works be appended.

**The Christian fears God, not men.**—“We who are judged under God, the watcher of all, and foresee eternal punishment from him, rightly are the only ones to seek innocence: because of the fullness of his knowledge, the impossibility of concealment, and the magnitude of torment—not prolonged but everlasting—fearing him whom the judge of those who fear must himself fear; fearing God, not the proconsul” (*Apology* 45, vol. I, col. 500).

**God urges us to repentance.**—“Is it good to repent, or not? Why ponder it? God commands it. Yet he does not merely command; he also exhorts. He invites with salvation as reward; when he swears, even saying ‘As I live,’ he desires to be believed. O blessed are we, for whose sake God swears! O most wretched, if we do not believe even the Lord when he swears!” (*On Repentance* 4, vol. I, col. 1233).

[Col. 0027A] **God kindly receives the sinner returning to him.**—“Who must we understand this Father to be? God, surely: no one is so much a father, no one so tender. Therefore he will receive you, his son, although you have squandered what you received from him and returned naked, because you returned; and he will rejoice more in your return than in another’s sobriety. But only if you repent from your soul … if you leave the swine, the unclean herd, if you seek your Father again though offended, saying, ‘I have sinned, Father, and am no longer worthy to be called yours.’ Confession of sins lightens as much as concealment aggravates. Confession is the counsel of satisfaction; concealment, of obstinacy” (ibid. [Col. 0027B] 8, vol. I, col. 1243).

“*Exomologesis* is the discipline of casting down and humbling a man, prescribing a manner of life that entices mercy. Concerning dress and food it commands lying in sackcloth and ashes, covering the body with filth, casting down the soul with sorrows, and changing by mournful treatment those things through which it sinned. It bids one know only plain food and drink, for the soul’s sake rather than the belly’s; generally to nourish prayers with fasts; to groan, weep, and cry out day and night to the Lord your God; to cast oneself at the presbyters’ feet, kneel before God’s beloved, and enjoin upon all the brethren the embassy of one’s supplication” (ibid. 9, vol. I, col. 1242).

**Repentance that does not cast off delicacies deserves ridicule.**—“Add to the expense; seek out the enormous fattening of game birds; clarify old wine. When someone asks for whom you lavish these things, say, ‘I have sinned against God and am in danger of perishing eternally. Therefore I now hang in suspense, waste away, and torment myself, that I may reconcile to myself the God whom I injured by sinning’” (ibid. 11, col. 1216).

[Col. 0027C] **To a Christian the world is like a prison.**—“If we reflect that the world itself is more truly a prison, we shall understand that you have gone out of prison rather than entered one. The world has greater darkness, which blinds men’s hearts; it puts on heavier chains, which bind men’s very souls; it breathes out worse uncleanness, the lusts of men. Finally, the world contains more accused persons, namely the entire human race. In the end it awaits the judgments not of the proconsul but of God” (*To the Martyrs* 2, vol. II, col. 622).

[Col. 0027D] **Riches are to be despised after Christ’s example.**—“In almost every place the Lord’s Scriptures admonish us to despise the age. No greater exhortation to contempt for money is available than that the Lord himself is found amid no riches: he always justifies the poor and condemns the rich. Thus he prepared disdain for wealth in advance against the loss that tries patience, showing through the rejection of riches that injuries to them too must be reckoned as nothing” (*On Patience* 7, vol. I, col. 1260).

**Why one should not grieve at the death of friends.**—“Since the resurrection of the dead is certain, grief of mind is needless, and so is the impatience of grief. Why grieve if you do not believe he has perished? What you think death is a departure. One who goes ahead is not to be mourned, though certainly to be missed. [Col. 0028A] That longing too must be tempered by patience. Why bear immoderately the departure of one whom you will soon follow? Moreover, impatience in such a matter both bodes ill for our hope and betrays faith. We injure Christ when we fail to accept calmly those he has called away, as though they were to be pitied” (ibid. 9, col. 1264).

**Praises of patience.**—“God is a fully competent trustee for patience. If you deposit an injury with him, he is avenger; if a loss, restorer; if pain, physician; if death, reviver. How much is patience permitted, since it has God as debtor? And deservedly: it protects all that pleases him and intervenes in all his commands. It fortifies faith, governs peace, assists love, teaches humility, awaits repentance, ratifies *exomologesis*, rules the flesh, preserves the spirit, bridles the tongue, restrains the hand, tramples temptations, drives away scandals, completes martyrdoms, consoles the poor, moderates the rich, does not overstrain the weak or consume the strong, delights the believer, invites the Gentile, commends servant to master and master to God, adorns woman and approves man; it is loved in the child, praised in youth, revered in age; in every sex and every age it is beautiful” (ibid. 15, col. 1270).

[Col. 0028B] **Against shows and theaters.**—“We are commanded to put away all immodesty. In this way, then, we are also separated from the theater, which is the private council-chamber of immodesty, where nothing is approved except what is disapproved elsewhere” (*On the Shows* 17, vol. I, col. 649).

[Col. 0028C] **Stage performers are always infamous.**—“They exclude stage performers from all honors and ornaments alike.… What a confession this is of an evil thing, when its most popular agents are not without a mark of disgrace!” (ibid. 22, col. 654).

**Christians are forbidden to attend comedies.**—“Will he think about God at that time, placed where there is nothing of God?… Will he learn chastity while staring at mimes? Indeed, at every show no greater stumbling-block will occur than the more elaborate dress of women and men, the very agreement, alliance, or dissension in applause, fanning between them the sparks of lust through their interaction. Finally, no one entering a show first thinks of anything except seeing and being seen.… May God turn from his own people so great a desire for deadly pleasure” (ibid. 25, col. 656).

[Col. 0028D] **The true pleasures of Christians in this age.**—“Now, if you think this span of life must be spent in delights, why are you so ungrateful that you neither account sufficient nor acknowledge the many great pleasures God has given you? What is more delightful than reconciliation with God the Father and Lord? Than revelation of truth? Than recognition of errors? Than forgiveness of so many former crimes? What greater pleasure than disgust with pleasure itself, contempt for the whole age, true freedom, a whole conscience, a sufficient life, and no fear of death?” (ibid. 29, col. 659).

**Evil priests are worse than the Jews.**—“What wickedness! The Jews laid hands on Christ once; these impious priests [Col. 0029A] assail his body daily. O hands that ought to be cut off! Let them consider whether it was already said by way of likeness, ‘If your hand scandalizes you, cut it off.’ What hands should be cut off more than those by which the Lord’s body is scandalized?” (*On Idolatry* 7, vol. I, col. 669).

**A Christian woman neglects—indeed dreads—pleasing men.**—“You who are perfect, that is, Christian and chaste, should know that a desire to be desired must not merely be undesired but abhorred: first, because the effort to please through beauty does not come from an uncorrupted conscience, for we know beauty naturally invites lust. Why, then, arouse that evil against yourself? Why invite what you profess yourself a stranger to? Further, we must not open a path to temptations, which sometimes—may God keep this from his own—prevail by pressing upon us, or at least move a stumbling-block in the spirit” (*On the Dress of Women* II.2, vol. I, col. 1517).

[Col. 0029B] **What kind of crime it is to use cosmetics.**—“In adorning the body one must not go beyond what simple and sufficient cleanliness desires, beyond what pleases the Lord. For women sin against him when they anoint the skin with preparations and stain the cheeks with rouge.… Evidently God’s workmanship displeases them: in themselves they convict and criticize the craftsman of all. For they criticize when they correct, when they add, taking those additions, of course, from the rival craftsman—that is, the devil” (ibid. 5, col. 1327).

**Image of the Christian woman.**—“Come forth now, you who are arrayed in the preparations and ornaments of the Apostles.… Submit your head to your husbands and you will be adorned enough; occupy your hands with wool and plant your feet at home, and they will please more than if clad in gold. Clothe yourselves in the silk of uprightness, the fine linen of holiness, the purple of chastity. Painted in this fashion, you will have God as your lover” (ibid. 13).

[Col. 0029C] **A Christian must flee the pleasures of the world.**—“You are a stranger to this world, a citizen of the Jerusalem above.… You have nothing to do with the joys of the age; indeed, you owe the contrary. ‘For the world will rejoice, but you will mourn.’ And he calls those who mourn blessed.… Everything in the age is imaginary and nothing true” (*On the Soldier’s Crown* 13, vol. II, col. 96).

**No prescription runs against truth.**—“No one can prescribe against truth: neither length of time, patronage of persons, nor privilege of regions. From these, indeed, custom generally takes its origin in some ignorance or simplicity, is strengthened into use through succession, and so is asserted against truth. But our Lord Christ called himself truth, not custom” (*On the Veiling of Virgins* 1, vol. II, col. 889).

[Col. 0029D] **Faith is not proved from persons, but persons from faith.**—“We do not prove faith from persons, but persons from faith. No one is wise unless faithful; no one great unless Christian; and no one Christian unless he has persevered to the end” (*Prescription against Heretics* 3, vol. II, col. 15).

**No knowledge other than that of Jesus Christ is to be sought.**—“We have no need of curiosity after Christ Jesus, nor of inquiry after the Gospel. When we believe, we desire to believe nothing further. For this we believe first: that there is nothing further which we ought to believe” (ibid. 17, vol. II, col. 20).
