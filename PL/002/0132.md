Restored by *exomologesis*. *Exomologesis* differs from repentance as an external act differs from an internal one; for repentance is located in the mind of the person who grieves, whereas *exomologesis* is located in the mouth of the person who confesses and in the other works that outwardly manifest and demonstrate that inward repentance. Our author has already discussed these at length above, chapter IX.

[Col. 1249A] **Like Pharos.** Pharos was the name of that very lofty Egyptian tower, counted among the seven wonders of the world, which Ptolemy Philadelphus—or, at any rate, one of the Ptolemies—erected on the island of Pharos, seven stadia from the harbor of Alexandria and connected to the mainland by either a bridge or a causeway. He built it at the enormous cost, according to Pliny (*Natural History*, XXXIII, 12), of 800 talents—which, if I have calculated correctly, equal 1,200,000 of our thalers—in order to display fires to ships sailing by night, warning them of shoals and indicating the harbor entrance. From it, all towers—such as the one at Capri in Sicily which collapsed in an earthquake shortly before the death of the emperor Tiberius (Suetonius, *Tiberius*, 74); another which the emperor Claudius, according to the same author (*Claudius*, 21), had raised in imitation of the Alexandrian Pharos in the harbor at Ostia that he himself had excavated; and, finally, all those that still stand today in maritime cities and are customarily illuminated by nocturnal fires for the benefit of sailors—are called by the general name “Pharoses.” This name is not, as Isidore supposed (*Origins*, XV, 2), derived from Greek, ἀπὸ τοῦ [Col. 1249B] φωτὸς ὁρᾷν, “from seeing the light there,” but is more probably Egyptian in origin, having been transferred to that tower from the island itself, to which the name Pharos had long before adhered.

**Bearing every mark.** That is, marked in the worst possible way—namely, by as many sins as though they were brands burned into the soul. See another example of humility of this kind which Tertullian offered concerning himself at the end of the final chapter of *On Baptism*.

**To be silent about those things.** He means, as it were, that he finds it difficult to bring this treatise to an end and is breaking it off almost against his will.

[Col. 1250A] **The first, Adam**, that is, the originator and the first of all.

**Into Paradise.** In *Apology*, chapter 47, our Tertullian calls Paradise a place of divine delight appointed to receive the spirits of the saints. He believed that Paradise still existed, that it was situated beneath the Equator, and that it was enclosed on both sides by the torrid zone as by a wall of fire, and thus rendered inaccessible to mortals. St. Thomas, *Summa theologiae* II-II, q. 164, art. 2, ad 5, appears not to depart very far from this opinion. This word did not arise among the Greeks from παρὰ and δεύω, “from irrigating,” as Suidas maintains, but is of Persian—indeed Hebrew, or rather Chaldaean—origin, being derived from *pardes* [the immediately preceding etymological wording is corrupt in the source], which, according to the force of the word, signifies a garden of myrtles or, more generally, any exceptionally pleasant place.

But how was Adam restored through repentance to Paradise? Certainly not to that earthly Paradise from which he had been expelled and to which every return had been barred after God “placed before the paradise of pleasure [Col. 1250B] the Cherubim and a flaming, turning sword, to guard the way to the tree of life” (Gen. 3:24). Therefore that Paradise must be understood spiritually and mystically as the state of grace into which Adam was restored through repentance.

**He is not silent.** He still preaches to us by his example: amid toil, among thorns and thistles, and in the sweat of his face, as though performing perpetual works of repentance, he cultivated the earth and ate his bread from it. Thus he would serve as a lesson to us that only through the works of laborious repentance can one reach the **HEAVENLY PARADISE**, thereafter to inhabit it forever.

# INDEX OF TERTULLIAN’S LATINITY

[Col. 1249C] We have already said a little, in the preface placed at the threshold of this edition, about the value of Tertullian’s Latin—though new, as no one denies, and generally inelegant and harsh. Dr. Lumper has more on this a little below; see, if you wish, vol. I, cols. 93–94. We have no intention of repeating these matters more fully here, nor will anyone suppose that a suitable place for a new dissertation is available at the extreme limits of our work. Readers should merely remember this above all: that attention must be paid to Tertullian’s novel expressions, since in them the new language of the Latin Church is born and bursts forth, as it were, in its first shoot. Nor should the first rudiments of a form of speech that has passed through the mouths of so many persons and centuries throughout the whole Christian world, continuing down to our own usage and enduring to the great praise of God and the salvation of souls, any longer be held in such contempt.

They should remember, moreover, that the unusual vocabulary of Tertullian’s Latin must be traced especially to three causes: first, to the difficult and utterly novel exposition of the Christian faith; next, to the bold use of Hellenisms, more frequent in Septimius because he was especially fond of writing in a Greek manner; [Col. 1249D] and finally, to his forensic practice and familiar expertise in the ancient Roman laws, through which he is very often found to have employed the pronouncements of jurists. To these must be added—as in other men whom nature has endowed exceptionally well—Tertullian’s innate disposition, spontaneously inclined toward unusual expressions, overflowing with the excessive abundance of his many fields of learning and, so to speak, belching from satiety, and finally willing to write rustically because of a certain inborn African roughness.

“What wonder is it,” says a recent author, “that the writings of Tertullian—a stern, impetuous, ardent man of luxuriant imagination and acute intellect—display the most evident marks of this disposition of his and have assumed an uncommon character? A harsh and obscure style of writing was characteristic of Africans. This therefore attached itself spontaneously to him as well. Yet in this very quality he is truly unique and always himself. Because of his difficult arrangement of words and his employment here and there of obsolete and novel terms, his discourse is often troublesome to readers. At one moment it creates disgust by its swollen grandiloquence; at another it produces obscurity by its broken composition. Yet if you excuse these features, I often seem to hear a man whom, in the gravity and fervor of his discourse, I would not judge inferior even to an orator of the Golden Age. Read the *Apologeticum*. Observe especially how, from its very beginning, he assails his opponents in vehement yet majestic language, stings them with acuteness, troubles their conscience, moves them to pity, and finally carries the mind away in admiration both of his own magnanimity and of the sublimity of the religion he is about to defend. Observe these things and, unless everything deceives me, you will thereby be abundantly persuaded that Tertullian, however great the stylistic faults from which he suffered, possessed outstanding eloquence.” Thus J. A. Coessen, *Commentatio de Tertulliano, Christi et religionis christianae adversus gentes apologeta* (“Dissertation on Tertullian as Apologist for Christ and the Christian Religion against the Gentiles”), *Annales Academiae* [Col. 1250D] *Rheno-Trajectinae*, 1823–1824.

No one, therefore, will find it surprising that all editors of Tertullian have compiled a glossary peculiar to him with such diligence. Moreover, from the many indexes of this kind furnished by the older editions, especially Rigault’s, we considered that the one which Christian Gottfried Schütz, professor of humane letters and eloquence at the University of Jena, appended to the Halle edition should be selected above all others. He rendered distinguished assistance to Salomon Semler in preparing the edition of Septimius. We have preferred his index to the others chiefly because, although it is somewhat shorter, or lists fewer words, it nevertheless probes the mysteries of Tertullian’s language more clearly through the abundance of its subject matter, its description of words, and the learning displayed in its definitions [Col. 1251A] and discussions. Not undeservedly, the author himself, with the agreement of most scholars highly experienced in patristic studies, considered it to serve as a continuous commentary. Words which are either missing here and there or insufficiently explained generally carry their own explanation with them, either in the fabric of the context or in the very copious notes of various scholars. Some, however, appear merely to have been cited so that they might be brought to notice—and not without deliberation, since it is not irrelevant to observe what formulas of expression Tertullian produced and what kind of language Christian literature fashioned for itself from its birth. —EDS.

**A and Ω.** II, 935 B: “Thus the Lord also assumed for himself the two letters of Greece, the first and the last, as figures of the beginning and the end converging in himself,” etc.

**ABDICARE** (*to disown, repudiate*). II, 928 C: “Having voluntarily repudiated marriage, they grow old.” I: “The father, now bearing with his submissive son in turn, who had previously borne with his son when he was disobedient and defiant, [Col. 1251B] disowned him.”

**ABDICERE**, to disown, reject. II, 394 C: “Thus the Creator rejected everything sought from man.”

**ABHINC**, henceforth, from this point. II, 564 A: “What is written from this point onward.”

**ABIRE** (*to depart*). I, 1283 B: “As a woman whose husband has departed from affairs,” that is, has departed from life. Thus the Greeks customarily use the verbs οἴχεσθαι, κατοίχεσθαι, ἀπέρχεσθαι, and ὑπογίνεσθαι, that is, “to die”; all of them signify departure and withdrawal.—I, 615 C: “He has now departed and must return,” a proverb. For its meaning, see I, 209 B; 616 D.

**ABLUERE** (*to wash*), to baptize. II, 806 A: “The flesh is washed.” II, 1011 A: “It is not permitted to be washed again.”

**ABNOCTARE** (*to spend the night*). II, 749 B: “Among the Celts, to spend the night beside the tombs of brave men.…” [The text cited reads *adnoctare*.]

**ABOLEFACERE**, to abolish. I, 452 B: “To destroy a city by giving it the character of a tavern,” that is, to exchange civil and honorable order for the confusion of a tavern. Scaliger corrects it to *abolefacere*, κνίσσᾷν [“to fill with the savor or reek of burnt sacrifice”; reading uncertain]. I, 1308 A: “He could likewise have [Col. 1251C] abolished it,” etc.

**ABOMINAMENTUM** (*abomination*). II, 637 A: “The man cast away his golden abominations.”

**ABORTIVUS** (*abortive*). II, 564 A: “A defective and, so to speak, abortive birth.” Irenaeus: “Formless and without appearance, like an abortion.” II, 331 C: “The premature and, in a manner, abortive Marcionites.”

**ABRUMPERE** (*to break off*). II, 47: “He plainly breaks himself away from the Church of the authentic rule,” an expression used ἀμεταβάτως, “intransitively” [or “without transposition”].

**ABRUPTE** (*abruptly*). II, 165 C: “If we employ this proposition so abruptly in our assumptions.”

**ABRUPTUM** (*a precipice, chasm*). II, 507 A: “Here too I leap across the very broad chasm in the interrupted Scripture.” The expression is taken from those who prefer to shorten their journey by making a leap rather than incur a loss by walking along a more convenient route. Thus Scaliger on Cardan: “You fly across the largest field with the slightest leap.” Tertullian presently explains himself, II, 507 D: “You have removed so much from the Scriptures”; that is, you have cut out, rejected, and broken off so much that enormous gaps remain in the continuous text of the books. In book V against the same opponent: “How many pits he has made in the [Col. 1251D] epistle by removing what he wished.”

**ABRUPTUS**, harsh, obstinate. II, 77 A: “As concerning one who is harsh, headlong, and desirous of death.” II, 247 B: “More rugged than the Caucasus.” The Caucasus is a very extensive mountain of Scythia whose ridges, as Pliny says (*Natural History*, VI, 5), turn in the Colchian region of Pontus toward the Riphaean Mountains, one flank descending toward the Euxine and Maeotis, the other toward the Caspian and Hyrcanian seas, under various names. Virgil, *Aeneid* IV: “The bristling Caucasus begot you from its hard crags,” etc. By these words *abruptum* is explained when applied to a savage, hard, intractable, and inhospitable person. II, 432 C: “Moreover, nothing is so unreasonable as that another should provide it.” II, 657 B: “Otherwise harsh and absurd.”

**ABSOLESCERE** (*to fall out of use*). II, 921 C: “The seedbed of the race falls out of use,” that is, so that it ceases to exist in the accustomed and customary manner.

**ABSOLVERE** (*to release, remove*). II, 76 B: “He removes the exceedingly tiresome scout’s shoe from his feet,” that is, takes it off. Among the Romans, when soldiers were discharged as unworthy of the privileges and [Col. 1252A] honor of military service, their shoes, among other things, were customarily removed. Balduinus, *De Calceo*, chapter 24. See also **SPECULATORIA**. I, 572 A: “It has been settled concerning their weakness and mediocrity,” that is, it has been most clearly demonstrated that they possess no strength.

**ABSTINERE** (*to withhold*). I, 302 A: “Since women were so thoroughly withheld from wine [Col. 1252B],” using *abstinerentur* for *abstinerent*.

**ABSTRUERE**, to remove light by placing a structure before it. II, 38 C (15). See **OBSTRUERE**. II, 428 A: “And he denies that a lamp should be obstructed.”

**ABUSUS.** II, 970 B: “About to proceed from confinement to the contest, *abusus*,” that is, almost consumed and exhausted by long use. Rigault prefers *obesus*, but this lacks the authority of the manuscripts. Nonius: “*Obesus*: slender and thin, gnawed all around and emaciated.” Naevius: “With body and breast everywhere wasted, senseless in mind, oppressed by a drowsy, sluggish heaviness.” Gellius, *Attic Nights*, XIX, 7.

**ACACIA.** I, 13: “Which *acacia* promises,” that is, ἀκακία, “integrity, innocence.” Rigault prefers the alternative reading: “We have to put on this incorruptibility over the new man, which the monarchy promises.” He is alluding to Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 15 and 2 Corinthians 5, which command Christians to hope confidently for the resurrection, which will clothe mortal bodies with immortality. For then it will come to pass that the souls of the blessed return to their bodies as to new dwellings—inasmuch as they are now eternal, [Col. 1252C] having been clothed with immortality as with a new garment, never thereafter to be worn away by any ages. By “monarchy” Septimius means God most good and most great, the sense in which he very frequently employed that word in his disputation *Against Praxeas*.

**ACATUS**, a small vessel. II, 469 A: “If you have never received stolen or illicit merchandise into your *acati*,” etc. Ἄκατος is a ship equipped for conducting trade. From it comes the diminutive ἀκάτιον, *acatium* (Pliny, *Natural History*, IX, 31). Here the author follows the entire procedure of nautical commerce with great precision, in order to strike Marcion more forcefully. For sailors receive merchandise under the merchants’ agreement; goods are delivered stamped with the mark of some title, or transmitted after being designated by a sign, loaded aboard, and so forth.

**ACCEDERE**, to equal. II, 1042 A: “But also Cleomachus, the boxer who had previously equaled the Tirynthian.” II, 76 A: “And a person from the ranks had now approached.” With Rigault one should read *decesserat*, “had withdrawn.” This was the soldier who was more steadfast than the other brethren—that is, his Christian fellow soldiers—[Col. 1252D] who had not wished to be exposed, judging that there was no betrayal of duty in so slight a concealment.

**ADORARE.** II, 550 A: “Lest they be approved by seriousness,” that is, sanctioned. Junius prefers *adornentur*, “be adorned.” For if you apply seriousness to a frivolous thing, you will appear to adorn it; festive gaiety befits it. II, 218 A: “I venerate the fullness of Scripture.”

II, 1000 C: “Itself also adulterous,” concerning the scripture known as *The Shepherd*. II, 156 B: “Let all adulterous crops be gathered.” I, 580 C: “Because we have fabricated an adulterous night.” [Col. 1254D]

**ADULTERARE** (*to adulterate*). I, 632 A: “But he who adulterates and courts”—namely, the truth. I, 519 A: “They adulterated it into philosophical sects.” II, 987 A: “He adulterates and violates himself.”

**ADULTERIUM** (*adultery, adulteration*). I, 519 B: “Adulterations of the saving discipline.” II, 51 A: “Adulterations of the Scriptures.” II, 401 A: “In this one place alone I marvel that Marcion’s hand was paralyzed in its adulteration,” that is, that, as though stupefied by its adulteration and corruption of the Scriptures, it lacked sensation. I, 1312 B: “Concerning the adulteration of unjust colors.”

**ADUMBRARE** (*to overshadow, veil*). II, 899 B: “Therefore so dangerous a face ought to be overshadowed,” that is, veiled. I, 645 A: “Later it was resolved to veil impiety with pleasure.” II, 1014 D: “Obscure things are illustrated by manifest things.”

**ADVOCARE** is the Greek παρακαλεῖν, “to exhort, console, summon to one’s aid.” II, 390 C: “To encourage the weak.”

**ADVOCATIO** properly means the company of patrons and friends [Col. 1255A] who attend someone in court. I, 270 A, C: “The advocacy of the Scriptures that is also to follow,” that is, their defense. II, 393 A: “Since you have received your consolation,” that is, παράκλησιν, “consolation.” I, 1267 A: “Accordingly, both consolation and laughter are promised to such persons.” II, 863 B: “Words of exhortation and advocacy,” that is, of consolation and aid. II, 93 B: “For whom the plea of necessity is most familiar,” that is, its allegation as an excuse. I, 270 A: “They employ hired advocacy.”

**ADVOCATOR**, παράκλητος, “advocate, comforter.” II, 393 A: “Just as I have proved him the advocate of beggars.”

**ADVOCATUS** (*advocate*). II, 931 A: “In this matter too you ought to recognize the Paraclete as advocate.” He alludes to the meaning of the word παράκλητος, “advocate”; but the author’s application is ridiculous. II, 972 A: “Insofar as he is Paraclete, that is, advocate.” I, 695 A: “No longer of advocates, but of angels.” By *advocati* he means those who gave a friend the benefit of their presence by affixing their seals, subscribing their names, and giving testimony. Thus Varro, *On Agriculture*, II, 5: “But you, Murrius, come as my advocate while I pay out the asses at the Parilia, so that if they later [Col. 1255B] demand them back from me, you may be able to bear witness.”

**AEDIFEX**, in place of *aedificator*, “builder.” I, 678 A: “From the likeness of that most provident builder.”

**AEDIFICARE**, to invite by setting forth an example. II, 540 A: “So that through the suspension of knowledge they may build up an opinion.” II, 139 C: “Admonishing by edifying them toward endurance of these things.” I, 1310 B: “They have learned to edify only this astonishment of women,” that is, to serve the work by doing and displaying something at which women may be astonished and marvel. II, 14 B: “To be built up toward ruin.” I, 624 B: “So that they may devote themselves to building up strength.”

**AEDIFICATIO** (*edification*). I, 1256 A: “No talkativeness in edification is disgraceful.”

**AEDIFICATORIUS** (*edificatory, building*). II, 782 B: “For the word that built death had crept into Eve while she was still a virgin.”

**AEMULATIO**, envy, hatred, and the distress experienced by the pagans when they saw their own numbers diminishing and the Christian religion gaining greater growth day by day. I, 277 A: “Once acquitted after the tribunal, he may laugh at your envy.” I, 308 A: “The Jews, from envy.” II, 52 A: “Proceeding, of course, from a cause of rivalry.” I, 536 A: [Col. 1255C] “The rivalry between the divine and the human matter,” that is, God thinks one way, man another. II, 408 A: “Rivalry with the law.” II, 928 A: “If anyone, through presumption concerning this providence of his, should provoke rivalry.” The meaning is that it is all the more possible, through God’s providence, for a woman to become pregnant if someone, by intercourse, challenges the assumption formed concerning her—that she is barren—as though intending to rival God, who makes barren women fruitful.

**AEMULUS** (*rival, opposed*). I, 277 B: “A certain principle of rival operation,” that is, actively rivalrous. See **RATIO**. II, 958 C: “In a rival manner,” that is, contrariwise. II, 278 B: “Who would not otherwise be fully good unless he were the rival of evil.”

**AENIGMA**, figure. II, 448 C: “Where, in the enigma of the stone.” II, 820 A: “Under the pretext of figures and enigmas.” II, 841 A: “So that the other kind cannot admit enigmas.”

**AEONEIUS.** II, 251 C: “Like the *oeoneian* sow.” Rigault emends this to *aeneian*. See **SCROFA**.

**AEQUIPARANTIA** (*equivalence*). II, 570 B: “Into rival equivalences of corporeal substances.”

[Col. 1255D] **AESCULAPIUS.** II, 1031 A: “Now to your Aesculapius.” He calls Aesculapius “yours” because he was especially worshiped in Africa and was believed to have been born of a Punic mother; previously he had belonged only to the people of Pergamum. The meaning is: the cloaks you once used were of the kind that even today the priests of Aesculapius employ.

**AESTIMARE** (*to estimate, perceive*). II, 82 B: “He perceived touch in the hands.” Rigault reads *extimavit* more correctly; see below.

**AESTIMATIO**, thought. I, 263–264 A: “And yet not even in this very way … toward consideration,” etc. [The parenthetical reference to Heumann is textually uncertain.] The meaning is: and yet they do not allow themselves to be admonished by this circumstance to consider whether perhaps truth underlies the doctrine commonly condemned. I, 1327 A: For *in aestimationem temporum*, “into an estimation of the times,” Rigault correctly restored *in extimatione temporum*, “at the extremity of the times,” that is, in the last times, at the end of the age.

**AESTUARE** (*to seethe, contend*). II, 134 C: “Since earthly offices also seethe over rank,” that is, contend for the dignity of rank [Col. 1256A] so that they may obtain first place. I, 497 A: “The prison always seethes with your people.”

**AESTUARIUM** (*surge, tideway*). I, 658 A: “What will you do when caught in that tideway of impious acclamations?”

**AETAS** (*age*). I, 316 A; 319 A: “Greater age,” that is, those greater in years. I, 1327 A: “Ages now brought to rest and drawn into the harbor of modesty.” In the same passage: “Because of the coldness of age.” II, 982 B: “Knowledge also has its ages in Christ.”

**AFFECTATIO**, imitation. I, 371 A: “But most of your people also, through imitation, sometimes,” etc.

**AFFECTIO**, προσποίησις, “assumption, affectation.” II, 1045 A: “Because of the different assumption of the dark garment.”

**AFFECTIOSUS** (*susceptible to impressions*). II, 682 B: “And it is naturally susceptible, lacking understanding.”

**AFFINIS** (*associated, participant*). II, 260 A: “And it is associated with both shamelessness and malice,” that is, a participant in them; so Cicero, *For Sulla*: “Associated with this suspicion.” II, 437 C: “Strangers and associated with no legal right.” I, 1261 A: “We shall be found participants in desire.” I, 1267 A: “Are those who are impatient participants in the same thing?” I, 674 A: “That they were participants in manifold idolatry.”

[Col. 1256B] **AFFINITAS** (*affinity, association*). I, 679 A: “So as not to proceed as far as associations with idolatry,” that is, to such a degree that we become participants in idolatry.

**AFFLATUS** (*breath*). II, 291 B: “The breath of God,” that is, the soul. II, 294 C: “Above all it must be maintained that the Greek Scripture specified this by naming ‘breath’ (πνοή), not ‘spirit’ (πνεῦμα).” I, 1231 B: “Other things completed by his breath.”

**Ἀγενεαλόγητος**, “without genealogy or kinship.” II, 73 A.

**AGERE** (*to act, perform, represent*). II, 26 B: “That Jesus Christ had acted,” that is, had assumed that human role and had performed everything foretold concerning Jesus Christ. II, 416 B: “And you act for him,” that is, you act as his representative and agent. II, 434 B: “To such an extent conducting his business.” II, 667 C: “But what are the dying said to yield—the *animus* or the *anima*?”

**AGINA**, Festus says, is the aperture into which the tongue of a balance is inserted—that is, the place in which the balance moves or turns. Hence those who are moved by a small profit are called *aginatores*. II, 234 B: “At what they call the middle point of the balance,” [Col. 1256C] concerning motion so evenly balanced that it inclines in neither direction and neither side outweighs the other.

**AGNITIO**, assent, approval. II, 155 B: “And from that recognition, peace.” II, 963 A: “Indeed, fasts will also obtain from God recognition of the mysteries,” that is, skill in interpreting sacred figures and images—namely, those divinely presented in dreams.

**AGNOSCERE**, to assent to, approve. II, 155 B: “Now acknowledging the prophecies of Montanus.”

**AGNOSCIBILIS** (*recognizable*). II, 877 A: “But it had preserved features recognizable to Peter.”

**AGONOTHETA**, ἀγωνοθέτης, the presiding official and judge of a contest—specifically a musical or dramatic contest. Those who presided over athletic contests were called *athlothetae*. II, 104 A: “Thus you will recognize that the judgment of the contest belongs to the same contest-president.” I, 624 A–B: “In which the living God is the contest-president.”

**Αἱρέσεις** (*haireseis*, “heresies”), so called by a Greek word from the meaning “choice,” which a person exercises either in establishing or in embracing them. II, 18 A.

[Col. 1256D] **Αἰών** (*aeon*). II, 550 B: Αἰῶνα τέλειον, “the perfect aeon.”

**ALIQUI**, in place of *aliquis*, “someone.” I, 315 A. This occurs very frequently.

**ALCINOUS**, king on the island of Corcyra. II, 1036 A. On his orchards, see Pliny, *Natural History*, XIX, 4, and Homer, *Odyssey*, VII.

**ALES** (*winged*), metaphorically meaning that something is exceedingly swift and swifter than any bird. I, 407 A: “Every spirit is winged.”

**ALEXANDER**, II, 1038 A, is Alexander Polyhistor, whom Suidas reports to have been a Milesian, to have been captured in war, to have served as a tutor in the household of Cornelius Lentulus, and afterward, upon being manumitted, to have been called by the name Cornelius. He appears to have written books for his mother concerning the affairs of the Phrygians and Egyptians. Richer understands the reference as being to the peoples of Alexandria and Africa.

**ALLECTIO** (*selection, enrollment*). II, 58 B: “A tested selection”—namely, of those to be admitted into ecclesiastical orders. For it was customary that this should be publicly announced before ordination. The announcement was made by posting their names and admonishing the people [Col. 1257A] that if anyone had any charge against them, he should prove it by manifest facts. Such was the Church’s tested selection, in contrast to the rash ordinations noted above among heretics. II, 514 B: “The Jews, however, are not sons of the Creator by nature, but by the selection of their fathers,” that is, by adoption.

**ALLEGERE**, to assume, adopt. I, 335 A: “On this side there is no ground for adopting humanity into divinity.” II, 808 A: “When the soul is adopted by God, it itself is what makes it possible for the soul to be adopted.”

**ALLEGORIA**, parable, enigma, figure. II, 421 C: “Through allegories and figures.” II, 835 B: “So that allegories may indeed be free of cloudiness.”

**ALLEGORICUS** (*allegorical*). II, 543 A: “An allegorical arrangement,” that is, figurative. See **CONVITIUM**. II, 996 A: “And when actors adapt allegorical gestures.” II, 836 A: “Allegorical Scriptures.”

**ALLEGORIZARE** (*to allegorize*). II, 401 A: “Which Christ allegorized in men.” II, 834 A: “We also maintain that the mention of garments in the Scriptures allegorizes the hope of the flesh.”

[Col. 1257B] **ALLOCUTIO** (*address*). II, 28 B: “The preface of an address,” that is, προσφώνησις, “an address or salutation.” II, 660 A: “Or addresses are delivered”—ὁμιλίαι, δημηγορίαι, “discourses, public speeches.”

**ALTEGRADIUS** (*high-stepping*). II, 913 A: “As though it were some beast rather than a bird … the other high-stepping creatures.”

**ALTERUTER** (*either of two; each of two*). I, 321 A: “From each of the two,” that is, from each party entering the contract. I, 471 A: “For one another,” that is, one in place of the other. II, 985 A: “Without either opposing alternative.”

**ALTILIS** (*fattened*). I, 651 A: “And because of Greece’s leisure you hate fattened men.” He means athletes or wrestlers, whom the directors of gymnasia customarily fed with the greatest care so that they might be distinguished by the utmost strength for contests. The Greeks were exceptionally devoted to such contests, enjoying leisure from public business. These, indeed, were the evils of a long peace. Septimius called them “fattened men,” just as Varro and Martial call cattle and hens *altiles*, meaning plump and fattened. I, 1246 B: “Seek out the enormous fattening of well-fed animals.”

**ALVEARIUM** (*trough*). II, 586 A: “Into the troughs of the mixture,” that is, basins and what are commonly called kneading-troughs.

[Col. 1257C] **ALVEUS**, belly, abdomen. I, 322 A: “The bellies of the bears themselves are sought.” II, 663 A: “And digested without bellies.”

**AMARE**, to be accustomed. II, 1033 A: “Being accustomed to dress according to the occasion.” II, 319 A: “But the unencumbered power of truth is accustomed to express itself in few words,” following the Greek usage φιλεῖ γενέσθαι, “is wont to occur.” I, 265 A: “They are accustomed to remain ignorant.” Heumann reads *amans*, “loving.” II, 133 B: “He was accustomed to invite to glory those whom he had called to salvation.”

**AMBITIO**, a wandering and manifold circuit. II, 978 B: “To them belongs the circuit of the body.” II, 900 A: “Because he did not naturally acquire the encircling covering of hair,” περιβόλην, “a covering.” II, 691 B: “The whole circumference of the belly.” II, 650 B: “But let the rivalry of each excellence see to itself,” that is, φιλοπρωτεία, “ambition for preeminence.”

**AMENTARE** (*to fit with a throwing-strap; hurl*). II, 439 C: “He hurled this proposition,” that is, he twisted and brandished it like a javelin. Lucan: “He fitted the javelin with a strap.” Hesychius: ἅμμα, δεσμός, “a fastening, a bond”; hence *amentum*, the strap of a javelin or spear.

**AMENTATIO** (*a fitting with straps; amenta*). I, 572 A: “By which you may fall upon your own swords and throwing-straps,” [Col. 1257D] that is, upon the straps attached to them.
