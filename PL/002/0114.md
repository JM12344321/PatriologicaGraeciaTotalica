And above, upon the Cherubim, through fourfold form made one,  
[Note: [Col. 1081C] “And above, upon the Cherubim, through fourfold form made one,” [Col. 1081D] etc. He alludes partly to the two Cherubim overshadowing the mercy seat, concerning which see Exodus 25, and partly to the four living creatures of Ezekiel 1, which are called Cherubim in Ezekiel 10 and which, below in chapter 7, the author interprets as the four Evangelists. We have therefore corrected, on the basis of these passages of Scripture, “They spread their wings” (*Pandebant pennas*) in place of *pinnas*, and likewise “and covered the commanded *sagmina*” in place of “the commands”; though meanwhile I do not understand what the word *sagmina* is intended to mean. For the meaning “sacred herbs,” which were called *sagmina* and were used in declarations of war or peace, is not appropriate to the sides of the mercy seat.]

They spread their wings and covered the commanded *sagmina* [meaning uncertain].

Outside the veil, the open portion of the sanctuary lay exposed,  
[Note: [Col. 1081D] “Outside the veil,” etc. *Exterius* is taken adverbially, in the sense “outside.” When, moreover, he says “and as many lamps,” he means seven; for the lampstand, consisting of twice three branches together with the central shaft, makes a total of seven. Hence it happens that, in the following chapter, he interprets the lampstand as the sevenfold Spirit.]

Facing it stood a massive basin of broad bronze,  
And the lampstand, with twice three branches on every side  
Joined to its central shaft, and as many lamps.  
Its golden substance filled the temple with light.

## CHAPTER VI

Thus the first, common and open region of the temple  
Represents the worship of the people abiding under the Law,  
In whose darkness the one, sevenfold Holy Spirit  
[Col. 1081B] Always shone and sheltered the people:  
This is the true lampstand and its living lamps,  
Shining through the Law and the prophets upon hearts brought into submission.

Hence the altar that was made is believed to be an image of the earth.  
Here the people’s works, which they always offered  
Only with blood, with life poured out in flowing gore,  
Once appeared openly before everyone on every side.  
Here too the Lord, made a sacrificial victim for all,  
Designates the whole earth—and the altar in particular.

Hence also the ineffable author of the New Covenant,  
[Note: [Col. 1081D] “Hence also the ineffable author of the New Covenant, the disciple John,” etc. Here too he ascribes to the same John both the Gospel—which he calls the New Covenant—and the Apocalypse, concerning which see more fully book IV against Marcion, chapter 5, since he cites Revelation 6. Here, however, the final letter of the word *discipulus* must be elided at the caesura, according to a practice customary among Christian poets and used by the author above.]

The disciple John, bears witness that he saw beneath such an altar  
The souls of those who suffered for the Name,  
Crying out for the vengeance of the mighty God for their slaughter.

Here, meanwhile, is rest. Beneath the body of the earth,  
In an unknown region, there lies an open place  
[Col. 1081C] Sustained by its own light; it is called Abraham’s bosom,  
[Note: [Col. 1082C] “It is called Abraham’s bosom,” etc. On this see above, book IV against Marcion, chapter 34. Here, besides writing—as he does there—that Abraham’s bosom is far removed from the darkness and fire of hell, [Col. 1082D] he adds that it is nevertheless beneath the earth. He also approaches more closely to the common opinion of theologians when he says that, after the Lord had suffered, one everlasting house was made from the twofold house—namely, Abraham’s bosom and eternal glory—when:

> The regions of heaven lay open, and the carved holy places.

And so, when he wrote this, he had not yet fallen into the error of the millenarians, concerning which see more fully the Prolegomena.]

Higher than the darkness and far removed from the fire,  
Yet beneath the earth. This is called the bronze altar,  
On which we recalled that there was a dark covering.  
It divides both regions and, by itself, reveals  
[Col. 1082A] The one separated from eternity by worship and temporal use.

Though their love is distant, they are not hostile to one another,  
Divided in time and space, yet bound in principle.  
It appears to be one house, although partitioned by a veil;  
And so, when the Lord had suffered and the veil was torn,  
The regions of heaven lay open, and the carved holy places;  
And what had once been a twofold house became one everlasting house.

## CHAPTER VII

The inner temple represents the people named after Christ,  
[Note: [Col. 1082D] “The inner temple,” etc. We have enclosed in parentheses the line:

> Here the proper person is truly bound, not a shadow,

although it remains corrupt and incomplete. We have also corrected *Aeriae pelles* (“sky-colored skins”) in place of *Aeria*, for he is explaining the skins of lambs with which the ark was covered in the color of heaven, as was stated in chapter 5. Finally, what he adds concerning the jar of manna is somewhat obscure.]

Following God’s own commands with heavenly worship.  
(Here the proper person is truly bound, not a shadow.)  
The established order declared it complete in accomplished realities.

Under the figure of the ark, it displays Christ’s venerable body,  
Joined through the wood to the sacred Spirit.  
[Col. 1082B] The sky-colored skins are flesh, born not from seed,  
Stretched upon the wood; at the same time, within it, the burning Spirit,  
Fashioned in a gold-bearing form, is joined to it,  
So that the flesh, once peace was granted, might flourish mingled with the Spirit.

The golden jar, when full, also bears a figure of the Lord’s flesh;  
It openly declares the Lord of the New Covenant.  
The manna, which is the true heavenly bread, delivered  
To him by the Father and given by him as a pledge to the saints,  
He will give perfectly to those who, as lovers of good works,  
Have always maintained the bonds of peace.

And the two inscribed tablets of the Law within the ark  
Signify that these things are always stored together in Christ,  
Who himself brought both the old and the new commandment:  
The ark of the most high Father, through whom the rich Father gave all things.

And the rod of storax itself produced the fruit of a nut  
[Col. 1082C] (This is the figure of the Virgin, who generated the body from her blood);  
Joined upon the wood, its sweet inward fruit,  
From the Holy Spirit, will soothe the bitterness of death,  
Just as Isaiah foretold Mary as the rod from the stock of Jesse,  
From whom the flower goes forth into the world.

[Col. 1083A] The altar gleaming with gold represents heaven on high,  
To which the holy prayers, sent without fault, ascended.  
The Lord spoke of this altar when he said that anyone who offers  
Gifts must first reconsider peace with his brother;  
Only then can his prayers blaze up to the stars.

Christ, the sole victor and first priest,  
Offered this incense, born not from a tree but from prayers.

For although the Cherubim is one, with twice two faces,  
It is the one Word, conveyed in fourfold order:  
The fullest commandments that Christ himself brought from the Father,  
The hoped-for consolations of the new life.

But the four times six wings are the proclamations of the ancient Word,  
[Note: [Col. 1083C] “But the four times six wings,” etc. Here again, in his customary manner, he makes one thing out of three passages—as he did above in chapter 5, to use his own expression, “in centonic fashion.” For the detail concerning four sets of six wings or feathers is read in Ezekiel 1. When, moreover, [Col. 1084C] he adds, “the number of the wings,” etc., he alludes to the twenty-four Hebrew books of the Old Testament, as well as to the letters of the Greek alphabet.]

Bearing witness to those things which we learn were afterward accomplished.  
Upon these wings the heavenly words fly throughout the world;  
In these too the blood of Christ is held enfolded,  
[Col. 1083B] Foretold by the prophetic mouths of the obscure seers.

The number of the wings signifies the ancient books,  
Showing that those twenty-four are altogether certain,  
Which sang of the Lord’s ways and of the seasons of peace.

We see that all these cohere with the New Covenant.  
Thus too does John; thus does the Spirit reveal to him  
As many white-robed elders besides, seated upon the throne,  
Crowned and restraining all things with the mitre,  
Standing at the Lord’s throne, beside the glassy sea beneath the fire;  
And four winged living creatures, full of eyes  
Within and without—signifying that mysteries lie open,  
And that all things closed are seen together through the Word.  
[Col. 1083C] For the glassy sea mingled with flame signifies  
[Col. 1084A] That the gifts of the washing were bestowed upon believers  
Together with the Spirit.

What loving-kindness of the Lord! Who could describe how great  
A pavement he prepared before the tribunal—yes, beforehand—  
[Note: [Col. 1083C] “And the pavement,” Obert; Paris.]  
So that those wishing to anticipate his court and judgment  
Might flee them, and ample opportunity might be granted to the one who hastens!

## CHAPTER VIII

Thus, therefore, the Law and the wondrous prophets sang;  
Thus all things belonging to the Old and New Covenants  
Remain conjoined: those sacred things and immense pronouncements of the words.  
Thus too the apostolic voices bear witness everywhere,  
And ultimately nothing of the Old is unjoined to the New.

Thus they err, and thus they twist deeds away from the sayings—  
Those who fashioned false ways and disparage the Lord and God,  
The eternal King who brought forth so vast a world;  
They seek another under a fictitious name,  
[Col. 1084B] Bereft of the minds which, in their frenzy, they have lost.  
They wish to affirm that Christ is alien to the Law,  
Neither Lord of the world nor willing the salvation of the flesh,  
Nor, by the Father’s power, himself the maker of the body.

One must flee from them willingly, with ears stopped,  
Lest they stain innocent hearts with their speech.

Therefore let us, whom so great a dignity from God has flooded,  
Whom the true and heavenly words of the teacher and master  
Have instructed in good things and to whom they have given upright memorials,  
Always honor the Lord and sing without end,  
Rejoicing in pure faith and certain salvation  
(For, truly born to God, we are nourished with everlasting bread),  
[Col. 1084C] Hoping with all our heart for eternal life.

# BOOK FIVE: ON THE VARIOUS HERESIES OF MARCION

[Col. 1083]

## SUMMARY

This fifth book unties many bonds and knots,  
And lays open whatever evils had lain concealed.

*[NOTE]*

For he treats almost all of Marcion’s heresies and responds briefly to his arguments. For this reason we have added to the title: **ON HIS VARIOUS HERESIES.**

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 1083D]

The first book set forth in order the words of the enemy,  
[Note: [Col. 1083D] “The first book set forth,” etc. In these books he repeatedly calls Marcion a renegade because he departed from the catholic faith, as may be seen more fully in *On the Prescription against Heretics*, chapter 30, and in book I against Marcion. Both in the summaries prefixed to the individual books [Col. 1084D] and here, however, we have corrected “And the things that the one God handed down in the New Covenant” in place of *Divis*, and “the expiatory rites of the Law” in place of “of the king.” We would also have wished for manuscript copies by which to examine what he says here concerning the third book; because it was so obscure and perhaps corrupt, I did not add it to the summary above.]

Which the mad renegade unlawfully contrived and produced.  
Next came the hope of the flesh and Christ’s victory,  
And a brief account of the forms of the false ways.

The following book then teaches the connected mysteries of the Law  
And the things that the one God handed down in the New Covenant.

The third: a nation created from a freeborn mother,  
And ministers consecrated by prophets and patriarchs,  
[Col. 1084D] Whom, Christ, you chose from all peoples, twice six in number;  
The times of the cycle under the designation “elders,”  
The periods of one preserved, who obeyed the author of the crime,  
Unknown, lawless, wandering, and abandoned with his offspring.  
[The wording of these lines is uncertain and probably corrupt.]

The fourth book likewise relates that the obscure expiatory rites of the Law  
Are a figure of the ancient sacrifice, which truly appeared,  
Long awaited by the righteous together with the holy offspring.

This fifth book unties many bonds and knots;  
It lays open whatever evils had lain concealed,  
Drawing out arguments, though not without a prophet as witness.

[Col. 1085A] And although we may defeat enemies fortified with powerful arms,  
[Note: [Col. 1085D] “And although we may defeat enemies,” etc. A fine passage against those who willingly occupy themselves with the books of heretics, since, even when their arguments are being refuted, according to Tertullian’s witness here:

> The guilt of the discourse scarcely leaves the tongue unstained.

The words “though with a two-edged charge” also pertain to this point. Furthermore, *inlicita*, with its final syllable lengthened, is again Tertullianic, as is *contamine*.]

The serpent has so mingled together all things unlawful, polluted,  
Unspeakable, and defiled—  
A road without light for the blind, a voice filled with contamination—  
That, while we contend that the Maker of the world himself  
Is the one God, who also spoke through the voice of the prophets,  
And prove that there is no other unknown god,  
The guilt of the discourse scarcely leaves the tongue unstained.

While in manifold embrace we follow with praise the one who is known,  
We condemn the other, belated seasons of the unknown god.  
Yet the many concealed poisons of deceit compel us  
To lay bare these words, though with a two-edged charge.

## CHAPTER II

Who, then, is this God whom you call true,  
Unknown to the peoples and, in short, alien to the world?  
[Col. 1085B] Did this one, whom no one knew before, come from on high?  
If he came to his own, why did he seek them so late? If not to his own, why  
Does he seize them like a robber and repeatedly fill a people abiding  
Under the Law with unknown utterances through that Law?

If he came to show mercy and give aid to all,  
To relieve those defeated by the grievous destruction of death,  
And to free from the foul bond of the flesh  
The inward man who is unjustly confined and mutilated,  
Why was this watchful, ever-kind benefactor so late—  
[Note: [Col. 1085D] “Earlier,” Paris.]  
He who previously had never revealed himself at all to anyone—  
Seeking aliens by his word after so many ages,  
Never awaited, unknown, and sent into the world?

## CHAPTER III

A shepherd seeking a sheep that he had not lost before  
[Note: [Col. 1085D] “A shepherd seeking a sheep,” etc. As though engaged upon another matter, while still persisting in the absurdity arising from the new god, he passes to a defense of marriage, which Marcion rejected. Concerning this, see above, book I against Marcion, the penultimate chapter.]

Ought to have cast off the flesh, as though he too were a victorious  
[Note: [Col. 1085D] “As,” Obert.]  
[Col. 1085C] Spirit, such as he had always been and such as he wished them to be;  
He ought to have rescued everywhere all expelled souls without bodies  
And left the despoiled flesh to the earth;  
In a single day he should have filled the world alike with corpses,  
Emptied the earth, and raised the souls to heaven.

The offspring of humanity would at once have ceased to be born,  
And thereafter no descendant of your race  
Would have been born or poured a new generation into the world.

Or, since it is shown that none of these things happened then,  
He ought to have established an end for the future race:  
He should have filled the marriage bed with an unwavering resolve,  
Made men in body produce fruit without seed,  
Made the beds of the female sex a cause of weariness,  
[Col. 1085D] Closed entirely the genital members of the flesh,  
And left no will in our mind and no faculty of ability.

After this, the inward man, infused into and adhering to flesh  
Joined by blood, could always have perished.  
The sheep is always perishing: is there, then, no power to save it?  
Since man is always being born under the charge of death,  
What is the shepherd’s work, if this is what his discoveries teach?  
The sheep is therefore proved to have been sought, but not rescued.

Yet now a choice exists to enter marriage,  
As there always has, for those whom a certain lineage has joined.  
Nations are born, and a multitude scarcely capable of being numbered;  
The living bodies of those being born receive souls.

Nor was it fitting for Paul, even though for the present time he urged certain people  
[Note: [Col. 1085D] “Certain people,” Obert.]  
To remain, as he himself did, in an equal condition,  
Because he perceived the many pressures of the short time.  
For he commanded other, tenderer ages to marry,  
[Col. 1086B] And not to defraud one another, but to render what was owed by agreement.

But who, by cunning deceit, persuaded you to remain  
Secure from the division of offspring and to live without love,  
To commit the crime of adultery and lose your life?  
And although it perishes, to falsify that fact with the appellation of a word?

For this reason he pours forth all the sweetness of his utterance:  
You must fearlessly do whatever pleases you—  
Chaste in appearance, secretly stained by crime.  
For this reason he deprived you of honorable marriage.

But why say more? It is doubtless good that you have been separated;  
It benefits the world that no one born from your seed should exist.  
At last the instruments of death will cease.

## CHAPTER IV

While you hope to preserve one part of man for salvation,  
Your entire man perishes through your profane doctrine—  
[Col. 1086C] Both the inward man and what you call the ancient enemy.

But man too is said to be breath alone;  
[Note: [Col. 1086D] “Nor,” Obert.]  
Nor is the flesh called the ancient man; nor are spirit and flesh  
Enemies, but true man is the two joined in one.  
[Note: [Col. 1086D] Obert adds “is.”]

Nor are they seen to possess private senses of their own:  
The flesh is governed, the spirit governs; it groans, rejoices, and grieves.  
[Note: [Col. 1086D] “Nor,” Obert.]  
The spirit loves the flesh and is itself most dear to its own flesh,  
Through which man appears and with which he is held commingled.  
[Note: [Col. 1086D] “Through which man appears,” etc. Here too, in Tertullianic idiom, *paret* is taken in the sense of *apparet*, “appears.” The following below, in chapter 9, is similar:

> Appearing to the holy patriarchs from the beginning of the world.

The poetic license of “he himself, rich in hope, opened” also belongs here, as does, below in chapter 8, “the black night covered.” The following line, moreover, is obscure and apparently corrupt:

> Seen from the one cut, the one sent accomplished it by suffering.]

It applies remedies to the flesh’s wounds and pours out tears.  
It eagerly takes up again through its members the nourishment of food,  
And wishes always to possess this flesh with itself, immortal—  
[Col. 1086D] The flesh whose breaking it laments and whose emptying through its limbs it grieves.

Death rules over that wretched flesh for an appointed time,  
[Col. 1087A] Until, renewed from light dust, when the dissolved flesh rises again,  
Hostile death shall at last fail.

This will be the supreme victory long awaited,  
Which the one venerable forever accomplished through the Father’s power,  
Having become true man. He carried the redeemed members of man  
Into heaven and, rich in hope, opened its approaches  
At the beginning of the race among all tongues. From there,  
Here where his work is always needed, he ministers with paternal devotion.  
Seen from the one cut, the one sent accomplished it by suffering.  
[This line is textually uncertain.]

## CHAPTER V

What now of those unspeakable voices? What of that ruined throng?

If it was not the Creator God himself who gave the laws—  
[Note: [Col. 1087D] “If it was not the Creator God himself who gave the laws.” Thus we correct the text in place of “If not God himself,” etc. This passage seems to intend what he has said repeatedly elsewhere and continues below in chapter 9: that it was always Christ—not God the Father—who was seen in former times, and therefore that he is the same God who appeared both in the Old Testament and in the New. On this matter, however, see more fully the author’s Paradoxes in the Prolegomena; likewise concerning the fact that he appears to call Christ himself “God the Creator,” and not only God the Father.]

Who laid a road through the waters from the path out of Egypt  
And freely gave them the settlements he had once promised—  
[Col. 1087B] Why did he come precisely to that nation and consecrated land?  
Why did he not seek other peoples or different kingdoms?

Why did he declare that, throughout the prophets, he had already been called  
By the name foretold, rather than by a name of his own?  
How too could he produce as his own the gracious gifts of baptism  
Which had been promised by another?

Those found in defilement after transgressing these commandments of God  
Longed for them and sought pardon from savage death.  
The gifts long awaited came deservedly—but to those  
Who had first heard them and now recognized them when found.

May the true hand of Christ grant this. The Father, the Creator himself,  
Remains forever from eternity and calls with a loving voice.  
What he prefigured he cherishes, prefigures, and equips;  
And as victor he now clothes in his own light what had been despised,  
So that the work of power may remain with everlasting praise.

## CHAPTER VI

[Col. 1087C]

What God can accomplish and what man can suffer,  
Let all recognize and live—this is what living Power has done.

But because neither prior predictions nor the final deeds  
Can persuade demented, raging men  
[Note: [Col. 1087D] “Bearing,” Obert.]  
That God became man, suffered, was buried,  
And rose again, let them at least learn to believe both truths  
On the basis of earthly reasoning, since so many witnesses of old,  
Embraced by the heavenly Word, proclaim them.

When the Lord Christ came into the world to be born as flesh,  
In the time of Augustus, the Roman king, for the first time  
The nations everywhere were numbered by decree in a census.  
That same king perhaps issued this decree because the supreme will,  
In whose hand was the king’s heart, acted through the laws.  
[Col. 1087D] He was the first to do so, and the registration was reduced to writing.

Then Joseph too, with Mary, his pregnant spouse,  
And likewise with the heavenly child, was himself enrolled.  
[Col. 1088A] Hence let those who, relying upon human documents, inquire  
For witnesses of the holy Word present their proofs,  
So that, defeated afterward by what they find even in this way,  
They may repent and seek pardon while there is time.

## CHAPTER VII

The Jews admit that they committed a grave crime,  
While they burn to disparage him  
[Note: [Col. 1088D] “Disparaging,” Obert.]  
And resist us. They neither say that his lineage was unknown,  
Nor can they say that they suspended upon the wood a man  
Who spoke the truth, being ignorant that the Lord’s flesh, born without seed,  
Had won the victory.

In part, however, they remain silent; in part, they triumph,  
For they labor to proclaim to the peoples that God was merely man.

Behold the error that conquers both you and them.  
It will support us when we prove certainties to you.  
They call God a man, while you falsely call a bodiless man a body;  
And alas, the manifold madness of your mind engulfs you.  
[Col. 1088B] As for the claim that each side has presumed, you both scatter it while sinking:  
For the deeds will then prove that man and God alike  
Were commingled, and the world will provide no few signs.

## CHAPTER VIII

When the Son of God himself assumed the members of flesh,  
Clothed with the purple-bordered robe, he endured blows from palms;  
His face was spat upon; a crown woven with thorns  
Pierced his head; and he himself was fastened to the wood.

He was given a bitter draught, and gall was mixed with vinegar.  
[Note: [Col. 1088D] “He was given a *scriblita*,” etc. The author misuses *scriblita*—with its penultimate syllable unnaturally shortened—for the bitter draught offered to Christ by the Jews, although in Plautus and Cato it is instead a kind of food or cake. He also follows the Greek reading, according to which they read “vinegar mixed with gall,” whereas most Latin writers have “wine.” This variant arose from the similarity of the words ὄξος (*oxos*, “vinegar”) and οἶνος (*oinos*, “wine”). Rufinus expressed both in his exposition of the Creed: “wine mixed with myrrh, and vinegar.”]

His garment was divided and lots were cast for it;  
Each person retained whatever he seized for himself.

Amid deep gloom, as God breathed forth silently  
From the body of flesh, the day, terrified into darkness,  
Fled with the sun.  
[Col. 1088C] One day occurred twice: black night covered its middle.

The mountains were shaken from their foundations throughout the world;  
The earth was moved; the tombs of the righteous opened;  
And all things that knew him feared to behold him suffering.

A soldier pierced his lifeless side with a weapon, opening it;  
Blood flowed forth, and water followed no less.

They conceal these things by compact and refuse to confess them,  
Wishing to hide the wickedness they committed.

Can a spirit, then, wear a garment without a body?  
Can it receive punishment or sustain a wound by violence?  
Does it die and rise, and does blood flow from it?  
From what flesh, since you deny that he possessed any?  
Or did he merely pretend—if you consider it safe to say so?  
Though in your headlong course you say still more by remaining silent.

Was not the faith, then, manifest? Were not all things established  
[Col. 1088D] Before the day when, as he suffered, he celebrated the Passover  
And handed down to his disciples that memorable deed?

Taking bread and likewise the liquid of the vine,  
[Col. 1089A] He said: “This is my body and my blood, which is poured out here  
For you”; and he commanded that this always be done afterward.

Of whose creation do you think the bread and wine to be,  
Which he called his body and blood? What must therefore be confessed?  
Did he not prove through the created things that he was the Maker of the world  
And that he bore a body consisting of flesh and blood?

## CHAPTER IX

This is God, and this is also true man, who spoke the truth:  
The Beginning from the Father, Light begotten from Light,  
Spirit and Word, the power in the Father’s image.  
[Note: [Col. 1089D] “Christ beneath the image,” Obert.]

He was always with the Father, united in glory and eternity;  
For he alone ministers the words of the One who contains all things,  
Whom he embraces upon earth and through whom he created everything.

He is the Son of God himself, the dearly beloved minister.  
From the Father he has his lineage, his name, and finally his kingdom:  
[Col. 1089B] Lord from the Lord, a river from the everlasting fountain.

Appearing to the holy patriarchs from the beginning of the world,  
He is the one whom everyone who admits that he saw God  
Saw—with God as witness.

From on high he revealed the Father’s memorable promises;  
He led out the people and struck the wicked nation.  
He himself was the pillar of light and of the cloud’s cold shade.  
He dried the sea and commanded the peoples to pass through the waves,  
While the enemies were entangled and covered by surge and sea.

Through the deserts he made a road for those who followed his commands.  
From heaven he sent bread down to the people beneath the shadows.  
He broke open the rock and watered the thirsty with a flood.

He spoke the commandment of the Law, given by God, to Moses,  
Amid thunder, the sound of the trumpet, and a pillar of flame—  
A terrifying sight to the trembling hearts of men.

After the full completion of twice twenty years and months,  
[Col. 1089C] The Jordan was divided, a road opened, and the water stood still;  
And the tribes divided among themselves the land promised by the Father.

[Col. 1090A] He himself, the Word of the Father, spoke through the mouth of the prophets  
And foretold that he would come to earth and become man,  
Manifestly announcing Christ to the earth.

## CHAPTER X

Awaited as help, the sole hope of life,  
The cleanser of savage flesh and the banisher of death,  
At last he came at the command of the almighty Father  
And clothed himself in human limbs.

Adam, the virgin, the dragon, and the tree had been the cause of ruin,  
And the road by which reckless death had conquered us all.  
Along this same road the shepherd went, seeking his sheep:  
By an angel, a virgin, his own flesh, and the healing remedy of the wood.

From the source by which man was conquered, those who had been conquered  
Were passing on to perish.  
Hence, interposed as the one man for all the captives,  
He patiently endured the hostile penalty in his body.  
[Col. 1090B] Despoiling death through his own death, he became the cause of salvation.

Moreover, after he had suffered all things upon earth  
In his own holy body and paid what we owed,  
He went to the underworld. There the souls bound for sin,  
Shut up beneath the weight of the Law without protection,  
Had long entreated the promised, hoped-for, and delayed deliverance.  
He gave them over to the rest of the saints and brought them back with light.

For on the third day, rising victorious with his body  
By the immense power of the Father, the way of salvation having been made,  
And bearing both man and God within the created order,  
He ascended into heaven, bringing back the captive  
Firstfruits—a gift and beloved figure for the Lord.

He sat beside the Father, having regained the power of light  
And the glory with which he had been fortified while conquering the enemy,  
Joined to us in flesh and united with the Spirit.

The Father will send him into the world as Lord and Christ,  
As King and God, with judgment and kingship bestowed upon him.  
[Col. 1090C]

# BY AN UNCERTAIN AUTHOR: ON THE JUDGMENT OF THE LORD  
*(C, G, S)*

[Col. 1089]

## NOTICE

The following poems too are commonly transmitted under Tertullian’s name. We do not believe that so great a man ever had Muses so hostile to him. Nevertheless, poor as they are, we permit them to be appended here, because they cast some light upon Tertullian; and they appear to have been composed at Carthage not far removed from his age. Let even the lees of the Punic amphora’s antiquity have their admirers. —RIG.

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 1089D]

Who will adapt the rustic Muses to my song?  
Who will crown the springtime breezes with rosy flowers?  
Who will crown the heavy ears of the summer harvest when ripe?  
Who will grant the swollen grapes upon autumn’s vines?  
Or who will always praise the tranquil olives of winter?  
Who will open anew the waters at their very springs,  
And cut leafy flowers from the wandering meadow?

Straightway I shall sing the breezes of ethereal light.  
Now may I be permitted to approach the light-bearing Muses,  
To disclose secret waters from a river’s summit,  
And joyful forests set beneath another star.

I shall sing as well of the eternal flames;  
Whence the swollen sea surges with its immense wave;  
[Col. 1090D] What force moves the solid earth and makes it tremble;  
Whence golden light first shone upon the newborn world;  
Who could fashion man from fertile clay;  
Whence the race could increase in the empty age;  
What great desire the people have for living;  
What offspring was created for evil and what for dying;  
Whence come the lovely fragrance and blush of rosy garlands;  
What makes the vine seethe with joyful grapes;  
How granaries are stretched full with tender stalks;  
How the tree grows dark with olives amid the frost;  
Who grants varied strength of growth to shoots  
And shelters the mother of the young beneath gentle shadows.

It is good to know what all the wonders of creation are,  
So that through them all one may recognize the true Lord,  
[Col. 1091A] Who prepared light, the seas, heaven, and earth,  
And adorned the new world with varied stars;  
Who first commanded cattle and birds to come forth;  
Who granted that the waters of the sea should be filled with fish,  
And compels the fertile sands to teem with living beings.

Such songs I shall draw forth through the concord of the Muses;  
I shall reveal the saving waters from their springs  
And the joyful rain arriving with gentle clouds,  
Flowing deeply and freely into the souls of men.  
May our new-plowed fields receive it in full streams.

## CHAPTER II

Come now: if anyone should desire to touch the secure and living water,  
Swiftly to restore through its streams the life he has lost,  
To escape the penalties of eternal flame,  
And, though ignorant of God and unaware of the coming age,  
[Col. 1091B] To attain instead the rewards of the life to come,  
Let him remember this one God and pray to him alone:

He placed the entire world in the balance beneath its threshold;  
He remains eternal and will always endure forever,  
Holding all ages, alone before every age,  
Unbegotten God without end, alone possessing  
The seat on high, alone towering above the lofty heavens;  
Alone almighty, whom all created things obey.

He formed man according to his good pleasure for eternity,  
And appointed him shepherd of the livestock and lord of the wild beasts.  
By speech alone he was able to stretch out heaven;  
By a word he could suspend the solid earth;  
More swiftly than speech he divided the water of the sea.

With his hands he lovingly fashioned the form dear to him,  
And willed that his own beautiful countenance should dwell within it;  
[Col. 1091C] With breath he breathed the air of life into its face.

## CHAPTER III

Forgetful of God, man rashly committed the one deed  
Which, once warned, he was never again to touch—  
The thing by which he would know evil and likewise distinguish what was right.  
Therefore God forbade him to touch the illicit fruit.

How many gifts of the world did God permit to man!  
How sweet a pledge of his own love did he seal!  
He granted him authority to subdue the birds, the sea, and the land,  
And bestowed commandments useful for his beloved salvation.

He placed beneath man’s dominion the lands and the lives of flying creatures,  
The winged race, every race of cattle and wild beasts,  
The creatures of the sea, and the misshapen monsters that swim.

But because the first man transgressed the Law in his first offense,  
[Col. 1091D] Forthwith abandoned the Lord’s commandments,  
And was perishing through his spouse, who urged all those evils,  
The woman first committed the evil of sin.  
Transgressing the Law, she deceived her husband.

Eve, seduced by deceit, opened the gates to death  
And engendered destruction for herself and all her race.  
Hence heedless wickedness, hence death like seed for eternity,  
Was scattered abroad; thereafter the savage crime spread more widely,  
And harsher suffering tormented the corrupted world.

Through envy the cunning serpent enticed man in this very matter;  
Thenceforth the peoples increasingly summoned down the consequences  
Of the evils they committed,  
And by evil deeds generated the seeds of unspeakable things.

## CHAPTER IV

Therefore the Lord alone, to whom supreme power belongs,  
[Col. 1092A] Who holds the lofty pinnacles of highest heaven  
And dwells for all ages in the exalted light,  
Mindful of the present and knowing the future beforehand,  
Preserves forever the decree concerning each offspring’s deserts.

He governs all souls and then their sluggish bodies,  
Which rationally move man as he wanders through many places.  
As soon as the burdens of the limbs, once laid down, give way,  
The souls, light after their earthly bonds are dissolved, will depart  
And take their proper regions in different places.

But other souls have been decreed to be called back at once  
To the joyful breezes and preserved entirely for the Law’s rewards,  
Until their bodies grow vigorous again in reanimated limbs.  
There the wicked will be confined in hidden darkness.

Then men will begin to weigh the rewards of their former life,  
To reckon their crime and offenses,  
[Col. 1092B] And to be held for punishments far removed from death;  
Remembering God’s judgments, they will stand before the righteous  
And await punishment for their own crime and that of their companions.

Or else they will live utterly like nameless beasts  
And vainly pour forth weeping to a God who is now deaf to them.

## CHAPTER V

Can almighty God, beneath whose law all things are governed,  
Not restore life after death?  
Or does the Lord of creation appear unable to do anything?

If he could drive away darkness and extend the light,  
And suddenly compose the whole mass by a word,  
Preparing all things from nothing by his mighty voice,  
Why could he not fashion from some material the familiar form  
That once existed and which he had fashioned before,  
And command a figure made in his likeness to rise again?

[Col. 1092C] Since all things belong to God, the earth will surely restore them all.  
At his command it will unravel whatever it once wove together.

If the flame of a funeral pyre happened to consume someone buried there,  
Or the sea scattered another through its blind waters;  
If fish satisfied their bellies with someone’s flesh,  
Or wild beasts fixed cruel death upon his limbs;  
If his plundered body lay as blood for the birds—  
These final things will not rob the great Lord of what is his.

The living must appear before God from death,  
And human beings must stand there after resuming their own forms.

Thus dry seeds are returned to empty lands  
And, buried deep within the furrows, rot in death.  
Do they not then come alive, their crowns of grain restored?  
Do the hardy grains not once more grow golden with life,  
And new harvests arise with manifold increase?

[Col. 1092D] All the stars fall and, born again, shine once more.  
Day dies with its light in the dense night,  
And soon night too perishes when things are uncovered again by light.  
Day after day rises anew from its own stars.

The sun too falls, though bright with splendor-bearing light;  
Light perishes as evening approaches the shadowed world.

The phoenix lives again, renewed from its own soot,  
And soon the bird—marvelous to say—rises again after its own funeral pyre.

In its season the tree, stripped of its leaves, puts forth shoots,  
And its branches bend again beneath the budding fruit.

## CHAPTER VI

Therefore, at the voice of God, as the world trembles throughout,  
And the powers of the lofty air are shaken to their depths,  
There will come an unfamiliar crash and the loudest rumblings of heaven  
[Col. 1093A] As God approaches, the judge of the entire world.

At once innumerable ministers hasten together  
And surround God with heavenly majesty.  
Angelic hosts will descend from heaven to earth—  
All the multitude of God, all endowed with divine power,  
All spirits with the countenance and form of strength.

They possess a fiery vigor and flame-bright bodies;  
The divine power of heaven flashes forth. At this the entire world rumbles;  
At this the whole earth, however vast, trembles and bellows,  
Laboring to give birth to the human beings whom it will learn  
That it has been commanded to restore.

All things marvel; the clouds are thrown into turmoil,  
And the shaken stars fear the sudden power from on high.  
Thus God comes amid the thunderous voice of the Mighty One.

At once, throughout every realm, as the tombs are broken open,  
All the earth pours forth bones from its broad chasms,  
[Col. 1093B] And the opened sand disgorges living peoples.

Limbs adhere to [*comis*; reading uncertain]; bones are joined to marrow;  
Interwoven sinews control the breathing bodies;  
At the same time the veins are stirred by the blood infused into them.  
The souls are released from their hollow prisons and restored to the opened bodies;  
Each rising organ returns to its proper part.

O wondrous faith! From this every age springs forth;  
[Note: [Col. 1093D] “From this every age springs forth,” etc. What the author adds points to the same thing: “children,” and:

> The world echoes, groaning with the wailing of infants.

These statements, however, must be understood to mean that children and infants too will rise again, yet not at the same age, but—as the Apostle writes in Ephesians 2—“to the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ.” For each person, as Blessed Augustine says in book XXII of *The City of God*, chapter 15, “will receive the measure of the body which he either possessed in youth, even if he died as an old man, or would have possessed had he not died earlier.” That age will be the one Christ reached, just short of thirty years.]
