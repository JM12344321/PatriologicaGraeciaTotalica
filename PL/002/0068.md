The Platonists confuse these matters by subtlety rather than truth. Every body, they say, must be either animate [Col. 0654A] or inanimate. If it is inanimate, it will be moved from without; but if animate, from within. The soul, however, will be moved neither from within, since it is not inanimate, nor from without, since it is itself rather what moves the body. Accordingly, it does not appear to be a body, since it is not moved from some quarter according to the form proper to corporeal things. [Note: [Col. 0653D] “According to the form proper to corporeal things.” That is, according to the law or nature of corporeal [Col. 0654D] things. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0654D] “From some quarter.” That is, under some directing agency. RIG.]

In reply to this, we shall first express our astonishment at the incongruity of a definition that appeals to categories which do not apply to the soul. [Note: [Col. 0654D] “For the soul cannot,” etc. Septimius posits a middle term between contradictory propositions, though this is hardly consistent with Aristotelian doctrine. For the Platonists do not seem to me to argue badly on this point: “Every body,” etc. RIG.] For the soul cannot be called either an animate or an inanimate body, since it is itself that makes the body animate if it is present, or inanimate if it is absent from it. Therefore, that which produces a condition cannot itself be that condition, so as to be called animate or inanimate. For “soul” is the name of its own substance. But if that which is soul does not admit of being called an animate or inanimate body, how [Col. 0654B] will it be brought under the form of animate and inanimate bodies?

Next, if it is characteristic of a body to be moved from without by something else—and we showed above that the soul is moved by another when it prophesies and [Note: [Col. 0653C] “The soul is moved by another, when it prophesies, when [Col. 0653D] it raves”—assuredly from without by another, etc. JUN.] when it raves, assuredly from without, since it is moved by another—I may rightly recognize as a body that which, according to the proposed example, is moved from without by another. Indeed, if being moved by another is characteristic of a body, how much more is moving another? The soul, however, moves the body, and its exertions become visible externally. For it is from the soul that the feet are impelled to walk, the hands to touch, the eyes to see, and the tongue to speak, as though by the motion of a figurine whose surface is actuated from within. [Note: [Col. 0654D] “By the motion of a figurine.” What would follow if we read *singulari motu*, “by an individual motion,” as though by a private motion? But if we retain *sigillario*, we shall understand an inward or automatic motion, produced by a certain artifice and organic power. It is well known that *sigillaria* were statuettes and small images of the gods; hence Arnobius, book VI: “Indeed, you consider the statuettes themselves to be gods.” LE PR.] Whence does an incorporeal soul possess this power? [Note: [Col. 0654C] *Visio* (“vision”): Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] How can an empty thing propel solid things?

But how, they ask, do the corporeal and [Col. 0654C] intellectual senses appear to be divided in the human being? They say that the qualities of corporeal things, such as earth [Note: [Col. 0654C] *Terra* (“earth”): Paris.] and fire, are reported by corporeal senses such as touch and sight; [Note: [Col. 0654D] *Tactui . . . visui* (“to touch . . . to sight”): Rhen. and others.] whereas incorporeal things, such as benevolence or malice, are apprehended by the intellectual senses. Accordingly, it is established that the soul is incorporeal, since its qualities are comprehended not by corporeal but by intellectual senses.

Certainly—if I do not overthrow this stage of the definition. For observe: I show that incorporeal things are subject to corporeal senses—sound to hearing, color to sight, odor to smell. Following their example, the soul too comes into contact with the body, lest you say that such things are reported through the corporeal [Col. 0655A] senses [Note: [Col. 0655C] *Censum* (“classification”): Paris.] precisely because they come into contact with corporeal things. Therefore, if it is established that incorporeal things also are apprehended by corporeal senses, why should not the soul, which is corporeal, be reported by incorporeal senses? The definition is certainly overthrown.

Among their more notable arguments will also be the claim that every body, in their judgment, is nourished by corporeal things, whereas the soul, being incorporeal, is nourished by incorporeal things, namely by the pursuits of wisdom. But this stage too will not stand, since [Note: [Col. 0655C] “Soranus, most learned in methodical medicine.” This was Soranus of Ephesus, as the Suda teaches, the son of Menander and Phoebe, *ἰατρεύσας ἐπὶ Τραϊανοῦ καὶ Ἀδριανοῦ βασιλέων*, “who practiced medicine under the emperors Trajan and Hadrian.” He composed many volumes, which the Suda cites; fragments also survive today, formerly published by Adrianus Turnebus. To these add the *Introduction to Medical Treatment*, transmitted with the minor works of Pliny, Oribasius, and Apuleius. It is established that there were two men named Soranus, the elder and the younger, and that both wrote on the ailments of women. Everyone admits that the fragments of Soranus the physician addressed to Cleopatra are spurious. Since these touch upon indecent subjects, they were expounded by Scioppius, a diligent investigator of sexual matters. [Col. 0655D] LE PR.] Soranus, the most learned authority on methodical medicine, replies that the soul is nourished by corporeal things as well; [Note: [Col. 0655D] “Indeed, when it is failing, it is very often sustained by food.” Horace:

> Let support at once be brought to the collapsing stomach.

RIG.] indeed, when it is failing, it is very often sustained by food. And why should it not be, since, when food is withdrawn, the soul wholly slips away from the body?

Thus Soranus himself, who commented most fully on the soul in four volumes and examined all the opinions of the philosophers, vindicates the corporeal substance [Col. 0655B] of the soul, even though he deprived it of immortality. For it is not given to everyone to believe what belongs to Christians. Therefore, just as Soranus himself demonstrates from facts that the soul is nourished by corporeal things, let the philosopher likewise show that it is fed by incorporeal things. Yet no one has ever poured the honeyed water of Plato’s eloquence into a person wavering at the soul’s departure, or [Note: [Col. 0655D] “Inserted crumbs from Aristotle’s minute discourse.” By “crumbs” and “minute discourse” he means *λεπτολογία* (*leptologia*), a subtle and concise style of writing such as Aristotle’s. And what will all those many souls of mountain-dwellers and barbarians do [Note: [Col. 0655D] “Of barbarians, mountain-dwellers, and boors.” Pithoeus approved this reading from a verse of Lucilius:

> The squalid, uncultivated mouths of boors and mountain-dwellers.

[Col. 0656C] But the manuscript of Agobard reads “of barbarians.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0655C] *Baronum* (“of boors”): Rig., Venice.], who lack the nourishment of wisdom, yet, though uneducated, abound in practical intelligence and continue to live without academies, Attic porticoes, or the prisons of Socrates—in short, though fasting from philosophy? [Note: [Col. 0655C] “From philosophy”: the same editions.] The nourishment supplied by studies benefits not the soul’s substance itself but its training, since it makes the soul not fatter [Col. 0656A] but more cultivated.

It is well, moreover, that the Stoics affirm even the arts to be corporeal. Thus the soul is corporeal on this ground also, if it is believed to be nourished by the arts. But the immense concentration of philosophy is often accustomed not even to notice what lies before its feet: [Note: [Col. 0656C] “Thus Thales into the well.” Laertius: *εἰς βόθρον*, “into a pit.” RIG.] thus Thales fell into the well. It is also accustomed, when it fails to understand its own opinions, to suspect a disorder of health: [Note: [Col. 0656C] “Thus Chrysippus to hellebore.” Lucian: *ἑαυτὸν ἐλλεβορίζει*, “he treats himself with hellebore.” RIG.] thus Chrysippus resorted to hellebore.

Something of this sort, I think, happened to philosophy when it denied that two bodies could occupy one place, having lost sight and recollection of pregnant women, who daily carry within the compass of a single womb not merely individual bodies, but sometimes two and three. [Note: [Col. 0655C] “Two bodies in one place”: Rig.] [Note: [Col. 0656C] *Alienata*: Jun.; *alienate*: Rhen.] [Note: [Col. 0656C] *Terrena* (“earthly”): Paris.] [Note: [Col. 0656C] *Praeferunt* (“carry before them”): Paris.] [Note: [Col. 0656C] “There is even found in the civil law a certain Greek woman who gave birth to five at once.” I do not think Septimius derived this from Julius Paulus, since the latter reports a numerically different delivery in book III, *Si pars hereditatis petatur*—namely, that in the Peloponnese a woman gave birth to quadruplets five times. Finally, Aristotle himself, in the book cited above, records that he had spoken elsewhere about the same matter, though that passage is not extant today. RIG.]

There is even found [Note: [Col. 0656D] “In the civil law.” In the books of the jurists, which even the ancient grammarians customarily cite in this fashion. The author of Question 115 says: “For it is written as an established rule in a certain little book of law that a woman once bore five children.” RIG.] in the civil law a certain Greek woman who gave birth to a quintuplet of children: the mother of all at once, the multiple parent of a single offspring, the numerous childbearer of a single womb, [Note: [Col. 0656D] “The numerous childbearer of a single womb, who, crowded with so many bodies—almost, I would say, with a populace.” Thus Manilius, *Astronomica* I, said of the triplet Horatii: “The birth was an entire battle line.”—For the manuscripts attest that this is how the passage should be read. RIG.] [Col. 0656B] who, crowded with so many bodies—almost, I would say, with a populace—was herself the sixth body. The whole creation [Note: [Col. 0656D] “The whole creation.” Everything established and created by God. RIG.] will testify that bodies destined to proceed from bodies are already there in that from which they proceed. That which is from another must necessarily be second; moreover, nothing is from another except while it is being begotten; but then there are two. [Note: [Col. 0656C] “But then it is absent”: Rig.]

## CHAPTER VII

As far as the philosophers are concerned, this is enough. As far as our own people are concerned, there is evidence in abundance, since for them the corporeality of the soul will shine forth in the Gospel itself. In the underworld, the soul of a certain man grieves; it is punished in flame and tormented in its tongue, and it begs for the comfort of dew from the finger of a happier soul.

Do you consider the fate of the rejoicing poor man [Note: [Col. 0656D] “Do you consider it an image?” A parable. RIG.] [Col. 0657A] and the sorrowing rich man to be an image? And why is the name of Lazarus given there, if the event is not real? [Note: [Col. 0657C] “And why the name of Lazarus?” The manuscript of Agobard reads “Eleazar.” Moreover, from the same argument based on the story of Lazarus, Irenaeus seems to share Septimius’s opinion concerning a certain corporeal condition of the soul, book II, chapters LXII and LXIII. Yet Septimius alone is castigated. I suppose this is because his more eloquent style made him conspicuous. RIG.—The error is wrongly attributed here to Irenaeus by which Tertullian imagines that souls retain a certain bodily outline, shadowy senses, and airy colors. Irenaeus intended only that certain distinctive marks and a certain order surviving the body remain in human minds, even when dwelling in heaven, by which human souls are distinguished from other spirits, namely angels. This is precisely what Irenaeus calls “the character of the body” and “the figure of the human being.” Dom Massuet treats these matters more fully at the cited passage and especially in his third dissertation, article XI. EDD.] But even if it must be believed to be an image, it will be testimony to the truth. For if the soul had no body, an image of the soul could not assume the image of a body; nor would Scripture falsely speak of corporeal members if they did not exist.

What, moreover, is that which is transferred to the underworld after its separation from the body? What is detained there? What is reserved for the Day of Judgment? To what did Christ too descend in dying? To the souls of the Patriarchs, I suppose. But why, if the soul is nothing beneath the earth? For it is nothing if it is not a body. [Note: [Col. 0657D] “For the soul is nothing if it is not a body.” Augustine observes: “Finally, Tertullian believed that the soul was a body for no other reason than that he could not conceive it as incorporeal; and therefore he feared that it would be nothing if it were not a body,” etc. Book X of *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, near the end. RIG.] For incorporeality is free from every kind of custody, immune from punishment and refreshment. That through which it is punished or refreshed will be a body. I shall give a fuller account of this in the proper place.

Therefore, if the soul receives in advance any torment or comfort [Col. 0657B] [Note: [Col. 0657C] *Praecepit* (“received beforehand”): Rig., Venice.] in the prison or lodging place of the underworld, in the fire or in Abraham’s bosom, the corporeality of the soul will have been proved. For incorporeality suffers nothing, since it possesses nothing through which [Note: [Col. 0657C] *Per* (“through”) omitted in Venice.] it can suffer; or, if it does possess such a thing, that thing will be a body. For insofar as everything corporeal is capable of suffering, so everything capable of suffering is corporeal.

## CHAPTER VIII

[Note: [Col. 0657D] “It would otherwise be rash and absurd to remove something from the category of corporeal things merely because it does not correspond to the other corporeal examples, wherever the particular differences among properties are found.” It is remarkable that after these words Augustine nevertheless becomes severely angry with our Septimius: “For indeed,” he says, “because he is acute, he is sometimes overcome by the truth when it becomes visible, contrary to his own opinion. For what truer statement could he have made than what he says in a certain passage: ‘Everything corporeal is capable of suffering’? He ought therefore to have changed the opinion according to which he had said a little earlier that even God is a body. For I do not think he was so foolish as to believe even the divine nature capable of suffering.” Septimius assuredly intended that both souls and God himself were bodies, but bodies of their own kind—that is, bodies possessing their own properties according to their particular distinction. By this he clearly indicated that he understood bodies of such a kind as would in no respect impair either the dignity of the soul or the divine majesty. Accordingly, the divine nature is not for that reason capable of suffering, although the nature of the soul is for that reason capable of suffering. For the soul possesses a body of its own kind through which it suffers; God too possesses a body of his own kind—that is, a divine body—and therefore one exempt from suffering. RIG.]

It would otherwise be rash and absurd to remove something from the category of corporeal things merely because it does not correspond to the other corporeal examples, wherever the particular differences among properties are found through which the magnificence of the Creator is marked by the diversity of those same works—so that they are as distinct as they are equal, as friendly as they are rival. Indeed, the philosophers themselves agree that all things consist of opposites, [Col. 0657C] [Note: [Col. 0658D] “According to the friendship and strife of Empedocles.” The verses of Empedocles on this matter read:

> At one time all things come together into one through Love,  
> At another, each is borne apart through the hatred of Strife.

RIG.] according to the Friendship and Strife of Empedocles.

[Col. 0658A] Thus, therefore, even if corporeal things stand in opposition to incorporeal things, corporeal things themselves also differ from one another, so that difference enlarges the number of their species without changing their genus: they are corporeal, yet numerous to the glory of God [Note: [Col. 0657C] “In the glory of God”: Rhen.], because varied; varied because diverse; some having one mode of perceiving qualities and others another; some having one kind of nourishment and others another; some invisible and others visible; some heavy and others light.

For they also say that the soul must be pronounced incorporeal for this reason: once it has departed, the bodies of the dead become heavier, although they ought to become lighter through being relieved of the weight of one body, if the soul is a body. “What then,” Soranus asks, “if they deny that the sea is a body because a ship becomes motionless and heavy when it is outside the sea?” How much more powerful, then, must the soul’s body be, since it carries about with the lightest mobility a body that afterward has such weight?

Furthermore, the [Note: [Col. 0658C] *Etsi invisibilis* (“even if invisible”): Rhen., Seml., Oberth., Jun. prefer “Even if the soul is invisible”; but “according to its condition.”] soul is invisible [Col. 0658B] both according to the condition of its own body, according to the property of its substance, and according to the nature of those to whom it has fallen to be invisible. The owl’s eyes do not know the sun; eagles endure it so fully that they judge the nobility of their offspring by the boldness of their pupils. Otherwise, they will not rear the chick that turns away from the sun’s ray, regarding it as degenerate. Thus something is invisible to one being but not to another, without being incorporeal on that account, merely because the power of sight [Note: [Col. 0658C] *Visus* (“sight”): Jun.] is not equal in all.

The sun is certainly a body, since it is fire; yet the eagle acknowledges it while the owl denies it, though the owl does not thereby prejudice the eagle’s judgment. Similarly, the body of the soul may perhaps be invisible to the flesh but visible to the spirit. Thus John, having been placed in the Spirit of God, sees the souls of the martyrs (Rev. VI, 20).

## CHAPTER IX

What now, if we also grant the soul a shape, [Note: [Col. 0659C] “What now, if we also grant the soul a shape?” Irenaeus, book II: “The figure of the human being”; and “The character of the body [Col. 0659D] to which it is adapted.” LAC.] despite Plato’s objections, as though the soul’s immortality would thereby be endangered? [Note: [Col. 0659D] “Despite Plato’s objections.” Clement of Alexandria cites Plato’s words in book V of the *Stromata*. [Note: [Col. 0659D] “From the certainty of grace.” By “certainty of grace” he means a grace whose existence is most certainly established. RIG.] For because we acknowledge spiritual gifts, we too, after John, have merited to attain prophecy. It appeared as a spirit, but not of an insubstantial and empty quality; rather, it even promised that it could be grasped: tender, luminous, of an airy color, [Note: [Col. 0660C] “Of an ethereal color”: Paris; Junius prefers “of an airy color.”] and in every respect of human form.” [Col. 0660B]

This was the vision; [Note: [Col. 0660C] Rhen. delete “This was the vision”; Pam.: “This is the vision.”] God is the witness, and the Apostle is a competent guarantor of gifts that would exist in the Church. Yet you would not believe [Note: [Col. 0660C] Pam. reads *nisi* (“unless”) for *nec si* (“not even if”).] even if the fact itself persuaded you of every particular! [Note: [Col. 0660C] Pam. inserts *ne* (“not”) before *credas* (“believe”).] If, then, the soul is a body, it is doubtless among those things that we professed above. Accordingly, the property of color too adheres to every body. What other color, therefore, would you assign to the soul than airy and luminous?

Not that its substance is itself air, although this was the opinion of Aenesidemus and Anaximenes, and, I believe, of Heraclitus according to some; nor that it is light, although this pleased Heraclides of Pontus. For the substance of thunderstones is not fiery merely because they flash with a gleaming redness; nor is the material of beryls watery merely because they shimmer with a filtered brilliance. [Col. 0661A] How many other things color associates that nature separates!

But since everything fine and translucent resembles air, such will be the soul, inasmuch as it is a breath and an offshoot of spirit. Indeed, because of the very fineness of its subtlety, belief in its corporeality is endangered.

Likewise, from your own present understanding conceive that no shape other than human is to be assigned to the human soul, and indeed the shape of the body that each soul bore about. For the present, let contemplation of the beginning lead us to this understanding. Consider again: when God breathed the breath of life into the human being’s face and the human being became a living soul, that whole breath was assuredly transmitted immediately through the face into the inner parts and diffused throughout every space of the body. At the same time, condensed by the divine breathing, it was impressed with every internal line of the body that it had filled, and, as it were, congealed in a mold.

Thus the corporeity [Col. 0661B] of the soul was solidified by condensation, and its shape was formed by impression. This will be the inner human being, another than the outer, yet doubly one. Thus the rich man in the underworld has a tongue, the poor man a finger, and Abraham a bosom. Through these outlines the souls of the martyrs beneath the altar are also understood.
