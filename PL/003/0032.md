**ARGUMENT.—**Caecilius, somewhat saddened by the rebuke in which Octavius had censured Minucius on Caecilius’ account, asks permission to dispute with Octavius about the truth of his own religion. Octavius agrees, together with his companion, [Col. 0240B] and Minucius sits between Caecilius and Octavius.

Accordingly, while all of us were absorbed in the pleasure of this spectacle, Caecilius paid no attention and did not [Col. 0241A] laugh at the contest; instead, silent, anxious, and withdrawn, he confessed by his expression that something—I knew not what—was troubling him. I said to him: “What is the matter? Why, Caecilius, do I not recognize that liveliness of yours? I miss even that cheerfulness in your eyes which you retain amid serious matters.”

Then he replied: “For some time now the speech of our Octavius has been sharply troubling and tormenting me. In assailing you, he rebuked you for negligence, so that indirectly he might accuse me more gravely of ignorance. I shall therefore go further: I have an entire controversy to pursue with Octavius. If you consent to my disputing with him as a man of his own sect, he will certainly now understand that it is easier to debate among companions than to join arguments in the manner of the wise. Only let us sit down upon those stone barriers thrown out to protect the baths and projecting into the deep, so that we may both rest after our walk and debate more attentively.”

At his word we sat down [Col. 0241B] in such a way that, of the three, they enclosed me in the middle by occupying either side. [Col. 0242A] This was not a matter of deference, rank, or honor—for friendship always receives or makes equals—but was so that, as an arbiter situated equally close to each, I might lend them my ears and, seated between them, keep the two disputants apart. Then Caecilius began as follows:

**CHAP. V.—“Although to you, my brother Marcus.”** The pagan Caecilius calls the Christian Minucius “brother,” since he was most closely joined to him by intimate friendship, as is indicated by Octavius’ words to Minucius concerning Caecilius in chap. 3: “A man who clings to your side at home and abroad.” RIGALTIUS.—“Concerning that which.” It must be *id, de quo scripserit* (“that concerning which he wrote”), Minucius. For so harsh an ellipsis does not befit an author so learned in Latin and so devoted to elegance. HEUMANN.—Yet such an ellipsis befits Cicero, *Letters*, book III, Ep. 6, §7: “So that your action may appear, to those who do not know on what terms we stand with one another, to be that of an estranged man …,” on which Cortius has more. It befits Lactantius 6.12.39: “But may you divert to better purposes what you would have spent upon superfluities.” Caesar, *Gallic War* I.1: “The third, who in their own language are called Celts.” LINDNER.

[Col. 0243B] **“Rejecting the one, approving [the other].”** Thus the manuscripts of R. read, apparently corruptly. Gronovius therefore rightly judges that τὸ *alterum*, the word “other,” should be inserted twice between the two verbs “rejecting” and “approving”; nearly all printed editors have followed him. EDD.

**“For the present.”** Gronovius appears plainly to condemn the expression *in praesentiarum* (“for the present”), not only in Minucius but also in Tacitus and Cornelius Nepos, as though scribes had corrupted it. But I consider that argument futile. *Inpraesentiarum* is thoroughly Latin and formed according to the analogy of the Latin language, arising from *in praesentia rerum* (“in the present state of affairs”), just as *illicet* comes from *ire licet*, and so forth. GRUNER, work cited, p. 212.

**“Of a most impartial judge.”** Heumann wonders that *judicii* (“judgment”) had not long since been substituted. I, for my part, wonder rather at that most learned man, who dared alter anything in so sound a passage. It plainly alludes to the representation of judges, who were depicted holding a balance in their hand. Arnobius praises the “balance of the just judge” in Psalm 139. [Col. 0243C] LINDNER.

**“Inclined.”** With equal facility Heumann substitutes *propensius* (“more inclined”). But the received reading can be defended just as readily. Certainly, the formula *propensus in alteram partem* (“inclined toward one side”) is a formula employed by Cicero himself in *To Atticus*, book VIII, Ep. 3: “The multitude, and especially every person of the lowest station, was inclined toward the other side.” As for Minucius’ joining the two words *propensus* (“inclined”) and *incumbas* (“lean”), to either of which the phrase “toward either side” may refer, this can seem strange and unusual only to someone who has not read Minucius in full, for he uses this mode of expression very frequently. Thus above, chap. 3: “The gentle wave, bathing us, stretched out”; and chap. 5: “Retracing the same road, we trod it again with our steps reversed.” In the same place: “The things suspended above us in heaven have been raised aloft.” Cortius noted similar prolix expressions on Cicero, book V, Ep. 7, n. 2, and likewise book XVI, Ep. 26, n. 1.

**“Lest not so much.”** As we once observed elsewhere, he imitates Cicero, *Paradoxes* 1.2: “I fear that this speech may appear to some of you to have been drawn from the disputations of Stoic philosophers, rather than from my own judgment.” WOPKENSIUS.

[Col. 0243D] **“If you … take your seat for me.”** From a certain Oxford edition Jacob Ouzelius restores: “If you … trust me” (*confidas*). I am indeed surprised at this, since, if that reading is accepted, no meaning arises except one that is exceedingly foolish and unsuitable. But that good man encountered difficulty in resolving the syntax because he did not recall that pronouns of this sort are not infrequently redundant (παρέλκειν) in the best authors, as very learned men have demonstrated with many examples: Dionysius Lambinus on Lucretius, book III, p. 292, and Peter Burman on Phaedrus, book I, fable 22.3. Observe the same thing also in Greek writers. Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, chap. 9: “And let no one, I pray, think it ridiculous that God should have a Son” (Καὶ μή μοι γελοῖόν τις νομίσῃ τὸν υἱὸν εἶναι τῷ θεῷ). So too Tatian in the *Address to the Greeks*, and others. DAVIS. VECHNERUS, *Hellenolexia*, book I, chap. 6, §5. I add that the Hebrews are not without this kind of pleonasm either—for example, Genesis 12:1 and 22:5—and indeed neither are we Germans. HEUMANN.

**“Some new man.”** The common editions, contrary to the manuscript’s authority, read *aliquis*, without any compelling reason. Cicero, *Academic Questions*, book IV, chap. 7: “Let some god inquire.” The same author, *On Duties*, book IV, chap. 7: “Unless some accident or occupation had destroyed his plan.” Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 19: “A certain Egyptian Manetho.” Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book I, chap. 13: “Even if some sudden accident”—so the manuscript—“promised that the matter could be accomplished more advantageously.” The same author, book XI, chap. 13: “And let some one of you, I pray, tell me”; and [Col. 0244B] shortly afterward: “Lest some modulated sound of fluent eloquence readily dazzle you.” See also G. J. Vossius, *Annals*, book IV, chap. 8. DAVIS.—The recently mentioned Lyon edition states that it read in the manuscript codex *novus aliqui eo loci*, saying in a note: “Thus the manuscript as edited by Heraldus, not *aliqui*, as Wouw., Gronov., and Davis maintain.” Yet, with the Lyon editor’s leave, it must be asserted that this reading belongs neither to the Royal manuscript nor to Heraldus. EDD.

**“Doubtful, uncertain.”** He joins the same words as Cicero does in *On the Orator*, book I, chap. 20. GRONOVIUS.

**“And that everything is more probable.”** Thus it stands in the manuscript; the printed editions omit “everything.” LINDNER.

**“All the more.”** The manuscript has *quo magis* (“all the more”). But the Roman edition’s reading, which, according to WOW., has *minus* (“less”), is preferable. LINDNER.

**“Of thoroughly investigating … to succumb.”** Thus the manuscript unquestionably reads. Yet this did not prevent Fulvius Ursinus from restoring: “rather of investigating the truth.” Jacob Ouzelius and Christopher Cellarius, however, [Col. 0244C] printed: “of thoroughly investigating the truth, rashly rather to succumb to any opinion.” Entirely wrongly, since the word “rather” is often understood without being expressed. Cornelius Nepos, *Datames* 8.1: “He determined to engage, rather than retreat with so great a force.” Livy 23.43.13: “That it was in their own interest rather than Hannibal’s.” Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* II.7: “They say that they should be gradually avoided and abandoned, rather than rejected outright.” Among the Greeks, however, the particle μᾶλλον (“rather”) is understood. Porphyry, *On Abstinence*, book II, §15: “Experience indeed testifies that the gods rejoice in this rather than in what is extravagantly costly” (Καὶ μαρτυρεῖ γε ἡ πεῖρα, ὅτι χαίρουσι τούτῳ οἱ θεοὶ ἢ τῷ πολυδαπάνῳ). Tatian, *Address to the Greeks*, chap. 61: “One ought to believe him who has precedence in age rather than Greeks who have drawn from the spring” (Χρὴ τῷ πρεσβεύοντι κατὰ τὴν ἡλικίαν πιστεύειν, ἤπερ ἀπὸ τῆς πηγῆς ἀρυσαμένοις Ἕλλησιν). Eusebius, however, *Preparation for the Gospel* X.2, reads there: μᾶλλον ἤπερ, “rather than.” See also Justin Martyr, *Apology* I, chap. 18. DAVIS.—Add those cited by J. H. Maius the Younger, *Sacred Observations*, book I, p. 26; Grabe on Irenaeus, book IV, chap. 23, fol. 309, note s, and likewise fol. 325, note B; Gronovius on book V, 21; Sanctius and Perizonius on *Minerva* IV.7, p. 692. LINDNER.

**“All must be indignant and must feel grief.”** This reading of the ancient exemplar ought not to have been disturbed. [Col. 0244D] It is sound, and is confirmed by the disputation of Octavius, who repeats Caecilius’ words in chap. XVI: “And since my brother burst out that he took it ill, was vexed, indignant, and grieved,” etc. RIGALTIUS.

All printed editions have “one must feel grief, and must feel grief,” wrongly claiming the manuscript codex as their authority. Similarly, a little below the same codex reads *audere* (“to dare”), which Heraldus and many after him accepted; but the Lyon editor again wrongly attributes *audire* (“to hear”) to the codex and, without consulting it, adds the following note to the word *audere*: “Thus Heraldus correctly printed it in place of the manuscript’s *audire*.” EDD.

**“That certain men should dare, and these untrained in learning.”** Thus we have printed, following the authority of the Royal manuscript. Previously the reading was “to hear certain men.” In the same manuscript, *rude* is wrongly written for *rudes*. We had observed, on the following passage of Arnobius, book III, that Christians were regarded as untrained in learning: “Is this that disdain of yours, this arrogant wisdom, by which you spurn us as uncultivated?” etc. HERALDUS.

**“And these.”** I do not understand how learned men could have altered this: Sabaeus to *horum* (“of these”), and another to *hos* (“these men”). It is precisely the construction in Hebrews 11:12: “From one man were born—and he as good as dead” (Ἀφ’ ἑνὸς ἐγεννήθησαν, ΚΑΙ ΤΑΥΤΑ νενεκρωμένου), where the Old Translator renders it correctly, though hesitantly, “and this one as good as dead,” but Beza corruptly, “and he now being dead.” For that is not what stands in the Greek. Achilles Tatius VII, p. 427: “And will you dare to die in this way on the most shameful charges, believed to be a murderer—and of Leucippe, at that?” (Καὶ τολμήσεις οὕτως ἐπὶ τοῖς αἰσχίστοις ἀποθανεῖν, νομιζόμενος φονεὺς, καὶ ταῦτα Λευκίππης). GRONOVIUS.

**“Unacquainted even with the meanest arts.”** This passage must be corrected by changing the punctuation. It had certainly been written: “strangers to literature, unacquainted even with the meanest arts.” He thus says that Christians are strangers and unacquainted with literature; concerning this reproach we made several observations on Arnobius. He added “even with the meanest arts.” Christians, therefore, were not only regarded by them as strangers to and unacquainted with literature, but also as enslaved to mean crafts; and even the teachers of the Church themselves were considered wool-workers, cobblers, and fullers—ἐριουργοί, σκυτοτόμοι καὶ κναφεῖς—as Origen observes in book III [Col. 0245C] *Against Celsus*. In Minucius, moreover, several words are found signifying one and the same thing, except that those which follow are clearer and serve, as it were, in place of an interpretation. One might therefore not unreasonably suspect them to be glosses, were this not almost constant throughout the whole work. Thus earlier, on chap. 3, we observed *emicaret, emergeret*: “gleamed forth, emerged.” HERALDUS.

**“Concerning the highest reality and its majesty.”** Heumann reads “the majesty of the highest reality,” that is, God. But, to conceal nothing of my judgment, the common reading pleases me more. I think that the expression in the common editions possesses a stronger and more vigorous meaning than Heumann’s. Minucius elegantly calls nature itself *summa rerum*, “the highest reality”; Cicero does the same in *On the Orator*, book II, chap. 31. “Majesty,” however, is that divine power itself by which all things are most wisely governed and through which the highest reality is administered. See the passages I collected in *Leipzig Miscellanies*, vol. I, part III, p. 532, and Graevius on Cicero, *For Milo*, chap. 30. MENCKENIUS.

[Col. 0245D] **“Throughout all those ages.”** Terence, *The Woman from Andros* I.1.28: “What nearly all young men do.” Likewise *The Eunuch* I.2.5: “Now you will grow warm more than enough.” The Greeks speak similarly: πλείονες δὴ πάντες, “very nearly all,” and πάμπολλα, “very many things.” Pareus in his Plautine lexicon and Pricaeus on Apuleius, p. 346, observe that the Latins also sometimes said *universos omnes*, “all without exception.” Wopkensius’ punctuation also pleases me: “Concerning which so many, throughout all ages, of very many schools,” that is, “so many of very many schools.” I prefer it to the emendation he formerly proposed, “through whole, through all ages,” with asyndeton. Heumann’s conjecture is more licenseful—“after the inquiry of so many schools of every age”—and must therefore be rejected. LINDNER.

**“Philosophy itself,”** that is, philosophers themselves: the abstract for the concrete. See Cortius on Cicero, book VIII, Ep. 4.5. LINDNER.

**“From investigation of the divine,”** that is, of God. Heumann does not hesitate to write: “From the investigation of divine things.” LINDNER.

**“The things suspended above us in heaven have been raised aloft.”** This entire passage has been printed according to the exemplar. Those who order *sublimata* (“elevated”) to be written in place of *sublata* (“raised aloft”) labor in vain. Things are “raised” when they are carried *sus* or *sursum*, that is, upward and on high. Thus *sublata sunt* means τὰ ὑψωθέντα, “things raised on high.” More refined writers used *sublata* for *suslata*. Here “raised aloft” is opposed to “submerged,” just as “suspended” is opposed to “deep beneath.” Minucius therefore rightly says that it is not permitted to scrutinize the things suspended above us in heaven, and that it is not religiously lawful to violate those that lie deep beneath the earth—as though those who dare penetrate its entrails inflicted a sexual violation upon the earth. Pliny has some most elegant remarks to this effect in book II, chap. 63, and in the preface to book XXXIII. RIGALTIUS.—**“Have been raised aloft.”** *Sustollere* is used of machines by which something is raised on high. Lucretius IV.904: “A machine raises it with a light effort”; Avienius likewise uses it of the stars in his *Aratus*, verse 631: “Where the stars then raise themselves.” Its opposite is “to submerge.” LINDNER.

**“Or it is granted to know.”** These words are read thus in the Royal manuscript: “That to scrutinize is permitted, or to *struprari* religiously lawful.” Hence Heraldus: “or to scrutinize is permitted, or to *struprari*”—through a printer’s error, [Col. 0246C] I believe, for *stuprari*, “to violate”—“is religiously lawful.” Joseph Scaliger thought it might be read *ruspare* or *ruspari*, which seems to approach the manuscript most closely. Cellarius reads “to inquire … religiously lawful”; Heumann, “to learn is permitted, or to scrutinize is religiously lawful.” That *stuprare*, “to violate,” can be explained as “to corrupt,” or to involve in error by empty and fearful opinions, as our author says, I do not doubt at all. Cicero himself, *To Atticus* 1.18, speaks of *stupratum judicium*, “a violated,” that is, corrupted judgment. LINDNER.

**“Let seeds be the origin of all things, compacted as nature coalesced within itself.”** Thus we printed the passage, following the authority of the Royal manuscript. Indeed, Epicurean teaching, from which these statements were drawn, demanded this reading, *principio* (“as the origin”), not *principia* (“first principles”), which was the common reading. HERALDUS.

**“What god is the author here?”** Heumann suspects that *his* (“of these”) should be read for *hic* (“here”). But this interchange is frequent, even in Cicero, book II, Ep. 5: “How these affairs stand,” that is, “how the affairs stand here.” LINDNER.

**“Its own matter.”** Thus the Royal manuscript, according to Heraldus. WOW. is mistaken in saying that it has “pure matter” and in judging that the text should read [Col. 0246D] “the lightest matter.” LINDNER.

**“Though it founded the earth by its weight.”** Thus we presented the text, scrupulously following the reading of the sole codex and the example of the most learned Heraldus. Although he made an excellent choice when he restored Sabaeus’ first reading, Rigaltius immediately did not hesitate to alter it, writing: “though weights founded the earth”—entirely unsuccessfully. GRONOVIUS.—Barthius thought it should be rewritten: “founded itself by its weight.” Cellarius, with others: “though its weight founded it.” Heumann: “though its own weight founded the earth.” LINDNER.

**“And though the sea flowed in from liquid.”** This is certainly not Latin, and Rigaltius’ conjectural restoration, “and though liquid—or *eliquor*—flowed into the sea,” seems to depart too far from the exemplar. I think we should restore: “though the sea flowed together from fluid.” Novatian, *On the Trinity*, chap. 5: “Let us believe in God the Father … who suspended heaven at a lofty height, made the earth firm in its low-lying mass, and spread out the seas in unconstrained fluid.” That passage is a twin to this one in Minucius. DAVIS.—Gronovius, however, retains the manuscript codex’s reading with Heraldus. GALLAND.—The sense is: “Though its own matter flowed from liquid into the sea.” Hence Rigaltius introduces this reading: “and though *eliquor* flowed into the sea,” [Col. 0247B] taking the last word as a substantive noun. Others read: “liquid flowed in.” Heumann: “it flowed together from liquid”; Menckenius approves this emendation on the basis of Tibullus, book IV.20:

> Just as the sea flowed together into the curving globe.

Menckenius, defending the manuscript reading, teaches that the verb *influere* (“flow in” or “swell”) properly applies to the sea as it rises and, as it were, mounts on high, and that it signifies the same as the German *anflüssen, anwachsen*, “to swell, increase.” LINDNER.

**“What superstition is there?”** Heumann reads: “and it is superstition.”

**“And is nourished.”** The Roman edition and WOW. read “and is nourished.” The manuscript has “is raised up.” Neither Cuper’s correction nor his interpretation—the former presented by Clericus in *Bibliothèque choisie*, vol. XXIV, p. 128, the latter in vol. XXVI, p. 459—deserves assent. HEUMANN.—After so many conjectures by the greatest scholars, I shall offer my own, such as it is, which can be reconciled with the manuscript very well: “Man and every animal that is born is given breath and taken away, as the elements,” etc. One that “is given breath” receives life; one “is taken away” is deprived of life. “Man and every animal” is the subject; “is given breath and taken away” is the predicate. Gronovius punctuates interrogatively [Col. 0247C] as follows: “Is it a voluntary combination of the elements?” LINDNER.—Surely this is no surprising example of commentators voluntarily entangling themselves in perplexities! In the codex it is read in the clearest possible light as “and is nourished,” not at all “is raised up.” Thus this heap of itching scholars’ conjectures collapses utterly; yet the Lyon editor stumbled into it again when, after writing “and is nourished,” etc., he added the following—which it is irksome even to repeat: “Thus the Roman edition, but the manuscript has ‘is raised up.’ Perhaps one should read: ‘and is taken away,’ that is, it receives and loses life.” P. 18. EDD.

**“As the elements are voluntarily combined … so they flow back into their source.”** The distinguished Gruner, work cited, p. 212, restored this passage—which learned men had disturbed in various ways—to its integrity solely by punctuation of this kind. This, he says, is unquestionably correct: for τὸ *ut*, the word *ut*, is placed for *uti* or *quemadmodum*, “as” or “in the manner in which,” and the following word *ita*, “so,” answers to it.

**“A voluntary combination.”** Heumann says that the true reading is “a fortuitous combination.” But I do not know whether the common reading may not be retained with equal right, especially since *casus voluntarius* (“a voluntary occurrence”; Cicero, *On Ends* 65) is the same as one that is [Col. 0247D] fortuitous. LINDNER.

**“Is dissolved.”** It should undoubtedly read “is resolved.” HEUMANN.—Yet simple verbs are often used in place of compounds. LINDNER.

**“All things return upon themselves.”** Heumann orders the words to be transposed: “return all things.”

**“With no judge.”** Because Heumann did not grasp the force of the word, he reads “with no directing auspice.” Menckenius likewise admits that he does not know what “judge” means here. What “judge” signifies in this passage will be demonstrated by Ovid, *Metamorphoses* I.19:

> God and a better nature settled this dispute.

LINDNER.

**“When these have been compacted.”** There was no reason for Heumann to rewrite it: “and when these have been compacted.” LINDNER.

**“Falling.”** Thus it stands in the manuscript from the Roman edition, the same Heumann testifies. Sabaeus and Balduinus, however, read “tottering.” LINDNER.

**“Flash brilliantly.”** I am not at all moved to read *promicare* instead, with the learned Heusinger. For *praemicare* itself has a very suitable meaning, well adapted to Minucius’ thought, since he wished by this word to signify the supreme and marvelous brilliance of thunderbolts. *Praemicare* means “to flash intensely,” since [Col. 0248B] the prefix *prae-* in compounds very frequently intensifies the meaning of the simple verb. It is an Apuleian word; that author writes in *Metamorphoses*, book V: “And an elegant lamp, filled with oil and brilliantly flashing with a clear light.” And in book X: “For also a helmet brilliantly flashing with splendor.” Book XI: “I see the orb filled with the exceedingly bright radiance of the brilliantly flashing moon.” And in the same book: “The first of them held out a brilliantly flashing lamp with a clear light.” MENCKENIUS.

**“So extensively.”** The distinguished Heumann should not have altered *adeo* (“so”) to *atque* or *ac ea* (“and those”). For this is a familiar Latin form of expression in which *adeo* is placed at the beginning of a clause, while the *ut* (“that”) which ought to follow and to be construed with what preceded is omitted. In full it would read: “So extensively do they fall … that it is readily apparent from this that the author of these things is not a god.” Here is a similar passage in Lactantius III.9.12: “If someone invited you to dinner and you were very well received there, would you seem sane if you valued the pleasure itself more highly than the author of the pleasure? So do philosophers relate everything to the body and absolutely nothing to the mind”; supply: “that it is readily apparent from this that they [Col. 0248C] value the pleasure more highly than its author.” Compare the same author II.1.12; II.6.6; V.1.17. LINDNER.

**“They crash into mountains.”** The Roman edition has “they demolish,” according to W. “They crash into,” which stands in the manuscript, is correct; for *irruere* means to rush or fall against something. Claudian, *Consulship of Mallius Theodorus*, verse 195, in the best manuscripts and editions:

> Undaunted, he will rush into the flames.

Prudentius, *Apotheosis*, verse 414:

> Behold, the savage legion rushed into the Gadarene swine.

Ammianus XVII.28: “The Parthiscus, rushing into these lands along winding courses, mingles with the Danube.” Avienius, *Description of the World*, verse 594, concerning the Achelous:

> It rushes into the surface of the Adriatic Sea.

These passages, assembled by Cellarius at Gronovius’ suggestion, were taken from Nicolaus Heinsius’ notes on Claudian’s *Consulship of Mallius*, verse 194. LINDNER.

**“They strike guilty men, and often also”—**that is, “often even.” [Col. 0248D] Caesar, *Civil War* I.9: “So that these things may be done more easily, may be settled on definite terms, and may be confirmed by an oath.” Lactantius 1.18.13: “For the very same gate stood open to Hercules also.” Others may judge by what right Heumann believes that Minucius wrote: “They strike not only guilty men but often religious men.” I add that the manuscript has “and often.” LINDNER.—Lest you trust his overly confident assertion: the manuscript in fact reads “often also.” EDD.

**“Or examination,”** that is, judgment. Heumann could have spared himself the labor by which he substitutes “discrimination.” LINDNER.

**“The onset of all things,”** that is, by periphrasis, all things; for the distinguished Ernesti shows in his *Key to Cicero* that the ancients used this word in periphrases. Wopkensius takes it to mean “the mass.” LINDNER.

**“To befall.”** Because Heumann thinks a verb is missing upon which the accusatives joined to infinitives might depend, he writes *constat evenire*, “it is established that it happens.” But someone might more suitably say that these constructions depend upon the verb “shall I speak,” provided the words are punctuated in the manner Gronovius showed us. LINDNER.

[Col. 0249B] **“When the fury rages.”** Heumann says there is no doubt that our author’s true reading is: “when the heat of war rages.” Yet I do not see why that most learned man is more offended by this impersonal passive, formed from a neuter active, than by such expressions as “there is going” and “there is standing,” especially since, besides “it has been raged,” there occurs in Velleius, book II, chap. 74: “The soldiers’ anger raged more fiercely against the Perusians than their commander’s intention did.” Curtius, book VIII, chap. 19.6: “The fury raged even against the houses.”

**“But it is also honored.”** This is the exemplar’s reading. Others have “but it is raised up”; Davisius, “but it is preferred”; Wopkensius deletes “but.” In my view absolutely nothing should be altered. Those who judged that “is honored” must be changed do not appear to have perceived the force of the word. For if wickedness is placed on a level with better men, it is not therefore immediately honored as well. Wickedness is already placed on a level with better men if it is permitted to be wicked with impunity. But it is honored as well if wicked men are placed in such a condition of fortune that others are compelled to venerate them as though they deserved it and to hold them in great honor. This is what the word *colere* means. Cicero several times joins *colere* (“honor”) and *observare* (“respect”), [Col. 0249C] as in *Letters to Friends* XIII.78: “He honors and respects me above all others.” And *On Duties* I.41: “Those whose lives have been proved honorable in worthy and important affairs … we ought to respect and honor as persons invested with some distinction and authority.” GRUNER, work cited.—By a rather harsh ellipsis, “in preference to them,” namely, “to better men,” would have to be understood. Therefore Davis’ conjecture “but it is preferred” should not be rejected—unless perhaps, with a lighter alteration, we prefer: “In peace also, wickedness is equated with better men to such a degree and is honored, that among the majority…”; or perhaps: “In peace also, wickedness is not only equated with better men, but is honored to such a degree that among the majority…”—that is, so that more evil people than good are prosperous. WOPKENSIUS.—Pliny VII.30 also has *sed et*: “But let us also review our own glory.” LINDNER.

**“Phalaris and Dionysius.”** He derives this from Cicero, who treated these matters at greater length in *On the Nature of the Gods*, book III, chaps. 32–33. DAVIS.

**“Now the wine-heavy vintage.”** Very near to *temetum*, “wine,” concerning which shortly afterward: “let wine be made”; hence also *temulenti*, “drunken” or “wine-soaked.” RIGALTIUS.

**“Now the crop is white.”** Heumann rewrites “now the white crop,” [Col. 0249D] but wrongly. For our author indifferently sometimes places adjectives before their nouns and sometimes after them: “Since fortune is either certain or nature uncertain”; chap. 7: “they provide caution against dangers, a remedy for diseases”; chap. 31: “he did not provide testimony as an affirmer, but sprinkled abuse as an orator.” WOW. notes that the manuscript has “golden,” but it does not; Herm. and Rig. testify to this. LINDNER.

**“So either.”** This reading is not Meursius’ emendation of the common *adeo ut* (“so that”), but the manuscript reading itself. LINDNER.]

**ARGUMENT.—**Caecilius begins his disputation. He first argues that everything in human affairs is doubtful and uncertain, and therefore that it is deplorable that Christians—generally uncultivated and illiterate—dare to determine anything with certainty concerning the highest reality and divine majesty. From this he argues that no providence governs the world and concludes that it is better to adhere to the religions handed down by tradition.

[Col. 0242B] Although to you, my brother Marcus, the point chiefly under investigation [Col. 0243A] is not doubtful, since you have carefully engaged in both ways of life, rejecting the one and approving the other, nevertheless for the present you must so frame your mind that you hold the balance of a most impartial judge and do not lean with an inclination toward either side, lest your verdict appear not so much to have arisen from our arguments as to have been produced from your own convictions.

Accordingly, if you take your seat for me as though you were some newcomer and ignorant of either side, it will be no difficult matter to reveal that everything in human affairs is doubtful, uncertain, and unresolved, and that everything is more probable than true. All the more astonishing, therefore, is it that some, out of weariness with thoroughly investigating the truth, should rashly succumb to any opinion rather than persevere in their inquiry with unyielding diligence.

Everyone must therefore be indignant and feel grief that certain men should dare—and these untrained in learning, [Col. 0245A] strangers to literature, unacquainted even with the meanest arts—to determine anything with certainty concerning the highest reality and its majesty, a question upon which, throughout all those ages and among very many schools, philosophy itself continues deliberating to this very day. And rightly so: human mediocrity is so far removed from the investigation of the divine that, concerning neither the things suspended above us in heaven and raised aloft nor those sunk in the depths below the earth, is it granted to us to know them, permitted to scrutinize them, or religiously lawful to violate them. We may justly regard ourselves as sufficiently fortunate and sufficiently wise if, in accordance with that ancient oracle of the Sage, [Col. 0245B] we know our own selves more intimately.

But since, by indulging in an insane and foolish labor, [Col. 0246A] we wander beyond the limits of our low estate and, though cast down upon the earth, transcend heaven itself and the very stars through our audacious desire, let us at least not entangle this error in empty and fearful opinions.

Let seeds be the origin of all things, compacted as nature coalesced within itself: what god is the author here? Let the members of the whole world have come together, been arranged, and taken form through fortuitous collisions: what god is their architect? Granted that fire kindled the stars, that heaven was suspended by its own matter, that the earth was founded [Col. 0246B] by its weight, and that the sea flowed in from liquid: whence comes this [Col. 0247A] religion, whence this fear? What superstition is there?

Man, and every animal that is born, receives breath and nourishment. Just as it is a voluntary combination of the elements, into which man and every animal is in turn divided, dissolved, and dispersed, so those elements flow back into their source and all things return upon themselves, with no craftsman, judge, or author.

Thus, when the seeds of fire are gathered together, ever-new suns shine; thus, when vapors are exhaled from the earth, mists continually grow; when these are compacted and massed together, clouds rise higher; when these same clouds descend, rains flow, winds blow, and hailstorms rattle; or when storm clouds [Col. 0248A] collide, thunder bellows, lightning flashes red, and thunderbolts flash brilliantly. So extensively do they fall: they crash into mountains and strike against trees; without discrimination they touch sacred and profane places; they strike guilty men, and often religious men also.

(III) Why should I speak of the variable and uncertain storms, by which, without order or examination, the course of all events is tossed about? In shipwrecks, the fates of the good and evil are mingled and their deserts confused; in fires, the same destruction befalls the innocent and the guilty; when a pestilential blight infects a region of the sky, all perish without distinction; and when the fury of war rages, [Col. 0249A] it is rather the better men who fall.

Even in peace, wickedness is not only equated with better men but is also honored, so that, in most cases, you do not know whether their depravity should be detested or their good fortune desired.

(IV) But if the world were governed by divine providence and the authority of some divinity, Phalaris and Dionysius would never have deserved a kingdom, Rutilius and Camillus would never have deserved exile, and Socrates would never have deserved poison.

(V) Behold the fruit-bearing plantations; behold, now the crop is white, now the wine-heavy vintage—and it is ruined by rain and beaten down by hail. So either the truth, uncertain to us, is hidden and suppressed, or else, as is more credible, Fortune, released from laws, rules through variable and slippery accidents.

**CHAP. VI.—“How much more venerable.”** The common editions had *quando*, “when.” But the laurel for this emendation is owed not to Meursius but to Heraldus, who printed it thus. LINDNER.

**“Priests of truth.”** This is the genuine reading of the Royal codex. The printed editions have the singular “priest.” EDD.

**“Fear was instilled in you beforehand.”** This reading of the ancient book is entirely correct. It evidently must be taken impersonally. RIGALTIUS.—The common editions wrongly read: “imbued to suppose rather than to know.” LINDNER.

[Col. 0250B] **“While the age was still uncultivated.”** These words are absent from Gronovius’ edition, through a printer’s error, I think. LINDNER.

**“At the very birth of the world.”** I do not think that the pronoun supplies the emphasis which Minucius wished to place in it if one prints “at the birth of the world itself,” as the manuscript would have it. It will supply the emphasis, however, if you write “at the very birth of the world,” just as below, “from the very cradle of the nascent empire.” Certainly, WOW. and Elmenhorst also printed it thus long ago, although an interchange of cases could be acceptable. What the author now puts forward in the person of Caecilius is found in its entirety as an opinion once accepted and approved by the Germans in Tacitus, *Germania*, chap. 34: “It seemed more reverent to believe the deeds of the gods than to know them.” GRONOVIUS.

**“They deserved to have either benevolent gods or kings.”** He calls them *faciles*, “benevolent,” meaning beneficent and deserving well of others. Pliny, book II: “This is the most ancient custom of returning gratitude to benefactors: that such persons should be enrolled among the divinities.” RIGALTIUS.—I wonder how it happened that the most learned Jacob Perizonius restored in this passage: “they had either fathers or kings,” since not [Col. 0250C] all those immediately enumerated were either fathers or kings. *Faciles* means mild and propitious, and therefore beneficent. Virgil, *Eclogues* III.9:

> The benevolent nymphs smiled.

Here Servius comments: “The nymphs,” he says, “indulged them because they are gentle and readily entreated”; so too in the *Georgics*: “And worship the benevolent Napaeae.” Prudentius, *Against Symmachus*, book II, verse 804:

> Thus the upright man and the defendant accused of a capital crime alike  
> Enjoy the same stars and the benevolence of a kindly heaven.

Hence Arnobius, book I, p. 5: “It follows that in a time of abundance the gods are not angry and unaccommodating.” No one is unaware, moreover, that among the pagans many persons were regarded as gods because of their services and inventions in the arts. See Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, p. 13A, Lyon edition of [Col. 0250D] 1616, and our author, chap. XX. DAVIS.—Scheffer restores “companions or associates,” supposing the word *familes* to have been written in abbreviated form with a small line above it. Heumann proposes “citizens,” because of what follows in chap. 20. But these corrections are of such a kind that one could suspect the weakness of the case merely from the disagreement among the parties. What is objectionable in the received reading? It coheres perfectly with Minucius’ remaining words. In chap. 21 our author says that men were regarded as gods because of the merit of their valor—there are the kings—or of their service—there are the benefactors. It is true that the preceding chapter mentions citizens, but not citizens in an absolute sense; rather, it calls them “citizens of good memory,” that is, persons who made themselves beneficent toward their fellow citizens by inventing some useful service or art and thereby obtained a good remembrance. It is also true, as Scheffer objects, that the opposition apparently demanded by the conjunction *vel*, “either,” is insufficiently exact. But Wopkensius rightly observes that no opposition has a place here, and that *vel* in both instances has the force of “and”; he adduces Severus, *Sacred History* I.53: “both sacred ornaments, public and private.”

[Col. 0251B] **“To worship gods belonging to their own communities.”** Minucius Felix calls those gods *municipes*, “members of the municipality” or “local gods,” who were consecrated with a special cult in individual cities or provinces. Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 24; Lactantius, book I; and Prudentius, *Against Symmachus* II likewise draw up catalogues of these. Sidonius Apollinaris, with his characteristic brevity, included two in each verse (*Poem* IX, verses 165 ff.); an ancient epigram assigns one to each line, in this manner:

> Dodona, Jupiter, is consecrated to you, etc.

Compare D. Martin, *Religion of the Gauls* II, p. 210; Burmann, *Latin Anthology*, pp. 147 ff. EDD.

**“The Eleusinians worship Ceres.”** The Eleusinians, in worshiping Ceres, cannot be celebrated as though they worshiped a goddess belonging to their own municipality, however much they may possess native rites of worship, since the ancient myths derive her from Sicily—or, if one wishes to indulge so fabulous a people, from Crete. Moreover, in the passages immediately following, he produces either great nations or famous cities. What reason is there for preferring the Eleusinians to them, even if they boasted that their city had been ruled by Celeus? One might suspect that the strokes of the letters in that [Col. 0251C] word were confused and that Minucius wrote: “the people of Henna worship Ceres.” Perhaps it was written *Henneios*, just as in chap. 51 the manuscript had *Henneis*, for which the editors restored “the people of Oeta.” GRONOVIUS.
