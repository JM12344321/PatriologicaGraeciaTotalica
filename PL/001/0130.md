## CHAPTER VII

[Col. 1240B]

May it be granted, Lord Christ, that your servants speak or hear about the discipline of repentance only up to this point: the point up to which hearers ought not to sin either. May they henceforth know nothing of repentance and require nothing from it. I am reluctant to append mention of a second—indeed now a final—hope, lest, by discussing the remaining aid of repentance, we appear to display further room for sinning. Far be it from anyone to interpret the matter as though the way to sin still lies open to him because the way to repentance lies open, or as though the abundance of heavenly mercy should produce license for human rashness. Let no one become worse because God is better, sinning as often as [Col. 1240C] he is pardoned [Note: [Col. 1240D] “Sinning as often as he is pardoned.” Who would not infer from these words that the Church’s doors always stood open to penitents, since he says that Christ pardons as often as one sins? Pamelius too has no hesitation in affirming from these words that the Church always granted pardon to sinners, and that if anything is encountered in the authors about a second and final repentance, it must all be understood of public repentance. To confirm this, he accumulates many passages from the authors which it would serve no purpose to explain, since our undertaking is merely to teach what Tertullian means here and how his words are to be understood. These words, “as often as he is pardoned,” therefore, you should not understand as though there were another opportunity for repentance after the second repentance. He meant nothing more than this: although some hope of pardon remains after baptism and a second and final repentance is granted, one must nevertheless not sin again after baptism. For in hundreds of passages—not to mention this chapter—he says that only one pardon remains after the washing, and no distinction between private and public treatment of graver crimes is found in those earliest times. Thus “as often” and “so often” are rightly spoken of the two pardons, so to speak, and the corresponding two sets of sins—namely, those remitted before and after baptism. Yet I would not dare affirm either that this was the opinion of the universal Church or that this custom was observed in absolutely all churches. ALBASP.].

In any case, his opportunities for escape will come to an end if there is no end to his offending. We have escaped once; henceforth [Col. 1241A] let us never again expose ourselves to dangers [Note: [Col. 1241D] “Henceforth let us never again expose ourselves to dangers.” “Henceforth” means “never hereafter.” RIG.], even if we seem likely to escape a second time. Most people, once delivered from shipwreck, thereafter divorce themselves from both ship and sea [Note: [Col. 1241D] “Divorce themselves from both ship and sea.” For, says Rhenanus, as in Publilius the mime-writer: “He wickedly accuses Neptune who suffers shipwreck a second time.” The expression is charming when he says that they “divorce themselves from both ship and sea,” borrowing the metaphor from those who send a bill of divorce to a wife. Similarly, in *On the Apparel of Women*, chapter 4, he spoke of a “divorce from heaven.” PAM.], and honor God’s benefit—their salvation, namely—by remembering the danger. I praise their fear; I cherish their modest restraint. They are unwilling to burden divine mercy a second time; they dread appearing to trample upon what they have obtained. With surely wholesome anxiety, they avoid experiencing again what they once learned to fear. Thus the restraint of rashness is an attestation of fear. And human fear is honor paid to God.

Yet that most obstinate enemy never allows his malice to be idle. Indeed, he rages most fiercely precisely when he perceives a person to have been fully liberated [Note: [Col. 1241C] By baptism, namely.]; he is most greatly inflamed while he is being extinguished. He must grieve and groan when pardon of sins has been granted, so many works of death in a person [Col. 1241B] have been destroyed, and so many records of his former condemnation have been erased. He grieves that Christ’s servant, who had been his sinner, will judge him and his angels. And so he watches, attacks, and besieges, to see whether he can strike the eyes with carnal desire, ensnare the mind with worldly allurements, overthrow faith by fear of earthly power, or turn it aside from the sure path by perverse traditions [Note: [Col. 1241C] Others read “conditions”; Pamelius found this in three manuscripts.] [Note: [Col. 1242D] “By perverse traditions.” Three manuscripts read “conditions.” Yet the former pleases me more, so that he is arguing against the Montanists, who were turning the faithful from the sure path by their perverse traditions concerning the impossibility of repentance after baptism. Moreover, from his addition of “perverse,” it is clear that the Author does not attack all traditions. But he treats these more fully in *On the Soldier’s Crown*, where he approves many traditions of the Church. PAM.]. He is never at a loss for scandals or temptations.

God, therefore, foreseeing these poisons of his, although the door of forgiveness had been closed and the bar of immersion shut, still permitted something to remain open. In the vestibule he placed [Note: [Col. 1242D] “In the vestibule he placed.” It was the custom and rite for penitents to remain in the church’s vestibule; he alludes to this. ALBASP.] a second repentance, to open for those who knock—but now only once, because it is now the second time; never again thereafter [Note: [Col. 1242D] “Never again thereafter.” Therefore there was only one repentance after baptism. ALBASP.], because the most recent occasion had proved fruitless. Is not this one further opportunity enough? You possess something you now [Note: [Col. 1241C] “Not” is absent from Rigaltius.] did not deserve; for you had lost what you had received. If the Lord’s indulgence grants you the means to restore what you had lost, be grateful for the repeated benefit, to say nothing of its enlargement. For it is a greater thing to restore than to give, since it is more wretched to have lost [Note: [Col. 1242D] “It is more wretched to have lost,” etc. It is far more wretched to lose by sinning the salvation you received in baptism than never to have received it at all. ALBASP.] than never to have received at all.

Yet the spirit is not immediately to be cut down and overthrown [Col. 1242A] by despair if someone has become liable to a second repentance. By all means let him be ashamed to sin again, but let him not be ashamed to repent again; let him be ashamed to come into danger a second time, but not to be delivered a second time. Let no one be ashamed: for a recurrent illness, the medicine must be repeated. You will show yourself grateful toward the Lord [Note: [Col. 1241C] “To the Lord,” in Semler.] if you do not refuse what the Lord offers you. You have offended, but you can still be reconciled. You have someone to whom you may make satisfaction—and one who is indeed willing.
