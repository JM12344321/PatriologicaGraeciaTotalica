24. In the consulship of the emperor Caesar Marcus Aurelius Bassianus Antoninus Augustus, son of Severus Augustus, for the second time, and Publius Septimius Geta, son of Severus Augustus, the seventh year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the thirteenth of Severus: as various heresies were raging in Africa, Tertullian seriously devoted himself to writing against them. [Col. 0070B] First among these comes the book *Against the Apelleians*, though it is now lost—that is, against the followers of Apelles, Marcion’s disciple. Of them the author says: “There still survive in this world those who remember them, even their own disciples and successors, so that those who come later cannot deny them.”

We think that in the same year he composed two books *Against Hermogenes*: one *On Matter Coeternal with God*, and another *On the Origin of the Soul from That Same Matter*. It is not sufficiently established when Hermogenes began to spread his heresies. Blessed Jerome, however, seems partly to indicate it in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers* when he relates that Theophilus of Antioch was the first to write against the heresy of Hermogenes. Although Theophilus flourished under the emperor Verus, it is proved from book 3 *To Autolycus* that he survived him, for there he computes the years both of the world and of Roman consulships and Caesars down to the death of the emperor Verus. We therefore think that Hermogenes spread his errors rather under the emperor Commodus, though he was still alive in the author’s day, as Tertullian himself testifies: “These”—speaking of Marcion, Valentinus, and Apelles, already dead—“we name as the more notorious and frequent adulterers of truth. But a certain Nigidius, of whom I know nothing, and Hermogenes, and many others, still walk about perverting the ways of the Lord” (*On Prescription against Heretics*, ch. 30). Philastrius, *On Heresies*, testifies that Hermogenes flourished in Africa, while the author adds a certain Nigidius as though he were his teacher. [Col. 0070C]

Since swarms of Valentinian heretics also still survived, led—besides the first men named above—by Theotimus, Axionicus, Alexander, and Prodicus, Tertullian composed the book *Against the Valentinians*, following blessed Irenaeus almost word for word. Of the first two the author says: “Theotimus labored much over images of the Law. Valentinus nowhere does so, yet the Valentinians do, because they act through Valentinus. Axionicus alone at Antioch, down to the present day, consoles the memory of Valentinus by preserving his rules intact” (vol. 3, *Against the Valentinians*, ch. 4, nos. 32–34; *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 15, no. 127; ch. 16, no. 131). Of the third he says: “For, as I read in a certain member of Valentinus’ faction, they do not at first think that Christ’s earthly and human substance was formed” (ibid., ch. 17; vol. 3, *Scorpiace*, final chapter). He then adds whom he means: “Besides, through a passion for argument and the shape of a heretical intellect, that Alexander made a place for himself.” And again: “But dismissing Alexander with his syllogisms” (*Against Praxeas*, ch. 3; *On Prescription against Heretics*, ch. 21). [Col. 0071A]

Finally, two passages concern the last: “But if Prodicus or Valentinus had then been present”; and “When another God is introduced against the Creator, it is evil; still worse when several are introduced, according to the Valentinians and Prodicans” (*Against the Valentinians*, ch. 5; *On Prescription against Heretics*, ch. 3 and ch. 52, heresy 52, no. 327; *On the Soul*, ch. 55). The reading cannot be *Procli* there, as it was in the printed copies. Besides what we proved in its proper place, this is demonstrated by the fact that Proclus or Proculus was not a Valentinian but a Montanist; some followers of Montanus were therefore called [non-Latin text omitted in the source] “according to Proclus.” Tertullian, while still Catholic, had written against Valentinus.

Indeed, since the author calls Proculus “the dignity of virginal old age and Christian eloquence,” we seem to have reached what they call the first stumbling stone against which he struck when he began to favor Montanus’ party. He was apparently seduced by admiration both for Proculus’ virginal old age—Proculus must then have been elderly, since even by Tertullian’s testimony he was a contemporary of the Valentinian founders—and for the Christian eloquence he discovered in his writings. The wretched man little remembered what he had written earlier, perhaps on the occasion of this very great man: “These simpletons are also accustomed to build themselves up toward ruin by certain persons captured by heresy: Why did she or he, most faithful, prudent, and experienced in the Church, pass over to that side? Whoever says this does not answer himself: those whom heresies could change should be judged neither prudent, faithful, nor experienced. What then if a bishop, a deacon, a widow, a virgin, a teacher, even a martyr, has fallen from the rule? Will heresies therefore seem to possess the truth? Do we prove faith from persons, or persons from faith?” [Col. 0071C] Otherwise it is hardly probable that the author would have fallen into so absurd a heresy except through some such occasion, perhaps even through familiar association with Proculus during his repeated visits to Rome. We shall later show that Proculus lived at Rome. This is especially striking because Tertullian had earlier called it blasphemy to say that the Paraclete promised by Christ first appeared in Montanus, yet in his subsequent writings he did not hesitate frequently to ascribe the name Paraclete to him.

In the consulship of Nummius Senecio Albinus and Fulvius Aemilianus, the eighth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the fourteenth of Severus: most writers say that Perpetua and Felicity suffered martyrdom, according to the Venerable Bede on Severus’ birthday, assigned by the martyrologies of Usuard and Rome to March 7. They also number with them Revocatus, Saturninus, and Secundulus, who suffered together in the Mauretanian city of Thuburbo.

Since the author mentions the most courageous martyr Perpetua in the book *On the Soul*, that book, written *Against the Philosophers and Heretics*, cannot have been composed earlier. In it he also alludes to the book *On Paradise*, which we conjecture was written on the occasion of what St Perpetua says she saw in paradise. [Col. 0072A] To strengthen the faithful in this persecution of Christians, which also extended throughout Africa, we think that at almost the same time he wrote the book *On the Hope of the Faithful*. Nevertheless we find the proverb entirely true that one error draws another after it: the subject of each book is a paradox, and in the said book *On the Soul* one can find not a few further opinions that are insufficiently sound. These are discussed more fully among the author’s paradoxes, together with the antidote. We add only this here: the book was written *On Every Condition of the Soul*, as Tertullian himself testifies, for he even calls it a commentary on the soul, treating whatever philosophers and heretics had ever discussed concerning the soul, so that neither philosophers nor theologians today require another commentary. [Col. 0072B]

## In the Year of the Lord 208

### § XIV. On books already savoring of Montanism: *On the Flesh of Christ against Four Heresies*, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, five books *Against Marcion*, *On the Soldier’s Crown*, *Scorpiace against the Gnostics*, and *Against Praxeas*

26. In the ninth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the fifteenth of Severus, in the consulship of Marcus Flavius Afer, son and grandson of Marcus, and Quintus Allius Fabius Catullinus Maximus, it is established from these words that Tertullian wrote the first book *Against Marcion*: “But now, how can it be that the Lord was revealed in the twelfth year of Tiberius Caesar, yet no substance of him has been discovered even by the fifteenth year of the emperor Severus?” (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chs. 2 and 45; book 1 *Against Marcion*, ch. 15; vol. 3, *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 7). It follows that at this time he must have published together *On the Flesh of Christ against Four Heresies*, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, and the five books *Against Marcion* (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 2; book 5 *Against Marcion*, ch. 10, no. 150; *On the Soldier’s Crown*, final chapter), since they were written at the same time. As we have said elsewhere, the fourth book *Against Marcion* is cited in *On the Flesh of Christ*; books 2 and 3 *Against Marcion* are cited in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*; and in book 1 *Against Marcion* the work *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* is cited. I therefore think that the first four books *Against Marcion* were written first, then the two intermediate works, and finally the fifth *Against Marcion*. [Col. 0072C]

If, as Onuphrius relates, the decennalia of Antoninus, the emperor Severus’ son, were celebrated in this year—perhaps anticipated by a whole year because of his departure for the British war, which Cassiodorus says occurred under these consuls—then the book *On the Soldier’s Crown* was also written in this year. If both events occurred in the following year, as seems more probable to me from the coin inscription “Antoninus Pius Augustus, consul for the third time; vows discharged to the god,” the reader may assign it there, provided he agrees with us that it preceded the British expedition. The inscription given above, with the image of a horseman holding a javelin—“Departure of the emperors”—also belongs to this expedition, for Severus took both sons with him. Since Publius Septimius Geta was first called emperor, Caesar, and Augustus and received tribunician power in the following year, as the consular fasti state, our conjecture is confirmed. [Col. 0073A] The inscriptions and consular fasti can indeed be reconciled with Onuphrius and with another inscription in which Antoninus is called “Augustus, holding tribunician power for the tenth time, consul for the third time designate,” by saying that his tenth year began under these consuls on May 1 and ended on May 1 in the following year; but we have preferred to assign it to the following year, lest we become more prolix here.

## In the Year of the Lord 209

27. In the tenth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the sixteenth of Severus, in the consulship of the emperor Caesar Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Augustus, son of Severus Augustus, for the third time, and Publius Septimius Geta, son of Severus Augustus: we think that after Antoninus’ decennalia were celebrated at Rome, Tertullian wrote the book *On the Soldier’s Crown*. [Col. 0073B] That it was written at Rome is clear from the words, “Blush, his Roman fellow-soldiers,” and its opening appears to refer to the decennalia: “Very recently there was a largess from the most excellent emperors.” We have thought it proper to append what follows, since it indicates the occasion for writing this book, in which he maintains—contrary to what others were muttering—that Christians were forbidden to wear crowns.

“The soldiers crowned with laurel were being paid off in the camp. One among them approached more truly a soldier of God, alone with uncovered head, holding his unused crown in his hand. One by one they point him out, mock him from afar, and gnash their teeth at him close at hand. A complaint reaches the tribune, and the man had now moved forward out of rank. The tribune immediately asked, ‘Why are you so different in dress?’ He denied that it was lawful for him to act with the rest. Asked his reasons, he answered, ‘I am a Christian.’ Then came consultations, adjournment of the case, and the accused man before the prefects. There he laid aside his cloak, took the military boot from his feet, and returned his sword”—for Herodian, book 2, makes clear at the beginning of Severus’ reign that soldiers customarily laid aside all military insignia when discharged. “And now, clothed in the red of hope for his own blood, he awaits Christ’s donative in prison.” [Col. 0073C]

“Then came opinions about him—I do not know whether from Christians, since there were no others from pagans—that he was rash, headstrong, and eager to die, because when questioned about his clothing he created trouble for the Name. Plainly it remains for them to plan also to refuse martyrdoms. Finally they mutter that so good and long a peace is being put in danger. I do not doubt that some are removing the Scriptures, packing their baggage, and preparing to flee from city to city. I know their shepherds too: lions in peace, deer in battle. But elsewhere we shall teach concerning the trials of confessions.”

By this he points either to the book *On Flight in Persecution* or, as is more pleasing, to *Scorpiace against the Gnostics* (*Against the Gnostics*, ch. 1), namely Prodicus, Scorpianus, and other Valentinians who opposed martyrdom and had already begun to break into Africa (on whom the reader may consult our annotations). Perhaps that work too was written at Rome. In it he explains at greatest length the question of confession—that is, that it is necessary to confess Christ—and describes in these words the most severe of all persecutions, which seems on the stated occasion to have flared up again after a long peace, since few martyrs under Severus are recorded at Rome and in Africa down to that time: “At present the heat is at its height, the very dog-days of persecution from this dog-headed creature” (by whom he means either the emperor Severus or, more probably, the prefect of the city of Rome during the emperor’s absence after his departure for Britain). “Fire has proved some Christians, the sword others, wild beasts others; still others, after tasting martyrdom under clubs and claws, hunger in prison.” [Col. 0074A]

We said Severus was absent in Britain because Eusebius in his *Chronicle*, Orosius, book 7, ch. 11, Paul the Deacon, book 8, ch. 1, and before them Spartianus, Herodian, and Dio in his life of Severus, mention the British expedition undertaken in this year, as we proved above. Tertullian himself indicates in the words cited above that Britain had not yet entirely submitted to the Romans: “Places in Britain inaccessible to the Romans [Col. 0074B] but subject to Christ” (*Against the Jews*, ch. 7), since Lucius, king of the Britons, had shortly before been converted to the faith with the aid of Pope St Eleutherus. Likewise: “The Britains are enclosed within the bounds of the Ocean.”

28. In the eleventh year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the seventeenth of Severus, in the consulship of Marcus Aurelius Claudius Pompeianus, son of Marcus, and Lollianus Avitus: while still at Rome, Tertullian seems to me to have written the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*. In these words he acknowledges that it had also been written in Greek: “Now that the issue peculiar to my opinion has arisen, I shall show in Latin too that our virgins ought to be veiled” (*On the Veiling of Virgins*, ch. 1). More on this subject in the following year.

It is also probable that shortly afterward, having returned to Carthage, he wrote the book *Against Praxeas*. Of Praxeas the author says: “He was the first to bring this kind of perversity from Asia to Rome, a man otherwise restless and, besides, swollen with boasting of martyrdom merely because of the ordinary brief weariness of imprisonment. The tares of Praxeas, sown here also”—that is, in Africa—“had borne fruit; then, having been exposed by one through whom God willed” (apparently Pope Zephyrinus, since blessed Optatus of Milevis, book 1 *Against Parmenian*, relates something of this kind concerning him), “they even seemed to have been uprooted. Finally the former teacher had given security for his correction, and the written bond remains among the Psychics, in whose presence the matter was then transacted; hence the silence. But those tares had everywhere shaken out their seed. Thus for some time he lay hidden through hypocrisy with deceitful vitality, and now he has broken out again” (*Against Praxeas*, ch. 1). [Col. 0074C–D]

From these words it is sufficiently clear both that Praxeas at one time retracted his heresy after being corrected by someone’s disputation, but relapsed and broke out again, and that he was still alive when this book was written. Yet if what Tertullian adds is true—that the prophecy of Montanus was expelled through Praxeas’ aid—then, since Praxeas is known to have flourished under Pope St Eleutherus, he must already have been old. We can scarcely think the author reliable in what has just been said. First, St Pacian writes these true statements about the Cataphrygian heretics: “First, they rely on several authorities: for example, the Greek Blastus belongs to them; Theodotus and Praxeas also once taught your people.” Second, the author himself elsewhere joins Blastus, Theodotus, and Praxeas as disciples, together with Victorinus, attributes to those called “according to Aeschines” the same heresy, and attacks it in this book. [Col. 0075A]

### § XV. The time and cause of his defection

29. In the twelfth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the eighteenth of Severus, in the consulship of Marcus Acilius Faustinus, son and grandson of Marcus, and Gaius Caesonius Triarius Rufinius, son of Gaius, what Cassiodorus in his *Chronicle* and the Venerable Bede, *Ecclesiastical History*, book 1, ch. 5, write about Severus after the authors cited above was completed: in Britain he extended from sea to sea, for a hundred and more miles, a rampart fortified with frequent towers, so as to make the portion of the province recovered by very severe battles safer from the rest. This is also supported by a law of the Augusti Severus and Antoninus, issued at York under this consulship on May 7, extant in the *Code*, book 3, title 32, law 1. [Col. 0075B] Indeed, inscriptions first appear then in which Severus is called “Britannicus Maximus, holding tribunician power for the eighteenth time,” Antoninus “Britannicus, holding tribunician power for the thirteenth time, acclaimed imperator twice, consul three times,” and finally one reading on one side “Publius Septimius Geta Pius Augustus Britannicus, holding tribunician power for the third time, consul twice,” and on the other “To the victory in Britain.” For this was only the third year since Geta had received tribunician power with the other insignia of imperial rule.

We think it was about this year that what blessed Jerome, Sophronius in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, and Nicephorus, book 4, chs. 12 and 34, write about Tertullian took place: after he had remained a presbyter in the Catholic Church until middle age, he fell into the doctrine of Montanus. [Col. 0075C] There is nevertheless great difficulty in Jerome’s statement that this happened through the envy and insults of the clergy of the Roman Church, and in Sophronius’ [non-Latin text omitted in the source] and Nicephorus’ translator’s phrase, “because of the envy of the Roman clergy.” This is especially difficult because the author nowhere complains of it, even in books written against the Church, and because Vincent of Lérins, though later than Jerome, omits this cause and says only that Tertullian, insufficiently tenacious of Catholic doctrine and the ancient faith, far more eloquent than fortunate, changed his opinion and fell into error, becoming a great trial in the Church. Abbot Trithemius follows him. [Col. 0075D]

30. One writer thinks the cause of envy toward the author was his eloquence. Some Catholics also follow Rhenanus’ conjecture, however suspect, and suppose that when Tertullian first began to favor Montanus’ heresy, he was immediately deprived of the Church’s communion before giving himself wholly to it; angered by this, he passed completely into Montanus’ party. But I do not see how Jerome could describe ecclesiastical excommunication not only by the term envy but also by that of insults. The author calls excommunication a divine censure and a preliminary judgment of the future judgment (*Apology*, ch. 39); blessed Jerome, in his letter to Heliodorus, calls it “a judgment before the day of judgment, by which one expelled from the Church is torn apart by the rabid mouth of a demon.” Jerome even acknowledges envy in saints when it means zeal (letter to Damasus on the prodigal and frugal sons). [Col. 0076A]

Therefore, if conjecture is permitted in an uncertain matter, we suppose that after Agrippinus’ death Tertullian was rejected in his attempt to obtain the bishopric of Carthage, perhaps through the envy of certain clergy of the Roman Church. After also suffering insults from them, he defected to Montanus’ party. Several passages in his preceding books could certainly have supplied fuel for their envy, since he tacitly censured the faults of certain clergy, perhaps Roman, just as later happened for the same reason to blessed Jerome and Paulinus (*On Spectacles*, ch. 25).

First, concerning attendance at spectacles: “While the tragic actor shouts,” he says, “that man”—namely someone from the clergy—“will reconsider the exclamations of some prophet. Amid the modes of effeminacy he will devise a psalm within himself; and while the athletes perform, he will say that one must not strike back. He may even be moved to mercy while gazing at the bites of bears and the nets of gladiators. May God avert from his own people so great a lust for deadly pleasure. What sort of thing is it to pass from the Church of God into the church of the devil, from heaven, as they say, into filth? To weary afterward in applauding an actor those hands you raised to the Lord? With the mouth by which you uttered Amen over the holy thing, to bear witness to a gladiator? To say [non-Latin text omitted in the source] to anyone at all except God and Christ?” [Col. 0076B]

Again, concerning makers of idols: “All day faith’s zeal will plead this point with groaning: that a Christian comes from idols into the Church; that he worships with hands which outside are worshipped against God; that he lays upon the Lord’s body hands which furnish bodies to demons. Nor is this enough. Let it be a small matter that they receive from other hands what they contaminate: those same hands deliver to others what they have contaminated. Makers of idols are chosen for the ecclesiastical order. O crime! The Jews laid hands upon Christ once; these men assault his body daily. O hands that ought to be cut off!” (*On Idolatry*, ch. 7; *On the Soldier’s Crown*, ch. 1.) [Col. 0076C]

Again, concerning pastors who flee in persecution: “Clearly it remains for him to plan even to refuse martyrdoms, since he rejects the prophecies of the same Holy Spirit. I know their pastors too: lions in peace, deer in battle.” Finally, concerning the veiling of virgins: “No one would have approved that custom which denies virgins while displaying them, except men of the same sort as the virgins themselves. Eyes such as the virgin has who wishes to be seen will wish to see a virgin. The same kinds of eyes desire one another. To be seen and to see belong to the same lust” (*On the Veiling of Virgins*, ch. 2). [Col. 0076D]

The author of the commentary on Paul’s epistles ascribed to St Ambrose seems to interpret that envy as though it were the author’s own envy toward the Roman clergy. “Corrupting charity through envy,” he says, “they reduced knowledge to nothing. For Tertullian and Novatian were men of no small learning; but because through jealousy they broke the bonds of charity, they turned to schism and created heresies for their own destruction.” These words also make my conjecture more probable from the fact that Novatian fell into schism and heresy because he was rejected for the Roman episcopate. [Col. 0077A]

If Tertullian had been excommunicated—and for entirely just causes—Pope St Zephyrinus perhaps pronounced the sentence even against him in his absence. First, in all books written before the fourteenth year of Severus’ reign, he frequently mentions the Paraclete’s new prophecy, and even the Holy Spirit of Montanus in the words cited above. He likewise occasionally mentions a female prophet, meaning at one time Priscilla and at another Maximilla, and says not a little about ecstasy in passages cited among the author’s paradoxes (*Against Praxeas*, ch. 1). Second, he began to call Catholics “Psychics,” that is, soulish people, both *Against Marcion* and *Against Praxeas*, as discussed more fully there. Finally, joining all these things together in *Against Praxeas*, [Col. 0077B] he slanders the Roman bishop—apparently Eleutherus—as though, having already acknowledged the prophecies of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, he had been ready by that acknowledgment to bring peace to the churches of Asia and Phrygia, had not Praxeas, by making what Tertullian calls false assertions concerning the prophets and their churches, forced him to recall letters of peace already issued and to desist from the purpose of receiving the charisms. He further says that Praxeas carried out the devil’s work by expelling prophecy and putting the Paraclete to flight. Certainly the author himself approves excommunication of heretics in more than one passage (*Scorpiace against the Gnostics*, ch. 1).

## In the Year of the Lord 212

### § XVI. On the final books of Tertullian the Montanist: *On Flight in Persecution*, *Exhortation to Chastity*, *On Monogamy*, *On Fasts against the Psychics*, *On Modesty*, *On Ecstasy*, and the seventh book *Against Apollonius*

31. In the thirteenth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the first of the reign of Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Bassianus Pius Felix Augustus Britannicus Germanicus, in the consulship of Quintus Epidius Rufus Lollianus Gentianus, son of Lucius, and Pomponius Bassus, we think Tertullian composed the book *On Flight in Persecution*. He did so before the death of the emperor Lucius Septimius Severus Pertinax Augustus, who died at York on February 4 of that year, more from grief than illness—being punished by God even in this, according to Orosius, book 7, ch. 11—before the eighteenth year of his reign had elapsed. For from May 15, AD 194, to the said day of this year there are only seventeen years, eight months, and twenty-one days. We therefore make this the first year of Antoninus, [Col. 0077D] or at least place the work before Antoninus’ return from Britain with his brother Publius Septimius Geta Felix Augustus Britannicus, his partner in the empire, while the persecution was still continuing (*To Scapula*, ch. 4).

Antoninus—“imbued with Christian milk,” as we said above from the author—put an end to this persecution, as even Sulpicius Severus attests (*Sacred History*, book 2), for he writes that from the death of Severus until Decius the churches enjoyed peace, except that Maximinus raged against the bishops. Blessed Cyprian, *On the Lapsed*, calls this a very long peace. Nevertheless the edicts of Severus appear to have remained in force for some time from the author’s words: “Unless perhaps we sin against senatorial decrees and imperial commands opposed to assemblies” (*On Fasts*, ch. 13; *On Flight in Persecution*, ch. 5). [Col. 0078A]

Since blessed Jerome, Sophronius, and Nicephorus assert in the places cited above that this work was written against the Church, it is probable that the Fabius addressed in it was a Roman presbyter. That he held some ecclesiastical ministry can certainly be inferred from these words: “But when the very leaders—that is, deacons, presbyters, and bishops—flee, how will the layman understand?” In our annotations we discuss more fully when, contrary to the author’s view, they are permitted to flee. He also mentions in this work the most holy martyr Rutilius. What Eusebius and Nicephorus write about Origen’s first and most severe persecution at Alexandria, before he castrated himself, should doubtless also be assigned to the reign of Severus.

For understanding the author, it is relevant that he says [Col. 0078B] it is “accursed before [non-Latin text omitted in the source] to call Caesar a god” (*Apology*, ch. 34). Herodian, book 3, recounts at length the rite observed when Severus was enrolled among the gods at Rome after death by his sons, once his ashes, brought with them from Britain in an alabaster urn according to Herodian, or a bronze one according to Spartianus, had been placed in the tomb of Antoninus.

We have also thought it should not be passed over here that, since Severus died on February 4, two laws in the *Code* should be corrected. In the first, book 6, title 45, law 1, “the fifth day before the Kalends of February” should be read instead of “January.” The other, book 6, title 37, law 3, should be ascribed to Antoninus Augustus alone, not to Severus and Antoninus Augusti, since it was issued on April 26, long after Severus’ death—especially as a law of Antoninus alone, issued on May 1, and several others issued under these consuls survive.

## [Col. 0078C] In the Year of the Lord 213

32. In the fourteenth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the second of Antoninus, in the consulship of Marcus Pomponius Asper for the second time and Asper—that is, the two Aspers, under whose consulship various laws of Antoninus are found to have been issued (we think the elder was the same man discussed above under AD 202)—Antoninus alone, according to Herodian and Spartianus, seized the empire. He had killed his brother Geta against their mother’s breast after Geta had reigned one year and twenty-one days, buried him in the Septizodium—which Severus had built, according to Eusebius, and which was named from the seven zones of heaven—and enrolled him among the gods. Antoninus also ordered many others put to death, however dear they had previously been to his father and himself. Among those to be mentioned are Papinian, teacher of Antoninus, Julian, and the jurist Tertullian, surnamed by Spartianus “the sanctuary of law,” and Sammonicus Serenus, whose numerous surviving books Spartianus mentions and who was afterward tutor to the Caesar Gordian. [Col. 0078D]

To this year we assign Tertullian’s book *Exhortation to Chastity*, since Nicephorus, book 4, ch. 54, lists it among the works written against the Catholic Church. In it Tertullian seems to have reached such madness as to think that the Church could exist even among three lay people—namely Montanus and his insane prophetesses Prisca and Maximilla—as he insists more fully elsewhere (*Exhortation to Chastity*, ch. 7; *On Modesty*, ch. 21).

To this year we also assign the disputation of the ecclesiastical writer Gaius against Proclus, mentioned by Eusebius, book 6, ch. 14, and blessed Jerome in the *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, and more fully by Nicephorus in the place just cited: “Gaius flourished,” he says, “under Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, while Antoninus, son of Severus, was emperor.” Gaius held and composed a very ample disputation against Proclus, defender of Montanus, reproving and refuting him for his rash audacity in introducing a new prophecy. [Col. 0079A]

We once thought that this disputation gave Tertullian the occasion to defect to Montanus’ party. It seems probable that Proclus, convicted in the disputation and refusing to repent, was excommunicated by Zephyrinus, together with all his followers. Among them was doubtless the author, since he followed Proclus’ group among the Montanists, [non-Latin text omitted in the source], rather than those called “according to Aeschines,” whom he attacked in *Against Praxeas*. But it is clear from the preceding discussion that Tertullian had already begun to defect and had even written against the Church.

Perhaps around this time also occurred the journey to Rome under Zephyrinus of Origen’s disciples Clement of Alexandria and Ammonius, mentioned by Eusebius and Nicephorus. Eusebius, book 6, ch. 12, says that Clement’s reason was his desire to visit that most ancient Church. At this period they also enumerate other ecclesiastical writers: Beryllus of Bostra in Arabia; Hippolytus, according to some bishop of Portus; and Alexander, first bishop of Cappadocia and afterward of Jerusalem. Blessed Jerome also inserts among them Minucius Felix, [Col. 0079C] who imitated the author’s *Apology* in many respects.

## In the Year of the Lord 214 [the source prints “CCX V,” evidently an OCR or typographical corruption]

33. In the fifteenth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the third of Antoninus, in the consulship of the emperor Caesar Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Augustus Britannicus Germanicus, son of the deified Severus, for the fourth time, and Publius Caelius Balbinus, son of Publius, for the second time, the emperor Antoninus, holding tribunician power for the sixteenth time, triumphed over the Britons. In this year we think Tertullian also wrote against the Church the book *On Monogamy*. Among other things it reads: “Since now the time has been still further shortened, about 160 years having elapsed since then” (*On Monogamy*, ch. 3).

Anyone wishing to reckon these years from the Passion of the Lord would go completely astray, as is more than sufficiently clear from the foregoing. Even if, according to the author’s erroneous calculation, only twenty-two years were counted from Christ’s Passion to the devastation of Jerusalem—others count twenty-five—not merely 160 but 188 years would have elapsed by the present year. [Col. 0079D] Some begin this reckoning from the apostles, because the words “after the apostles” precede it; we too held this view when writing our annotations on the passage. But Saints Peter and Paul first suffered in Nero’s final year, which fell in AD 69, from which only 146 years are counted to this point; and St John the Evangelist lived down to Trajan, dying only around AD 100, from which there are no more than 114 years.

Those who count these approximately 160 years from the time of the apostle Paul come closer, and we accept their opinion. But since he first suffered martyrdom together with the apostle Peter, as already stated, we think they should be counted from the time when he wrote the First Epistle to the Corinthians, namely around AD 54. First, it is clear from the epistles themselves that both letters to the Corinthians were written after Paul had converted them to the faith. That conversion is thought to have occurred in AD 51, when he remained there for a year and six months. Second, if the letter was written from Ephesus, as certain Greek manuscripts—more correctly—have it, it is sufficiently clear from the accounts of Paul’s journeys that he was at Ephesus in the said year, which fell in the twelfth year of the emperor Claudius. If it was written from Philippi, as more Latin and Greek manuscripts profess, Acts of the Apostles, chapter 20, testifies that he traveled through Philippi in AD 55 after leaving Ephesus around the feast of Passover. [Col. 0080A–B]

That the author should be understood in this way is also gathered from the cited words, which he had read at approximately 1 Corinthians 7:29: “Since now the time has been shortened.” That this falls in the present year, not the fifteenth of Severus—as some learned men conjecture—is clear from the fact that Severus’ fifteenth year is the same as AD 208. From the year stated above to that date only 155 years are completed, and the calculation cannot be extended farther because the Corinthians were first initiated into the Catholic faith in AD 51.

This year was also distinguished by the consulship of three men of imperial rank. Besides Antoninus and Balbinus—the latter reigned fourth after him jointly with Pupienus—Marcus Antonius Gordianus, son of Marcus, was appointed suffect consul from the Kalends of March; he was afterward called emperor, Caesar, and Augustus, and was killed by Balbinus. Likewise, from the Kalends of May, Publius Helvius Pertinax, son of the deified Pertinax and his grandson, entered office and was killed not long afterward by Antoninus. [Col. 0080C]
