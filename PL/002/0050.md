And he himself will illuminate the hidden things of darkness—assuredly through Christ, since he promised Christ [Note: [Col. 0485D] “Christ” in the Venetian edition; “of Christ” in Wouwer’s codex] as an illumination (Isa. 42:6); he also declared himself to be the lamp [Col. 0485C] that searches hearts and reins (Ps. 7:10). From him, too, will come praise for each person, since from him, as judge, comes praise’s opposite as well.

“Surely,” you say [Note: [Col. 0486D] “he says” in another edition], “here at least he interprets ‘the god of this world’ as the god of the world when he says: ‘We have been made a spectacle to the world, to angels, and to human beings.’ For if by ‘world’ he had meant the human beings of the world, he would not subsequently also have named human beings.” On the contrary: precisely so that you would not argue in this way, the providence of the Holy Spirit demonstrated what he had meant [Note: [Col. 0486D] “how he had spoken” in Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür] by “We have been made a spectacle to the world”: namely, to the angels who minister to the world and to the human beings to whom they minister. Evidently a man of such constancy—not to mention the Holy Spirit—was afraid to name the God of the world openly, especially when writing to the children whom he had begotten in the Gospel, although he could not be seen to preach against him unless he did so explicitly!

[Col. 0485D] I do not maintain that it was according to the Creator’s law that [Col. 0486A] the man who had his father’s wife was found displeasing; the discipline followed may have been that of common and public religion. But when Paul condemns him to be handed over to Satan, he is the herald of the condemning God. Let him see also how Paul said, “for the destruction of the flesh, so that the spirit may be saved on the day of the Lord,” since he passed judgment both on the destruction of the flesh and on the salvation of the spirit; and, in ordering evil to be removed from their midst, he recalled a most frequently repeated sentence of the Creator.

“Purge out the old leaven, so that you may be a new batch of dough, as you are unleavened.” The unleavened loaves, then, were figures of us in the Creator’s dispensation. So too, “Christ our Passover has been sacrificed.” Why is Christ the Passover, unless the Passover was a figure of Christ through the likeness of the saving blood and of Christ as the sacrificial animal? Why does he clothe us and Christ with images drawn from the Creator’s solemn rites [Note: [Col. 0486D] “imbues” in Rhenanus and the Venetian edition], if those images were not ours?

Moreover, in turning us away [Col. 0486B] from fornication, he makes manifest the resurrection of the flesh. “The body,” he says, “is not for fornication, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body”—as a temple is for God, and God for the temple. Will the temple, then, perish for God, and God for the temple? Yet you see: “He who raised the Lord will also raise us”; he will raise us in the body as well, because the body is for the Lord, and the Lord for the body. And it is well that he adds: “Do you not know that your bodies are members of Christ?” What will the heretic say? That the members of Christ will not rise, because they are no longer ours?

[Note: [Col. 0485D] “For we were bought at a great price.” [Col. 0486D] To prove against Marcion that Christ assumed true flesh, he says that we were redeemed either at a great price or at no price: at no price, if he was merely a phantom; at a great price, if he bore our sins in his own body. LE PR.]

“For we were bought at a great price” [Note: [Col. 0486D] “at a price” omitted by Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür]. Indeed, at no price at all if Christ was a phantom and possessed no bodily substance that he could pay out for our bodies. Therefore [Note: [Col. 0486D] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür add “and”] Christ possessed something with which to redeem us; and if at some great price he redeemed these bodies, in which that same fornication must not be committed [Note: [Col. 0486D] “admitted,” Ciacconius], [Col. 0486C] since they are now Christ’s members rather than ours, he will surely preserve for himself what he purchased at great cost. How, then, shall we now honor God? How shall we bear God in a body destined to perish?

Next comes the dispute concerning marriage, which Marcion prohibits more uncompromisingly than the Apostle. For although the Apostle prefers the good of continence, he nevertheless permits marriage both to be contracted and to be exercised, and advises that it should be preserved rather than dissolved. Christ, to be sure, forbids divorce (Matt. 5 and 19), whereas Moses permits it. Marcion, by entirely removing sexual intercourse from believers—for his catechumens may look to themselves—and ordering the repudiation of a woman married previously [Note: [Col. 0486D] “marriage,” Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür; “those married,” Junius], follows whose judgment, Moses’ or Christ’s? Yet Christ’s Apostle also, when he commands a woman not to leave her husband, or, if she has left, to remain unmarried or be reconciled [Col. 0486D] to her husband, both permits divorce, since he did not prohibit it altogether, [Col. 0487A] and confirms marriage, since he first forbade its dissolution and, if it should happen to have been dissolved, wished it to be restored.

But what causes does he give for continence? “Because the time has been shortened.” I had supposed it was because there is another God in Christ! And yet from the one from whom the shortening of time comes will also come whatever accords with that shortening of time. No one makes provision for another’s time. You affirm your God to be a very little God, Marcion, since the Creator’s time constrains him in some respect.

Certainly, in laying down the rule that one must marry “only in the Lord,” lest any believer contract marriage with a pagan, he upholds the Creator’s law, which everywhere prohibits marriages with foreigners. “But even if there are those who are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth,” it is evident how he said this: not as though those who are so called truly exist [Note: [Col. 0487D] “because,” another reading; Semler adds “but because they exist”; François reads “but because it exists”], when in fact they do not. For he began with idols, intending to discuss things sacrificed to idols: [Col. 0487B] “We know that an idol is nothing.” Yet even Marcion does not deny that the Creator is God. Therefore the Apostle cannot be thought to have placed the Creator among those who are called gods and yet do not exist, since, even if they did exist, nevertheless for us there would be “one God, the Father, from whom are all things” [Note: [Col. 0487D] “from whom are all things” is read by Semler in the Venetian edition].

“From whom are all things” for us—unless he is the one to whom all things belong? What are these “all things”? You have them in what precedes: “All things are yours, whether Paul or Apollos or Cephas, or the world, or life, or death, or things present, or things to come.” Thus he makes the Creator God of all things, since from him come the world, life, and death, which cannot belong to another god. Therefore Christ too, among all things, comes from him.

In teaching that everyone ought to live by his own labor [Note: [Col. 0487D] “Teaching everyone to live by his own labor.” Here, by various examples, he proves that everyone ought to procure his livelihood by his own labor. Thus the blessed Apostle once said that his hands had supplied the things he needed. Those who serve the Gospel ought to live from it. LE PR.], he had offered enough preliminary examples from soldiers, shepherds, and farmers; but he lacked divine authority. He therefore [Col. 0487C] sets against the ungrateful [Note: [Col. 0488D] “gratuitously,” Junius] the Creator’s law—the law he was supposedly destroying—for his own god had no such law. “You shall not muzzle,” he says, “an ox while it is threshing”; and he adds: “Does the Lord have concern for oxen?” Is he even [Note: [Col. 0488D] “concerning,” Pamelius] kindly to oxen for the sake of human beings? “For it was written for our sake,” he says. He thus proved both that the law is allegorical in accordance with our interpretation and that it supports those who live from the Gospel; and for this reason he proved that the evangelists belong to no other god than the one whose law provided for them when it said, “For it was written for our sake” [Note: [Col. 0488D] “for” omitted by François]. But he did not wish to use the law’s authority, because he preferred to labor without payment. He referred this to his own glory, which he said no one would nullify, not to the destruction of the law, whose provision he proved another would use.

But look: blind Marcion also stumbles against the rock from which our fathers [Col. 0487D] drank in the wilderness. For if that rock was Christ, it was surely the Creator’s Christ, since the people too belonged to the Creator. As a figure of what [Note: [Col. 0488D] “A figure of an alien sacrament.” This may be explained as “a figure of him who is alien to the sacrament.” For *extraneus* is customarily construed with the genitive, as above in *Against Marcion*, book II: “Who is alien to evil, unless he is also its enemy?” RHEN.] alien sacrament does he interpret it? Or was it so that he might teach this very thing: that the ancient events were figurative of Christ, who must therefore be reckoned as belonging to them?

[Col. 0488A] For when he is about to recount the remainder of the people’s fate, he premises: “These things were made examples for us.” Tell me: were examples for human beings belonging to another, hitherto unknown god made by the Creator? Or does one god borrow examples from another—and indeed from a rival? Does he terrify me on his own behalf by means of the one from whom he transfers my faith? Will his adversary make me better for him? Now, if I commit the same sins as the people, am I to suffer the same things, or not? If not the same, then in vain he sets before me as objects of fear things that I shall not suffer. But from whom shall I suffer what I am to suffer? If from the Creator, of what kind are the punishments that it belongs to him to inflict? And how will it make sense that a jealous God should punish his rival’s sinner rather than, conversely, cherish him? If from that other god—yet he does not know how to punish. Thus this entire proposition of the Apostle stands on no rational basis unless it belongs to the Creator’s discipline.

Finally, [Col. 0488B] in his conclusion he corresponds to his preface: “But these things, just as they happened to them, were written to admonish us, upon whom the ends of the ages have come.” O Creator, already both foreknowing and admonishing Christians who belong to another god!

I pass over some matters when they are parallel to those already discussed, and sometimes dispose of them briefly. A great argument for another god, indeed: the permission of every kind of food, contrary to the law—as if we ourselves did not also confess that the law’s burdens have been remitted, but remitted by the one who imposed them and promised renewal. Thus he who took foods away also restored them, as he had granted them from the beginning. Besides, if some other god had been the destroyer of our God, there would have been nothing he would have prohibited his own people more strongly than living from his adversary’s resources. (2 Cor. 11:14.)

## CHAPTER VIII

[Col. 0488C] “The head of the man is Christ.” Which Christ—the one who is not the man’s author? For “head” is used to denote authority, and authority will belong to none other than the author. Of what man, then, is he the head? Surely of the man about whom he adds: “For the man ought not to cover his head, since he is the image of God.” Therefore, if he is the image of the Creator—for it was the Creator who, looking upon Christ, his Word, as the man who was to come, said, “Let us make man according to our image and likeness”—how can I have as my head someone other than the one whose image I am? Since I am the image of the Creator, there is no place in me for another head.

But why must a woman have authority over her head? If it is because she was made from the man and for the man according to the Creator’s institution, then here too the Apostle upheld his discipline, since he explains the grounds of that discipline from the Creator’s institution.

[Col. 0488D] He also adds, “because of the angels.” Which angels—that is, whose? If the Creator’s apostate angels, reasonably so: that the face which caused them to stumble should bear a certain mark in the guise of humility and in the concealment of its beauty. But [Col. 0489A] if because of the angels of the other god, what does she fear, if even the Marcionites themselves do not lust after women?

We have already frequently shown that heresies are placed by the Apostle among evils as an evil, and that those “approved” are to be understood as those who avoid heresies as an evil. Likewise, with regard to the sacrament of the bread and the cup, we have already proved in the Gospel (Luke 22) the reality of the Lord’s body and blood [Note: [Col. 0489D] “We have proved the reality of the Lord’s body and blood.” Namely in the preceding book; this was done against Marcion’s perverse opinion that Christ’s flesh was phantasmal. LE PR.], against Marcion’s phantom. And that every mention of judgment properly belongs to the Creator, as the judging God, has been treated throughout almost the whole work.

I now speak of spiritual gifts: these too were promised with reference to Christ by the Creator, under that most just preliminary rule, as I judge it, according to which the fulfillment should be believed to belong to none other than the one whose promise has been proved. Isaiah declared (Isa. 11:1): “A rod will go forth from the root of Jesse” [Note: [Col. 0489D] Pamelius adds “from the rod”], “and a flower will rise from the root; [Col. 0489B] the Spirit of the Lord will rest upon him.” He then enumerates its forms: “the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, and the spirit of knowledge and religion; the spirit of the fear of God will fill him.”

For under the figure of the flower he showed that Christ would arise from a rod sprung from the root of Jesse—that is, from a virgin of the lineage of David, son of Jesse—and that in this Christ the whole substance of the Spirit would abide. This was not as though it would subsequently come upon him, since he had always been the Spirit of God, even before the flesh; lest from this you argue that the prophecy pertains to that Christ who, merely as a man descended solely from the stock of David, would afterward obtain the spirit of his god. Rather, from the time when he flowered in flesh taken from the line of David, the whole operation of spiritual grace was to rest upon him, and was to cease and come to an end as regards [Col. 0489C] the Jews. The event itself bears witness to this, since from that time nothing of the Creator’s Spirit has breathed among them, after the wise and prudent master-builder, counselor, and prophet was taken away from Judaea (Isa. 3:2); so that this is the meaning of “The Law and the Prophets were until John” (Luke 16:16).

Now learn how he also declared that the gifts would come after Christ had been received into heaven: “He ascended on high,” that is, into heaven; “he led captivity captive,” that is, death or human servitude; “he gave gifts” [Note: [Col. 0489D] “he gave things given,” Pamelius] “to the sons of men,” that is, the free gifts that we call *charismata*. With elegance he said “to the sons of men,” not indiscriminately “to men,” thereby showing us to be sons of men—that is, truly men—the sons, namely, of the Apostles. “For in the Gospel,” he says, “I begot you.” And (Gal. 4:19): “My children, whom I am again bringing to birth.”

There is also that promise of the Spirit made without qualification [Col. 0489D] through Joel (Joel 2): “In the last days [Note: [Col. 0489D] ‘times,’ Semler] I will pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh, and their sons and daughters will prophesy; and upon my male and female servants I will pour out of my Spirit.” And assuredly, if the Creator promised the grace of the Spirit for [Col. 0490A] the last days, while Christ, the dispenser of spiritual gifts, appeared in the last days—as the Apostle says (Gal. 4:4), “But when the time was fulfilled, God sent his Son,” and again (1 Cor. 7:29), “Because the time has now been shortened”—it is evident from the preaching concerning the final times that this grace of the Spirit belongs to the Christ of the preacher who foretold it.

Compare, then, the Apostle’s several kinds with Isaiah’s (Isa. 11). “To one,” he says, “is given through the Spirit a word of wisdom”; Isaiah immediately likewise placed the spirit of wisdom. “To another, a word of knowledge”: this will be the word of understanding and counsel. “To another, faith in the same Spirit”: this will be the spirit of religion and the fear of God. “To another, the gift of healings; to another, of mighty works”: this will be the spirit of might. “To another, prophecy; to another, discernment of spirits; to another, kinds of tongues; to another, interpretation [Col. 0490B] of tongues”: this will be the spirit of knowledge. You see the Apostle agreeing with the Prophet both in making the distribution of the one Spirit and in explaining spirituality [Note: [Col. 0489D] “speciality,” Venetian edition].

I can say that the very man who likened the unity of various *charismata*, through the many and diverse members of our body, to an articulated whole is thereby shown to acknowledge the same Lord of both the human body and the Holy Spirit. He did not will that any rank [Note: [Col. 0489D] “ranks,” Rhenanus] of the *charismata* should exist in the body of the Spirit which he had not also assigned in the human body. He also instructed the Apostle concerning love, which must be preferred to all *charismata*, by that chief commandment which Christ too approved: “You shall love the Lord with all your heart, and all your strength, and all your soul, and your neighbor [Note: [Col. 0490D] “your neighbor,” François] as yourself” [Note: [Col. 0490D] “self” omitted by Oberthür].

And although it was written in the law, he recalls that the Creator would speak “in other tongues and with other lips”; by this recollection he confirms the gift of tongues, and here too he cannot be thought to have confirmed an alien gift by the Creator’s proclamation.

Likewise, in prescribing silence for women in the church, so that they should not speak, at least for the purpose of learning [Note: [Col. 0490D] “teaching,” Rhenanus; “speaking,” Pamelius] [Note: [Col. 0490D] “glory,” Semler], while elsewhere he has already shown that they too possess the right of prophesying, since he imposes a veil even on a woman who prophesies, he takes from the law his authority for a woman’s subjection—a law which, as I have said once for all, he should not have acknowledged except for the purpose of destroying it.

But now, so that we may leave spiritual gifts behind, the facts themselves will have to prove which of us rashly claims them for his own God, and whether [Note: [Col. 0490D] “because,” Paris and François; “from,” Rhenanus] the following objection can be made against our side: that although the Creator promised these things for his own Christ, who has not yet been revealed, as destined for the Jews alone, they will have [Col. 0490D] their operations in their own time, in their own Christ, and among their own people.

[Note: [Col. 0490D] Let Marcion, then, produce the gifts of his god. Let him bring forward his own prophecies concerning his god, provided that those prophets were moved by the Spirit of God; then I will certainly obey them. LE PR.]

Let Marcion, then, produce the gifts of his god: some prophets who spoke not from human understanding but from the Spirit of God, who both foretold future events [Col. 0491A] and exposed the secrets of the heart. Let him publish some psalm, some vision, some prayer—provided it is spiritual, in ecstasy, that is, in madness—if an interpretation of tongues has accompanied it. Let him prove to me that even [Note: [Col. 0491D] “even to me,” Pamelius] a woman among his own people has prophesied—one, namely, of those more holy women of his, a woman who speaks great things [Note: [Col. 0491D] “I shall regard as great,” Venetian edition].

[Note: [Col. 0491D] “One of those more holy women of his.” Perhaps he indicates Maximilla, a follower of Montanus; although I would not rashly deny that he is here censuring many women, such as Helena of Simon Magus, Philumena of Apelles, Marcellina of Carpocrates, and that woman of Marcion himself whom he had sent to Rome to prepare a house for him. LE PR.]

If all these things are more readily produced by me, and if they assuredly agree with the Creator’s rules, arrangements, and disciplines, then without doubt both Christ, the Spirit, and the Apostle belong to my God. Whoever wishes to demand it has my declaration.

## CHAPTER IX

Meanwhile the Marcionite will produce nothing of this kind [Note: [Col. 0491D] “of such a kind,” Venetian edition] (1 Cor. 15), since he is afraid even now [Note: [Col. 0491D] “even,” Latin edition] to declare whose Christ has the better claim still not to have been revealed [Note: [Col. 0491D] “whose, exactly,” Latin edition]. Just as mine is to be awaited because he was proclaimed from the beginning, so his is not to be awaited precisely because he was not from the beginning. We do better not to believe in a Christ still to come than the heretic does to believe in none at all.

[Note: [Col. 0491D] How some at that time denied the resurrection of the dead. He means the Sadducees, whom we discussed at sufficient length above. LE PR.]

We must first examine how some people at that time denied the resurrection of the dead. Assuredly, they did so in the same way as people do now, since it is always the resurrection of the flesh that is denied. Many of the philosophers, however, claiming that the soul is divine, promise its preservation; and the common people themselves honor the departed on the assumption that their souls continue to exist. Bodies, on the other hand, whether immediately consumed by fire or by beasts, or even buried with the greatest care, plainly are nevertheless destroyed by time. If, therefore, the Apostle refutes those who deny the resurrection of the flesh, he certainly maintains against them the very thing they denied: namely, the resurrection of the flesh. [Col. 0491C] There you have the answer in brief. What follows is now added for completeness.

For the very expression “resurrection of the dead” requires us to defend the proper meanings of the words. Nothing is “dead” [Note: [Col. 0491D] “of the dead,” Paris; Semler accordingly omits “by the word”] in the proper sense of the word except that which has lost the soul by whose power it lived. It is the body that loses the soul and, by losing it, becomes dead; thus the term “dead” properly applies to the body. Further, if resurrection is the resurrection of the dead, and the dead is nothing other than the body, the resurrection will be that of the body.

Likewise, the term *resurrection* claims for itself no other thing than that which has fallen. For something that has never fallen at all but has always lain there may be said to rise; but to rise again belongs only to something that has fallen. Because it rises again after having fallen, it is said to “re-surge.” For the syllable *re-* is always used to denote repetition. We therefore say that the body falls into the earth through death, as the fact itself also bears witness, according to God’s law; for it was said to the body (Gen. 3:19): “Earth you are, and into the earth you will go.” Thus what comes from the earth will go into the earth. It is this that departs, which will go into the earth [Note: [Col. 0492D] “What goes into the earth falls; it departs,” Ciacconius]; it is this that rises again, which falls.

“Because through a man came death, and through a man resurrection.” Here Christ’s body is shown to me in the name “man,” for a man consists of a body, as we have already often taught. And if “in Christ we are all made alive” just as we are put to death in Adam, then, since [Note: [Col. 0492D] “in what manner,” Latin edition] we are put to death bodily in Adam, it is necessary that we likewise be made alive bodily in Christ. Otherwise [Col. 0492B] the parallel does not hold, unless the being-made-alive in Christ corresponds in the same substance to the being-put-to-death in Adam.

But he has inserted something further concerning Christ, which must not be omitted because of the present dispute. For the resurrection of the flesh will be proved all the more strongly, the more I show that Christ belongs to the God with whom the resurrection of the flesh is an article of belief. When he says, “For he must reign until he puts his enemies beneath his feet,” he thereby already proclaims a God who avenges, and consequently the very God who promised this to Christ (Ps. 109): “Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet”; “The Lord will send forth the rod of your power from Zion, and he will rule” [Note: [Col. 0492D] “you will rule,” François; “and rule,” another reading] “in the midst of your enemies”; “with you,” and the rest [Note: [Col. 0492D] “and the rest” omitted by Semler].

But it is necessary for me to defend as belonging to my position [Col. 0492C] those Scriptures which [Note: [Col. 0491D] The Jews endeavor to wrest from us—namely, the rabbis, who, to defend their obstinacy, endeavor to snatch that passage from our hands, explaining “he will rule in the midst of your enemies” as referring to Hezekiah. LE PR.] the Jews also endeavor to wrest from us. They say, indeed, that this Psalm (Ps. 109) was sung concerning Hezekiah, because he sat at the right hand of the Temple and God turned away and destroyed his enemies (2 Kings 19). Consequently, they say that “Before the morning star, from the womb I begot you” also applies to Hezekiah [Note: [Col. 0492D] “And to Hezekiah’s birth.” The blessed Epiphanius rightly refutes this against the Ebionites, *Heresy* 30, proving from Scripture that Hezekiah had been born eleven years before Isaiah foretold, in chapter 7, “Behold, a virgin will conceive.” It was enough for the blessed Justin to say that Hezekiah was not born from a virgin. PAM.], and to [Note: [Col. 0492D] “before,” Pithou’s codex] Hezekiah’s birth.

[Note: [Col. 0492D] “We publish the Gospels,” etc. Here he declares that Marcion rejected both the first three chapters of Luke’s Gospel and the whole Gospel of Matthew, since Tertullian wishes to adduce evidence from Luke 2 and Matthew 4, which the blessed Justin likewise cites a little earlier. PAM.]

We set forth the Gospels, concerning whose credibility we surely ought by now, in so extensive a work, [Note: [Col. 0492D] “In so extensive a work we ought to have confirmed them.” “Them” means, of course, the Jews. RIG.] to have convinced these men; they declare that the Lord’s birth occurred at night. Thus “before the morning star” is fulfilled both by the star through which he was made known to the Magi and by the angel’s testimony, for the angel announced to the shepherds at night that Christ had just then been born; and also by the place of birth, since people arrive at an inn for the night. Perhaps it was also mystically arranged that Christ should be born at night, since he was to be the light of truth amid the darkness of ignorance.

Nor would God have said, “I begot you,” unless he spoke to a child who was his son [Note: [Col. 0493C] “true,” Ciacconius, approved by Junius]. For even though he says of the whole people (Isa. 1), “I begot sons,” he did not add, “from the womb.” Why, moreover, did he add “from the womb”—so pointlessly, as though anyone could doubt that a human being was born from a womb—unless he wished it to be understood with more precise attention as referring to Christ: “From the womb I begot you,” that is, from the womb alone, without a man’s seed, [Note: [Col. 0493C] “Assigning the flesh from the womb to the Spirit.” In that Psalm the Holy Spirit applied the words “from the womb” to signify the flesh. RIG.] the Spirit assigning the flesh from the womb?

This too follows here [Note: [Col. 0493C] “This follows from the passage itself,” Oberthür]: “You are a priest forever.” Yet Hezekiah was neither a priest nor, even if he had been one, a priest forever. “According to the order,” he says, “of Melchizedek.” What has [Col. 0493B] Hezekiah to do with Melchizedek, priest of the Most High, and indeed uncircumcised, who blessed the already circumcised Abraham after receiving from him the offering of tithes?

But the order of Melchizedek will apply to Christ, since Christ is the proper and lawful high priest of God, the chief priest of the uncircumcised priesthood, then established among the nations, by whom he was to be received more readily. When at last he comes, he will deem the circumcision, and the race of Abraham which will one day acknowledge him, worthy of acceptance and blessing [Note: [Col. 0493C] Semler adds “and”].

There is also another Psalm (Ps. 71), beginning thus: “O God, give your judgment to the king,” that is, to Christ who is to reign; “and your righteousness to the king’s son,” that is, to the people of Christ. For those who are reborn in him are his sons. But [Note: [Col. 0494C] “thus inserted,” Latin edition, Semler, and Oberthür] this Psalm too will be said to have been sung for Solomon. Yet could not those details which apply to Christ alone teach [Col. 0493C] that all the other details too pertain not to Solomon but to Christ?

“He will descend,” it says, “like rain upon a fleece, and like drops dripping upon the earth,” describing his gentle and imperceptible descent [Col. 0494A] from heaven into the flesh. But even if Solomon descended from somewhere, he did not descend like rain, because he did not descend from heaven. I shall, however, present the more straightforward statements. “He will rule,” it says, “from sea to sea, and from the river to the ends of the earth.” This was given to Christ alone. Solomon, by contrast, ruled over the single and small territory of Judaea. “All kings will worship him”—whom would they all worship, if not Christ? “And all nations will serve him”—whom would they all serve, if not Christ? “His name will endure forever”—whose name is eternal, if not Christ’s? “His name will remain before the sun,” for before the sun was the Word of God, that is, Christ. “And all nations will be blessed in him”; no nation is blessed in Solomon, but every nation is blessed in Christ.

What if this Psalm now also demonstrates that he is God [Note: [Col. 0494C] “my God,” Rhenanus with the Venetian edition]? “And they will call him blessed, because blessed is the Lord God of Israel, who alone performs wonders. Blessed is the name of his glory, and the whole earth will be filled with his glory.” By contrast, Solomon—I dare say it—even lost the glory he had possessed in God, after being drawn by a woman all the way into idolatry.

Accordingly, since this statement too is placed in the middle of the Psalm, “His enemies will lick the dust,” being of course subjected beneath his feet, it pertains to the point on account of which I both introduced this Psalm and defended it as belonging to my position. Thus I have confirmed that the one who will obtain [Note: [Col. 0494C] “the one who is to obtain,” Pamelius] both the glory of the kingdom and the subjection of his enemies, according to the Creator’s arrangement, must be believed to be none other than the Creator’s Christ.

Yet I do not believe that Tertullian’s text was of this form. For to the Apostle’s question, “What will those do who are baptized for the dead, if the dead do not rise?” it would have been an absurd response: “The Kalends of February would make petitions for the dead.” That institution which Septimius believed to have existed among certain people in the age of the Apostles—being baptized for the dead—would not thereby be assisted in any way. Indeed, the Apostle’s question presupposes that the dead do not rise. And so, to one saying that the dead do not rise, who would have responded so foolishly that the Kalends of February would make petitions for the dead? For dead people who do not rise, the February rites (*februa*) would have been as absurd and useless as baptism.

Let us therefore see what Septimius meant. He cites the Apostle’s words: “What will those do who are baptized for the dead, if the dead do not rise?” He thinks these words signify that it had then been instituted among certain people that they should be baptized on behalf of the dead. He then says: “Let that institution answer for itself, whether it rests upon any rational basis.” And immediately he compares with it a pagan custom: “The Kalends of February,” he says, “correspond to it and may be compared with it: making petitions for the dead”—*τῷ εὔχεσθαι ὑπὲρ τῶν νεκρῶν*, “with praying on behalf of the dead”—that is, with that rite or institution, namely being baptized or making petitions on behalf of the dead. > The month of February has its vows for the god Dis.

Likewise:

> After the worship of the gods above, in the neighboring month of February,  
> Numa gives funeral offerings to the ancestral spirits of his kin.
