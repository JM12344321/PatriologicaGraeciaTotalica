## CHAPTER XIX. On the Laws Enacted against Christians, and the Emperors’ Hatred or Favor toward Them

### ARTICLE I. How Tertullian Shows That the First Laws against Christians Were Enacted by Nero, a Most Wicked Emperor, and Then by Domitian, Who Shared in Nero’s Cruelty, but Who Nevertheless Restored Those Whom He Had Banished

[Col. 1006A]

Tertullian demonstrated more than sufficiently how absurdly the pagans alleged that they were bound by their laws to harass and kill Christians, when they themselves violated or scorned with impunity the institutions, customs, and more sacred laws of their ancestors. For, as our Author says, they praised the ancients but lived each day in a novel fashion; and by excluding antiquity from their affairs and duties, they brought the venerable authority of their fathers to ruin (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 6, p. 305). But he did not think all this sufficient, and attacks the very head of the accusation. He therefore makes it manifest that the laws enacted against Christians were most unjust and were established by the worst and most criminal rulers. [Col. 1006B] No less clearly does he demonstrate that good and pious rulers either never raged against Christians or never ordered that violence be done to them. This he shows by a brief survey of the emperors from the beginning of the Christian religion down to his own time (Idem, *Ad Nationes*, book I, chap. 10, p. 572).

For when the Christian name began to become widely known throughout the world (Idem, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 290), the emperor Tiberius, as we observed above (supra, chap. 8, art. 4), wished to include Christ among the gods; and although the senate prevented him, he nevertheless persisted in his opinion. So far was he from enacting any law against Christians that, as Tertullian says, he even threatened their accusers with danger—that is, with punishment and execution [Col. 1006C] (Tertullian, ibid.).

Nor did our Author ever believe that the emperors Caligula and Claudius decreed anything against those same Christians. For in the same passage he added: “Consult your records; there you will find that Nero was the first to rage with the imperial sword against this sect when it was just then arising at Rome” (Tertullian, loc. cit.). We shall explain below why the first persecution of the Christians is said to have been stirred up by this emperor (*Dissert. on Lucius Caecilius*, book *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*, chap. 6, art. 1). We shall therefore observe only that some have thought that by these words, “your records,” our Author designated either the *Annals* of Cornelius Tacitus or, more probably, Suetonius’ account of the life of Nero. For elsewhere he said: “We read the *Lives of the Caesars*: Nero was the first to stain the rising faith with blood” [Col. 1006D] (Tertullian, *Scorpiace*, chap. 15). This conjecture does not, indeed, appear wholly inept. Yet what prevents us from supposing that by the term “record” he also meant the secret or public acts of the Romans, in which, as we noted above (supra, chap. 9, art. 3), extraordinary events were preserved in written form?

However the matter may stand, the Christian religion assuredly even glories in Nero, the most wicked tyrant of all, or, as Tertullian puts it, “such an inaugurator of our condemnation” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 293). For, having been condemned by him, it is thereby proved to be good and true. For, as he continues: “Anyone who knows him can understand that nothing but some great good was condemned [Col. 1007A] by Nero” (Idem, ibid.). But he pursues this point somewhat more fully in his book *Ad Nationes*: “Under Nero,” he says, “condemnation gained force, so that from this point you may weigh it by the character of the persecutor. If that ruler was pious, then Christians are impious; if he was just and chaste, then Christians are unjust and incestuous; if he was not a public enemy, then we are public enemies. That condemner demonstrated what sort of people we are, since he punished virtues that were, of course, rivals—that is, contrary—to his own most wicked morals. And yet, when all else had been erased, this one institution of Nero alone remained—just, no doubt, because it was unlike its author” (Idem, *Ad Nationes*, book I, chap. 7, p. 567).

The pagans could not, therefore, object to the Christians that they were bound by laws enacted by that most impious tyrant. For the first law sanctioned against them, enacted by one who led a life infamous for so many [Col. 1007B] and such horrendous crimes, certainly made manifest to everyone how chaste and innocent the Christians’ manner of life was. Furthermore, that most bitter enemy of all good condemned nothing but what was good and equitable. The pagans therefore could never be bound by his most unjust laws; and the Christians rightly and deservedly gloried in having been condemned by so impious a man. Finally, through his cruelty and crimes hitherto unheard of, he became so horrifying to everyone that all his monuments were erased and abolished. For, as Suetonius says, he was judged a public enemy by the senate and sentenced to be punished according to ancestral custom (Suetonius, book VI, *Life of Nero*, § 49). In Dio’s *Epitome* we read that the soldiers smashed and demolished his statues (Dio, *Epitome*, p. 196); and, if Plutarch is to be believed, the gladiator Spiculus, [Col. 1007C] after they had been dragged through the city, was buried beneath them in the forum and killed (Plutarch, *Life of Galba*, p. 1056).

But although the pagans rightly and deservedly rescinded all Nero’s acts and decrees, their blind fury against the Christians nevertheless caused Nero’s decree against them alone to remain when everything else had been erased. But Tertullian, mocking their madness, added: “Just, indeed, because unlike its author”—that is, it was as unjust as it was like the impious Nero himself and his other abominable deeds and laws.

Tertullian continues: “Domitian too had attempted it, a portion of Nero in cruelty; but, being also human, he readily suppressed what he had begun and even restored those whom he had banished” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 293). Eusebius reproduced all these words in Greek and cites another witness, Hegesippus, who recorded that Domitian, [Col. 1007D] after questioning certain relatives of Christ about their ancestry and the kingdom of Christ himself, enacted nothing harsher against them, but dismissed them free and, by issuing an edict, checked the persecution that had been stirred up against adherents of the Christian religion: καταπαῦσαι διὰ προστάγματος τὸν κατὰ τῆς Ἐκκλησίας διωγμόν (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, chap. 20, p. 90). Finally, concerning both Domitian and Nero, our Author added: “Such have always been our persecutors: unjust, impious, shameful men, whom even you pagans have been accustomed to condemn, and whose condemned victims you have customarily restored” (Tertullian, ibid.).

In vain, therefore, and quite ineptly, those same pagans tried [Col. 1008A] to excuse their cruelty toward Christians under the empty pretext of edicts issued by these emperors. But we shall discuss both of those emperors, Domitian and Nero, and the persecution stirred up against the Christians, in another dissertation (infra, chap. 16, art. 3; *Dissert. on Lucius Caecilius*, chap. 6, arts. 1 and 2).

Moreover, if these two most wicked emperors sanctioned certain most unjust laws against the Christians, it is certainly established that the others who reigned in the interval between them, or after Domitian down to Tertullian’s age, either did not harass the Christians, favored them, or issued no edicts against them, but instead amended earlier ones. How certain this is, you will indeed recognize from these words of Tertullian, with which he challenges the pagans [Col. 1008B] as follows: “Of all the rulers from that time down to the present who possessed wisdom divine and human, produce any one who waged war upon the Christians” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 294). With what assurance, I ask, would he have dared to address the pagans so confidently, unless this had been entirely true? And certainly he had Melito, cited by Eusebius (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, chap. 26, p. 148), and several others, as we have shown elsewhere (*Dissert. on Lucius Caecilius*, chap. 6, art. 3), as supporters and advocates of his opinion, which he himself further confirms, as will be clear from the following articles.

### ARTICLE II. An Examination of Whether Marcus Aurelius Favored the Christian Religion Because Christian Soldiers Obtained from God, Not without a Great Miracle, Rain for His Army When It Was Dying of Thirst, and How He Did Not Remove the Penalties Imposed upon Those Same Christians, but Openly Rendered Them Ineffectual

[Col. 1008C]

The pagans abused the most unjust laws of the worst emperors in order to torture Christians with all the greater perversity because they knew with certainty that far better emperors of their own had either issued decrees in the Christians’ favor or had been their patrons and defenders. Tertullian calls these emperors by name, though without observing the chronological order of each. For he begins with Marcus Aurelius, whom he asserts to have been a protector of the Christians. Nor, he says, will anyone ever deny it: “If the letters of that most serious emperor are sought out, in which he testifies that the German thirst [Col. 1008D] was dispelled by rain obtained through the prayers of soldiers who happened to be Christians” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 295). It is indeed certain, and proved by the unanimous agreement of all, whether pagan or Christian, that these events occurred when Marcus Aurelius was drawing up his battle line to fight against the Germans and Sarmatians. For then his soldiers, who were dying of thirst, were revived by rain obtained from heaven. But someone may call into question whether it was obtained through the prayers of pagans or of Christians.

For several pagan writers attribute this rain to the prayer and piety of that same emperor. First among these we encounter Capitolinus, whose words concerning him are as follows: “By his own prayers he wrested lightning [Col. 1009A] from heaven against the enemy’s contrivance, while rain was obtained for his men when they were suffering from thirst” (Capitolinus, *Life of Marcus Antoninus*, p. 305). Themistius speaks more fully in a public oration to Theodosius: Ἀντωνίνῳ τῴ Ῥωμαίων ἀυτοκράτορι τοῦ στρατεύματος ὑπὸ δίψους αὐτῳ πιεζομένου, ἀναχὼν τὼ χεῖρε ὁ βασιλεὺς πρὸς τὸν οὐρανὸν· Ταύτῃ, ἔφη, τῇ χεῖρι προυτρεψάμην σε, καὶ ἱκέτυσα τὸν ζωῆς δοτήρα, ᾗ ζωὴν οὐκ ἀφελόμην, καὶ ὅυτω κατῄδεσε τὸν θεὸν τᾖ εὐχᾖ, ὥστε ἐξ αἰθρίας ἦλθον νεφέλαι ὐδροφεροῦσαι τοῖς στρατιώταις. “When the army of Antoninus, emperor of the Romans, was suffering from thirst, he raised his hand to heaven, saying: ‘With this hand I wished as a suppliant to appease you, and I prayed to the giver of life, since with it I have deprived no one of life.’ By that prayer he appeased the divine power, and immediately, when the sky had cleared, a great abundance of rain burst forth to quench the soldiers’ thirst” (Themistius, [Col. 1009B] Oration 15, *To Theodosius*, p. 191). He then immediately added that he remembered seeing a painting represented in a picture in which the emperor was depicted in the midst of the battle line and the soldiers were shown catching the rain in their helmets. Baronius, moreover, presented a representation of the column upon which that miraculous rain is seen being granted by Jupiter as giver of rain and wielder of thunder (Baronius, under the year of Christ 176, § 24).

In Dio’s *Epitome*, however, we read that a report had spread that Arnuphis, an Egyptian magician, invoked Mercury by certain magical arts, together with other demons who dwell in the air, and by their agency wrested forth this rain (Dio, *Epitome*, p. 274). Although Xiphilinus strives with all his might to accuse Dio of falsehood there (Xiphilinus, ibid., p. 275), Claudian, uncertain and doubtful in mind, nevertheless set out both opinions in these verses [Col. 1009C]:

> … Whether Chaldean spells, in a magician’s rite,  
> Armed the gods, or—as I believe—the whole  
> Service of the Thunderer was merited by Marcus’ character.  
> (Claudian, *On the Sixth Consulship of Honorius*, v. 357 ff.)

But this doubt of the pagans, also noted by Lampridius, arose from nothing other than their innate hatred of Christians and their customary flattery of their rulers (Lampridius, *Life of Heliogabalus*, p. 141).

For Eusebius quite rightly asserts that greater credence should be given to our own writers, who, as devotees of truth, committed the event itself to writing simply and candidly (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chap. 5, p. 169). And lest anyone deny him credence, he cites two witnesses. The first is Apollinaris, who had recorded in writing [Col. 1009D] that Aurelius gave that legion of Christian soldiers the name “Thundering” for this reason: because it had obtained that rain by divine favor. But the pagans, doubtless seizing upon the occasion supplied by this cognomen, invented the claim that it had been brought about by Jupiter the Thunderer. The second witness praised by Eusebius is our own Tertullian, whose words from the *Apologeticus*, transcribed by us, he cited in Greek translation. But he could also have cited another of his books, in which he affirms no less clearly and confidently that this astonishing rain was granted by God in response to the Christian soldiers’ prayers. For he wrote as follows to Scapula, the pagan governor: “Marcus Aurelius, during the German expedition, obtained rains in that drought through the prayers made to God by Christian soldiers. When have droughts not also been driven away by our kneeling [Col. 1010A] and fasting? Then even the people, crying out to the God of gods, who alone is mighty, bore witness to our God under the name of Jupiter” (Tertullian, *To Scapula*, chap. 4). From these words, moreover, you may readily perceive that there is nothing at all surprising if pagan writers proclaimed that the miraculous rain had been granted by their Jupiter, since they were accustomed to attribute similar miracles, worked through the prayers of Christians, to that same Jupiter. But, as Tertullian very aptly observes, unwittingly and unwillingly they acclaimed our true and almighty God under the name of Jupiter.

Someone may perhaps ask why, in this latter passage, he pronounced so confidently that this rain was granted in response to Christian prayers, when in the *Apologeticus* he added the adverb “perhaps.” Did he at some point hesitate in doubt [Col. 1010B] on this matter, or was that adverb improperly inserted into his text? For it does not occur in Eusebius’ Greek text. Yet we do not dare assert this against the authority of the manuscripts. Is it not more probable, then, that the adverb belongs not to one who doubts but to one who mocks the pagans, who foolishly believed that this rain had been given by their fictitious Jupiter?

The matter would be more evident to everyone if the letters of the emperor Aurelius cited by our Author had come down to us. Some, however, suspect that they are those of which a copy is printed after the First *Apology* of Justin Martyr. But we have already noted that document to be spurious and adulterated (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 369).

From these words of Tertullian, “if they are sought out,” Scaliger thinks it may be inferred [Col. 1010C] that Tertullian had never read them (Scaliger, *Observations on Eusebius’ Chronicle*, at the year 2189). He attempts to establish this also by the testimony of Orosius. But how could Tertullian so confidently urge the pagans to read them unless he himself had read either the letters or what was written in them? Moreover, Eusebius concludes from these same words of Tertullian that these letters existed in Tertullian’s time: εἴσετι νῦν φέρεσθαι (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chap. 5, p. 169). Why, then, Scaliger argues, did Eusebius not transcribe them in full with his customary diligence, if they truly existed at that time? But that objection is altogether feeble unless it is clearly proved that there is absolutely no document of this kind that Eusebius failed to transcribe. Scaliger, or anyone else, will never be able to prove this.

[Col. 1010D] Moreover, Marcus Aurelius, mindful of so great a benefit received from heaven through the prayers of Christians, showed them favor insofar as he could. For since he was unable wholly to suppress the exceedingly great fury of the pagans against the Christians, “Just as,” says Tertullian, “he did not openly remove the penalty from such persons, so in another way he openly rendered it ineffectual, even adding condemnation for the accusers, and indeed a more severe one” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 296). For he did not dare entirely to remove, relax, and remit the penalties that had previously been established against Christians by the laws of other emperors. Yet, in order to restrain the insolence of the pagans, who never ceased accusing them, he imposed upon these accusers other penalties, more severe than those imposed by Trajan, whom our Author cites in the same passage, as we shall shortly see; [Col. 1011A] and by this prudent compromise he protected the Christians for his part.

Moreover, Eusebius transcribed for us the complete text of a law addressed in the name of Marcus Aurelius Antoninus to the Council of Asia and issued in favor of the Christians, the concluding words of which are these: Εἰ δέ τὶς ἐπιμένοι τίνα τῶν τοιούτῶν εἷς πράγματα φέρων ὡς δὴ τοιοῦτον, ἐκεῖνος ὁ καταφερόμενος ἀπολελύσθω τοῦ ἐγκλήματος, καὶ ἐὰν φαίνεται τοιούτος ὥν· ὁ δὲ καταφέρων, ἔνοχος ἔσθαι δίκης. “But if anyone still persists in causing trouble for any of them on the ground that he is a Christian, the accused shall be acquitted of the charge, even if it is established that he is in fact a Christian. The informer himself, however, shall suffer punishment” (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, chap. 14, p. 127). We know, indeed, that some have thought this law to have proceeded not from Marcus Aurelius [Col. 1011B] but from Antoninus Pius. Yet if this opinion is true, it equally serves Tertullian’s purpose. For he also places this same Antoninus Pius among the good rulers who sanctioned no edicts against Christians (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 297).

Perhaps someone will ask us how Tertullian can extol in such praises Marcus Aurelius’ solicitude and favor toward Christians, when during his reign, as some think, the fourth persecution of the Christian Church arose, as we shall observe later (*Dissert. on Lucius Caecilius*, chap. 1, art. 3, and chap. 6, art. 3). But that very law cited immediately above supplies us with an answer. For although penalties were decreed in it against the accusers of Christians, the gods [Col. 1011C] of the pagans were nevertheless first praised. But this commendation increased rather than extinguished the raging frenzy of the pagans against them. What, moreover, of the fact that those same pagans were convinced that other laws issued against the Christians were not abrogated by these more recent ones? Besides, what could laws accomplish against the unbridled fury of a populace blinded by various superstitions and against the mad assaults of the mob? Were not almost innumerable martyrs, as Eusebius relates, most cruelly killed in these times in contempt of these laws (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chap. 5, p. 153)? Nothing, therefore, should be inferred from this against Marcus Aurelius’ favorable disposition toward Christians, since he permitted what he could not prevent.

### ARTICLE III. An Examination of the Grounds upon Which Tertullian Asserts That the Laws Written against Christians Were Mitigated by the Emperor Trajan, but That No Laws against Them Were Enacted by Hadrian, Investigator of All Curiosities; by Vespasian, Conqueror of the Jews; by Pius; or by Verus—or Severus—the Emperors

[Col. 1011D]

Tertullian retraces his steps and passes from Marcus Aurelius to Trajan, who was earlier than he. Trajan, as Tertullian himself puts it, partially frustrated the laws enacted against Christians by forbidding Christians to be sought out (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 296). Earlier decrees of the emperors had ordered that they be sought out and punished; but Trajan, as we showed above (supra, chap. 7, art. 2), did indeed forbid their being sought out, while ordering that those who were brought before the authorities be punished.

[Col. 1012A] But his successor Hadrian established no law whatever against them, although he seemed bound to be more hostile to them than others. For he was, says our Author, “an investigator of all curiosities” (Idem, ibid., p. 297)—that is, wholly devoted to every kind of magical art. And, according to Xiphilinus, he was certainly reproached with the vice of being πριεργοτατος καὶ μαντείαις μαγγανείαις τε παντοδαπαῖς ἐχρῆτο: “exceedingly inquisitive, and practicing divination and every magical art” (Xiphilinus, *Epitome of Dio*, p. 262). Ammianus Marcellinus, moreover, when speaking of Julian the Apostate, says: “He was excessively devoted to the consultation of omens, so that in this respect he seemed to equal the emperor Hadrian, being superstitious rather than a lawful observer of sacred rites” (Ammianus Marcellinus, book XXV, chap. 4, p. 427). Nevertheless, says Tertullian, no law [Col. 1012B] against Christians was sanctioned by this Hadrian. Accordingly, Melito wrote in his *Apology* to Marcus Aurelius Antoninus that Nero’s and Domitian’s lack of judgment had been corrected by rescripts of subsequent emperors: Ἐν οἶς ὁ μὲν πάππος σου Ἁδριανὸς πολλοῖς μὲν ἀλλοις, καὶ Φουνδάνῳ δὲ τῳ ἀνθυπάτῳ τὴς Ἀσίας γράφων φαίνεται. “Among these, your grandfather Hadrian sent letters not only to many others but also to Fundanus, proconsul of Asia” (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, chap. 26, p. 148). Jerome further attests that, when Hadrian visited Eleusis and was initiated into almost all the sacred rites of Greece, a book in defense of our Christian religion was presented to him; it so greatly astonished everyone that his excellent intelligence brought a most severe persecution to an end (Jerome, *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, § 19, and Letter 83, *To Magnus*). But [Col. 1012C] the pagans, finding in the impious emperor’s many superstitions an opportunity to harass Christians with impunity, raged against them most bitterly, although he enacted no law against them and indeed afterward calmed the storm that had arisen.

Then our Author proceeds still farther backward from Hadrian to Vespasian, who likewise, he says, issued no edict against Christians, although he was “the conqueror of the Jews” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 5, p. 294), whom almost all pagans customarily confused with those same Christians. Who does not know that the Jewish nation was conquered by him, and that at Rome he celebrated a triumph over them together with his son Titus? That he issued no decree concerning Christians is attested by Eusebius, who recorded [Col. 1012D] that the second persecution after Nero was stirred up by Domitian: Καὶπερ τοῦ πατρὸς αὐτοῦ Οὐεσπασιανοῦ μηδὲν καθ` ἡμῶν ἄτοπον ἐπινοήσαντος. “Although his father Vespasian had never attempted to inflict any injury upon us” (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, chap. 17, p. 88).

What more need be said? Pius also, who ruled long after Vespasian, Tertullian continues, neither approved nor signed any bloody laws against Christians (Tertullian, loc. cit.). For Melito, in the *Apology* already cited by us, addresses Marcus Aurelius in these words preserved by Eusebius: Ὁ δὲ πατὴρ σου, καὶ σοῦ τὰ σύμπαντα διοικοῦντος αὐτῷ, ταῖς πόλεσι περὶ τοῦ μηδὲν νεωτερίζειν περὶ ἡμῶν ἔγραψεν· ἐν οἶς, καὶ πρὸς Λαρισσαίους, καὶ πρὸς [Col. 1013A] Θεσσαλονικεῖς, καὶ πρὸς Ἀθηναίους, καὶ πρὸς πάντας Ἔλλήνας. “Your father also, during the time when you administered all affairs together with him, wrote to the cities that they were not to stir up disturbances against us—specifically to the people of Larissa, the Thessalonians, the Athenians, and finally to all the Greeks” (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, chap. 26, p. 148). Several learned men of our own age also think that he enacted, in favor of the Christians, the law which, as we noted a little above, others ascribe to Marcus Aurelius (preceding article).

The sixth and last of these more equitable emperors is named by Tertullian in these words: “No Verus impressed laws hostile to Christians” (Tertullian, loc. cit.); we must doubtless understand, “upon the public tablets, so that they might be promulgated.” But it is worth observing here [Col. 1013B] that these words, “No Verus,” are omitted in Eusebius’ text, which renders this passage of Tertullian in Greek (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chap. 5, p. 170). And certainly this same Verus, made co-ruler by Marcus Aurelius, reigned with him for nine years at most and died before him. For this reason, some suspect that this name was corrupted in Tertullian’s text and that “Severus” should be read. Pamelius, indeed, advises us that this reading is actually found in one Vatican manuscript and another at Liège. But because it is not read in all the other manuscripts, including those of better quality, we dare decide nothing against the authority of so many codices. Could not Tertullian also have mentioned him because, following the example of Aurelius, his colleague who had been adopted by him, he enacted no law against Christians and had devised nothing [Col. 1013C] against them?

Yet if anyone rather obstinately maintains that the name Severus was written there, we shall not strongly resist. For in another book Tertullian commends Severus’ singular inclination and benevolence toward Christians, all of whom—at least during the early years of his reign—he embraced because of a miracle performed for his benefit. But let us hear his words, if you please: “Severus himself,” he says, “the father of Antoninus Caracalla, was mindful of the Christians. For he sought out the Christian Proculus, surnamed Torpacion, the steward of Euhodias, who had once cured him by means of oil, and kept him in his palace until the man’s death. Antoninus too knew him very well, having been reared on Christian milk” (Tertullian, *To Scapula*, chap. 4). He thus informs us that the emperor Severus was miraculously [Col. 1013D] relieved of an illness by oil that Proculus had given him. And lest anyone either excessively obstinate or altogether unbelieving raise a doubt concerning so great a miracle, he specifies both Proculus’ surname and his office. He was called Torpacion, he says, and was the steward of Euhodias. But it is now exceedingly difficult to say who this person was.

In another book, however, Tertullian relates that the errors of the Valentinians were attacked not only by Justin Martyr, Miltiades, and Irenaeus, but also by Proculus, of whom he speaks as follows: “Our Proculus, the distinction of virginal old age and Christian eloquence; all of them, in every work of faith as in this one, I would wish to equal” (Idem, *Against the Valentinians*, chap. 5). But because [Col. 1014A] this book was composed by Tertullian after his defection to the Montanists, and Proculus is called “our Proculus,” some think it may be inferred that he was so called because he was attached to the same errors of the Montanists. Accordingly, they add that he is designated by these words of Jerome: “Gaius, under Antoninus the son of Severus, conducted a notable disputation against Proculus, a follower of Montanus” (Jerome, *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, § 19). But since this man was a Montanist, he certainly does not appear to have been the same as Proculus Torpacion. Moreover, the latter is called the steward of Euhodias, whereas the former was highly renowned for the distinction of his Christian eloquence.

But we learn from Dio’s *Epitome* that Evodus (Εὔοδον) was the tutor of Antoninus Caracalla, τοῦ τροφέος αὐτοῦ (Dio, *Epitome*, p. 333). Proculus could therefore have been the steward of this Evodus, or [Col. 1014B] Euhodias. For at this very time, as Tertullian says, he was residing in Severus’ palace, where Caracalla came to know him and, through his influence, was reared on Christian milk.

Moreover, whatever conclusion may be reached concerning this Proculus Torpacion, there is no room for doubt that Severus favored the Christians because his health had been restored to him in a wondrous manner. For he not only retained this Christian Proculus in his palace, but also, as Tertullian further asserts, “Severus, knowing that most distinguished women and most distinguished men belonged to this sect, not only did them no injury but even honored them with his testimony and openly resisted the populace when it raged against us” (Tertullian, *To Scapula*, chap. 4). He therefore truly favored the Christians, and not slightly, and showed himself to be not a secret but a public [Col. 1014C] patron and defender of them. Why, indeed, should he not have favored those from whom he had not only received the greatest benefit, but whose supreme loyalty toward himself he had also always experienced? For among the factions of Cassius, Niger, and Albinus which arose from the very beginning of his reign, as we saw above (supra, chap. 2, art. 1) and shall say again below, no Christian was ever found.

Nor should you say that this conflicts with what we stated above (ibid.), namely that a persecution against the Christians was stirred up by this same emperor Severus. For it was provoked not at the beginning of his reign, but after ten years and more. It is, however, established that until that time he favored the Christians to no small degree.

We think, moreover, that there is no fair judge of these matters [Col. 1014D] who will not readily understand from what has thus far been said how powerful is this argument of our Author, by whose weight he utterly overwhelms the pagans who claimed that they were bound by their laws to torture and destroy Christians. For he demonstrated that those laws were both most unjust and enacted by most unjust rulers. No less clearly did he disclose that they had been corrected and amended by several others endowed with uprightness and equity. Finally, those who enacted no law against the Christians were undoubtedly endeavoring to safeguard and protect them insofar as the injustice of the times permitted.

## CHAPTER XX. On Crimes of Injured Divine Majesty, of Which Christians Were Accused by Pagans Who Were Themselves Guilty of Them, as Falsely as Shamelessly

[Col. 1015A]

### ARTICLE I. How Invincibly Tertullian Demonstrates That Christians Were Undeservedly Accused of Infanticide—That Is, of Killing an Infant in Their Sacred Assemblies, Then Eating Its Flesh and Drinking Its Blood—and of Most Shameful Incest; and How He Utterly Overthrows the Pagans’ Empty Cavils, with Reference to Dog-Faced Men, Shadow-Feet, and Antipodes

No other pretext remained to the pagans for harassing and destroying Christians except to accuse them, by whatever means, of some capital [Col. 1015B] crime. But they had advanced to such a degree of fury and madness that they declared them guilty not merely of one enormity but of all crimes, and, as Tertullian says, enemies “of the gods, the emperors, the laws, morality, and the whole of nature” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 2, p. 277). But who, struck by this altogether horrendous accusation, would not immediately be stupefied? For how could Christians be guilty of so many and such great crimes when, as we have already demonstrated, they not only worshiped the one true God with the highest veneration, but also possessed the chastest morals and a most pious manner of life? So false an accusation therefore collapsed of itself. But because the Christian religion, as Tertullian puts it, “begs no indulgence for its cause” (ibid., chap. 1, p. 259), nor, as defendants commonly do, seeks any favor [Col. 1015C] or partiality, it therefore wishes and asks this one thing alone: that it be examined according to the stricter prescriptions of the laws. Accordingly, Tertullian undertakes to examine and refute individually all the charges brought against Christians, and demonstrates most manifestly that those same Christians were as falsely accused of these enormities as it was certain that the pagans were in truth guilty of them.

For what could be further from the truth than to charge with injured divine majesty those who, as we have said, always worshiped the true God both privately and publicly in their assemblies, and preferred to be killed by any of the most atrocious tortures rather than deny this one God or venerate false gods with even merely feigned and simulated worship? By contrast, the pagans were most evidently convicted of this supreme impiety, for they [Col. 1015D] worshiped fabricated gods, as we shall show below, whom they admitted to be infamous and defiled by every kind of most shameful crime.

Yet they never ceased crying out that Christians were guilty of offenses against the divine majesty because in their assemblies and amid their more sacred mysteries they most shamelessly polluted themselves with utterly abominable crimes. For, says Tertullian, they were called “most criminal on account of the sacrament of infanticide” (ibid., chap. 7, p. 306)—that is, the infant whom they were said to kill there and devour. Then, after that accursed banquet, they were said to be polluted by most shameful incest, because “the overturners of the lamps—the dogs, namely the procurers—provide the darkness and then also [Col. 1016A] the shamelessness required by impious lusts”; these too are words of our Author (ibid.). In our manuscripts and in the early editions, the word *then* is absent, nor does it seem very necessary there. Tertullian’s meaning, which he relates more briefly in another book, is this: after the lamps have been overturned and extinguished by dogs, darkness, performing the office of most impure procurers, provides the shamelessness required by impious lusts (Idem, *Ad Nationes*, book I, chaps. 15 and 16). These were the principal and capital crimes which the pagans, as we have noted elsewhere more than once (*Dissert. on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 8, art. 1 ff.), charged against Christians with as much long-continued shamelessness as the ease with which they were refuted and confuted by the ancient Fathers of the Church.

Our Tertullian, however, utterly overthrows [Col. 1016B] this false accusation by various cogent arguments. First, he addresses the pagans themselves in this manner: “You are forever saying that we are guilty of such great crimes; yet throughout the long time in which this has been said, you have not taken care to uncover, investigate, and disclose them” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 7, p. 307). Yet no one should be condemned before being convicted of a crime: “Therefore either uncover them,” he says, “if you believe them, and convict the Christians of these crimes, or do not believe them, because you have not uncovered them” (ibid.). Indeed, through this deliberate concealment and negligence, a presumption is established (ibid.), and by the surest prior judgment the Christians’ innocence and supreme moral integrity are proved: because “you yourselves do not dare to uncover” (ibid.) those crimes of which you accuse them as falsely as rashly, although their case has never, as we have seen, been heard before you condemn, torture, and kill them.
