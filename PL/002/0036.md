# CHAPTER II

You now have from us a ready answer to the *Antitheses* [Note: [Col. 0363D] “Antithesis,” another reading.]. I now pass to the demonstration that the Gospel—certainly not the Jewish Gospel, but the Pontic one—is at any rate adulterated, first laying out the order of the argument upon which we are entering.

We establish first of all that the authors of the Gospel Instrument are apostles, upon whom the duty of promulgating the Gospel was imposed by the Lord himself; and apostolic men as well [Note: [Col. 0363D] “And if apostolic men, nevertheless not alone,” etc. He says that the true Gospel was written only by apostles or apostolic men, in order to close the way against objections drawn from the corrupted copies of heretics, such as those of the Ebionites, Encratites, Nazarenes, Marcionites, etc. LE PR.], though not apostolic men alone, but together with apostles [Col. 0363C] and after apostles [Note: [Col. 0363D] “Apostolic men,” Jun.]. For the preaching of disciples might become suspect of ambition for glory if it were not supported by the authority of their masters—or rather by that of Christ, who [Note: [Col. 0364D] “Who,” feminine, Seml.] made the apostles their masters.

Accordingly, among the apostles John and Matthew instill the faith into us; among apostolic men Luke and Mark renew it, all beginning from the same rules with respect to the one God, the Creator, and his Christ, born of a virgin, the fulfillment of the Law and the Prophets. It does not matter if the arrangement of the narrative varies, provided that there is agreement concerning the chief article of the faith—an article concerning which there is no agreement with Marcion.

Marcion, by contrast, assigns no author to the Gospel—his own Gospel, that is—as though it had not been permissible for him also to fabricate a title for a work whose very body it was no sacrilege for him to overthrow. # CHAPTER III

Marcion, however, having obtained Paul’s epistle to the Galatians, [Col. 0364C] which even censures the apostles themselves (Gal. II) for not walking with a straight step according to the truth of the Gospel and at the same time accuses certain false apostles of perverting the Gospel of Christ, strives to destroy the standing of those Gospels that are published as their own and under the names of apostles, or even of apostolic men, so that he may transfer to his own Gospel the credibility that he takes away from them.

Now even if Peter, John, and James, who were regarded as pillars, were censured, the reason is manifest. For they seemed to vary their fellowship out of respect for persons. And yet, since Paul himself became all things to all people so that he might gain everyone (1 Cor. IX, 19), Peter too could have had this same purpose when acting somewhat differently from what he taught.

Likewise, even if false apostles had slipped in, their character also was disclosed: they were defending circumcision [Col. 0364D] and Jewish observances [Note: [Col. 0364D] “Jewish observances.” As in Galatians IV: [Col. 0365D] “observing days and months and seasons and years.” This interpretation must therefore be noted against modern heretics, who do not shrink from adducing that passage against the observances of the Catholic Church, although the author expressly interprets them as Jewish observances—and does so not only here but also below, book V, chapter 4, where he treats this passage. PAM.] [Note: [Col. 0364D] “Pride,” Cod. Pithae.; Seml.]. Thus Paul was censuring them not for their preaching but for their conduct; and he would equally have censured them if they had erred in anything concerning God the Creator [Col. 0365A] or his Christ.

The individual points must therefore be distinguished. If Marcion complains that the apostles should be held suspect of transgression and dissimulation, even to the point of corrupting the Gospel, he is already accusing Christ by accusing those whom Christ chose. But if the apostles did transmit the Gospel intact and were censured only for inconsistency in their fellowship, while false apostles interpolated their truth and our compilations derive from those interpolations, which, then, will be that authentic Instrument of the apostles which suffered the adulterations? Which one enlightened Paul, and through him Luke?

Or if it was so utterly destroyed—obliterated as though by some deluge, by such a flood of falsifiers—then Marcion himself no longer possesses the true Gospel. Or if the Gospel possessed by Marcion alone is the true one, that is, the apostles’ Gospel, how does it agree with ours, [Col. 0365B] which is attributed not to the apostles but to Luke? Or if the Gospel Marcion uses is not immediately to be attributed to Luke merely because it agrees with ours—because ours, of course, has been adulterated even in its title—whereas in all other respects it belongs to the apostles, then ours too, which agrees with his, likewise belongs to the apostles, though it too has been adulterated in its title.

# CHAPTER IV

The rope of contention [Note: [Col. 0365D] Fran. omits “therefore.”] must therefore be pulled, with equal effort wavering to and fro. I say that mine is true; Marcion says that his is. I affirm that Marcion’s is adulterated; Marcion says the same of mine. Who will decide between us, unless it is the reckoning of time, which prescribes authority for the Gospel found to be earlier and prejudges as corrupted the Gospel proved [Note: [Col. 0365D] “Found,” another reading; Seml. conjectures that both words were absent.] to be later?

For insofar as falsehood is a corruption of truth, truth must necessarily precede falsehood. The thing itself will be prior to the injury done to it, and the material prior to the rivalry directed against it. Otherwise, how absurd it would be if, after we had proved ours the earlier and Marcion’s the later, ours should seem to have been false before it possessed any truth as material for its falsehood, while Marcion’s should be believed to have experienced rivalry from ours before it was even published! And finally, how absurd that the later should be regarded as the truer, after so many and such great works and testimonies of the Christian religion had already been published in the world—works that certainly could not have been published without the truth of the Gospel, that is, before the truth of the Gospel!

So far, then, as Luke’s Gospel is concerned, since the version shared between us and Marcion is disputed as to its truth, the Gospel according to us is so much older than Marcion that Marcion himself once believed it. For in the first fervor of his faith he even contributed money [Col. 0365D] to the catholic Church, which was soon afterward cast out along with him when he defected from our truth to his own heresy.

What now if the Marcionites deny [Col. 0366A] his original adherence to our faith, even against the evidence of his own letter? What if they do not even acknowledge the letter? They certainly not only admit that the *Antitheses* are Marcion’s but even display [Note: [Col. 0365D] “Bring forth,” Lat. Jun.] them prominently. From them I obtain sufficient proof.

For if the Gospel attributed to Luke among us—whether it is also so attributed among the Marcionites we shall see—is the very Gospel that Marcion censures through his *Antitheses* as having been interpolated by defenders of Judaism in order to incorporate it with the Law and the Prophets, so that they might thereby also attach Christ to them [Note: [Col. 0366D] “Fashion Christ from them,” Seml.], he certainly could not have censured anything except what he had already found. No one censures future things that he does not know will occur; correction does not precede the fault.

Marcion alone, and Marcion first, appeared as the corrector of a Gospel that had been overturned from the age of Tiberius down to that of Antoninus. Christ had waited for him so long, already regretting [Col. 0366B] that he had hastily sent the apostles ahead without Marcion’s protection! But heresy is an affair of human rashness, not of divine authority; it always “corrects” the Gospels by corrupting them. Even if Marcion were a disciple, nevertheless “the disciple is not above the master” (Matt. X, 24). Even if [Note: [Col. 0366D] Venice omits “if.”] Marcion were an apostle: “Whether it was I,” says Paul (1 Cor. XV, 11), “or they, so we preach.” Even if Marcion were a prophet: “The spirits of the prophets will be subject to the prophets” (1 Cor. XIV, 32), for they are spirits not of overthrow but of peace. Even if [Note: [Col. 0366D] Jun. omits “if.”] Marcion were an angel, he should more readily be declared anathema than an evangelist, because he preached a different Gospel (Gal. I, 8).

Accordingly, while he corrects, he confirms both: ours as the earlier, since he corrects what he found; and his as the later, since by constructing it out of the correction of ours he made it both his own and new.

# CHAPTER VI

But from this point we enter upon another stage. As we promised, we now call Marcion’s Gospel itself into court, intending by this means also to prove it adulterated. Certainly, the entire purpose for which he labored—even by preparing the way with the *Antitheses*—compels him to establish a diversity between the Old and New Testaments, and consequently to separate [Note: [Col. 0368D] “Being about to separate,” Par. Fran.; “who was to separate,” Jun.] his own Christ from the Creator, as the Christ of another God and as a stranger to the Law and the Prophets.

[Note: [Col. 0367D] “Certainly, for this reason he erased everything contrary to his position.” It is characteristic of all heretics [Col. 0368D] to eradicate from the Scriptures those things that are contrary to their position—and indeed from our Scriptures too. Thus they reject the two books of Maccabees because these support the prayers of the saints after this life and sacrifices for the dead; the Epistle of James and the Apocalypse because they support the merits of good works; and finally the Second Epistle of Peter because they perceive that their own heresies in these last times were foretold there. PAM.]

Certainly, for this reason he erased everything contrary to his position that agreed with the Creator, on the pretense that it had been interwoven by the Creator’s defenders, while preserving what suited his own position. These passages we shall confront; these we shall embrace, if they belong more to us. If they strike down Marcion’s presumption, it will then also be established that the other passages were erased through the same defect of heretical blindness by which these were preserved.

Such will be the purpose and method of our little work, subject, of course, to the condition agreed upon by both sides. Marcion has established that there is one Christ who, in the time of Tiberius, was revealed by a God previously unknown for the salvation of all nations, and another Christ who was appointed by God the Creator to restore the Jewish state and is to come at some future time. Between these he rends open a vast and absolute [Note: [Col. 0368D] “The difference between the just and the good.” That is, between the severe and the gentle, between the one who judges and the one who shows mercy, between the one who punishes and the one who spares, between the Creator and the Redeemer. RIG.] difference: as great as that between the just and the good, between the Law and the Gospel, and between Judaism and Christianity.

Hence also comes our preliminary rule, by which we firmly establish that Christ, if he belongs to another God, must have nothing in common with the Creator. Otherwise, he must be declared the Creator’s Christ if he administers the Creator’s dispensations, fulfills his prophecies, supports his laws, makes his promises present, restores his mighty works, re-establishes his judgments, or expresses his dispositions and distinctive qualities.

I ask you, reader, to remember this agreement and this preliminary rule everywhere, and begin to recognize whether the Christ before you is Marcion’s or the Creator’s.
