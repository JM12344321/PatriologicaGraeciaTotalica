This paradox is attributed to Tertullian by Rufinus, in his *Apology for Origen*, and by St. Jerome, in Books II and III of his *Apology against the Same*. The former thinks that nothing should be definitively settled; the latter speaks with considerable hesitation, although he seems more inclined to regard the opinion as condemned. For in his *Apology to Pammachius against the Errors of John of Jerusalem* he declares in express terms that the ecclesiastical opinion is that God daily creates new souls and infuses them into bodies already conceived, citing in support of this John V, Zechariah XII, and Psalm XXXII. Meanwhile, however, in Epistle [Col. 0181D] 99, addressed to Marcellinus and Anapsychia, he speaks more cautiously, writing that this indeed seems true to him, but referring the matter to St. Augustine, since he is unwilling to define anything with certainty, having read that Augustine still held his judgment in suspense: he alludes to Augustine’s letters written to him, and to Epistle 157, addressed to Optatus, *On the Origin of Souls*. In that letter Augustine condemns Tertullian not so much for deriving souls by propagation as for making them corporeal—concerning which paradox more will be said shortly. For he says:

> Even those who assert that souls are propagated from that single soul which God gave to the first man, and who thus say that they are derived from parents, if they follow Tertullian’s opinion, certainly contend that they are not spirits but bodies, and that they arise from corporeal seeds. What more perverse statement could be made? Nor is it surprising [Col. 0182A] that Tertullian imagined this, since he believes that even God the Creator himself is nothing unless he is a body.

But more will be said below about that paradox also, from the same Augustine. Gennadius, however, in his book *On Ecclesiastical Dogmas*—which this passage alone makes clear is not by St. Augustine—after recounting Origen’s opinion and the opinion already mentioned, which he calls that of the Luciferians, Cyril, and certain Latins, among whom Tertullian was undoubtedly included, defines that, once the body has been formed, the soul is created and infused by God. Nor does the author of the *Dialogue on the Origin of Souls*, among the works of Sts. Jerome and Augustine, teach otherwise. We, however, have preferred to teach nothing on this point as an article of faith, since the Church, even after so many councils have since been celebrated, has, so far as I know, left nothing expressly defined. It will suffice that we added certain remarks in the Annotations, in which we indicated, first, that St. Jerome’s opinion is no more conclusively proved, [Col. 0182B] and, secondly, briefly answered from St. Augustine and Lactantius the absurd arguments of Tertullian, who thinks that the soul arises from corporeal seeds.

### 5. On the Sex of the Soul  
*Ibid.*, chapter 36.

Tertullian indeed deduces this from the foregoing paradox. For if the soul is sown together with the body, that conclusion seems to follow. But St. Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechesis* 4, and St. Augustine, Book I of *The Soul and Its Origin*, addressed to Vincentius, chapter 8, expressly define the contrary opinion. We have reproduced some of Augustine’s words in our annotations, to which we refer the reader. Augustine writes as follows:

> All souls, those of men as well as those of women, are beautiful and alike; only the members of the body create the distinction.

### [Col. 0182C] 6. That the Soul, Rather Than the Flesh, Is Sinful  
Vol. III, *On the Soul*, the whole of chapter 40, and chapter 58, no. 650; and Book V *Against Marcion*, chapter 10, no. 165.

Peter Lombard alludes to this paradox in the following words and refutes it at the same time; we also cited his words there, from Book II of the *Sentences*, chapter 31:

> Some believed that original sin is derived through the soul, and not only through the flesh, because they thought that not only the flesh but also the soul is derived by propagation. And therefore, just as flesh is generated from corrupted flesh, so too they held that from a sinful soul a sinful soul, infected by original corruption, is derived from those who beget it. But this, he says, the Catholic faith rejects and condemns as contrary to the truth, for it admits that not souls [Col. 0182D] but the flesh alone is derived by propagation.

At the same place, no. 455, we cited, in support of the contrary opinion, passages from Sts. Ambrose and Augustine quoted by the same Lombard.

### 7. On the Corporeal Soul  
Vol. I, *Apology*, chapter 48, nos. 608 and 609; Vol. III, *On the Soul*, the whole of chapters 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 22, 36, and 37; *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 11, no. 98; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 17, no. 131, chapter 45, no. 320, and chapter 53, no. 384; Book V *Against Marcion*, chapter 10, no. 158, and chapter 15, no. 248. That it is therefore not properly a spirit: *ibid.*, *On the Soul*, chapter 11; and Vol. IV, in the fragments of the book *On the Origin of the Soul against Hermogenes*.

St. Augustine, [Col. 0183A] in Book X of *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, the final two chapters, and in his book *On Heresies*, and Fulgentius, in Book II of *On Faith, to Peter*, charge the Author with this paradox. Augustine, however, reproves him not so much because he makes the soul corporeal—which, in a certain sense, some interpret acceptably in Hilary, Cassian, and the Commentary on Job attributed to Jerome—as because he calls it shaped and colored. Thus he writes in the former passage:

> Finally, Tertullian believed the soul to be a body for no other reason than that he was unable to conceive it as incorporeal, and therefore feared that it would be nothing if it were not a body. And although he is certainly acute, he is sometimes overcome by the truth when it appears contrary to his own opinion. For when he assigned the soul an airy and luminous color, he came to the senses, with which he attempted to furnish it member by member, as though it were a body, and said: “This will be the inner man, [Col. 0183B] another than the outer, one man in twofold form. He too has his own eyes and ears, with which the people ought to have seen and heard God; he also has the other limbs, through which he acts in thoughts and functions in dreams.” Behold the ears and eyes with which the people ought to have heard and seen God: those which the soul employs in dreams! Yet if anyone were to see Tertullian himself in a dream, Tertullian would never say that he had been seen by someone whom he himself had not in turn seen, or that the man had spoken with him when he had not spoken with the man. Finally, if the soul sees itself in dreams, when the members of its body are plainly lying in one place while it wanders through the various images which it sees, who has ever seen it in a dream as airy and luminous in color, except perhaps as he sees other things which he similarly sees falsely? For he can see this too, [Col. 0183C] but far be it from him to believe, once awake, that the soul is such. Otherwise, whenever he sees himself differently—as happens more frequently—either his soul will have been changed, or even then it is not the substance of the soul that is seen, but an incorporeal image of the body, which is formed in a wondrous manner, just as it is in thought. For what Ethiopian does not almost always see himself as black in dreams? Or, if he saw himself in another color, was he not all the more astonished, if his memory remained with him? Yet I do not know whether he would ever have seen himself with an airy and luminous color if he had never read or heard Tertullian.
>
> What of the fact that people are led by such visions and wish to dictate to us from the Scriptures that something of this sort is not the soul but God himself, such as he was figuratively shown to the spirits of the saints, and such as he is also represented in allegorical discourse? For those visions are like such [Col. 0183D] sayings. Thus they err, setting up in their hearts images of vain opinion, and failing to understand that the saints judged such visions of their own in the same manner as they judge when they read or hear such things divinely spoken in figures: just as the seven ears of grain and the seven cows signify seven years; and just as the linen sheet tied at its four corners, like a dish full of various animals, signifies the world with all its nations. So too with the rest, and much more with those things in which incorporeal realities are signified by corporeal things—not by actual things, but by images.

And again in the following chapter:

> Tertullian nevertheless refused to allow the soul to grow in substance as the body does, even stating the reason for his fear: lest, he says, it should also be said to diminish in substance and thus be believed destined to fail. Yet because he extends it locally through the body, he found no way out concerning the growth [Col. 0184A] of the soul, which he wishes to expand from a tiny seed until it equals the body in size. He says: “But its force, in which its natural endowments remain implanted, while the measure of the substance with which it was breathed forth from the beginning remains intact, is gradually extended with the flesh throughout the limbs.” Perhaps we would not understand this unless, by employing a comparison with things we see, he had made his meaning clear. He says: “Take a fixed weight of gold or silver, as yet an unworked mass. Its form is compact and for the present smaller than what it will be, yet it contains within the boundary of its measure the whole nature of gold or silver. Then, when the mass is spread into a sheet, it becomes greater than its original form through the expansion of its fixed weight, not through addition, since it is extended but not increased. Yet in this manner too it is increased while being extended; for it may increase in form, though not in quantity. Then also [Col. 0184B] the brightness of the gold or silver itself is enhanced. It had indeed already been present in the mass, but more obscurely, though not altogether absent. Then other forms and shapes are added according to the pliability of the material and the direction given to it by the craftsman, who contributes nothing to its measure except its form. Thus the soul’s growth must also be understood as not substantial but elicitive.”
>
> Who would have believed that a man with such a mind could have been so eloquent? But these things should inspire fear, not laughter; for he would never have been driven to this if he had been capable of conceiving something which both exists and is not a body. What, moreover, could be more absurd than to suppose that a mass of some metal can grow in one part while it is being hammered unless it diminishes in another, or increase in breadth unless it is reduced in thickness? Or that there is any body which, while retaining the quantity proper to its nature, grows in every direction without becoming rarer? How, [Col. 0184C] then, will the soul fill, from that drop of seed, the full size of the body which it animates, if the soul itself is also a body whose substance grows by no addition? How, I ask, will it fill the flesh which it gives life unless it becomes rarer in proportion to the greater mass that it animates? He was evidently afraid that it would fail by diminution if it grew, yet was not afraid that it would fail by becoming rarer while not growing.
>
> But why should I linger any longer, when both the discussion is proceeding to greater length than the need to conclude permits, and my opinion is already sufficiently clear—both what I hold with certainty, and where I still hesitate, and why I hesitate?

Again, in the latter passage:

> He calls the soul immortal indeed, but contends that it is a shaped body.

Thus far St. Augustine. In our own Annotations [Col. 0184D] on chapter 6, we also confirmed, from St. Gregory, Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory Nazianzen, and Claudianus’ *On the State of the Soul*, the Platonists’ arguments concerning the incorporeal soul. In chapters 7 and partly 8, we showed from Sts. Justin Martyr, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Epiphanius, and the aforementioned Claudianus that some of the Author’s own arguments, including certain arguments from Scripture, do not establish his conclusion. In chapter 9, nos. 109 and 110, we cited both Plato and Clement of Alexandria against the soul’s having a bodily form. Then in chapter 11 we quoted a passage from the Author’s *On Repentance*, chapter 3, no. 12, where he had previously held the contrary, calling the soul a spirit brought to completion by the breath of God. Nor can it truly be concluded from Isaiah XLII and LVII, which he cites there in his support, that the soul is not called a spirit in other passages of Scripture. Moreover, the sense in which the soul may be [Col. 0185A] called a body is explained by him in *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 35, no. 124, in these words:

> Even if I grant, he says, that something among substances is incorporeal, nevertheless the substance itself is the body of each thing.

It seems, therefore, that he was led into the absurdity of assigning the soul an airy and luminous color through certain false revelations of a Montanist woman—not Priscilla or Maximilla, as we once thought, for they had long before perished by hanging, but one of their disciples—as is clear from the aforementioned *On the Soul*, chapter 9, no. 111. St. Jerome somewhere attributes this same paradox to Origen, and so this Antidote may also be useful for Origen’s works.

### [Col. 0185B] 8. That the Soul Can Suffer Nothing without the Body  
Vol. I, *Apology*, chapter 48, no. 609; *On the Testimony of the Soul*, chapter 4, no. 14.

The Magdeburg Centuriators place this not among the paradoxes but among the dogmas of faith, and they confirm it from the passage of Tertullian just cited, but in vain. For the Author himself, as though retracting what he had declared without sufficient caution at the beginning of his faith, and having afterward been better instructed in the Catholic faith, develops at length and eloquently, throughout the final chapter of *On the Soul*, the doctrine that souls suffer in the underworld in the meantime—that is, until the Last Judgment. Among other things he writes:

> What, then, will happen during this interval? Shall we sleep? But souls do not sleep even in the living. How exceedingly unjust it would be if, even in the underworld, offenders should still fare well! Does not the soul by itself suffice to establish a claim to suffering? [Col. 0185C] How often, while the body is unharmed, is the soul alone tormented by bitterness, anger, and often by a weariness whose cause it does not itself know? Thus the soul knows how to suffer in the underworld without the flesh, because even while in the unharmed flesh it suffers, if it so wills. If it does this by its own choice in life, how much more by the judgment of God after death? For divine judgment pursues even thoughts alone and bare intentions. Therefore it is most fitting that the soul, even without awaiting the flesh, should be punished for what it committed without the flesh as its partner. In short, since we understand that prison which the Gospel presents (Matthew V) to mean the underworld, and interpret the last farthing as every smallest offense that must be paid for there, no one will doubt that the soul makes some payment in the underworld.

He also repeatedly teaches the same thing in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 17, nos. 133 and 134. At that [Col. 0185D] chapter we also adduced similar testimonies from the Author of the *Questions and Answers* among the works of St. Justin Martyr, from Lactantius, and from St. Augustine, and indicated that this opinion accords with that of the Fathers.

### 9. That the Souls of the Saints Are Cherished and Rejoice in Abraham’s Bosom, or Paradise, or beneath the Altar—that Is, in a Place Not Heavenly, but Nevertheless above the Underworld—until the Day of the Lord’s Judgment  
Vol. I, *Apology*, chapter 47, no. 601; Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chapter 7, nos. 94, 95, 96, and 97; chapter 9, no. 124; chapter 55, nos. 606, 608, 610, and 611; chapter 56, no. 622; and the final chapter, no. 644; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 17, nos. 132 and 134; Book III *Against Marcion*, chapter 34, nos. 548, 549, 550, and 551; and *Scorpiace against the Gnostics*, chapter 12, no. 95; Vol. IV, *On the [Col. 0186A] Judgment of the Lord*, chapter 11, no. 15; and in the fragments of the book *On Paradise*. Finally, Vol. V, Novatian’s *On the Trinity*, chapter 1, no. 10.

This paradox, not only in Tertullian and Novatian but also in other ancient writers, was occasioned both by the account or parable of Lazarus (Luke XVI), whom the rich man saw in Abraham’s bosom, and by the vision of St. John the Apostle in Revelation VI, in which he saw the souls of the martyrs beneath the altar, clothed in white robes, and they were told to rest until the number of their fellow servants should be complete, and finally by Christ’s words to the thief (Luke XXIII): “Today you will be with me in paradise.” For since they saw that the state of souls without bodies until the future Judgment had to be distinguished [Col. 0186B] from the more perfect state of souls with their bodies after the universal resurrection, they designated the former by the terms “Abraham’s bosom,” “peaceful rest beneath the altar,” and “paradise”; and, by contrast, the latter state as “the heavenly bosom,” “the higher altar,” and “the heavenly harbor.” They did not, however, suppose that the souls of the saints were meanwhile deprived of the glory of beholding God, as today’s Anabaptists and Cornelius Agrippa, their follower, maintain, since they had read in the Apostle (Philippians I): “I have a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ.” Likewise (II Corinthians V): “We are willing to be absent from the body and to be present with the Lord.” And concerning the very vision of God: “Now we see through a mirror in an enigma, but then face to face.”

Certainly the Author himself seems to indicate his opinion [Col. 0186C] in the following words of the aforementioned *On the Soul*, chapter 56, no. 622:

> Until that perfection, proportioned to the measure of angelic fullness, is promised.

If, meanwhile, any passage is found in him or in other Fathers which does not admit this reasonable interpretation, the reader should remember that this error does not detract from the learning and piety of such illustrious writers, since in their time the Church had not yet determined anything certain to be believed concerning this article. But after the Ecumenical Council of Florence—which, following the first condemnation issued in the decretal letters of Innocent III and Benedict XII, defined the matter—it is not permissible to hold otherwise. Its decree against the errors of the Armenians contains these words:

> We define that the souls of those who, after receiving baptism, incurred no stain whatsoever [Col. 0186D] of sin, and also those souls which, after contracting the stain of sin, have been purified either while in their bodies or after being divested of those bodies, as was said above, are immediately received into heaven and clearly behold the triune and one Lord himself as he is, although one does so more perfectly than another according to the diversity of their merits; but that the souls of those who die in actual mortal sin or in original sin alone are to be punished, although with unequal punishments.

What is more, the cited passages of Scripture do not support the other opinion if examined more diligently. First, although it is sufficiently agreed that Lazarus and the other Fathers of the Old Testament were in Abraham’s bosom, and that the thief was there also, or in paradise, for some time, they were nevertheless led from there into heaven by Christ, according to Psalm LXVII and Ephesians IV: “Ascending [Col. 0187A] on high, Christ led captivity captive”—that is, as the Apostle himself interprets it, “above all the heavens.”

Secondly, in his Fourth Sermon *On the Solemnity of the Saints*, St. Augustine interprets the white robes in the cited passage (Revelation VI) as the reward of baptism, the prize of martyrdom, and the joy of the heavenly homeland. In support he cites the following words (Revelation VII): “They shall stand before the throne in the sight of the Lamb, clothed in white robes, and palms in their hands.” From this he concludes: “They stand crowned in the sight of the Lamb, before the throne of God, and cannot in any way be separated there from the contemplation of his glory.”

We therefore cited similar passages from Cyprian and from the same St. Augustine, as well as from Sts. Ambrose and Chrysostom—among whom certain obscure passages are also found—in our Annotations on St. Cyprian’s Epistle 16, no. 19. Feuardentius likewise [Col. 0187B] noted several passages in St. Irenaeus in our favor, contrary to the opinion of others, Book I, chapter 2, no. 3, and elsewhere. If anyone desires more, let him consult Sixtus of Siena, *Sacred Library*, Book VI, Annotations 264 and 345.

Meanwhile we shall add that, where the Author says in *On the Soul*, chapter 55, that paradise stands open only to the martyrs, although elsewhere he calls it the common receptacle of all the saints, Blessed Cyprian, his disciple, explains the matter thus in Epistle 32, addressed to Antoninus:

> It is one thing to stand awaiting pardon, another to attain glory; one thing, after being sent to prison, not to come out from there until one has paid the last farthing, another immediately to receive the reward of faith and virtue; one thing to be cleansed from sins by the torment of prolonged suffering and to be purified for a long time by fire, another to have purged one’s sins through suffering; one thing, finally, to remain in suspense on the Day of Judgment awaiting the Lord’s sentence, another [Col. 0187C] immediately to be crowned by the Lord.

At the same place, no. 59, and on Tertullian’s *On the Soul*, chapter 55, no. 611, we noted that this same passage serves to confirm the tradition of the Church concerning Purgatory. Moreover, that affirmation of Blessed Augustine in Book XX of *The City of God* is altogether contrary to the Author, who wishes all souls, even those of the faithful of earlier times, to be kept apart until the Day of Judgment:

> From the time when Christ’s blood delivered the ancient saints from the underworld, good believers thereafter, redeemed by the shedding of that price, know nothing whatever of the underworld.

### 10. That Every Incorporeal Manifestation of the Souls of the Dead Is a Deception  
Vol. III, *On the Soul*, chapter 57, no. 643.

[Col. 0187D] At first sight this indeed seems a paradox, but Tertullian is arguing deliberately against the heretical disciples of Simon Magus, who even promised that they could summon the souls of the prophets from the underworld. Tertullian thinks that in the case of Samuel’s soul (I Kings XXVIII), this could have been permitted to the divining spirit only by falsehood: it was Satan transfigured as an angel of light. We noted there, no. 638, that certain other Fathers also doubted this. He then explains himself by adding that the underworld lies open to no soul, proving this from Abraham’s words to the rich man (Luke XVI).

Since, meanwhile, he admits that certain souls can be recalled into bodies by God’s power, why could they not also, by a special privilege of God, be manifested outside the body—especially since, as we also observed there, no. 643, we possess in the Books of Maccabees the example of the apparitions of Onias [Col. 0188A] the priest and Jeremiah the Prophet? However the matter may stand, St. Augustine wrote beautifully on this subject in *On the Care to Be Taken for the Dead*, chapters 10, 11, 15, and 16:

> Certain visions are related; it is reported that some dead persons appeared to the living, either in dreams or in some other manner, and showed the places where their bodies lay unburied to people who did not know, warning them that the burial which they lacked should be provided for them. If we reply that these things are false, we shall seem shamelessly to contradict both the writings of certain faithful persons—for example, St. Ambrose’s Sermon *On the Discovery of the Bodies of Sts. Gervasius and Protasius*, St. Lucian’s *On the Discovery of the Body of St. Stephen the Protomartyr*, and those of others—and the experience of those who affirm that such things happened to them. I should therefore believe, he says, that they occur through angelic operations, whether permitted [Col. 0188B] or commanded from above. This is sometimes done to good purpose, whether for some kind of consolation of the living, or so that through such admonitions humanity may be commended to the human race in the matter of burial.

Then, after presenting many arguments on both sides, he concludes as follows:

> Divine Scripture also testifies that some of the dead are sent to the living, just as Paul, conversely, was taken up alive into paradise. For the Prophet Samuel, though dead, foretold future events to the living King Saul—although some, among whom, as I have said, is our Author, think that it was not Samuel himself, who could not have been summoned by magical arts, but that some spirit suited to such evil works represented his likeness—since the book of Ecclesiasticus, which tradition says was written by Jesus son of Sirach and which, because of a certain similarity of style, is declared to be Solomon’s [Col. 0188C] even by Tertullian himself in one place, states in the Praise of the Fathers (chapter XLVI) that Samuel prophesied even after death.
>
> But if this book is rejected because it is not in the Hebrew canon, what shall we say of Moses, who is certainly said in Deuteronomy to have died, and in the Gospel to have appeared to the living with Elijah, who had not died (Matthew XVII)? This also resolves the question how martyrs show, through the very benefits granted to those who pray, that they take part in human affairs, if the dead do not know what the living are doing. For we have heard, not from uncertain rumor [Col. 0188D] but from trustworthy witnesses, that Felix the Confessor appeared not only through the effects of benefits but also to the very sight of men when Nola was being attacked by barbarians—a fact which St. Paulinus himself also attests in his Poem on Felix.
>
> But these things are manifested by divine agency in a manner far different from the customary order assigned to each class of creatures. Yet this question, he says, surpasses the powers of my understanding: namely, how the martyrs assist those whom it is certain are assisted through them. This matter is too lofty for me to reach and too hidden for me to investigate. Therefore I do not dare to determine which of these two explanations is true, or whether perhaps both are: that sometimes these things occur through the actual presence of the martyrs, and sometimes through angels assuming the person of the martyrs. I would prefer to inquire about these matters from those who know them.

The author of the book *On the Spirit and the Soul*, chapter 26, extant among the works of Blessed Augustine, condensed these very words.

### [Col. 0189A] 11. That the Souls of the Worst Men Are Changed into Demons after Death  
St. Augustine, *On Heresies*, addressed to Quodvultdeus.

This paradox should be attributed not so much to Tertullian as to the Tertullianists. Above, at the end of the Author’s Life, we noted that Blessed Isidore, Honorius, Rabanus, and Gratian expressly attribute it to them in their Catalogues of Heretics. Consequently, we judge that in Blessed Augustine also one should read “they say” and, a little later, “they believe,” instead of “he says” and “he believes”; especially since nothing of the sort is found in Tertullian’s writings, and since the Epistle 59 of Blessed Jerome to Avitus and Book II *Against Rufinus* attribute it not to Tertullian anywhere, but to Origen.

The opinion is so absurd that it needs no other refutation than the fact that [Col. 0189B] it was condemned under the name of heresy both by the aforementioned writers and, though somewhat less explicitly, by the Sixth Synod in Origen and his accomplices, Act 11, in the Epistle of Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem. It arose from the school of Plato, who, as the Author himself testifies in *On the Soul*, chapters 23, 24, and 28, said that souls depart from here to the heavens whence they came and afterward return here again. Origen and the other heretics plainly attributed something further to him, so that what the Author repeatedly says is most true: the philosophers are the patriarchs of the heretics.

Indeed, he himself expressly denies what would follow by the same reasoning, namely, that good men are changed into angels. Thus he writes in *Against the Valentinians*, chapter 32, no. 362:

> And yet I must turn aside to that place where, after death, there is no marrying at all; where one is clothed upon rather than stripped; where, even if I am stripped of my sex, I am reckoned among the angels, [Col. 0189C] but am not an angel, male or female.

Finally, in *On the Soul*, chapter 56, no. 622, he promises human beings not the substance of angels, but “that perfection proportioned to the measure of angelic fullness.” Then, in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 62, no. 428, he says:

> Finally, he did not say, “They shall be angels,” lest he deny that they are human beings, but “Like angels”—“so as to deny men” [as printed; reading uncertain]. He did not remove the substance to which he attributed the likeness.

I therefore cannot sufficiently express my astonishment—as I also noted on Book III *Against Marcion*, chapter 9, no. 68—that Guido the Carmelite, in *On Heresies*, chapter 36, attributes both these heresies to Tertullian. Philastrius more correctly places them, in *On Heresies*, chapter 36, among the unnamed heresies without any certain author.

### 12. On Baptizing Heretics, Because among Them Everything Is [Col. 0189D] Foreign and Contrary to the Truth  
Vol. III, *On Prescription against Heretics*, chapter 12, nos. 78 and 80; *On Baptism*, chapter 15, no. 106; Vol. IV, among the fragments of the Greek book *On Baptism*; Vol. V, *On Modesty*, chapter 19, no. 162.

Concerning this paradox of Agrippinus of Carthage and the other fellow bishops, of Tertullian, and afterward of St. Cyprian and the other bishops of Africa and Cappadocia, lest I be too lengthy here, the reader should consult our Annotations on St. Cyprian’s Epistles LXX to Januarius, LXXI to Quintus, LXXII to Stephen, LXXIII to Jubaianus, LXXIV to Pompeius, and LXXVI to Magnus; likewise the Council of Carthage celebrated under Cyprian himself; his Life at the years of the Lord 252 and 256; likewise Firmilian’s Epistle to Cyprian, LXXV among [Col. 0190A] his works; and finally our Annotations on the fragments of Tertullian’s book *On Baptism*, and in our Life of Tertullian at the year of the Lord 201.

To these I add that no one should find it surprising if Agrippinus and his fellow bishops issued such a decree concerning the baptism of those returning from heresy to the Catholic Church, and if Tertullian approved it in the already mentioned book written in Greek. For most heretics down to that time did not confess the same Christ and therefore did not agree upon the Rule of Faith handed down by the Apostles—which the later Fathers even to this day call the Creed—although it had always been customary to “seal,” as the Author says, faith in the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, with mention of the Church necessarily added. This may be seen at greater length in our Annotations on [Col. 0190B] *On Baptism*, no. 2, among the ceremonies of Baptism, and chapter 6, nos. 45, 46, and 47.

Charles Bovius certainly interprets in the same manner Apostolic Canon 47 and chapter 15 of Book VI of the *Apostolic Constitutions* of Clement of Rome. Hence the First Council of Nicaea, canon 19, the contemporary Council of Laodicea, canon 8, and the Second Council of Arles, canon 17, define that the Paulianists and Cataphrygians must be baptized. To these the Second Council of Constantinople, canon 7, adds the Eunomians, Sabellians, and Galatian heretics; and finally the Sixth Synod, celebrated in Trullo, adds the Manichaeans, Valentinians, Marcionites, and similar groups.

But in Tertullian’s time the Hermogenians chiefly flourished—whom St. Augustine, *On Heresies*, chapters 41, 44, and 45, reports as holding the same opinions as the Praxeanists—along with the Praxeans, Cataphrygians, Valentinians, Marcionites, [Col. 0190C] the Apelleians who followed Marcion, and finally the Quintillianists. Against the latter the Author expressly composed the Latin book from which the fragments of the Greek book *On Baptism* were taken.

Concerning them, the Author’s statement in *On Prescription against Heretics* is true: “Among them everything is foreign and contrary to our truth”; and likewise his statement in *On Baptism*: “We and they do not have the same God or the same Christ; therefore neither do we have the same baptism, because he is not the same.” This is sufficiently clear concerning the Hermogenians, Praxeans, Cataphrygians, and Quintillianists, who either acknowledged only one person in the Godhead or were Patripassians.

Concerning the Marcionites, Valentinians, and Apelleians, Augustine himself doubts, in Book III of *On Baptism*, chapter 15, whether they baptized with the Gospel words “in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,” [Col. 0190D] because they held erroneous opinions both about God and about Christ. Therefore we too may believe—as Blessed Augustine writes of the Paulianists—that they did not retain the rule of baptism and, as the Second and Sixth Synods phrase it, baptized with only one immersion.

Meanwhile, however, St. Augustine, in Book VI of *On Baptism*, chapter 25, acknowledges as true baptism even that in whose prayers certain statements contrary to the Catholic faith are inserted, provided it is consecrated with the Gospel words. Accordingly, in his seven books *On Baptism against the Donatists*, who attacked the baptism of all heretics, he customarily supplies an antidote to statements of St. Cyprian similar to those of the Author, because Cyprian had cited them generally against all heretics.

In particular, to the statement that there is one baptism and that the baptism of heretics is not the baptism of truth—which occurs in *On Modesty*, [Col. 0191A] Book II, chapter 3—St. Augustine responds in Book V, chapters 21 and 29, and Book VI, chapters 8, 9, 12, and 13. To the statement, “No one can be built up by that whereby he is destroyed; no one is enlightened by him through whom he is darkened,” he responds in Book IV, chapters 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, and 17; and likewise to the aforementioned words, “We and they do not have the same God or the same Christ,” in the same place and in Book V, chapter 26. To the statement, “Among heretics everything is foreign or contrary to the truth,” he responds in Book VI, chapter 40. Finally, to the statement, “Since they do not rightly possess baptism, neither can they receive it, because they do not possess it,” he responds in Book VII, chapters 29, 33, 35, and 47.

Moreover, this one point must be repeated here: Augustine more than once admits that neither Agrippinus nor Cyprian and his associates would have issued so general a decree against the baptism of heretics if anything had been defined by any plenary council, as afterward it was [Col. 0191B] by the First General Council of Nicaea, with the Paulianists alone excepted, as has already been said.

### 13. On Christ as the Spirit of God  
Vol. I, *Apology*, chapter 21, no. 314; Vol. II, *On Prayer*, chapter 1, no. 2; Vol. III, *Against Marcion*, Book I, chapter 19, no. 127; Book III, chapter 6, nos. 38 and 39, and chapter 16; Book IV, chapter 21, no. 314; and *Against Praxeas*, chapter 26, no. 155.

Concerning this phrase of the ancients, we noted the words of St. Jerome on chapter IV of the Epistle to the Galatians, and in his Epistle to Pammachius and Oceanus *On the Errors of Origen*, in the book of St. Cyprian *On the Vanity of Idols*, no. 71:

> Many, through ignorance of the Scriptures—as Firmianus also does in the eighth book of his Epistles to Demetrianus—assert that the Holy Spirit is often called the Father and often the Son.

This is true to such an extent that, besides Lactantius, the same usage may be found [Col. 0191C] in the authors of the books *On Mounts Sinai and Zion* and *Against the Jews Who Persecuted Christ*, among our works of St. Cyprian. Undoubtedly related to this is the fact that Tertullian so often calls Christ the Spirit of God, doing so in the same sense in which God is called spirit in John III and I John IV.

Otherwise, it is clear from the same book *Against Praxeas*, chapter 2, no. 24; chapter 4, no. 40; chapter 8, no. 90; chapter 9, no. 61; chapter 13, nos. 75 and 78; that same chapter 26, no. 158; chapter 30, no. 183; and finally chapter 30, no. 182, that he, like the other ancients, distinguished three persons in the Godhead: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Among these passages by far the clearest is that in which he calls the Holy Spirit “the third divine power of the Godhead” and “the third name of the Majesty.”
