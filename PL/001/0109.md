Octav.* ch. 9, art. 4, and ch. 20, art. 3).

## ARTICLE V. It Is Shown How Gladiators, Scythians, Massagetae, and Derbices Ate Human Flesh, and How, After Embracing the Christian Religion, They Abandoned These Savage Customs

[Col. 1028A]

Tertullian proceeds and makes it plain to everyone that the pagans not only drank human blood but also fed upon human flesh. First, after the spectacles had ended, the gladiators, he says, “dine on dishes of wild beasts taken from the arena” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 9, p. 322); that is, while dining they ate the flesh of the wild beasts they had shortly before killed in the circus. But those beasts had only just devoured the raw flesh of men whom they had slaughtered in those same spectacles and could not yet have digested it. Accordingly, those most cruel [Col. 1028B] gladiators sought out, as our Author says, “the containers of the very bears, still suffering indigestion from human entrails” (ibid.). So indeed it stands in Rigault’s edition; but in the earlier editions, instead of the word *cruditantes*, we read *trucidantibus*, plainly in error. In our manuscripts and in other codices, however, the reading is consistently *cruditantibus adhuc de visceribus humanis*, that is, while the human entrails were still raw and not yet digested in the gut and belly of the bears that had devoured them. Yet who would dare determine with certainty whether this is the true reading without the aid of some more accurate codex? Whatever the reading may be, it is sufficiently clear that Tertullian meant nothing other than that the gladiators sought and ate the flesh of bears that shortly before had devoured human entrails and flesh, which were therefore still undigested in their stomachs.

[Col. 1028C] But, you will say, is not the noun *alvei* also corrupt, and should not *alvi*, the receptacles of food, rather be written? Not unreasonably, provided that you say Tertullian used the better part to signify the whole and support this emendation with at least one codex. For not merely the guts of bears, but other parts of their flesh as well, were customarily eaten, as may be gathered from the words of Apuleius, in which he describes the most magnificent preparations for a gladiatorial show: “We carried to our lodging a she-bear,” he says, “which surpassed the others in bodily bulk, as though it were to be prepared for food” (Apuleius, bk. IV, *Metamorphoses*, p. 59). What, then, prevents us from retaining the reading common to all the codices, *alvei*, by which are meant the vessels [Col. 1028D] in which the flesh of bears, only partially cooked, was contained—the flesh which the gladiators eagerly sought and ate? Hence Tertullian concludes: “Consequently, a man belches up the flesh of a bear that had fed upon a man” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 9, p. 323), by whom it had shortly before been killed.

But far more savage than these gladiators were undoubtedly others, of whom he does not speak quite so affirmatively, but only in this manner: “It is also said that among certain pagan Scythians every deceased person is eaten by his own people” (ibid.). Yet he could certainly have pronounced this without hesitation. For from Strabo we learn that guests were sacrificed by the Scythians, who ate their flesh and used their skulls in place of drinking cups [Col. 1029A] (Strabo, bk. VII, *Geography*, p. 298). Then, a little later, he records the following concerning the Massagetae, either Scythian peoples or their neighbors: [non-Latin text omitted] “They consider the best kind of death to be when those worn out by old age are cut to pieces together with sheep’s flesh and devoured along with it” (ibid., p. 513). Strabo is followed by Eusebius, who added the Derbices to the Massagetae and related that both peoples sacrificed the parents whom they held most dear when they were already exhausted by age, and used their flesh as though it were the most sumptuous banquet (Eusebius, bk. III, *Praeparatio Evangelica*, ch. 4, p. 11). If you desire something expressed more clearly, consult Pliny (Pliny, bk. VI, *Natural History*, ch. 17), and Solinus, who follows him (Solinus, ch. 15, p. 34); they explicitly [Col. 1029B] affirm that the Scythians were anthropophagi, feeding upon human bodies. From Herodotus too you will learn that the Melanchlaeni, so called from their black clothing, lived according to Scythian customs and were accustomed to eat human flesh (Herodotus, bk. IV, §107). To this may be added Mela, whom you may hear narrating the following: “Just as the Essedones furnish drinking cups from the heads of their parents, so they furnish them from the heads of their bitterest enemies. Among the anthropophagi, the very banquets themselves are prepared from human entrails” (Mela, bk. II, *De Situ Orbis*, ch. 1, p. 27).

Furthermore, Eusebius, already cited by us, asserted in the same place that as soon as the Massagetae and Derbices professed the Christian religion, they abandoned these savage and inhuman customs (Eusebius, loc. cit.). Moreover, in our dissertation on the book of Minucius Felix (*Dissertat.* [Col. 1029C] *in Minut. Octav.* ch. 9, art. 2), and in the other passages cited there, we have shown that Christians were never cognizant of or guilty of this anthropophagy, but that pagans alone were so in actual fact.

## ARTICLE VI. How Clearly Tertullian Shows That Christians Were Most Falsely Accused of Secret Incest in Their Sacred Assemblies, Whereas the Pagans Openly and Repeatedly Defiled Themselves with It, Following the Example of Jupiter

We have already made clear to everyone by what arguments, and by arguments of what force, our Tertullian utterly crushed the altogether senseless calumny of the pagans, by which they most shamelessly lied that Christians defiled themselves with infamous incest in their assemblies. For how could they have wished to pollute their sacred mysteries with incest [Col. 1029D] so frequently repeated, when, as we have shown elsewhere, they observed with the greatest zeal either the chastity of marriage prescribed to them by Christ or perpetual virginity, although this was not commanded? Yet such was the shamelessness of the pagans that they most falsely charged them with the crime of secret incest, although in fact it was perpetrated by the pagans themselves not only in secret but “in every light,” as Tertullian says, “and every night, and with the whole sky as witness” (Tertullian, bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 16, p. 581), as boldly as it was done with impunity. Why indeed should they not have perpetrated so great a crime, when, as he with perfect justice presses them, “Jupiter himself taught” them (idem, *Apologeticum*, ch. 9, p. 325), that is, taught them by his own example to commit incest? For who does not know how truthfully Athenagoras [Col. 1030A] (*Legatio pro Christianis*) and other Fathers of the Church objected to these same pagans what was openly proclaimed by their writers: that this same Jupiter had begotten children by his own mother and by his daughter Core, or Proserpina? Who, moreover, has touched even superficially the books published by the profane poets without immediately recoiling in horror when they introduce Juno boasting that she was at once the sister and wife of that same Jupiter? Yet, [non-Latin text omitted] says Justin Martyr, [non-Latin text omitted] “Everyone considers it noble to be an imitator of the gods” (Justin, *Apology* I). Even if the pagans had denied that they had actually imitated him, they nevertheless could not have held it as a fault in Christians to do what they admitted had been done by Jupiter, whom they called the supreme god. We have discussed these crimes of his more than sufficiently in our preceding dissertations. [Col. 1030B]

The Persians, moreover, imitated that supreme—or rather supremely impious—Jupiter; Ctesias, says our Author, testifies that “they have intercourse with their own mothers” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 9, p. 325). Our older manuscript codices present the form *Chteseas*, while in the book *Ad Nationes* it is written *Thesias* (bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 16, p. 581). We have treated the name Ctesias and that abominable Persian crime in our other dissertations (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1259, and *Dissert. in Arnob.* ch. 24, art. 2; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1239, and *Dissert. in Minut.* ch. 20, art. 2).

Tertullian then concludes from the following that the Macedonians had fallen under grave suspicion of incest, or even proof of it: “Because,” he says, “when they first heard [Col. 1030C] the tragedy of Oedipus, seeing the anguish caused by incest, they said [non-Latin text omitted] [non-Latin text omitted]” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 9, p. 325). It is astonishing how corruptly and variously these Greek words are written in our manuscripts and in other printed codices. For in three of the older ones the reading is [non-Latin text omitted] or [non-Latin text omitted]; in two others, *elline eistena ligera*; in the first Basel edition of 1512, [non-Latin text omitted]. In the second, of 1528, only *emene* is written in Latin characters, and the other words are omitted. Finally, in the book *Ad Nationes* the reading, likewise in Latin letters, is *Elaune ten metera*. Those Greek words were therefore corrupted by scribes ignorant of Greek, and one cannot easily say what Tertullian actually wrote.

Gothofredus, however, who was the first to have these books printed, [Col. 1030D] has no doubt whatever that this latter reading of the Agobardinus codex is the true one, and that Tertullian himself wrote it in Greek letters: [non-Latin text omitted]. Rigault, by contrast, asserts that the ancient codices read [non-Latin text omitted]. But he ought to have indicated which codices these were, and whether they differed from ours. For apart from the codices already noted and read by us, he possessed no other, but only certain Fulda readings that were not entirely reliable. But what judgment can you make concerning the true reading of Greek words corrupted by scribes unless you have sound codices at hand? Consider, nevertheless, whether Tertullian’s meaning is expressed by these Latin words: “Come, advance against your mother.” For [Col. 1031A] when Oedipus, a warrior king, was brought upon the stage and represented as weeping over the incest he had unknowingly committed with his mother, the Macedonians, mocking the weeping of this warlike king, assailed him with this taunt: “Come, advance against your mother,” as though he were about to subdue some metropolis. That this was the meaning of our Author may perhaps be inferred, not implausibly, from his book *Ad Nationes*, where he explains the matter more fully in these words: “When Oedipus first entered the stage of those Macedonians, his eyes destroyed, they received him with laughter and mockery. The tragic actor, dismayed, drew back his mask and said, ‘Surely, my lords, I have not displeased you?’ The Macedonians replied: ‘No, you indeed performed beautifully’” (the manuscript codex reads *beautiful*). From this, however, he argues as follows: “Either the writer was utterly frivolous, if he invented it, or Oedipus utterly mad, if he did it; and thereafter you used to say one to another, *elaune ten matera*” [Col. 1031B] (Tertullian, bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 16, p. 581). You clearly hear Tertullian recounting that the masked Oedipus was at first received by the Macedonians with laughter and mockery, though they later acknowledged that he did not displease them; and then, conversing among themselves, they uttered these words to one another in turn: *Elaune ten metera*. If these words are genuine, they signify that one Macedonian said to another: “Drive, goad, spur, thrust your mother on, and imitate Oedipus.” For in that sense the verb [non-Latin text omitted] is used by Aristophanes (Aristophanes, *Electra*, vv. 735 and 740), Homer, and other Greek writers. Whatever word Tertullian may have used, through it he not unjustly charges the Macedonians with incest, since they either praised it in Oedipus or, following his example, urged one another to perpetrate it.

[Col. 1031C] Finally, he proves that not only the Macedonians but all other pagans as well were cognizant of this shameful act, from the fact that, as we have said, they exposed their newborn children. For the parents did not later recognize those children whom they had earlier exposed to the mercy of strangers, nor were they recognized by them. It therefore frequently happened that, through this ignorance, those lustful parents inflicted rape and incest upon their own children (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 9, p. 526, and bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 16, p. 582). Tertullian proves this by an especially disastrous example that occurred in Rome shortly before he wrote the books *Ad Nationes* and, as it appears, during the reign of Commodus. For at that time, as he says, Fuscianus was prefect of the City, when a certain boy, having gone beyond the threshold of his father’s house, was carried off by [Col. 1031D] a certain petty Greek and taken to Asia. When the boy had grown older, he was brought back to Rome, where his own father, no longer recognizing him, violated him. Later his parents recognized him by certain marks on his body and, Tertullian says, “treated themselves with the noose.” Fuscianus assigned their property to the boy, “not as an inheritance,” as Tertullian says, “but as payment for rape and incest” (idem, bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 16, p. 582).

There does, however, appear to be room for doubt whether that wicked father was condemned not only by Fuscianus but also by the senate. For the beginning of this story reads as follows in the Agobardinus codex: “Such a tragedy broke out when Fuscianus was prefect of the City, after the senate’s punishment had been adjudged. Through the accidental negligence of his attendants, having advanced on his way beyond the door, [Col. 1032A] he was dragged away by passers-by and lost to his home. The petty Greek who had reared him, or had seized him from the threshold in Greek fashion, entered. Thence he was removed” (ibid., etc.). But several scribal errors are exposed there. First, Rigault was so persuaded that *reatus* (“guilt”) should be read there instead of *senatus* (“senate”) that he inserted it into Tertullian’s text itself. Second, he removed from it the words “The man who had reared him” and “or from the threshold”; and besides this he thinks the word “entered” should also be removed. He gives the following reason for the first emendation: if this case had been adjudged by the senate, it ought not subsequently to have been adjudged by Fuscianus. The reading should therefore rather be: “when Fuscianus had adjudged the punishment of guilt.” This conjecture indeed seems quite probable. Yet what if something is missing there, and Tertullian intended to signify that Fuscianus’s sentence [Col. 1032B] had been approved by the senate? But Rigault himself has not told us why he wholly removed from our Author’s text the words “The man who had reared him.” Are they so useless there that they ought to have been amputated without warning anyone? Did he not later say, “the foster-child in boyhood”? Finally, he suspects that “or from the threshold” and “entered” should be removed because the latter is an interpretation of the former. But let others judge these matters. For our part, if room were given for conjecture, we would suspect that instead of the words “having advanced on his way,” the reading should be “the boy having advanced,” since it is of course the boy who is under discussion there, and his boyhood is mentioned shortly afterward.

However corrupt this passage may be, the history of this abominable incest is no less certain. Tertullian therefore rightly concludes from it that there were many other parents guilty of the same crime [Col. 1032C] who, because it remained hidden, escaped the punishments due for so grave an offense. Who, moreover, will deny that not only from this story but also from the many other examples we have reported (*Dissertat. in Minut. Octav.* ch. 10, art. 2), the pagans are most clearly convicted of the incest of which they accused Christians with a most shameless falsehood? Let us now, therefore, proceed to their other no less false accusations.

## ARTICLE VII. How Absurdly the Pagans Alleged That Christians Worshipped the Head of an Ass; Who Gave Occasion for This Fable; With What Shamelessness a Certain Jewish Scoundrel Carried About a Picture of That Ass with an Utterly Ridiculous Inscription; and How Truly Those Same Pagans Worshipped Whole Asses and Other Beasts, Either Living or Depicted

[Col. 1032D]

By another calumny, no less serious than the rest, the pagans vainly attempted to defame Christians as though they worshipped, as Tertullian says, “an ass’s head” as a god. But this, he says, was merely the dream of wakeful and envious men who strove by every means to slander Christians. He does not deny, however, that Cornelius Tacitus gave occasion for this false accusation. For as rashly as falsely he had written in his histories that when the Jews, wandering through the vast deserts of Arabia, were suffering from thirst, they were shown springs of water by certain wild asses and quenched their thirst with them; therefore, in testimony of their gratitude, as he asserted, they consecrated an image of an ass. [Col. 1033A] And because the Christian religion was closely related to the Jewish, the pagans therefore invented the story that Christians too worshipped the head of an ass (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 365 ff., and bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 11, p. 577). Elsewhere, however, we have utterly demolished this most senseless accusation, both insofar as it concerns Jews and insofar as it concerns Christians (*Dissertat. in Minut. Octav.* ch. 11, art. 1).

It remains, therefore, for us to examine a particular occurrence described by our Tertullian in two books as follows: “Quite recently, a new representation of our God was publicly displayed in this city, when a certain criminal hired to evade the beasts exhibited a picture with an inscription of this kind: THE GOD OF THE CHRISTIANS, ONOCHOETES. He had ass’s ears, one hoofed foot, [Col. 1033B] carried a book, and wore a toga” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 372). In another book, however, he explains this somewhat more fully: “Now rumor has supplied an account of our God. For quite recently, in this city, a certain utterly depraved man, a deserter even from his own religion, a Jew only by the loss of his foreskin—though even more so because of the bites of the beasts, to which he hires himself out and by which he is now flayed over his whole body every day as he goes about—displayed against us a picture bearing this public inscription: ONOCHOETES. He had ass’s ears and wore a toga with a book, with one hoofed foot” (idem, bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 14, p. 579).

First, then, he said that this happened recently, that is, a short time before he composed both books, and “quite recently in this city,” in which he was residing. There is therefore no room to doubt the truth of this occurrence. [Col. 1033C] Second, its author was a certain Jew, a deserter from his own—that is, the Jewish—religion, who was then a Jew only by the loss of his foreskin, that is, solely by circumcision, while professing the impious worship of the pagans. But this was an utterly depraved man, convicted by public judgment of several crimes and therefore condemned to the beasts, so that he would fight them in his appointed turn. Yet because he was sometimes permitted to be absent from that combat, he voluntarily hired himself out each day to evade the beasts—that is, to escape their attacks and bites by fighting them—in order to make a greater profit. For, as Cassiodorus very aptly observes: “A detestable occupation, an ill-fated contest: to wish to contend with wild beasts, which a man cannot doubt he will find stronger than himself. His sole confidence, therefore, lies in deceiving them; his only consolation in trickery. [Col. 1033D] If he does not deserve to escape the beast, he may sometimes be unable even to find a burial” (Cassiodorus, bk. V, *Variae*, epist. 42, p. 94). Such was that circumcised Jew, of whom Tertullian says that, in addition to circumcision, “he is now flayed over his whole body every day”; that is, each day he received many wounds from the bites of beasts. But men of this kind were called *bestiarii*, concerning whom we discussed matters shortly above (*supra*, ch.).

Accordingly, at the public games that scoundrelly pseudo-Jew, in order to mock the Christian religion, carried about the utterly ridiculous image described by Tertullian, bearing the inscription: THE GOD OF THE CHRISTIANS, ONOCHOETES (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 372); or, as the name appears in our manuscripts [Col. 1034A] and others, *Onochitis*; in others, *Onochorsitis*, or *Onochortissis*, or *Onochotasis*; in the old editions, *Onychites*; and finally, in some, [non-Latin text omitted]. In the unpublished copy of the book *Ad Nationes* and in other printed copies, however, this name is written in three ways: first, *Onocholtes*; second, *Onochoites*; and third, *Onochoete* (idem, bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 14, p. 579). But the very variety and discrepancy of the readings of this single name make it clear that it is corrupt. Hence several learned men have labored considerably to repair it. Some think that *Onochoetes* should be read, so that the word would mean an ass who was a teacher and priest of sacred rites, by whom others were to be initiated. Others prefer *Onochorsites*, from an ass’s head; others, *Onocoites*, that is, of asinine origin and lineage. Still others propose *Onotacoustes*, in Greek [non-Latin text omitted]. [Col. 1034B] But why should we labor to discover this single word in the inscription when the *Apologeticum* states that the two Latin words *Deus Christianorum* (“God of the Christians”) preceded it? What if that latter name was invented by the criminal jester and later corrupted by scribes? What if, moreover, it is some common Punic name, now wholly obsolete? Ought not the explanation given by our Author of this name and picture to suffice for us? “He had,” he says, “ass’s ears, one hoofed foot, carried a book, and wore a toga.” In the book *Ad Nationes* he expressed this in different words, but words signifying exactly the same thing.

That depicted ass, moreover, was clad in a toga and carried a book, plainly in mockery [Col. 1034C] of the Christians, who held the sacred Gospels in the highest honor and were accustomed to carry them with them and read them publicly in their assemblies.

But the Christians and Tertullian laughed at that monstrosity of divinity, which had arisen from the mind of the impious scoundrel, and at its name and form. Why should they not have laughed at it, since it was, as he says, “deprived of the authority of antiquity and weakened by the character of its author” (idem, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 373, and bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 14, p. 579)?

Nor indeed should anyone object to us that the Gnostics had not strayed so far from this, since long before, as we observed after Epiphanius (Epiphanius, *Haereses* 26, p. 91; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 244; *Dissertat. in Minut.* ch. 11, art. 1), they had imagined Sabaoth to be the seventh of the Aeons and the creator of heaven and earth, and some babbled that he bore the form [Col. 1034D] of an ass, others that of a pig. For this ridiculous image of Sabaoth was entirely different from the hideous figure of the Christians’ God that the most wicked and disgraceful traitor to the Jewish religion had most foolishly devised.

Perhaps the white ass approached it more closely, which, as Suidas relates (Suidas, s.v. [non-Latin text omitted]) and as we have noted elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Minut.*, loc. cit.), a certain Zeno of Alexandria, a deserter from the Jewish religion, is said to have led through the Jewish synagogue on the Sabbath. Yet even if it were proved that this was done before Tertullian’s time, it nevertheless differed greatly from the picture that the pseudo-Jew carried about in mockery of the Christians’ God, not in a synagogue but [Col. 1035A] at the pagans’ public spectacles. Yet it could not altogether absurdly be said that one of these scoundrelly pseudo-Jews gave the other the occasion to do these things.

Although both those monstrosities of divinity were utterly detestable, the pagans nevertheless could not completely and absolutely reject either of them without immediately condemning themselves for wickedness and madness. For Tertullian proves this clearly in the words with which he addresses them: “You will not deny that all beasts of burden, and whole asses together with their Epona, are worshipped by you” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 365). Again, in the first book *Ad Nationes*: “Indeed, you worship whole asses, together with their Epona; and you consecrate all beasts of burden, cattle, and wild animals alike, together with their mangers.” Therefore he presses them thus: “But those pagans [Col. 1035B] ought immediately to have worshipped that composite name invented by the Jew” (bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 11, p. 577), “because they have accepted gods with mixed canine and leonine heads, gods horned like goats and rams, goats from the loins downward, serpents from the legs downward, and birds at the feet or back” (idem, *Apologeticum*, ibid.). Similarly, in his book *Ad Nationes*: “Among you are gods with dogs’ heads and lions’ heads; gods horned like oxen, rams, and goats; gods goat-begotten or serpentine; and winged at the feet, forehead, and back” (bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 14, p. 579). We have explained all these matters in other dissertations and have heard Minucius disputing against the pagans in a similar fashion (*Dissertat. in Minut. Octav.* ch. 14, art. 2, and *Dissertat. in Arnob.* ch. 20, art. 1).

But Tertullian cuts them down with their own sword as follows. [Col. 1035C] If, he says, you charge it as a fault against us that we worship the head of an ass as God, you certainly prove yourselves far more wicked. For you declare to be gods, and actually worship, not only the head of an ass or certain parts of beasts, but whole asses and very many other entire beasts as well. What greater folly, I ask you, can there be than for someone to be accused of a single crime by another who admits himself guilty of the same crime and of several still graver ones? But these things, says Tertullian, are superfluous. For just as it was certain that beasts were worshipped by the pagans, so it was false that Christians worshipped an ass’s head or asses. They worshipped one supreme God, who created all things and can neither be seen nor comprehended by anyone.

## ARTICLE VIII. How Thoughtlessly the Pagans Charged Christians with the Worship of Christ’s Cross, Although They Themselves Worshipped Every Kind of Cross in the Images of Their Gods, in Trophies, Standards, and the Adornment of Military Ensigns; Of What Parts Tertullian Thought Christ’s Cross Was Composed; Why He Called the Draperies of the Cross *Siphara*, and How a Person with Outstretched Hands Presents the Figure of the Cross

[Col. 1035D]

The pagans further charged that Christians had injured and violated the supreme majesty of God by their religious worship of the cross upon which Christ had been affixed. But by what reasoning, do you suppose, did Tertullian refute this accusation, repeated by them again and again? By none other, certainly, than by turning against the pagans themselves [Col. 1036A] the weapon they had hurled. For he remains silent on whether Christians actually worshipped that cross and shows merely, by various arguments, that the pagans were altogether thoughtless in reproaching them for such worship, since they themselves everywhere venerated crosses of every kind.

What then, you will say? Did he by some deceptive silence deny or conceal that Christ’s cross was honored by his disciples? By no means. For why could he have denied or concealed this, when he admits that at nearly every action of their lives they fortified their foreheads with the sign of the cross? Why, I ask, did they so frequently place that sacred sign upon their foreheads unless they were fully persuaded that it afforded them some protection and was worthy [Col. 1036B] of their veneration (Tertullian, *De Corona*, ch. 3, and bk. II, *Ad Uxorem*, ch. 5)? But concerning this most ancient custom of worshipping Christ’s cross we have argued at greater length against the heretics in our other dissertations (*Dissertat. in Minut.* ch. 11, art. 3; *in Lactant.* ch. 19, art. 1; and *in Lucil. Caecil.* ch. 5, art. 2). Tertullian therefore could not deny something that in his day was practiced daily by all Christians and was also sufficiently well known to the pagans themselves (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 366).

Accordingly, to those who objected to the worship of Christ’s cross he gave no reply other than this very brief one: “Whoever thinks us devotees of the cross will be our fellow-worshipper”; or, as in another book: “Whoever declares us priests of the cross will be our fellow-worshipper” (idem, [Col. 1036C] bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 12, p. 578). The noun *consecraneus*, as is noted in the ancient glosses, means the same as “companion in sacred rites.” He therefore openly asserts that the pagans were utterly thoughtless in daring to accuse Christians of this superstitious and impious worship because they venerated Christ’s cross, when the pagans themselves worshipped a great many crosses.

Before examining the arguments by which he endeavors to prove this, however, we must observe what he said the form of Christ’s cross was. “The whole cross,” he says, “is attributed to us, namely with its yard and with that projecting seat” (ibid., bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, loc. cit.). He thus teaches us that Christ’s cross was entire and complete, since it was composed of three parts: [Col. 1036D] an upright piece of wood; the yard, or another transverse piece of wood; and the seat, or small support, attached to and projecting from the cross. Elsewhere we have observed (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 597) that a controversy arose among the learned over whether that support, or projecting piece of wood, was placed beneath the entire body of the crucified man or beneath his feet alone. But since our Tertullian calls it a “seat,” by this name he undoubtedly indicates clearly enough that it was positioned in the middle of the cross and that the crucified man’s body rested upon it, lest by its heavier weight it slip downward or fall when the hands were torn. Some think that this seat is designated by these words of our same Tertullian in another book: “The outer horns of the cross; for even on a ship’s yard, which is part of a cross, the extremities are called horns, while [Col. 1037A] the middle stake of the upright belongs to the unicorn” (Tertullian, *Adversus Judaeos*, ch. 2). They admit, however, that this passage is not entirely uncorrupted and that it therefore cannot be determined with certainty whether the latter words instead denote the upright wood of the cross. But it seems beyond doubt that he calls Christ’s cross entire and complete, composed of both the upright and transverse pieces of wood and the seat.

In order to curb the rashness of the pagans who reproached Christians for worshipping this cross, he shows by accumulated arguments that the pagans worshipped not only many other entire crosses but even parts of crosses. For, as he says, “Attic Pallas and Pharian Ceres, who stand forth without an image as a rough stake and an unshaped piece of wood, are no different from the upright post of a cross” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, [Col. 1037B] ch. 16, p. 366). He repeats this in almost the same words in the book *Ad Nationes* (bk. I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 11, p. 577). But in our manuscript copies the reading is *Ceres Fariam*; in the earlier printed editions, *Ceres Farrea*. This reading pleases several scholars because they think this name was given to Ceres because she is said to have discovered spelt and grain. Others prefer the reading *Pharia*, or *Faria*, as in the book *Ad Nationes*. For they suppose that Ceres was called by that epithet because she was worshipped on Pharos, the island of Egypt. We have made some observations about her in our dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius Felix (*Dissertat. in Minut.* ch. 14, art. 4).

More than once in other dissertations (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 734 ff., and *Dissertat. in Arnob.* ch. [Col. 1037C] 20, art. 1), we have noted that before the pagans fashioned the images of their gods in human form, some regarded and worshipped as gods a scimitar, others a sword, blade, or spear, some a stone, and others a rough piece of wood or an unworked stake. This custom persisted longer among certain peoples. Tertullian appears to indicate that even in his own time there still existed an image of Pallas in the region of Attica and one of Ceres on the island of Pharos that consisted merely of a rough stake and an unshaped piece of wood without any figure. This can certainly be confirmed from the material we have reported in the dissertations cited above. The pagans, therefore, in worshipping such a stake fixed in the ground as a sacred divinity, were unquestionably worshipping part of a cross.
