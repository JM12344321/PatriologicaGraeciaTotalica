There is nothing surprising, however, if arguments are also seized upon from the Apostle’s own body of writings, since heresies must exist; and they could not exist unless Scripture were also capable of being wrongly understood. Thus heresies, having seized upon [Note: [Col. 0850C] Rhenanus reads “having arisen” (*natae*) for “having seized upon” (*nactae*).] the two men presented by the Apostle—the inner man, that is, the soul, [Col. 0850C] and the outer man, that is, the flesh—have assigned salvation to the soul, that is, to the inner man, but destruction to the flesh, that is, to the outer man, because it is written to the Corinthians: “For even if our outer man is corrupted, yet the inner man is renewed from [Note: [Col. 0850C] “From” omitted by Rigault and the Venice edition.] day to day.”

Yet neither is the soul by itself a man, since it was subsequently inserted into the formed being already called “man”; nor is the flesh without the soul a man, since after the soul’s departure it is designated a corpse. Thus the word “man” is, as it were, the clasp joining the two interwoven substances: under that designation they cannot exist except [Note: [Col. 0850D] “Except” is absent from Rhenanus.] in union.

[Note: [Col. 0850D] “Moreover, the Apostle [means] the inner man,” etc. The Apostle uses “inner man” and “outer man” in different senses in Romans VII and II Corinthians IV. There the outer man is concupiscence, while the inner man is love and spirit—that is, the man who desires and the man renewed in spirit. Here, however, the outer man is the bodily man himself insofar as he is visible, tangible, and, finally, perceptible and capable of suffering, and can receive injuries from without; the inner man is the man insofar as he consists of the invisible mind, which bears the body’s afflictions bravely and nobly. LAC.]

Moreover, by the “inner man” the Apostle prefers us to understand [Note: [Col. 0850D] “Not so much the soul as the mind and intelligence,” etc. [Col. 0851D] Philosophers either observe or assume a distinction between *anima* and *animus*. For *animus* is understood as the mind, namely insofar as it performs intellectual functions; *anima*, however, insofar as it gives life to the body and manages the operations of the senses and the functions of the limbs. The latter is understood as the nature and substance of the thing, the former as its excellent disposition. LAC.] not [Col. 0851A] so much the soul as the mind and intelligence—that is, not the substance itself, but the substance’s disposition. For when he writes to the Ephesians that Christ dwells “into the inner man,” [Note: [Col. 0851D] “Christ dwells into the inner man.” Here too he has reproduced the Greek, *εἰς τὸν ἔσω ἄνθρωπον* (“into the inner man”), joining this phrase to what follows, whereas all other editions, including the Syriac, join it to what precedes. PAM.] he surely means that the Lord is to be brought into the inmost [Note: [Col. 0851D] “To be brought into the inmost.” A word commonly frequent in that age. *Intimare* means “to make inward.” RHEN.] affections [Note: [Col. 0851C] “Initiated,” Rigault and the Venice edition.]. He therefore added (Eph. III), “Through faith,” and “In your hearts,” and “In love,” presenting faith and love not as constituent substances of the soul, but as its conceptions. By saying “in your hearts,” however—and hearts are constituent substances of the flesh—he has already assigned to the flesh that very inner man whom he located in the heart.

Now examine how he declared that the outer man is corrupted, but that the inner man is renewed from [Note: [Col. 0851C] “From” is absent from Rhenanus, Rigault, the Venice edition, and Semler.] day to day. You must not affirm that this corruption is the corruption of the flesh which it will suffer when it passes away forever from the day of death onward. Rather, it is the corruption which, during the course of this life, before death and right up [Col. 0851B] to death, the flesh will undergo through harassments and pressures, torments and punishments, for the sake of the Name. For the inner man likewise will surely have to be renewed here through the prompting of the Spirit, advancing in faith and discipline from [Note: [Col. 0851C] “From” is absent from Rhenanus, Rigault, the Venice edition, and Semler.] day to day—not there, that is, not after the resurrection, where we shall certainly not be renewed from [Note: [Col. 0851C] “From” is absent from Rhenanus, Rigault, the Venice edition, and Semler.] day to day, but once and for all to perfection.

Learn this from what follows: “For that which is present,” he says (II Cor. VI), “the temporal and light burden of our affliction, [Note: [Col. 0851D] “Through excess unto excess.” So it is read in the manuscripts. He has rendered Paul’s Greek, *καθ’ ὑπερβολὴν εἰς ὑπερβολήν* (“according to excess unto excess”). So below, “we were burdened beyond measure, beyond our strength,” which Paul expressed as *καθ’ ὑπερβολὴν* [Col. 0852D] *ὑπὲρ δύναμιν* (“exceedingly beyond our strength”). RIG.] through excess unto excess, [Note: [Col. 0851C] “Unto excess” omitted by Rhenanus, Semler, and Pamelius.] will produce in [Note: [Col. 0851C] “To be produced,” in Rhenanus and Semler.] us an eternal weight of glory, while we look not at the things that are seen,” that is, sufferings, “but at the things that are not seen,” that is, rewards; “for the things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are not seen are eternal.” [Note: [Col. 0851C] “Are” is absent from Rigault and the Venice edition.]

For he declares that the pressures and injuries by which [Col. 0851C] the outer man is corrupted should be despised because they are light and temporal, preferring instead the weight of eternal and invisible rewards and of glory, as compensation for the hardships through which the flesh is corrupted by suffering here. So entirely is this not the corruption which they ascribe to the outer man as the flesh’s everlasting destruction, in order to exclude the resurrection.

Likewise elsewhere he says (Rom. VIII): “Provided that [Note: [Col. 0852D] “We suffer together, so that we may also be glorified together.” Here too he properly translates the Greek words *εἴπερ*, “provided that,” and *συνδοξασθῶμεν*, “we may be glorified together.” Likewise *λογίζομαι*, “I reckon.” “To suffer together” is understood as “to suffer jointly,” and therefore the word *compassio* (“shared suffering”), which the Author uses shortly afterward, must be understood in the same way. PAM.] we suffer together, so that we may also be glorified together; [Col. 0852A] for I reckon that the sufferings of this time are not worthy to be compared with the future glory which is to be revealed in us.” Here too he shows that the hardships are less than their rewards. Moreover, if it is through the flesh that we suffer together—and it belongs properly to the flesh to be corrupted by sufferings—then to that same flesh will also belong what is promised in return for shared suffering.

And so fully does he assign the property of enduring pressure to the flesh [Note: [Col. 0852D] “So that above too he says” (namely Paul): “When we came,” etc. By “above,” the Author means again the Second Epistle to the Corinthians, whose passages are principally treated in this chapter; for this text is found in II Corinthians. Unless perhaps someone prefers to suppose that he follows Marcion’s ordering, observed in the books against Marcion, since Marcion places the Epistle to the Romans—from which he had produced the immediately preceding testimony—after the Epistles to the Corinthians. PAM.] that above too he says [Note: [Col. 0851C] “Says,” Rhenanus and Semler.]: “But when we came into Macedonia, our flesh had no relief.” Then, to assign shared suffering [Note: [Col. 0851D] Rhenanus and Semler add “and.”] to the soul as well, he says (II Cor. VII): [Note: [Col. 0852D] “Afflicted in all things,” he says. This too is word for word from the Greek: *ἀλλ’ ἐν παντὶ θλιβόμενοι* (“but afflicted in every way”). PAM.] “Afflicted in all things: conflicts without,” which were, of course, assaulting the flesh; “fear within,” which was, of course, distressing the soul.

Thus, even if the outer man is corrupted, he will be understood [Note: [Col. 0852C] “Would be understood,” Rhenanus and Semler.] to be corrupted not by losing the resurrection, but by enduring harassment. And this does not occur without the inner man. Thus it will belong to both [Col. 0852B] to be glorified together, just as it belongs to both to suffer together. In accordance with their partnership in hardships, their partnership [Note: [Col. 0852C] Rhenanus and Semler read “also.”] in rewards must likewise follow.

## CHAPTER XLI

He continues to pursue the same argument, preferring recompenses to afflictions. “For we know,” he says (II Cor. V), “that even if our earthly house of this [Note: [Col. 0852C] “Our,” Rhenanus and Semler.] tabernacle is dissolved, we have a house not made by hands, eternal in heaven”; that is, through the dissolution of our flesh by sufferings, [Note: [Col. 0852C] Rhenanus adds “that.”] we shall obtain a dwelling in heaven. He was mindful of the Gospel’s declaration [Note: [Col. 0852C] *Diffinitionis*, Rhenanus and Semler.] (Matt. V): “Blessed are those who suffer persecution for righteousness’ sake, because theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

Yet he did not deny the restoration of the flesh merely because he set the reward as compensation over against it, since compensation is owed to the very thing to which dissolution is assigned [Note: [Col. 0852C] “Reckoned,” Rhenanus and Semler.], namely, the flesh. But because he had called the flesh a “house,” he elegantly chose to use the word “house” also in comparing the reward, promising to the very house which will be dissolved through suffering a better house through the resurrection. For the Lord too promises many mansions, as though they were houses, with the Father (John XIV).

Although it [Note: [Col. 0852D] “Nor,” Rigault and the Venice edition.] may also be understood of the dwelling place of the world, [Note: [Col. 0852D] “Although it may also be understood of the dwelling place [Col. 0853C] of the world,” etc. He signifies the human body by two metaphors, that of a dwelling and that of a garment: a dwelling in this life, and a garment of glory in the next. Even the pagan Seneca seems to acknowledge both—the first in Epistle 120, the second in Epistle 92. LE PR.] whose dissolution is followed by the promise of an eternal abode in heaven, the statements that follow, which plainly concern the flesh, show that the preceding statements wholly [Note: [Col. 0853C] “Not,” Rhenanus, Semler, and Pamelius.] concern the flesh.

For the Apostle makes a distinction when he adds: “For in this too we groan, [Note: [Col. 0853C] “In this we groan,” Pamelius and Franeker.] desiring to be clothed over with our dwelling which is from heaven, provided that when clothed [Note: [Col. 0853C] “Unclothed,” Semler.] we are not found naked”; that is, we wish to be clothed over with the heavenly power of eternity before we are stripped of the flesh. For the privilege of this grace awaits those who will be found in the flesh at the coming of the Lord [Note: [Col. 0853D] “Will be found in the flesh.” Here he speaks of the Lord’s second coming, at which the Judge of all will weigh and examine sins. Those who are found in the flesh—that is, alive—will present themselves before the Lord together with that same flesh; those who are not found in the flesh will assume the flesh, for he will be the Judge of the living and the dead. He explains this in Question 109. Blessed Justin: *οὐ πάντες κοιμηθησόμεθα. κρινεῖ οὖν ζῶντας μὲν τοὺς τότε ζῶντας, νεκροὺς δὲ τοὺς ἀνισταμένους ἐκ τῶν νεκρῶν.* “We shall not all sleep. He will therefore judge as the living those who are then alive, and as the dead those who rise from the dead.” LE PR.] and who, because of the hardships of Antichrist’s times, will deserve—through the shortcut of having death canceled by transformation—to join those who are rising, just as he writes to the Thessalonians (I Thess. IV): “For this we say to you in the word of the Lord: we who are alive, who remain until [Col. 0853B] the coming of the Lord, shall not precede those who have fallen asleep. For the Lord himself, at the command and the voice of the Archangel [Note: [Col. 0853C] “Angel,” Rhenanus and Semler.] and at the trumpet of God, will descend from heaven; and the dead in Christ will rise first. Then we, together with them, shall at once [Note: [Col. 0853C] “At once” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.] be caught up in the clouds to meet the Lord [Note: [Col. 0853C] “Christ,” Rhenanus and Semler.] in the air; and so we shall always be with the Lord.”

## CHAPTER XLII

He presents [Note: [Col. 0853C] “Presented,” Pamelius and Franeker.] their transformation to the Corinthians (I Cor. XV), saying: “We shall indeed all rise, but we shall not all be transformed, in an atom, in the momentary movement of an eye, at the last trumpet”—that is, only those, of course, who are found in the flesh. “And the dead,” he says, “will rise first, and [Note: [Col. 0853C] Rhenanus and Semler do not have “first, and.”] we shall be transformed.”

Once you have first taken account of this arrangement, [Note: [Col. 0854C] “Position,” Rhenanus and Semler.] therefore, you will refer the remainder back to the preceding sense. For when he adds, “For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal [Col. 0853C] must put on immortality,” this will be that dwelling from heaven with which, groaning in this flesh, we desire to be clothed over [Note: [Col. 0854C] “To be clothed over,” Pamelius and Franeker.]—surely over the flesh in [Col. 0854A] which we shall be found. For he says that we who are [Note: [Col. 0854C] “May be,” Rhenanus and Semler.] in the tabernacle are burdened, because we do not wish to be unclothed, but rather to be clothed over, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life—that is, while it is transformed [Note: [Col. 0854C] Others read “while we transform.”] by putting on what comes from heaven.

For who, while in the flesh, would not desire to put on immortality and continue in life, cheating death by a transformation in its place, so that he need not experience the lower regions, which will exact payment down to the last farthing? The one who is to obtain transformation after the resurrection, by contrast, has already experienced the lower regions.

From this point, then, we establish that the flesh will in every respect [Note: [Col. 0854C] “In every respect indeed,” Rhenanus and Semler.] rise, and that it [Note: [Col. 0854C] “That flesh,” Pamelius and Franeker.], when the transformation supervenes, will assume an angelic condition. Otherwise, if only in those who are found in the flesh must the mortal be transformed so that it may be swallowed up by [Col. 0854B] life—that is, so that the flesh may be swallowed up by that heavenly and eternal over-garment—then those found dead will not obtain life, since they have already been deprived of life’s material and, so to speak, food, namely the flesh. Or else they too must necessarily receive it, so that in them also what is mortal may be swallowed up by life, if they are to obtain life.

“But in the dead,” you say, “this mortal thing will already have been swallowed up.” Certainly not in all of them. For how many men may be found to have died only the day before, their corpses so fresh that nothing in [Note: [Col. 0854C] “In” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.] them could be seen to have been swallowed up! For surely by “swallowed up” you mean nothing other than overtaken, abolished, taken away from every sense—something which has ceased by every means to appear. Yet even the most ancient corpses of giants cannot be shown to have been swallowed up, since their frameworks still survive. We have already spoken about this elsewhere.

But quite recently in [Col. 0854C] this city, when the foundations of the Odeum [Note: [Col. 0854C] Rhenanus reads *Hodie* (“today”); others *dei*; the Wouwer manuscript *eo die* (“on that day”).] were being laid and the sacrilegious disturbance of so many ancient tombs took place, [Note: [Col. 0854C] “When the foundations of the Odeum.” There were various *odea* at Rome—that is, places to which spectators from the theater sometimes withdrew if rain came on, for there were colonnades there. Poems were also recited in these places, and grain offered for sale [Col. 0854C] was customarily displayed there. [Col. 0854D] Cases concerning grain were also conducted there. Thus Pollux, book VIII: *τὰς δὲ ἐπὶ τῷ σίτῳ δίκας ἐν τῷ ὠδείῳ ἐδίκαζον· σῖτος δέ ἐστιν αἱ ὀφειλόμενοι τροφαί.* “Cases concerning food were adjudicated in the Odeum; and ‘food’ means any sustenance owed.” LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 0854D] “The sacrilegious disturbance of ancient tombs,” etc. When the foundations of the Odeum were being laid—for a new Odeum had been built at Carthage, about which he is perhaps speaking here—countless bones, not yet decayed or consumed, were dug up, including those of giants, which were monstrous. This was indeed akin to sacrilege, for the crime of violating a tomb is sacrilege, and the action against its violator carries infamy. Nor is it surprising that Tertullian, highly experienced in law, did not forget this; we have remarked on it elsewhere. LE PR.] the people recoiled in horror at bones nearly five hundred years old [Note: [Col. 0854D] “Nearly five hundred years old.” Who will give credence to Jerome Cardan, who reports that his father’s body, because of various exercises and a precise regimen of life, remained intact for twenty years? LE PR.] which were still moist, and at hair that still gave off its odor. [Note: [Col. 0854D] “And hair that still gave off its odor.” The same Cardan recorded in writing, in *Various Matters*, VIII, chapter 4, that the body of Cicero’s daughter Tullia [Col. 0855D] was found in the time of Sixtus IV without having suffered any deterioration. Indeed, the hair upon it appeared interwoven with a golden net; the passage of more than 1,500 years had had no effect upon the hair or upon the whole body. LE PR.]

[Col. 0855A] [Note: [Col. 0855D] It is established that not only bones endure, but also teeth. Plato in the *Phaedo*: *ἔνια δὲ μέρη τοῦ σώματος, καὶ ἐὰν σαπῇ, ὀστᾶ τε καὶ νεῦρα καὶ τὰ τοιαῦτα πάντα ὅμως, ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν, ἀθάνατά ἐστιν.* “Some parts of the body, even if it decays—bones, sinews, and all such things—are nevertheless, so to speak, immortal.” RIG.] It is established that not only bones endure, but teeth too remain incorrupt; they are preserved like seeds of the body that is to bear fruit in the resurrection.

Finally, even if at that time what is mortal is found to have been swallowed up in all the dead, it will certainly have been swallowed up by death, certainly by the passage of time, certainly through age. But will it have been swallowed up by [Note: [Col. 0855C] “From,” Rhenanus and Semler.] life, by the over-garment, by the accession of immortality? Moreover, by saying that what is mortal will be swallowed up by these things, [Note: [Col. 0855C] “Because by these,” Rhenanus and Semler.] he has denied that it will be swallowed up by the others. And surely it is fitting that this should be accomplished and bestowed by divine powers, not by natural laws.

Therefore, since what is mortal must be swallowed up by life, it must necessarily be produced in every case so that it may be swallowed up, and swallowed up so that it may be transformed. If you say that a fire must be kindled, you cannot affirm that the material by which it is kindled is necessary in one case but not in another.

So too when he reinforces the point: “Provided that, once unclothed, we are not found naked” [Note: [Col. 0855C] “We shall be found naked,” Rhenanus and the Venice edition.], he is speaking, of course, about those who will not be found alive or in the flesh on the day of the Lord. He has called those whom he previously described as unclothed “naked” for no other reason than that he wished them to be understood as clothed again with the same substance of which they had been stripped. For they will be found naked because the flesh has been laid aside, or partly torn apart or worn away; this too can be called nakedness. Then [Note: [Col. 0855C] Pamelius and Franeker add “and.”] they will receive it back, so that, reclothed with the flesh, they may also be clothed over with immortality. For being clothed over cannot apply unless one is already clothed.

## CHAPTER XLIII

Accordingly, when he says: “Therefore, [Note: [Col. 0855D] ‘Always confident.’ He translated the Greek *θαρροῦντες* (‘being confident’). LE PR.] always confident and knowing that, while [Note: [Col. 0855D] ‘We remain in the body, we are absent from the Lord.’ He also explains the Greek *ἐκδημοῦντες* (‘being away from home’). LE PR.] we remain in the body, we are absent [Note: [Col. 0855C] ‘May be absent,’ Rhenanus and Semler.] from the Lord—for we walk by faith, not by sight,” it is clear that this too does not serve to disparage the flesh, as though it separated us from the Lord. For here the exhortation is directed toward scorning this life, since we are absent from the Lord as long as we live, walking by faith, not by sight—that is, by hope, not by reality.

He therefore adds: “But being confident, and considering it better [Note: [Col. 0855C] “Saying,” Rhenanus and Semler.] rather to be absent from the body and to be present with the Lord”—that is, so that we may walk by sight rather than by faith, by reality rather than by hope. You see how here too [Note: [Col. 0855C] “Here too,” Pamelius and Franeker.] he relates contempt for bodies to the excellence of martyrdom. For no one who has become absent from the body is immediately present with the Lord except by the prerogative of martyrdom, since he is to lodge in paradise, [Note: [Col. 0855D] “In paradise, of course,” Rhenanus and Semler.] not in the lower regions. [Note: [Col. 0856C] Others read *diversurus*.]

[Note: [Col. 0855D] “But had the Apostle run out of words?” Theophylact prudently observed that the Apostle avoided the terms “death” and “life,” calling death “presence with the Lord,” or “remaining with him,” but calling life “absence” [Col. 0856D] and “being away from home.” LAC.] But had the Apostle run out of words to signify departure from the body? Or does he speak in this unusual way for a reason? For since he wished to signify a temporary absence from the body, he said that we are away from home in relation to it, because one who is away from home will also return to his dwelling.

Hence he also says to everyone: “And therefore [Note: [Col. 0856C] Rhenanus and Semler delete “And therefore.”] we strive,” he says (II Cor. V), “whether absent or present, to be pleasing to God. [Note: [Col. 0856C] “To please him,” Paris and Franeker.] For we must all be manifested before the judgment seat [Note: [Col. 0856C] “Before the judgment seat,” Pamelius and Franeker.] of Christ Jesus.” If all of us, then the whole of us; [Col. 0856B] if the whole of us, then both our inner and outer parts—that is, both souls and bodies: [Note: [Col. 0856D] “So that each one,” he says, “may receive.” The Vulgate has: “that each one may receive the things proper to the body.” The loss of a single letter alone produced the variant reading. The Vulgate reads *ἴδια τοῦ σώματος* (“the things proper to the body”), but Tertullian *διὰ τοῦ σώματος* (“through the body”); it is so read in Greek manuscripts and is followed by the Syriac, Theophylact, and Oecumenius. In Tertullian, the English manuscript had “the past things of the body.” LAC.] “So that each one,” he says, “may receive [Note: [Col. 0856C] Franeker, Rigault, and the Venice edition add “the things.”] through the body according to what he has done, whether good or evil.”

I ask, then, how do you read this? For he has constructed it obscurely through hyperbaton: [Note: [Col. 0856D] “For he has constructed it obscurely through hyperbaton.” The grammatical figure of hyperbaton occurs when a word is transposed, as though the sentence had become confused and obstructed. A word placed farther away produces hyperbaton, as would happen if “through the body” were connected with the verb “has done.” LAC.] does it refer to the things that must be received through the body, or to the things that were done through the body? But if it means the things that must be received through the body, then the resurrection is undoubtedly bodily; and if it means the things done through the body, [Note: [Col. 0856D] “They must certainly be repaid through the body.” That is, expiated or rewarded. Theodoret gives a similar explanation, inferring the resurrection with Tertullian. LAC.] then they must certainly be repaid through the body through which they were also done. Thus this entire discussion of the Apostle from its beginning, woven to a conclusion of such a kind as demonstrates the resurrection of the flesh, must be understood according to those things that accord with its conclusion.

## CHAPTER XLIV

[Col. 0856C] For if you look back still further, to the point from which mention of the outer and inner man was introduced, will you not find both the dignity and the hope of the flesh intact? For while [Note: [Col. 0856C] “When,” Rhenanus and Semler.] he speaks of the light which God has made to shine in our hearts, for the illumination of the knowledge of his glory in the person of Christ, he says that we possess this treasure in earthen vessels—that is, in the flesh. Is it to be destroyed because it is earthen, in accordance with its origin in [Note: [Col. 0856D] Pamelius and Franeker add “from.”] clay, or will it [Col. 0857A] be exalted because [Note: [Col. 0857D] “The repository of the divine treasure.” He calls the flesh the repository of the divine treasure, not without allusion to ancient rites and to a common expression among philosophers. For they called the flesh a tomb, as I have already shown from Plato, and Tertullian himself called it a coffin and a tomb. Yet treasures were formerly deposited in tombs—a fact better known than almost anything else. The flesh, therefore, is such a tomb that it encloses the divine treasure together with the soul. Moreover, a *conditorium* is a tomb. Petronius Arbiter: “She even followed the deceased into the tomb and began to guard him after he had been placed in the underground chamber, according to the Greek custom.” LAC.] it is the repository of the divine treasure?

Indeed, if that true light itself, which is in the person of Christ, contains life within itself, and that life is entrusted together with the light to the flesh, will the flesh to which life is entrusted perish? Certainly—if the treasure itself is also to perish. For things destined to perish are entrusted to things destined to perish, just as new wine is entrusted to old wineskins.

Likewise, when he adds: “Always carrying about the mortification of Christ Jesus in our body,” what kind of thing is this which, after being God’s temple, can now also be called Christ’s tomb? Why, moreover, do we carry the Lord’s mortification about in the body? “So that the life [Note: [Col. 0857C] Pamelius and Franeker add “of Jesus.”],” he says, “may also be manifested.” Where? “In the body.” [Note: [Col. 0857C] Pamelius and Franeker add “our.”] In what sort of body? “In a mortal one.” Therefore in the flesh—certainly mortal according to guilt, but also capable of life according to grace.

See how great that grace is: that in the flesh the life of Christ should be manifested. [Col. 0857B] Will the eternal, perpetual, incorruptible life of Christ—which is now also the life of God—be manifested in something foreign to salvation, [Note: [Col. 0857C] “To salvation,” Rhenanus and Semler.] in a substance destined for everlasting dissolution? Or at what time will the Lord’s life be manifested in our body? Will it be the life which [Note: [Col. 0857C] “By which,” Rhenanus and Semler.] he lived until his Passion, which was manifested not only among the Jews but has now also been disclosed to all nations? Rather, he signifies the life which broke apart the adamantine gates of death and the bronze bars of the lower regions, and which from that time onward is also ours.

Therefore, “it will be manifested in the body.” When? After death. How? When we rise in the body, just as Christ did. For lest anyone argue that the life of Jesus is now manifested in our body through the discipline of holiness, patience, righteousness, and wisdom, in which the Lord’s life flourished, the Apostle’s most provident intention insists: “For if we who live are delivered over to death because of Jesus, so that his life may be manifested in our mortal body,” he says that this will take place in our body after we have died. [Note: [Col. 0857C] “And so,” Pamelius and Franeker.] But if it will occur then, how can it occur unless the body has been raised?

Accordingly, also in the conclusion he says: [Col. 0858A] “Knowing that he who raised Jesus will also raise us with him, because he has already risen [Note: [Col. 0857C] “Has risen,” Rhenanus and Semler.] from the dead.” Is this not because “with him” has the sense “just like him”? But if just like him, then certainly not without the flesh.

## CHAPTER XLV

But again, with another sort of blindness, they stumble upon two men—the old and the new—because the Apostle admonishes us (Eph. IV) to put off the old man, [Note: [Col. 0857D] “Who is corrupted through the concupiscence of deception.” What he translates here with “through,” he renders below with “according to,” which better expresses the Greek word *κατά*. Meanwhile, the expression “the concupiscence of deception,” in the singular, is peculiar to the Author and, after him, to Hilary, *On the Trinity*, book XII, whereas all other editions of Ephesians IV, Syriac and Greek as well as Latin, read it in the plural. Yet we certainly read, according to three Vatican manuscripts, “to be renewed in the spirit of perception,” instead of “in the perception of the spirit”: first, because blessed Cyprian also so reads it in book III, *To Quirinus*, chapter 2; and secondly, because the Greek is *τῷ πνεύματι τοῦ νοῦς* (“in the spirit of the mind”), which the Latin translator rendered “in the spirit of the mind.” PAM.] “who is corrupted through the concupiscence of deception,” but to “be renewed in the spirit of perception and put on the new man, who according to God was created in righteousness and the holiness of truth.” By distinguishing here too according to two substances, they assign oldness to the flesh and newness to the soul, and maintain everlasting corruption for the old man, that is, for the flesh.

Yet if this is understood according to substances, neither is the soul the new [Col. 0858B] man merely because it came later, nor is the flesh the old man merely because it came first. For how very little time there was between God’s hand and his breath! I would dare say that even if the soul were much earlier than the flesh, the flesh, by the very fact that it waited to be filled with the soul, made itself prior. For every completion and perfection, [Note: [Col. 0858D] “Even if last in order.” It should be written *postumat*, not *posthumat*, both here and above in the book *Against the Valentinians*, chapter 55, and in the *Scorpiace Against the Gnostics*, as is evident from ancient monumental inscriptions cited by Aldus in his *Orthography*. William Canter likewise observed this in *New Readings*, book II, chapter 10, adding that *postumus* is derived not from one born after his father was buried, as Rhenanus and most suppose, but from the adverb *post*, just as *extumus* comes from *extra* and *citumus* from *citra*. It can therefore refer indifferently both to one who comes in the last place and to one born after his father’s death, as Cujacius too states somewhere. PAM.] even if last in order, nevertheless anticipates [Note: [Col. 0858C] “Nevertheless” is disregarded by Rhenanus and Semler.] in effect. That is more truly first without which the prior things cannot exist.

If the flesh is the old man, when did it become so? From the beginning? Yet Adam was wholly new, and no one becomes old from something new. Moreover, ever since the blessing of procreation, flesh and soul come into being simultaneously, without any reckoning of time; and the things which are also sown together [Note: [Col. 0858C] Pamelius and Franeker read “formed.”] in the womb—as we taught in our commentary on the soul—make these two men contemporaneous in conception and coeval [Note: [Col. 0858C] “Of equal age,” Pamelius, Paris, and Rigault.] in birth, [Note: [Col. 0858D] “They make them contemporaneous in conception and coeval in birth.” He coined these unusual verbs because *contemporaneus* and *coaetaneus* are Latin words. But he claims more license for himself than is proper. LE PR.] although they are indeed of two substances, but not also of two ages. [Col. 0858C] Thus they produce one man, since neither is prior.

It is more immediate and natural to say that we are wholly either old or new; for we do not know how we could be one of them only in part. But the Apostle plainly identifies the old man: “Put off,” [Note: [Col. 0858C] “Putting off,” Pamelius and Franeker; “putting off,” Junius.] he says, “the old man according to your former manner of life,” [Col. 0859A] not according to the old age of some substance. For he does not command us to put off the flesh, but those things which elsewhere too he shows to be fleshly, accusing works, not bodies. Concerning these he also adds here:

“Putting away falsehood, speak the truth, each one to his neighbor, because we are members of one another. Be angry, but do not sin. Let not the sun go down upon your anger. Nor give place to the devil. [Note: [Col. 0859D] “Place to the devil,” Rhenanus and Semler.] Let the one who used to steal [Note: [Col. 0859D] “Who steals,” Rhenanus and Semler.] steal no longer, but rather let him labor, working with his hands, so that he may have something to share with one in need. Let no foul speech proceed from your mouth, but only such as is best for the building up of faith, so that it may bestow grace upon those who hear. And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, in whom you have been sealed for the day of redemption. Let all bitterness and wrath and clamor and blasphemy and indignation [Col. 0859B] [Note: [Col. 0859D] Rhenanus and Semler do not have “and indignation.”] be removed from you, together with all malice. But be kind to one another, merciful, forgiving one another, just as God also forgave you in Christ.”

Therefore, those who consider the flesh to be the old man—why do they not hasten their own death, so that, once the old man has been put off, they may fulfill the Apostle’s commands? For we, who have believed that the whole faith must be administered in the flesh—indeed, also through the flesh, to which belong the mouth for uttering every excellent word, the tongue for refraining from blasphemy, the heart for refraining from indignation, and the hands for working and giving—maintain that both the oldness and the newness of man pertain to a moral, not a substantial, distinction.

And thus we likewise acknowledge that the man who was old according to his former manner of life is the same man who is said to be corrupted according to [Col. 0859C] the concupiscence of deception, just as he is old according to his former manner of life—not according to the flesh through everlasting destruction. On the contrary, the man remains safe in his flesh and remains the same man, since what he has put off is a corrupt mode of conduct, not bodily substance.
