## CHAPTER IV

[Col. 1309A] A woman’s outward presentation comprises two forms [Note: [Col. 1309C] “A woman’s outward presentation comprises two forms: cultivation and adornment.” On account of this distinction, two titles would suit these two books: this one, *On the Cultivation of Women*—that is, on gold, gems, and clothing; the following one, *On the Adornment of Women*—that is, on care of the hair and skin, etc. RIG.]: cultivation and adornment. ## CHAPTER VIII

Likewise, even their slaves wear the distinction of colored garments. They also misuse for wall hangings, in place of paintings, Tyrian, hyacinthine, and those royal fabrics that you laboriously unravel and refashion. Among them purple is cheaper than red ocher.

For what rightful honor can garments possess through the adulteration [Col. 1312B] of wrongful colors? [Note: [Col. 1311D] “What rightful honor can garments possess through the adulteration of wrongful colors?” He censures the luxury of women who introduced into garments colors not native to the fleece or wool—namely, purple, hyacinth, and scarlet. He therefore concludes that colors which wool does not possess as its own are wrongful and consequently adulterous. He soon explains the argument and says that wool of this kind, steeped in colors not its own, lends garments a wrongful honor. Garments dyed with a foreign and ostentatious color possess a wrongful honor, splendor, or brilliance that does not pertain to Christian decency and modesty. RIG.]

These things, therefore, which do not come from God, the author of nature, are not naturally excellent; they are thus understood to come from the devil, the corrupter of nature. For they cannot belong to anyone else if they do not belong to God, because things that are not God’s must necessarily belong to his rival; and no one besides the devil and his angels is God’s rival.

But if the materials come from God [Note: [Col. 1312D] “But if the materials come from God, it does not immediately follow that products of this kind made from them do also.” After that most severe censor has proscribed hyacinthine, purple, and sky-blue garments, and other instruments of luxury of that kind, under a harsh condemnation, he answers the objection of those who repeatedly asserted that all the things he attacked so vehemently had been created by God, just like everything else; that those marvelous colors were made from the juices of plants and the secretions of shellfish; and that plants and shellfish were rightly counted among the things created by God and created for the sake of human beings. He therefore dissolves the sophistry by making a distinction. He acknowledges that the materials come from God—plants, I mean, and shellfish, their juices, and those famous secretions of shellfish. But he denies that the use and enjoyment of those things comes from God when they are converted into preparations for wickedness and luxury. He proves this by the example of spectacles—the circus, the arena, and the stage. A human being may indeed look upon or praise horses, panthers, and the voice as things created by God and as divine gifts. Yet it does not immediately follow that the use made of those things comes from God—for example, the use of horses for the insane factions in the circus, of panthers or lions for foul disembowelments and savage slaughters, or of stage performers’ voices for empty or indecent stories. These things do not come from God, any more than idolatry does, even though the incense, wine, and fire through which idolatry is performed were created by God. “But,” he says, “if the materials come from God, it does not immediately follow that products of this kind made from them do also; for all those profane pleasures of worldly spectacles, just like idolatry itself—as we have published our own volume about them—consist of things belonging to God,” etc. RIG.—“About them” (another reading has “about those things”), “his own volume”: namely, the book *On the Spectacles*. Yet a Christian ought not on that account be affected by the frenzies of the circus, the atrocities of the arena, or the obscenities of the stage merely because God gave the horse, the panther, and the voice to humankind. Materials, or created things, have a twofold evaluation—that is, they are assessed and valued from two different perspectives. A Christian, for example, will assess a horse differently from a pagan. A Christian will use a horse to support the weakness of nature or age; to arrive more promptly for the celebration of sacrificial offerings or for administering or hearing the word of God; to assist a brother in distress more swiftly; or to arrange more conveniently for a man wounded by robbers to be carried to an inn. But a pagan will buy a horse at a higher price than a tutor and will take greater care over its training than over his son’s, so that it may race around the turning posts in the circus with practiced bends, outrun its opponent, and seize the palm—with no other result than that it is exalted by the shouts of a maddened populace:

> Conspicuous in flight before the rest, and first to raise dust upon the plain.

Tertullian therefore says that one and the same material, one and the same animal, possesses two utterly different evaluations. The former, which we have illustrated in the Christian, is a truly praiseworthy and holy evaluation descending from God; the latter, which we have illustrated in the pagan, is foreign, alien, and adulterous. “And the evaluation,” he says, “descending from God therefore accuses and condemns the evaluation alien to God, as guilty, namely, of worldly and thoroughly un-Christian glory.” RIG.] accuses what is alien to God, as guilty, namely, of worldly glory.

## [SUMMARY]

[Col. 1315A] **SUMMARY.—**This book has almost the same subject as the preceding one, except that it censures the care of the hair and skin more particularly.

I. He teaches that the chastity befitting God’s female servants, by which they differ from pagans, consists wholly in simple cleanliness. Chapter I.

II. Excessive attention to pleasing through adornment opens the way to temptations and places those who look upon it in danger. Chapter II.

III. One must not glory in the flesh unless, when it has been mangled for Christ, it has endured so that the spirit may be crowned in it. Chapter III.

IV. Beauty will offer no spectacle to a believing husband, but will be an object of suspicion to an unbelieving one. Chapter IV.

V. God the craftsman is censured by those who anoint their skin with cosmetics, stain their cheeks, and smear their eyes. Chapter V.

VI. Those who, like German and Gallic women, change their hair with saffron dye [Col. 1315B] or attach curling arrangements made from another person’s hair make an evil omen for themselves and reserve destruction for themselves. Chapters VI–VIII.

VII. The colors of garments, works of gold, and precious stones come not from God but from sinful angels. Chapters IX–XI.

VIII. Indeed, Scripture establishes that the enticements of beauty are associated with bodies offered for prostitution. Chapter XII.

IX. Finally, their works ought to shine; they must cast away the luxuries by which the strength of faith can be rendered effeminate, since in the age of the Christians especially their lives were then passed amid the sword.
