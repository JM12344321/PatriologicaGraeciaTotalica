Besides this work of Tertullian, there survive, among those written after he had already fallen away from orthodoxy, the five books Against Marcion, which may unquestionably be charged with Montanist errors. For even if Tertullian’s own declaration (IV.22), where he says that his own party and the “Psychics” [Col. 0137A] disagree on the subject of ecstasy, were considered highly doubtful, and even if one were unwilling to take account of the visions by which, he says, those doctrines were confirmed (III.24; V.8), nevertheless—since all these books Against Marcion were not published at the same time—in the first book devoted to that subject he calls the discipline of Montanus his own, and represents himself as defending, on the authority of the Paraclete, his own decrees against repeated marriages (ch. 29); and the chronology itself shows that this book was written after he had already begun to belong to the Montanist party.

Since many of those who have labored to determine the date of Tertullian’s books have never even thought to doubt the latter point that we have undertaken to discuss, while others have said much about it incorrectly, we believe that, after repeated consideration of the matter, we ought to discuss it more precisely. And, to speak first [Col. 0137B] of the first book, no one says that its date is disputed. Quite rightly and properly; for there is an explicit and decisive declaration on the matter by Tertullian himself (I.15), who acknowledges that he composed this book in Severus’s fifteenth year. But when scholars say nothing about the date of the others, by their very silence they imply that they belong to the same period. This is far from being the case. For from the words with which Tertullian concluded the first book—[Note: [Col. 0137D] “If anyone thinks that we have accomplished too little, let him hope that what remains has been reserved for its own time, as has also the examination of the Scriptures used by Marcion.”]—it is fairly clearly inferred that this book was published separately and the others composed at a different time.

Although we do not venture to say whether he wrote the second, third, and fourth books separately or together, or at what time, we nevertheless affirm as beyond doubt that Tertullian did not publish the fifth book at the same time [Col. 0137C] as the others. For the book On the Resurrection is later than the one he composed On the Flesh of Christ (On the Resurrection, ch. 2), in which latter work (On the Flesh of Christ, ch. 7) he cites a passage from the fourth book Against Marcion, which we shall discuss below. Yet in the fifth book Against Marcion, ch. 10, he refers back to the work he had written On the Resurrection; from this it is clear that Tertullian brought the fifth book to light at a different time from that at which he published the fourth. Likewise, in the first book Against Marcion, ch. 1, he promises that he will someday complete a work On the Prescription of Heretics; but in On the Flesh of Christ, ch. 2, he says that such a work had already been written by him. This too therefore shows that the first book was not published in the same production as all the others.

Since this is so, we may confidently affirm that the first book Against Marcion appeared in A.D. 207—or, if one prefers, 208—[Col. 0137D] since Severus’s fifteenth year falls within both; but as to when the others were written, nothing can be said except that the fifth is not of the same date as the rest.

## § 11.—Tillemont’s Contrary Opinion Corrected

We had feared that some might raise against these arguments of ours an objection like the one we discussed in connection with the Scorpiace: namely, that the books Against Marcion cited there are not those still surviving, but other books that had, so to speak, been withdrawn and had long since been lost. We see that Tillemont has in fact done approximately this (Mémoires pour servir à l’hist. Eccl., [Col. 0138A] vol. III, p. 666, note 19), for he defends that view most vigorously. We must therefore set forth what can be answered in support of our position and examine what Tillemont argued on the other side—but both very briefly, and in such a way that we now entirely pass over those points which we established by Tertullian’s own explicit testimony.

Thus only this question can be disputed: whether, in On the Flesh of Christ, Tertullian cited the fourth book Against Marcion that still survives. I certainly think that he did: first, because in that book he examined Marcion’s scriptural text, and there (IV.19) is found precisely what he touched upon in On the Flesh of Christ; secondly, because, when he was producing against Marcion the first book that still survives, he had not yet published On the Prescription of Heretics. But just as the latter had already appeared publicly when Tertullian composed On the Flesh of Christ, as we have just proved, so it is plainly necessary that by then the first of the surviving books Against Marcion had also already been published. [Col. 0138B] Indeed, when he wrote this book, Tertullian had withdrawn and discarded the two little works in which he had formerly attacked Marcion; hence it is evident that in On the Flesh of Christ he could not have referred his readers to them.

But let us nevertheless hear Tillemont’s argument to the contrary. He says first that the following works of Tertullian—the Scorpiace, On the Flesh of Christ, On the Soul, and On the Resurrection—may be said to precede the surviving books Against Marcion, and that the books cited in them under that title are those which perished long ago. As regards the Scorpiace, I do not object, [Col. 0138C] for that question has been sufficiently treated; but concerning the others I do not agree. We have just stated what judgment must be made about the work entitled On the Flesh of Christ; if anyone wishes to resist that judgment, we shall hear him. And when we demonstrated this, it simultaneously became clear that what Tillemont maintained to the contrary was even further from applying to On the Resurrection—which is undoubtedly later than On the Flesh of Christ.

Finally, in On the Soul, ch. 21, Tertullian says that he had taught Marcion that the human soul is moved from no external source. Since he said much about this matter in the second surviving book Against Marcion, ch. 6 ff., it is not absurd to say that he had that book in view in the former work. Yet we shall not insist upon this stubbornly, since we have not used the testimony of On the Soul.

The second objection is positively childish. In the passage from On the Flesh of Christ that we cited [Col. 0138D] as evidence, Tertullian called the book Against Marcion to which he referred a “little book”; consequently, he cannot have meant to cite the fourth of the surviving books, which is not a little book but a large volume. What a foolish conclusion! As though Tertullian had not called the whole work Against Marcion a “little work” (I.1; I.31; II.1; etc.), or as though something that constitutes part of a larger work could not be called a “little book.” But on that reasoning, it must be said that Tertullian cited a work not yet completed! Could this truly have caused scruple to so eminent a man, who has a thousand times invoked in his commentaries his own books that had not yet been completed? And who will deny that Tertullian acted in the same way, if he has read our defense of our argument?

Finally, Tillemont says that Tertullian gives one account of the place in which holy men are kept from [Col. 0139A] death until the day of the Last Judgment in On the Soul, ch. 55, and On the Resurrection, ch. 43, but another in Against Marcion IV.34. I hear him. But do those books conflict in the passages that Tertullian says he had treated in his disputation against Marcion? Tillemont has never proved this; nor will anyone say that such a conclusion can be admitted merely because Tertullian departed from himself in other matters that do not pertain to the specific issue.

## § 12.—The Book *On the Pallium*. Discussion of the Threefold Power of the Present Empire, and Inquiry into the Year in Which Geta Assumed the Dignity of Augustus

Let us now take up the little book On the Pallium—obscure indeed and sufficiently confused, yet filled with a vast knowledge of all antiquity. [Col. 0139B] Since the opinions of the most learned men concerning its date diverge so greatly, we must either examine them candidly or present our own.

Here, however, there first arises a question that is easily resolved: what is that “threefold power of the present empire, with God favoring so many Augusti as one,” to which Tertullian attributes the prosperity of those times? [Note: [Col. 0139D] Ch. 2: “How many cities has the threefold power of the present empire either brought forth, enlarged, or restored, with God favoring so many Augusti as one! How many census rolls have been transcribed!”] Some (Jac. Gutherius, De officiis domus Augustae, Leipzig ed., p. 277) maintain that these words indicate Severus alone, because he had overcome those three rivals for the empire, Julianus, Niger, and Albinus; others understand Severus together with Niger and Albinus (the Magdeburg Centuriators, cent. III, p. 241); others Albinus, Severus, and Antoninus (Pamelius, Life of Tertullian, p. 10 D, 14 F); and still others understand Severus with his two sons Bassianus [Col. 0139C] and Geta.

But I approve none of the first three opinions. Gutherius’s view is so contorted and utterly foolish that I scarcely know whether anyone has ever followed things so plainly perverse and false. I shall not dwell upon the fact that the good man failed to see that this is called the threefold power of the present empire, that God is said to have favored so many Augusti as one, and that they too are said to have brought forth, enlarged, and restored many cities. Let someone tell me whether these things can be affirmed of Julianus, Niger, and Albinus—especially when they had already been killed. Nor shall I say how badly he applied Herodian’s words, III.7, where Herodian marvels that Severus ἕνα ἄνδρα τρεῖς καθελόντα βασιλέας ἤδη κρατοῦντας (“one man having overthrown three emperors who were already in power”); for these matters were already sufficiently discussed by Boecler (Annotations on Herodian, p. 420).

[Col. 0139D] I wish to examine only this one point, which is especially relevant to our subject: whether Geta was never made Augustus while Severus was alive. Since Gutherius maintains this, he first fails to distinguish Geta, Severus’s brother, from Geta Caesar, Severus’s son, as he ought to have done. For when Spartianus (Severus, ch. 10) says that Severus called his son Bassianus Caesar in order to remove his brother Geta from the hope of imperial power which the latter had conceived, is it not reasonable that he was speaking of Geta, Severus’s brother? Secondly, the fact that in many headings of the Code the names of the Augusti Severus and Antoninus are found, but never Geta’s, [Col. 0140A] contributes absolutely nothing to the matter. For from the time when Geta assumed the dignity of Augustus, as will be shown below, he was always his father’s companion in the war; no one should wonder that in wartime he was unable to devote thought to laws affecting the commonwealth.

What is altogether intolerable, however, is that Gutherius denies that Geta was called Augustus during his father’s lifetime and uses, to prove this, coins on which Geta’s second tribunician power is recorded. For since he himself adds that after his father’s death Geta reigned with his brother for only ten months and fifteen days, it is plainly necessary that Geta had been called Augustus before his father’s death.

The second opinion, that of the very learned men who composed the Magdeburg Centuries of sacred history, holds that Severus together with Niger and Albinus constituted that threefold power. To speak with Tillemont, it is rightly [Col. 0140B] convicted of error because it treats the source of the Roman Empire’s evils as the source from which flowed that great imperial prosperity of which Tertullian speaks.

And am I to think more favorably of Pamelius, from whose case the truth is usually as far removed as possible? He could even call Albinus an Augustus—although everyone knows that Albinus undertook hostilities against Severus because the dignity of Augustus had been denied him (see Dio Cassius, LXXV.4, p. 1258)—and make Caracalla his colleague in imperial power, though Caracalla was not granted the honors of Augustus until Albinus had long since been killed. If, with Scaliger, I were to say that he asserted these things in jest, I should be saying too little: he has cast such darkness over the truth.

It therefore remains only that we understand this threefold power as Severus together with his sons, now Augusti. [Col. 0140C] Of them God can be said to have favored many “as one,” to use Septimius’s expression, since, if we wish to speak properly, Severus alone reigned, while the other two were Augusti more in name than in reality. Hence, if we establish in what year Geta was raised to the dignity of Augustus, these words will contribute no more to knowledge and understanding of the book’s date [reading uncertain; the context may require “contribute not a little”].

Dodwell, indeed, shrewdly observed, as he did many other things (Camden Lectures XIX, § 21, p. 621 ff.), and confirmed with many examples, that the Roman emperors took care that the tribunician powers of their sons should be reckoned from their imperial quinquennial or decennial anniversaries. But who does not know that A.D. 208 marked Severus’s fifteenth anniversary and Caracalla’s tenth? Accordingly, scarcely anyone can doubt that in the same year the dignity of Augustus was conferred upon Geta; and what prevents us from saying that, according to the custom of the Roman emperors, the same thing befell Geta on the very same day on which it had once befallen Severus and Caracalla?

[Col. 0140D] And if this fact might perhaps seem doubtful because it could have happened earlier, or because emperors had not followed this one rule everywhere—although that is not likely in the case of Severus, who was an ardent student of Chaldaean astrology and carefully observed favorable days—the coins nevertheless place it beyond all doubt. For if we attend to the numbering of Geta’s tribunician power, by which the years of this dignity can be determined with the greatest accuracy, it does not advance beyond the fourth on Geta’s coins. But Geta held supreme rule jointly with Caracalla until approximately February [Col. 0141A] A.D. 212. [Note: [Col. 0141D] It is not so necessary for us to confirm this with many arguments, for Tillemont has sufficiently performed that task in his work entitled Histoire des empereurs, vol. II, part I, Brussels ed., p. 185, and in the notes, p. 391.] The fourth year of Geta’s tribunician power must therefore rightly be assigned to the middle of A.D. 211; if we count backward from it, it follows that Geta assumed the dignity of Augustus after half of A.D. 208 had elapsed. From this it is also evident that Tertullian’s On the Pallium is later than the middle of that year.

## § 13.—The View of Basnage and Walch, Who Maintained That This Book Was Published in A.D. 211, Is Rendered Doubtful, and the Little Book Is Assigned to A.D. 208

But because a more precise account is required of the year in which this book was written, those who discuss the matter more minutely, so far as I can see, adopt the view that it must be placed in the year 211. We need not concern ourselves [Col. 0141B] with the others, who state more generally that this work was composed toward the end of Severus’s reign. But those who have subjected the matter more strictly to chronological precision—Samuel Basnage (Annales politico-ecclesiastici, vol. II, p. 217), and the venerable Walch of Göttingen, who follows him (Antiquitates pallii philosophici veterum Christianorum, p. 227 ff.)—these men, I say, determine that this book was written in the year we have named, since there are certain similarities between that year’s events and the matters Tertullian mentioned here.

Yet after carefully examining the matter, my mind has inclined more toward the view that this little book was composed approximately in the year 208. Therefore, with the permission of that venerable man—who, I hope, will readily pardon an honest person who wishes to discover the truth without any contentiousness—[Col. 0141C] I shall explain very briefly both the considerations that move me to doubt somewhat the truth of his opinion and those that appear in some measure to commend the view I would like to adopt.

The entire opinion of those who think otherwise is evidently established by the similarity between what Tertullian says and what we know happened in A.D. 211. But although this argument does not seem objectionable in itself, it nevertheless possesses little strength, I think, if the same similarity exists between Tertullian’s statements and what occurred in A.D. 208; and it loses all probative force if it can be shown that what occurred in A.D. 211 was entirely different from what he recorded in our book. Both conditions are plainly present here.

[Col. 0141D] For, to speak first of the latter point: when Tertullian committed our little book to writing, Africa was already free from earthquakes (ch. 2); there was the repose of peace and abundance, and all was well through both the empire and the heavens (ch. 1). Yet at the time when he wrote to Scapula the words with which he sought to turn him away from cruelty toward Christians—a work composed approximately in A.D. 211, as we shall show below—the Africans had already been terrified for a year by rains, fire, thunder, an eclipse of the sun, and other events. Since Tertullian maintains that all these things were punishments inflicted upon the worshipers of the gods because they had kindled so many blazes of misery [Col. 0142A] against Christians, it is plainly necessary that this persecution of the Christians had already extended throughout an entire year.

Suppose, then, that the little book On the Pallium was written in A.D. 211: does not Tertullian contradict himself by ascribing both the highest prosperity and the gravest calamities to the same period? I do not deny that it might perhaps be said that, when these calamities had abated for a while, quiet and tranquil intervals, as it were, intervened. But since the records of ancient history have transmitted absolutely nothing from which this circumstance might in some way be inferred, would it be permissible to invent it in order to assist an opinion that seems almost to have reached its end?

Let those calamities, meanwhile, have been intermittent for a time; shall we nevertheless also say that the extreme injuries committed against Christians were interrupted? [Col. 0142B] You will say yes, for how could prosperous times have existed unless these too had ceased somewhat? But I have learned that Tertullian pursued his enemies, as it were, into every hiding place, and used even the slightest circumstance to convict of error and wickedness those who opposed the cause of the Christians. Would I then suppose that this same Tertullian would have argued against them simply by saying that, just as the gravest calamities had followed the adversaries’ injuries against Christians, so in turn the happiest times had followed their goodwill toward them?

There is still another point readily at hand. For when Tertullian composed this little book On the Pallium, there was the repose of peace, and in truth the world was “the empire’s most cultivated estate, tilled and delightful beyond Alcinous’s orchard and Midas’s rose garden” (ch. 2). How was this so? [Col. 0142C] “Every aconite of hostility” had been rooted out. Let this book, then, have been written at the beginning of A.D. 211; for at the beginning of February Severus ceased to be among the living, and the “threefold power of the empire” also ceased. Yet at that time the war against the Britons had not yet ended, nor did it ever end thereafter while Severus lived; only after his death did Antoninus finally make peace with the Britons (see Herodian, III.15, p. 173). Since, therefore, in that year there was certainly neither the repose of peace nor a world that was the empire’s most cultivated estate, a more suitable setting must be assigned to this book—namely, the closing part of the year 208—especially because everything Tertullian relates here agrees perfectly with it.

For if you ask about the threefold power of the present empire, [Col. 0142D] Geta had indeed only just assumed the dignity of Augustus. If you ask about the Carthaginians made happy by a new favor, the coins had already long since revealed them as such. [Note: [Col. 0142D] See Spanheim in his immortal work De praestantia et usu numismatum, diss. XII, vol. II, p. 443, who says that those coins of Severus and Antoninus bearing *Felix Karthago* relate to the restoration to that city of the right to strike coins. If the very common coins that celebrate *Indulgentia Augg. in Carthag.* refer to this same matter—or at least to a related one, as seems to me—this occurred approximately in A.D. 204 or 205, because on some of them it is combined with Caracalla’s sixth tribunician power. They were certainly issued before the little book On the Pallium was written, for nowhere on them is Geta’s name found, but only those of Severus and Caracalla.] If you ask about the “cactus and bramble of treacherous intimacy,” Plautianus, Severus’s closest intimate, had already been removed from the scene in A.D. 204 (see Tillemont, Histoire des Empereurs, loc. cit., p. 413 ff.). If you ask about “the hope of scorched Campania from its mountains” (ch. 2), Dio [Col. 0143A] Cassius expressly recorded it (LXXVI.2, p. 1272) at the time when Plautianus perished. Finally, if you ask about every aconite of hostility being rooted out and the repose of peace, these things certainly existed before Severus departed for Britain.

But where, then, had the barbarians been shut out? At that time they had not yet been kept out by the British wall, but they were nevertheless held back from the very frontiers of the Roman Empire by the defenses of the Roman legions. And since Tertullian speaks of many barbarians, what reason is there for us to understand the Britons alone? Therefore, since in A.D. 208 all those things mentioned by Tertullian were present; since no other year can be proposed to which they might be attributed with any greater right; and since, finally, the very recent memory of Geta’s dignity appears to have provided Tertullian with the occasion to mention it, [Col. 0143B] our conclusion—that On the Pallium belongs to A.D. 208—appears to fall little short of certainty.

## § 14.—It Is Proved That the Book *To Scapula* Appeared at the Beginning of the Reign of Antoninus Caracalla, in A.D. 211

The last book of this class—and, I believe, perhaps the last of all Tertullian’s works—is the one he wrote to Scapula, governor of Africa, whose cruelty against Christians undoubtedly occurred after the edict of the emperor Severus. For Hilarianus, whom Tertullian mentions in our little book, was governor of Africa at the time when Felicitas and Perpetua were put to death with their companions—an event which all acknowledge to have occurred after the laws enacted by Severus.

Moreover, from the very words with which Tertullian charges Scapula [Col. 0143C] with the greatest injustice, judging (ch. 4) that he could have discharged the duty of his jurisdiction while nevertheless remembering humanity, since nothing more was commanded than that those who confessed should be condemned, while those who denied should be recalled to torture—from these words, I say, it is fairly clearly inferred that certain laws of Severus hostile to Christians then existed. For before those laws had been issued, when the governors acted solely by the authority of those measures by which earlier emperors had harassed Christians, Tertullian argued vehemently against their force in the Apology.

[Col. 0143D] This point might indeed seem slight and need no distinct argument, had not Pamelius (Life of Tertullian, p. 13 C) thought that this book should be assigned to A.D. 195. How carelessly he argued is shown by the very words with which he says he was induced to think so: namely, that Tertullian included the supporters of Albinus among the emperor’s enemies (ch. 2).

Now, if it is true that the calamities by which Scapula persecuted the Christians came after Severus’s law, it is no less evident that they did not occur during Severus’s reign, although I see that this has pleased some scholars. For in the passage (On the Pallium, ch. 4) where Tertullian says that Severus, Antoninus’s father, had been mindful of Christians, and that Antoninus himself, nourished on Christian milk, had known Proculus, let someone tell me why he called Severus “Antoninus’s father,” a detail plainly irrelevant to the matter, or why he cited Antoninus himself as a witness, unless these words were written during Antoninus’s reign.

Nor indeed should this be doubted because we find no tortures of Christians under Caracalla recorded by other authors, or because such tortures could also be later than Caracalla’s time. [Col. 0144A] [Col. 0144A] The reason why no one else recorded them undoubtedly lies in the fact that they neither lasted long nor occurred outside Africa. For Tertullian now says that the Christian household was being attacked by the governor of the legion and of Mauretania, and he asks Scapula at least to spare Carthage and the province and to imitate the example of Arrius Antoninus, who in Asia had once diverted Christians from torture rather than dragged them to punishment.

[Col. 0144B] That these events were not later than Caracalla’s reign is established by the fact that Jerome (On Illustrious Men, ch. 73, Fabricius ed., p. 126) says that Tertullian did not live beyond the time of Antoninus; that, moreover, no hostilities against Christians of every kind are found down to Decius [Note: [Col. 0144D] Samuel Basnage sufficiently defended this against Scaliger and others, loc. cit., p. 218, no. 11.]; and that what is related in our little book agrees excellently with Caracalla’s period.

Although it has not been established with complete certainty in what year this defense of the Christians was composed, it nevertheless seems possible, by a measure of probability appropriate to such a matter, to claim it for the first year of Antoninus’s reign. For the solar eclipse which Tertullian says occurred in the previous year (ch. 3) was assigned by Antoninus Lalovera to A.D. 210. [Note: [Col. 0144D] In his letter to Joannes Bessonus, published by Petrus Possinus in the notes to the Acts of Perpetua and Felicitas, in Ruinart’s Acta primorum martyrum sincera, Amsterdam ed., p. 119.] Although Tillemont resists this on the ground that Tertullian said the sun could not have suffered this through an ordinary eclipse, since it was positioned at its own *hypsoma*, nevertheless Scaliger, the most expert in these matters, long ago ridiculed this method of argument employed by Septimius, who knows how repeatedly to turn everything—even things foolish enough—to his own purpose [Col. 0144C] (Animadversions on Eusebius’s Chronicle, p. 10).

Secondly, since Caracalla never enacted any law against Christians, these calamities inflicted upon them by Scapula must surely have been remnants of Severus’s cruelty. They appear to have been wholly extinguished when Caracalla, seeking to secure the empire for himself, recalled those who had been sent into exile (see Dio Cassius, LXXVII.3). Since he did this shortly after killing his brother Geta at the beginning of A.D. 212, we do not unreasonably say that Scapula acted against the Christians in A.D. 211.

# ARTICLE IV. On the Books of Tertullian Whose Date, Doctrine, or Both Are Unknown

[Col. 0144D]

## § 1.—The Third Class of Tertullian’s Books: Those Which, Although of Uncertain Date, Are Nevertheless Known to Have Been Written Before His Defection

This third discussion will bring to a close the Septimian studies that we have undertaken. Since we have thus far treated those books whose date and doctrine could be demonstrated by some definite method and reasoning, we must now append the others, which do not reveal their date [Col. 0145A] with sufficient precision, although their doctrine is nevertheless not very obscure—except in a few cases that appear to possess an equivocal character.

We shall therefore first adduce those earlier works which show that Tertullian was not yet wholly committed to the Montanist party; next, those upon which the marks of Montanism are by no means lightly impressed; and finally, those which leave the reader in doubt whether Tertullian, when he composed them, had been separated from the common Church or not.

When we say that the date of these three classes of Septimian works cannot be assigned with sufficient certainty, we do not mean that the date to be attributed to each is entirely unknown—for something can also be argued about it with probability—but rather that the precise year in which the books were written cannot be determined. This we venture [Col. 0145B] to deny, because there are in them no certain indications that can be assigned to a particular and definite year. But let us come to the matter proposed.

## § 2.—The Book *On Prayer*, Written Before the Defection and Published in 196 or 197
