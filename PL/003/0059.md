## ARTICLE IV. That it is false that Christians worship the genitals of a priest; that the pagans are convicted of fouler enormities; and what occasion gave rise to the accusation that Christians practiced that most shameful veneration

Caecilius further charges the Christians with another deed, full indeed of infamy and disgrace, yet in such a way that he does not venture to accuse them positively of so great an outrage: “Others,” he says, “report that they worship the genitals of their own bishop and priest, and adore his sexual nature as though he were their parent.” [Col. 0516A] Certainly nothing is more frivolous than this calumny, or less grounded in truth. Caecilius, moreover, says that it was practiced, not according to everyone, but only according to certain persons who circulated as a false rumor what they did not dare to affirm.

Octavius therefore refutes this wholly fictitious charge, most unjustly branded upon the Christians, by his customary twofold reply. First, he denies that they ever did such a thing and declares it a ridiculous tale and sheer calumny. He then asserts that the pagans were defiled by obscenities far fouler—things which it was unlawful for chaste ears either to name or to hear. But it is better that these matters be expressed in his words rather than ours:

> “He who invents tales against us about the worship of a priest’s male organs is trying to transfer his own practices to us. For such things may perhaps be sacred rites of their lewdness, among people with whom every sex prostitutes itself through every member; among whom utter impurity is called sophistication; who envy the license of prostitutes; who lick men about the waist, fastening their lustful mouths upon their groins—men of evil tongue even when silent, who grow weary of their impurity before they become ashamed of it. What wickedness! They perpetrate upon themselves a deed of the utmost depravity, such as neither a more tender age can endure nor a harsher slavery compel. We are not permitted even to hear of these and similar outrages; to defend them at greater length is itself shameful.”

Although he said that it was unlawful to explain these utterly shameful pagan enormities more explicitly, or for any Christian to hear them, or for any other person to defend them, [Col. 0516B–C] they were nevertheless no less true and well known to everyone. For, besides the various testimonies concerning them given by poets and other writers, especially Martial, the most holy Apostle, in his Epistle to the Romans alone, reproached the Gentiles with several extremely serious offenses, with all the greater justice because he knew with absolute certainty that they were true. (St Paul, *Epistle to the Romans*, chap. I, verses 16 ff.)

Our author therefore rightly turns the pagans’ argument back upon them. No one, indeed, was ever able to prove that the Christians were guilty of the great crime of which they were suspected. On the contrary, their most devout manner of life entirely and utterly dispelled so ridiculous a suspicion. The Gentiles, however, were certainly convicted of this and still greater crimes by their own authors, as has been said, and by other witnesses whose credibility was beyond all question. [Col. 0516D]

Yet someone will object: although the claim that Christians worshiped a priest’s genitals was wholly at variance with the truth, it is nevertheless incredible that a public rumor concerning so horrible a crime could have been spread unless at least some occasion for it had been given. To anyone wishing to know what that occasion was, we reply that Christians who, during the earlier centuries of the Church, performed public penance for their sins and graver offenses prostrated themselves outside the doors and upon the threshold of the church at everyone’s feet, especially those of the priests, so that they might obtain their pardon. When the Gentiles heard of this through vague and uncertain rumor, they fabricated from it the tale that Christians worshiped a priest’s genitals. [Col. 0517A]

For concerning penitents of this sort, whom we have discussed more fully elsewhere, Tertullian writes above all:

> “Exomologesis is a discipline for prostrating and humbling a person. . . . It generally consists in sustaining prayers by fasts, groaning, weeping, and crying aloud day and night to the Lord one’s God; falling at the feet of the presbyters and kneeling before those dear to God; and enjoining upon all the brethren the office of intercession on one’s behalf.”  
> (Tertullian, *On Penance*, chap. 6, p. 146.)

Again, although at that time he had become entangled in the errors of the Montanists and had departed from the true doctrine of the Church, he says:

> “And indeed, when bringing the repentance of an adulterer into the church to entreat the brotherhood, you prostrate him in the midst, clothed in sackcloth and covered with ashes, composed in shame and horror, before the widows and before the presbyters, grasping the hem of everyone’s garment, licking everyone’s footprints, and clinging to everyone’s knees.”  
> (The same, *On Modesty*, chap. 13, p. 729.)

Eusebius also relates that Natalis the confessor, after being thoroughly deceived by the heretics Asclepiodotus and Theodotus the money-changer, had accepted a salary and been made bishop of their sect. But after he had often been warned of so great a crime by nocturnal visions and had disregarded them, he was most severely scourged throughout the night by holy angels:

> Ὥστε ἕωθεν ἀναστῆναι, καὶ ἐνδυσάμενον σάκκον, καὶ σποδὸν καταπασάμενον μετὰ πολλῆς σπουδῆς, καὶ δακρύων προσπεσεῖν Ζεφυρίνῳ τῷ ἐπισκόπῳ, κυλιόμενον ὑπὸ τοὺς πόδας οὐ μόνον τῶν ἐν τῷ κλήρῳ, ἀλλὰ καὶ τῶν λαικῶν, συγχέαι τε τοῖς δάκρυσι τὴν εὔσπλαγχνον ἐκκλησίαν τοῦ ἐλεήμονος Χριστοῦ.
>
> “So that, rising at first light, putting on sackcloth, and sprinkling himself with ashes, he hastened with many tears and fell at the feet of Bishop Zephyrinus; rolling at the feet not only of the clergy but also of the laity, he moved and profoundly stirred by his tears the compassionate Church of the merciful Christ.” [Col. 0517B–C]

Who, then, will deny that it is extremely probable that the pagans seized upon this pious and holy custom—which they either did not sufficiently understand or maliciously interpreted—as an opportunity falsely to accuse the Christians of the shameful worship of which our author speaks?

# CHAPTER XII. OTHER ACCUSATIONS MADE BY CAECILIUS, AND OCTAVIUS’ ANSWERS TO THEM, ARE EXAMINED

[Col. 0517D]

## ARTICLE I. How undeservedly Caecilius condemns the Christians’ assemblies and fasts, and calls them men of a desperate faction, a light-shunning nation, recognizing one another by secret signs and loving one another before they are acquainted

By means of insults rather than legitimate accusations, the pagans endeavored to bring the Christians into disrepute. For the things which the former charged as abominations and crimes, the latter maintained to be excellent, commendable, and properly instituted. Such, beyond doubt, is that practice which Caecilius imputes to the Christians as though it were some crime: namely, that through nocturnal gatherings and solemn fasts “they are bound together not by some sacred rite, but by an expiatory crime.” [Col. 0518A] Yet they readily acknowledged that this was just as true as it was false that anything other than the holiest actions took place in those assemblies, which, as Caecilius says, they were accustomed to hold on a solemn day. This is most clearly proved by the passages concerning them which we have cited elsewhere from the *Apology* of Justin Martyr and which will be produced below from Tertullian. Indeed, that point has already been sufficiently demonstrated by what we have thus far advanced against the shameless calumnies of Caecilius, through which he wrongly alleged that crimes as unspeakable as they were incredible were committed in those assemblies.

Would you like another witness, in this matter above every possible objection, to be presented to you? We shall produce Pliny, certainly a pagan, to whom the emperor Trajan had assigned the task of diligently investigating the crimes of which the Christians were accused. After examining everything with the greatest diligence he could employ, he wrote to Trajan that those Christians who, as he says, had abandoned the Christian faith affirmed:

> “That this had been the sum of either their guilt or their error: that they had been accustomed to assemble on a fixed day before dawn and to recite responsively among themselves a hymn to Christ as to a god; and to bind themselves by an oath, not to commit any crime, but not to commit theft, robbery, or adultery, not to break their word, and not to refuse to return a deposit when called upon. When these things had been completed, it was their custom to depart and then to assemble again to take food—ordinary food, however, and harmless.”  
> (Pliny, book X, *Letter to Trajan*, [Col. 0518B–C] p. 376.)

What could be clearer or more effective than this testimony—which we shall see transcribed by Tertullian—for proving the sanctity of Christian assemblies? (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 2.)

Thus no Christian was ashamed not only of these gatherings, but also of “the solemn fasts, worthy of every commendation”; rather, he gloried in them. For this reason Gregory of Nazianzus said in one of his orations against Julian the Apostate:

> “Such are we, worn out by long fasts and half-dead. Such are we who keep vigils in vain and trifle at nocturnal stations, yet nevertheless overthrow you.”  
> (Gregory of Nazianzus, Oration 4, p. 299.)

Someone, therefore, will doubtless be astonished at Gronovius, who suspects that in the text of Minucius, instead of *solemnibus jejuniis*, “solemn fasts,” one should read *olentibus jejuniis*, “malodorous fasts.” [Col. 0518D] Were not the Christians’ fasts—and above all the fasts of Lent—solemn at that time? Did Tertullian not write concerning them, “On Easter Day the observance of fasting is common and, as it were, public”? (Tertullian, *On Prayer*, chap. 14.) But if the observance was common and public, why was it not also solemn?

Gronovius continues: But these Christian fasts are taken in a bad sense by Caecilius, who plays the part of the pagans. Yet what prevents that pagan from having called them “solemn,” either in mockery or because he was speaking the truth? Anyone who desires more information about these and other Christian fasts should consult Irenaeus, cited by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chap. 24, p. 192; Tertullian, cited just above, *To His Wife*, book II, chap. 4; *On Baptism*, chap. 20; *On Flight*, chap. 1; *On Fasting*, chaps. 13 and 14; Athanasius, *Apology to the Emperor Constantius*, p. 310, and *Apology for His Flight*, p. 334; Cotelerius’ notes on Hermas, pp. 72 ff., and on book V of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, chap. 15, pp. 245 ff. We have discussed Christian vigils in vol. I of the *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation 1, chap. 6, article 3, pp. 712 ff.; fasting in the same work, book I, dissertation 4, article 5, p. 53, and article 23, p. 67; also book II, dissertation 1, chap. 7, article 2, p. 330; and book III, dissertation 1, chap. 1, article 7, p. 884; chap. 11, article 7, pp. 1038 ff.; and chap. 12, article 4, p. 1049.

Utterly intolerable, therefore, was the shamelessness of Caecilius and the other Gentiles, who dared to call Christians—adorned with the chastest conduct and assiduously devoted to virtue—men of a deplorable, unlawful, and desperate faction; a secretive, light-shunning nation, mute in public and babbling in corners; men who recognized one another by secret marks and tokens before they were acquainted, and who finally despised present torments and death altogether. [Col. 0519A–B] For, Tertullian said, precisely because we Christians, by merely repudiating our name, can immediately escape every kind of punishment and death, “we are regarded as desperate and ruined,” as Lactantius likewise stated. (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 50, p. 44; Lactantius, *Institutes*, book V, chap. 9, p. 463.)

Yet the more shameless was the insolence and effrontery of Caecilius and the other Gentiles, the greater certainly was the moderation of Octavius in answering on behalf of the Christians. For what, I ask you, could be more moderate than his reply?

> “Nor are we disdainful, if we all hold to the one good; gathered together, we are as peaceful as we are individually. Nor do we babble in corners, if you are either ashamed or afraid to hear us publicly.”

In this way he attenuates and refutes each particular head of the accusation. First, he denies that Christians are members of an unlawful faction because, he says, they all hold to the one good. But there can be no unlawful faction without opposing parties that contrive something against the public good. For this reason some suspect that instead of *fastidiosi*, “disdainful,” one should read *factiosi*, “factious.” The emendation would indeed not be a bad one if supported by the authority of some manuscript. [Col. 0519C–D] For who knows whether Minucius did not connect the word *factiosi* with the statement “we hold to the good”? What if he refutes the accusation of an unlawful faction with the words “we are all gathered together with the same peacefulness as when alone”? Yet if there is any room to doubt the true reading, our author nevertheless declares and proves most clearly that Christians cannot be called members of an unlawful faction.

Moreover, in this passage he follows closely in the footsteps of Tertullian, who demonstrates much more plainly, in the following words, that the unlawful faction was not that of the Christians but of the Gentiles themselves:

> “This association of Christians is certainly deservedly unlawful—if it is comparable to unlawful associations; deservedly to be condemned—if it is not unlike associations that deserve condemnation. If anyone complains about it on the charge ordinarily made against factions, for whose destruction have we ever assembled? We are the same when gathered together as when scattered, the same collectively as individually, injuring no one and grieving no one. When upright and good people meet, when the devout and the chaste assemble, it must not be called a faction but a council. Conversely, the name ‘faction’ must be applied to those who conspire in hatred of good and upright people, who clamor for the blood of the innocent—that is, of Christians.”  
> (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chaps. 39 and 40, p. 36.)

But more will be said about these matters when we discuss this book of Tertullian.

If the Christian community could not be called an unlawful faction, much less could it be called a “desperate” one, since, as Minucius says, “secure in the hope of future happiness, we are animated by faith in the present majesty of God.” [Col. 0520A–B] Thus it was not through any despair, but through the surest hope of eternity, that they most steadfastly endured every kind of torment for professing their religion; indeed, as we have said, they gloried in such torments. Lactantius presses the point more sharply: it was by no means the Christians, who “do not spare their bodies,” but the Gentiles themselves who ought to have been called desperate, since “nothing can be more desperate than to torture and tear apart someone whom you know to be innocent.” (Lactantius, *Institutes*, book V, chap. 9, p. 485.) Something more must be said about these matters later.

The pagans had no better right to object that the Christians were a secretive and light-shunning nation, mute in public and babbling in corners. For they preached their doctrine openly, and the pagans could readily “hear them publicly,” unless they were either ashamed that their own impiety and crimes should be exposed or afraid that the true religion should be demonstrated to them. [Col. 0520C]

Caecilius next objects that Christians recognize one another by secret marks and tokens. Octavius answers as truthfully as simply:

> “We readily recognize one another, not by a mark upon the body, as you suppose, but by the sign of innocence and modesty.”

We have nevertheless observed that the Carpocratians imprinted a certain mark upon the rear part of their adherents’ right ear, by which they doubtless distinguished them from other people. (*Apparatus*, vol. I, book II, dissertation 3, chap. 3, article 3, §12, p. 293.) But neither Caecilius nor Octavius is speaking of these or other heretics; they are speaking of Christians in general, who recognized one another, as our author says, by no sign other than that of innocence and modesty. Their innocence is established by what has been said thus far and by what will be said below; likewise their modesty, especially as they stood in the church or approached it, is clear from our annotations on the books of Clement of Alexandria. (*Apparatus*, vol. I, book III, dissertation 1, chap. 6, article 4, p. 713.) [Col. 0520D]

Finally, in answer to Caecilius’ last taunt concerning the Christians’ mutual affection, Minucius replies that Christians loved one another with mutual and sincere love both because they did not know how to hate others and because they were brothers who had one Father, as Tertullian had also said, partners in faith and coheirs of hope. Octavius further insists that the pagans reproached them for this mutual love, which surely ought to be esteemed most highly, because, as he declares in addressing them, “you rage in mutual hatred and recognize one another as brothers only when it comes expressly to parricide”; or, as Tertullian, whom we cited just above, says, “you are more ready to kill one another . . . because among them every name of blood-kinship is a mere pretense of affection.” [Col. 0521A] (Tertullian, loc. cit.) But these matters are treated more fully in our dissertation on this book of Tertullian. (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chap. 39, p. 35.) Meanwhile, concerning that brotherly charity of the Christians and the name “brethren,” see Lucian, *The Death of Peregrinus*, p. 996, and, if you please, our observations in the preceding volume, pp. 473 and 999.

## ARTICLE II. By what right Caecilius said that Christians despised temples as though they were tombs, and that they had no temples, no altars, and no known images; and the foolish cavils of the more recent Iconoclasts are refuted

[Col. 0521B]

It certainly appeared to Caecilius a most serious crime that Christians despised temples dedicated to the gods of the Gentiles just as they did tombs. “They scorn temples,” he said, “as though they were *busta*,” that is, tombs of dead men. For although the word *bustum* is derived from the verb *uro*, “I burn,” and originally signified the place in which the bodies of the dead were customarily burned, it is nevertheless commonly used by reputable authors to mean a tomb, as it is here by our author. [Col. 0521C]

If you ask for a witness, we shall immediately produce Tertullian, from whom Minucius appears to have drawn his material. He had previously said to the pagans: “We despise temples no less than tombs.” (Tertullian, *On the Shows*, chap. 13.) You see that the meaning of *busta* is clearly explained by Tertullian, who also confirms that Christians despised the temples of the gods as tombs of deceased human beings. Why should they not have despised them in this way, when Clement of Alexandria and others demonstrated, as we have noted elsewhere, that the temples of the gods were in fact the tombs of dead human beings? (*Apparatus*, vol. I, book III, dissertation 1, chap. 7, article 3, pp. 72 ff.)

Caecilius’ second complaint and accusation was that Christians not only despised the temples of the gods, but, in order to conceal their mysteries more securely, had no temples, no altars, and no images. Listen, I ask you, to the manner in which he complains about them:

> “Why do they have no altars, no temples, no known images? . . . Unless it is because whatever it is that they worship and conceal is either punishable or shameful.” [Col. 0521D]

Is this not the same complaint made in Origen by the pagan Celsus, who accused the Christians of avoiding the dedication—or rather the erection and installation—of altars, statues, and temples: βωμοὺς, καὶ ἀγάλματα, καὶ νεὼς ἱδρύσθαι φεύγειν, “of avoiding the erection of altars, cult-images, and temples”? (Origen, *Against Celsus*, book VIII, p. 389.)

What, then, do you suppose Minucius answered to this accusation? He freely concedes that Christians had no altars and no images—indeed, not even any temples or shrines. He gives the following reason why they did not possess any of them:

> “Do you think that we conceal what we worship because we have no shrines and altars? For what image could I fashion for God, when, if you judge rightly, the human being himself is God’s image? What temple could I build for him, when this entire world, fashioned by his workmanship, cannot contain him? And when I, a human being, dwell more spaciously, should I confine the power of so great a majesty within a little sanctuary?”

Cyprian transcribed from this passage of our author—and thereby approved—these final words concerning a temple. (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 15.) [Col. 0522A]

A little sanctuary (*aedicula*), as we may observe in passing, was a small chapel, an annex to some sacred building, or a niche in which the statue or image of some god was placed. Thus Cicero says: “What of the fact that Licinia, a Vestal Virgin, . . . dedicated an altar, a little sanctuary, and a sacred couch beneath the Sacred Rock?” (Cicero, Oration 29, *For His House*, p. 427.) Apuleius, for his part, says: “In the very center of the little sanctuary sat an image of the goddess Hippona.” (Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, book III, near the end.) Finally, to omit Pliny, Tertullian certainly distinguished temples or shrines, altars, and little sanctuaries in the following words, just as our author did: “For it makes no difference whether you build it or adorn it, if you have furnished his temple, his altar, or his little sanctuary.” (Tertullian, *On Idolatry*, chap. 8, p. 107.) Nevertheless, both authors use the name *aedicula* to designate any temple whatsoever, precisely in order to show more clearly how much smaller and less august it is than God’s infinite majesty. [Col. 0522B–C]

Minucius therefore testifies that the Christians had other and far more excellent temples, shrines, altars, sacrificial altars, and victims than the pagans, as well as other and far more sacred sacrifices:

> “Should not God,” he says, “rather be dedicated in our mind and consecrated in our innermost heart? Shall I offer to the Lord sacrificial animals and victims which he produced for my use, so that I may cast his own gift back to him? That would be ingratitude, when the acceptable victim is a good disposition, a pure mind, and a sincere conscience. Thus he who cultivates innocence makes supplication to the Lord; he who cultivates justice pours a libation to God; he who abstains from fraud propitiates God; he who rescues a human being from danger slaughters the best victim. These are our sacrifices; these are God’s sacred rites.”

Just as Octavius answers Caecilius, so Origen likewise answers the pagan Celsus, who, as we said shortly before, raised the same objection. Celsus fails to see:

> ὅτι βωμοί, “that the altars” of each one of us are “our own minds, from which” truly spiritual and sweet-smelling incense rises upward, together with prayers from a pure conscience:
>
> Ἀγάλματα δὲ καὶ πρέποντα Θεῷ, οὐκ ὑπὸ βαναύσων τεχνιτῶν κατεσκευασμένα.
>
> “The images to be dedicated to God and worthy of him are not works made by mechanical craftsmen, but virtues carved and formed within us by the Word of God, in imitation of the Firstborn of all creation, in whom are the exemplars of justice, temperance, courage, wisdom, and the other virtues. These are the statues—ἀγάλματα, ‘cult-images’—to be dedicated to God in the souls of those who, in accordance with God’s command, practice justice, courage, piety, and the remaining virtues.”  
> (Origen, *Against Celsus*, book VIII, p. 389.) [Col. 0522D–0523A]

In the same manner, therefore, our Minucius, Origen, and other Christian writers of the same period openly declare that Christians had no temples or shrines, no sacred buildings, no altars, and no images. They all speak of these things in one and the same manner. Indeed, if there is any distinction, it is that Minucius added the word “known” to “images,” as though wishing to signify that Christians did possess some images, but that these were not known and were kept secretly and in concealment.

But observe, I ask you, the foolish cavils of the Calvinists and other Protestants, who admit whatever pleases them in the writings of the most ancient Fathers and reject whatever displeases them. For they openly acknowledge with Dallaeus that in the time of our Minucius, and from the first centuries of the Church, Christians did indeed have their own temples, but no altars and no images. Let us consider for a moment with what temerity they distinguish things which those most ancient writers did not distinguish. [Col. 0523B]

It is well that Dallaeus and other Protestants concede that Christians had their own temples in the earliest age of the Church. But with what effrontery could they have denied what those Fathers of the Church assert with absolute certainty? Tertullian, as we have seen elsewhere, expressly declares that they possessed temples, which he called “churches,” following the Apostle, although the Calvinists reject that designation. Would you like another testimony from him, written even against the Catholic Church?

> “The remaining frenzies of lust, impious against both bodies and sexes beyond the laws of nature, we exclude not merely from the threshold but from every roof of the church.”

We have already noted that Clement of Alexandria likewise called them by the Greek name ἐκκλησίαν, “church.” (*Apparatus*, p. 741; Tertullian, *On Idolatry*, chap. 7, p. 107; *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chap. 13, p. 201; *On Modesty*, chap. 4, p. 718; *Apparatus*, vol. I, p. 713; Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus*, book III, chap. 11, p. 255.) [Col. 0523C]

This is proved no less clearly by the Christian assemblies which, as we have often seen, they were accustomed to hold in buildings and temples; and, moreover, by the public penance of offenders who, prostrate at the doors of the church, implored the prayers of the faithful and the mercy of the priests. Finally, it is demonstrated most manifestly by the authority of Arnobius and Lactantius, who reproached the pagans for destroying or burning the Christians’ meeting places and churches.

Eusebius relates more concerning the destruction and restoration of those churches, calling them προσευκτηρίων τοὺς οἴκους, “houses of prayer” or “sacred buildings,” and ἐκκλησίας, “churches”; elsewhere, τοὺς οἴκους ἐν οἷς συνήγοντο, “their buildings in which they held their assemblies”; and finally, οἴκους ἐκκλησιῶν, “buildings of the churches.” (Arnobius, *Against the Gentiles*, book IV, p. 152; Lactantius, *Institutes*, book V, chap. 11; Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VIII, chap. 2, pp. 293–294; ibid., chap. 17, p. 316; ibid., book IX, chap. 9, p. 362.) [Col. 0523D]

But why is there any need to prove at greater length something that is entirely certain and that our adversaries have never been able to deny? I would therefore have you observe their changeable and cunning disposition. They confess that Christians did indeed have their own temples, yet temples fashioned not like ours, but only like theirs. If you do not believe us, here are Dallaeus’ exact words:

> “For Minucius’ statement must not be understood as though at that time there were no buildings in which members of the Christian communion held sacred assemblies, both to be instructed concerning divine matters and to make common supplication to God—buildings such as those which today are called Protestant temples.”  
> (Dallaeus, *On Images*, book I, chap. 3, p. 52.)

But by what argument, I ask you, have he and others of the same sect ever proved that the temples of the most ancient Christians resembled theirs rather than ours? [Col. 0524A–B] By not even the slightest argument. Is it therefore because they have utterly removed altars, sacrificial altars, statues, and images from their temples, just as they have removed the sacrifice of the Mass, that they proclaim the most ancient Christian temples to have resembled their own?

Yet the same plain evidence that proves that the Christians built temples can demonstrate that altars were placed and sacrificial altars erected in those ancient temples and churches. For Tertullian declares it most explicitly in the following words:

> “Will your station not be solemn if you have stood at the altar of God?”  
> (Tertullian, *On Prayer*, chap. 14, p. 155.)

Cyprian, moreover, wrote to the people of Furni concerning not only an altar but also a sacrifice, which our adversaries so insolently reject:

> “It was established long ago in a council of bishops that no one should appoint any cleric or minister of God as a guardian or administrator in his will, since those who have individually been honored with the divine priesthood and appointed to clerical ministry ought to serve only the altar and sacrifices and to devote themselves to prayers and supplications.”

He then says that his episcopal predecessors had decreed:

> “That no brother departing from this life should name a cleric as guardian or administrator; and that if anyone did so, no offering should be made for him, nor should a sacrifice be celebrated for his falling asleep. For he does not deserve to be named at the altar of God in the priests’ prayer who wished to call priests and ministers away from the altar. And therefore, since Victor dared to appoint a presbyter as guardian, no offering for his falling asleep is to be made among you, nor is any supplication in his name to be frequently offered in the church.”  
> (Cyprian, Epistle 1, *To the Presbyters*.) [Col. 0524C–D]

You see clearly that he distinguishes the altar and sacrifice from the Christians’ prayers, supplications, and petitions. In their churches, therefore, there were altars in the true and proper sense, and at that time sacrifice was customarily offered for the dead.

In another letter to Pope Cornelius, Cyprian writes that a document of the Novatians, who falsely accused Cornelius, was rejected “while God’s priests sat in council and the altar had been set in place.” (The same, Epistle 45, p. 87.) And, to omit many other passages readily found by anyone in his writings, he congratulates Pope Lucius on his return from exile, since “the authority of the priesthood has increased all the more, so that its bishop might stand beside the altar of God.”

We have now proved something that even the very celebrated editor of these works of Cyprian, although an adherent of the Church of England, freely and openly acknowledges. It is therefore just as certain that altars were placed in Christian churches or temples as it is that Christians built churches in which, as Dallaeus admits, they held their sacred assemblies. For, as he says:

> “It is sufficiently clear not only from Eusebius’ *History* but also from several other monuments of the ancient Church that Christians already possessed sacred buildings of this kind. If they lacked them anywhere, this resulted from the violence and injustice of the pagans, who unlawfully begrudged devout people this opportunity and freedom, not from any religious principle of the Christians themselves.”  
> (Dallaeus, *On Images*, book I, chap. 3, pp. 52 ff.) [Col. 0525A]

But tell me, I ask you, my dear Dallaeus: you admit what you could no longer conceal—that Christians possessed their own sacred buildings; you deny that they had altars; you deny that they had images. Where is your good faith? Did Minucius not say all these things in exactly the same manner: “Why do they have no altars, no temples, no known images?” Why, therefore, do you concede one and deny another? Has there ever been, to use your own words, “any greater or more unjust exercise of force than to assume for yourself the right and authority to alter at your pleasure the very thoughts of men—and indeed of men who died many centuries ago”? (Dallaeus, *On Images*, book I, p. 52.)

For Minucius appears less completely to deny that Christians had temples and altars than that they had images. Caecilius did not thoughtlessly add the phrase “known images,” meaning, as we have said, images that were public and known to others. We have sufficiently demonstrated that Christians constructed temples and altars in which they customarily offered a true sacrifice. [Col. 0525B–C] Let us now see by what argument the same Dallaeus contends that they had no statues or images.

He replies that “it is possible” that Caecilius, like any other Gentile, remained uncertain whether Christians “secretly worshiped a deity of shameful and dishonorable form.” For Caecilius himself said in the same passage: “They make every effort to conceal whatever it is that they worship.”

Is it truly because you think this “possible” that you assume the right to conclude from it that neither this Gentile nor the Christian Minucius denied that Christians possessed their own temples and altars, but only denied that they possessed images, although they speak of all these things in one and the same manner? And from where did you learn that the Christians’ images were less known to the pagans than their sacred buildings, which they were accustomed to enter only in secret? [Col. 0525D]

Dallaeus nevertheless insists that it would have been easy and obvious for Octavius to answer that Christians possessed certain images and altars, but ones very different from the images and altars of the pagans. So far is Octavius from giving such an answer that, on the contrary, he says that no temple should be built for God, whom the entire world cannot contain, much less a single little sanctuary; but that a good disposition, a pure mind, and a sincere conscience are the best and most acceptable sacrificial offerings and victims to God; and, finally, that the human being is God’s image.

Yet nothing is more insubstantial or frivolous than this reply. For Jews and Gentiles, among whom no one is unaware that many altars, victims, and sacrifices existed, use the same manner of speaking as Minucius. Thus, in Philo the Jew’s book *On Victims*, we read:

> Τοῦ Θεοῦ θυσιαστήριον ἐστιν ἡ εὐχάριστος τοῦ σοφοῦ ψυχή, παγεῖσα ἐκ τελείων ἀριθμῶν ἀτμήτων καὶ ἀδιαιρέτων.
>
> “The altar of God is understood to be the grateful soul of the wise person, compacted from perfect numbers that cannot be cut or divided.”

He had written much more to the same effect shortly before. (Philo, *On Victims*, p. 851.) [Col. 0526A]

The pagan Seneca, cited by Lactantius, says:

> “The benefit itself is not what is counted out or handed over. So too, even in the case of victims, although they be fat and gleam with gold, the honor paid to the gods does not lie in them, but in the devout and upright mind of those who worship.”  
> (Seneca, *On Benefits*, book I, chap. 6; Lactantius, *Institutes*, book VI, chap. 25, p. 638.)

Even earlier Cicero had said:

> “The best worship of the gods, and likewise the purest and fullest of piety, is that we should always venerate them with a pure, whole, and uncorrupted mind and voice.”  
> (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, book II, p. 224, line 42.)

If you wish for more, consult Porphyry and the many other authors cited by Lescalopier on this passage of Cicero. But who has ever said that by these words the Jews or Gentiles denied that true altars, sacrificial altars, victims, and sacrifices existed among them? (Porphyry, *On Abstinence from Animals*, book II, near the end; Lescalopier, on *On the Nature of the Gods*, book II, §72, p. 347.) [Col. 0526B]

What, moreover, will you do with Tertullian, whom Minucius follows so closely? Did he not also write:

> “We despise temples no less than tombs; we recognize neither kind of altar; we worship neither kind of image”?  
> (Tertullian, *On the Shows*, chap. 13, p. 96.)

Does he not there deny, just as openly as Minucius, that Christians possessed any temples, altars, or images whatsoever? Does he not also elsewhere answer the pagan Scapula:

> “We sacrifice for the emperor’s welfare, but to our God and his, and in the manner God has commanded—with pure prayer”?  
> (The same, *To Scapula*, chap. 2, p. 86.)

Did he on that account deny that Christians possessed any temples, altars, or sacrificial altars at all? By no means, as has been proved from his other words cited shortly before and as Dallaeus himself concedes. [Col. 0526C]
