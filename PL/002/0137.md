DIVORCE, SEPARATION (*DIVORTIUM*). II, 223 B, “Which was to result from the separation of the moisture.” II, 741 A, “Often the soul is stirred more powerfully at the very moment of separation.” II, 90 C, “For he commands a long separation from idolatry.” I, 463 A, “But that merely discordant men fought against you out of resentment arising solely from the separation.”

TEACHING (*DOCTRINA*). II, 37 B, “So suitable that it is reckoned as belonging to another teaching,” that is, so worthy that it is judged to belong to a more recondite teaching (as is said afterward), one by no means commonly known. “A more recondite teaching,” that is, a secret one. II, 45 B, “They are reckoned no less apostolic because of the kinship of their teaching.”

PROOF, LESSON (*DOCUMENTUM*). II, 315 A, “For diversity in spiritual proofs was appropriate to no human being except Christ.” II, 142 C, “I shall, of course, first present splendid proofs, as though they were the documentary evidence for our case.” II, 967 B, [Col. 1292D] “And the efficacy of powers, and the tongue of proofs.” Rigault reads: “and the proofs consisting in tongues”; rightly. II, 963 C, “For we are now hastening to new proofs,” that is, to the New Testament.

DOMESTIC, OF THE HOUSEHOLD (*DOMESTICUS*). I, 380 A, “Namely, to God’s own household people by favor inherited from the fathers.” I, 470 A, “And now to the elders of the household.” II, 52 A, “Nor is that which it emulates ever its own possession.”

MISTRESS (*DOMINA*). I, 619 A, “Mother Church, the mistress, from her own breasts.”

GIFT, BOUNTY (*DONATIVUM*). II, 862 A, “But the gift of God is eternal life.” II, 489 C, “The gifts which are called *charismata*.”

DOWRY (*DOS*). I, 1229 A, “After humanity had been condemned together with the world’s dowry.” By “the world’s dowry” he means sins, through which humanity fell from divine grace. Albaspinaeus understands it as the human miseries assigned to mortals as their dowry after sin. II, 885 B, “The flesh followed the soul under the title of dowry.”

[Col. 1293A] DRAGON (*DRACO*). I, 1284 A, “Attending to the auspices of their punishment together with the dragon itself.” The dragon was Aesculapius, a serpent so prolific that, unless its females were destroyed by fire, there would be no resisting its fecundity, says Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXIX, chap. 4. For this reason the Romans once resolved that, after the fire had been extinguished, rites of propitiation should be performed with the dragon—that is, with Aesculapius—so that he might not oppose Vesta’s fire, but might instead provide for the welfare of the commonwealth and prove the innocence of the Vestal Virgin whose reputation and life were in danger. Yet there can be no doubt that the author, by the same stroke, also censured Satan—who is called the dragon in the Scriptures—and his work, as he demonstrates in the book *On Idolatry*.

DRAGON-ORNAMENT (*DRACONTARIUM*). Some explain this as a crown made from the herb *dracontia*, useful against poisons; others, as one made from *draconite* gems; others, as the insignia of a *draconarius*, that is, a standard-bearer who carried a dragon. Junius and Heraldus, drawing on Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus*, book II, final chapter, explain it as a winding gold necklace shaped like dragons, with which people adorned the neck [Col. 1293B] or hand. The Greeks call them *dracontes*. See Lucian, *Amores*, and Jerome, *To Marcella*. II, 101 B, “Why do you condemn your head by means of a little fillet or a dragon-ornament?”

DOUBT (*DUBITARE*). I, 389 A, “If it is doubted that they are ancient,” in place of “whether they are ancient.” II, 579 A, “A doubted outcome,” that is, uncertain, doubtful.

DRAW, DRIVE (*DUCERE*). I, 1261 B, “He himself draws the iron through his own body.”

LEADER (*DUCATOR*): commander. II, 634 C, “When its leader had to suffer in it.”

LEADERSHIP, GUIDANCE (*DUCATUS*). II, 329 B, “The account also of his errors, from whom he borrowed his guidance.” II, 988 A, “Hence the guidance of the preceding idolatry.”

DUPLICITY, DOUBLE MEANING (*DUPLICITAS*). II, 499 C, “We know that certain meanings can admit ambiguity because of the sound of the pronunciation or the manner of punctuation, when a double meaning intervenes in them.”

WITH DIFFICULTY (*DURE*). I, 1205 C, “Lest anyone believe with greater difficulty,” that is, find it harder to believe. II, 797 B, “Because the resurrection of the flesh is believed with greater difficulty.”

[Col. 1293C] HARDNESS OF HEART (*DURICORDIA*). II, 479 A, “You shall circumcise your hardness of heart.”

HARDNESS (*DURITIA*). I, 1239 B, “Now the hardness of raging.” II, 282 B, “Unless the hardness of the barriers resists, it will be no more monstrous than Pharaoh’s hardness.” II, 302 B, “The people’s hardness had necessitated such remedies.” II, 302 A, “After the people’s hardness, the hardness of the law was subdued.” II, 942 A, “Let it be reckoned as that kind of hardness committed”—concerning repudiation.

## E

DRINK DOWN (*EBIBERE*). II, 723 B, “Adam drank down sleep before he thirsted for rest.” II, 1011 C, “If you wish to drink down all knowledge from the Apostle.” I, 481 A, “But an earthquake also drank down the Corinthian Sea.”

INTOXICATING DRINK (*EBRIAMEN*). II, 965 B, “He shall drink neither wine nor intoxicating drink”; an unusual word coined to render the Greek *μέθυσμα* (*methysma*), “intoxicating drink.”

ECSTASY (*ECSTASIS*). II, 413 C, “That the ecstasy—that is, the madness—of grace is fitting.” II, 491 A, “In ecstasy, that is, madness.” II, 665 B, “For an ecstasy of the Holy Spirit fell upon him,” the operative power of prophecy. II, 659 B; 725 B, “This power we call ecstasy, a departure from the senses and a condition resembling madness.”

UNTIMELY BIRTH (*ECTROMA*), Greek *ἔκτρωμα* (*ektrōma*), an abortion or miscarriage. II, 20 A.

LEARN THOROUGHLY, PRACTICE (*EDISCERE*). II, 175 A, “He was always practicing,” that is, *ἐμελετᾶτο* (*emeletato*), “he practiced.” II, 764 B, “And he was learning thoroughly to judge the human race in the condition of flesh not yet born.” II, 724 A, “Learning through the concealment of his presence what his future absence would be.”

PUBLIC PRESENTATION (*EDITIO*). I, 638 A, “Among whom testamentary spectacles also render funeral honors to the memories of private persons,” that is, gifts and games presented by heirs or legatees, when the testator had bequeathed something from his property by will for the presentation of games.

BRING FORTH, WITHDRAW (*EDUCERE*). II, 906 B, “They are withdrawn from the virgins,” that is, they remove themselves from the census or rank of virgins. I, 402 A, “For neither did he bring himself before the public.”

[Col. 1294A] EDIBLE (*EDULIS*), of the third declension. II, 521 C, “The prohibition of certain foods.”

HARDENED (*EDURUS*). II, 878 C, “When hardened”—concerning a corpse.

EFFECT (*EFFECTUS*). II, 589 B, “For the force of the will certainly gives effect to the thought.” Irenaeus: *Ἡ θέλησις τοίνυν δύναμις ἐγένετο τῆς ἐννοίας*—“The will, therefore, became the power of the thought.”

FASHIONED (*EFFIGIATUS*). II, 659 B, “For he affirms that everything fashioned is composite and constructed.”

FIGURE, FORM (*EFFIGIES*). I, 386 A, “The forms of letters, indicators and guardians of things.” He alludes not merely to the invention of letters, which the ancient Cadmus brought from Phoenicia into Greece, but also to the use of letters: by carved and figured animals they expressed the meaning of the mind. Porphyry, also called Malchus, reports in his *Life of Pythagoras* that there are three kinds of letters: epistolary, hieroglyphic, and symbolic. II, 659 A, “What now, inasmuch as we also assign a form to the soul?”

STRIKE OUT (*EFFLIGERE*). II, 384 B, “They had threshed the plucked ears of grain with their hands,” that is, by beating they had shaken and drawn them out.

SMOKE OUT (*EFFUMIGARE*). I, 621 A, “Like a snake charmed [Col. 1294B] or smoked out,” that is, weakened by incantation and driven away by particular kinds of smoke.

*Εἶδος* (*eidos*) in Greek means “form”; from it the diminutive *εἴδωλον* (*eidōlon*) is derived, which has likewise produced our word *formula*, “little form.” I, 665 A.

LABOR TO OBTAIN, PRODUCE (*ELABORARE*). II, 585 A, “Let us labor to obtain the approval of the act,” that is, let us procure it by continual labor. II, 866 B, “Who is commanded to labor toward that goal.” II, 988 B, “I too sometimes produce murder by adultery.” Latinus reads, “I labor for adultery.” It is a Hellenism: *τῇ μοιχείᾳ φονέομαι*, “I commit murder through adultery.” On this matter, see *Apology*, chap. 15.

OLIVE GROVE (*ELAEON*), Greek *ἐλαιών* (*elaiōn*). II, 459 B, “But at night he withdrew into the olive grove.”

*Ἔλαυνε τὴν μητέρα*—“He drove the mother.” I, 325 A. In its place I, 581 B more correctly reads: *Ἔλαυνε εἰς τὴν μητέρα*—“Drive at the mother!” This concerns the Macedonians who greeted with laughter the spectacle of the murdered Oedipus and thereby gave their fellow spectators in the same theater this shameful occasion to ridicule them.

[Col. 1294C] CHOICE (*ELECTIO*). II, 51 A, “Following from their own choice.” The sense of the whole passage is: These people are not called Christians from possessing Christ—a fact which they themselves admit and confirm by following, according to their own choice, that is, their own judgment, the names of heretics.

ELECTRUM, a metal mixed from gold and silver and constituting a certain third species. II, 220 A. See Pliny, *Natural History*, book XI, chap. 40; book XXXIII, chap. 4.

ELEMENTAL (*ELEMENTITIUS*). II, 703 A, “Even if, according to the philosophers, it were classified among elemental substances.”

CALL FORTH (*ELICERE*). II, 698 B, “We know also that magicians call forth [spirits] for the investigation of hidden things,” etc. The verb is peculiar to sacrifices and the evocation of shades and gods. Thus Ovid, *Fasti* III: “They call you down from heaven, Jupiter”; and Arnobius, book V: “By what sacrificial rites Jupiter can be called down to earth.”

DASH DOWN, CRUSH (*ELIDERE*), to suffocate the breath. I, 411 A, “If they crush boys for the utterance of an oracle.” Junius understands this of sacrifices of boys, a divinatory practice called *βρεφομαντεία* (*brephomanteia*, infant-divination) or [Col. 1294D] *paedomancy*. Magicians are said to “crush boys for the utterance of an oracle,” that is, *ἀποτραχηλίζειν* (*apotrachelizein*), “to break their necks,” “for the utterance of an oracle,” that is, so that demons, as though appeased by the sacrifice of children’s blood, might be induced to utter the pronouncements of their oracles. La Cerda, Heraldus, and Rigault refer it to the manner of a soothsayer’s collapse. For those under enchantment fell down as though epileptic, and afterward, unconscious of themselves, were roused to the utterance of an oracle and foretold many things prophetically. II, 109 B, “Not because danger is to be crushed under the proper name of persecution.” I, 703 A, “But if you strive to crush us,” that is, to crush our faith. II, 797 C, “While they are refuted concerning the resurrection of the flesh before they are refuted concerning the union of the divinity.” II, 863 A, “Raising up what was crushed.” I, 651 A, “The devil crushed the first human beings.” I, 1224 A, “And temptations directed against fullness and immoderation of the belly are crushed by abstinence.”

[Col. 1295A] REFINER (*ELIMATOR*), purifier. II, 447 A, “That Christ is the true arbiter and refiner of human stains.”

THOUGHT (*ENNOEA*), Greek *ἔννοια* (*ennoia*). II, 551 A.

IRREGULARLY LONG (*ENORME*), extending beyond the proper bounds of writing. I, 387 A, “It is not so much difficult for us to explain as irregularly long.”

ENORMITY, DEPARTURE FROM THE NORM (*ENORMITAS*). II, 961 A, “He placed before the corruption of riches the enormity of voracity.” II, 248 B, “By the very enormity of curiosity.” I, 1323 A, “What enormities of fine and woven hairpieces,” that is, enormous hairpieces, whether fine or woven, whether they are wigs or the protruding structures built beneath them; for there were these two kinds of hairpieces. I, 640 A, “The enormity of the obelisk.” II, 983 C, “By the enormity of discipline,” that is, because they depart from the norm of discipline.

ENTELECHY (*ENTELECHIA*), Greek *ἐντελέχεια* (*entelecheia*). II, 703 B, “And Aristotle’s Entelechies.” See Ernesti’s *Clavis*.

CLEAR OF CLOUDS, ILLUMINATE (*ENUBILARE*), to render the light bright. I, 459 A, “They illuminated their vestibules with exceedingly lofty and brilliant lamps.” Havercamp, following the Fulda manuscript, prefers *nubilabant*, “they clouded.” The greater and more numerous the burning torches or lamps, the blacker the soot drawn onto the doorposts. For this corresponds to what he said of laurel garlands: “they built up in front.” II, 450 C, “So that he had not first cleared away this blindness of that man.” II, 652 A, “These things will have to be illuminated for Christians.”

ENTHYMESIS (*ENTHIMESIS*), Greek *ἐνθύμησις* (*enthymēsis*), thought, reflection. II, 20 A; 556 A, “And the whole Enthymesis—that is, the animation—together with the passion,” etc. Irenaeus: “She had laid aside her former intention together with the passion that had befallen her.”

*Ἐπερχόμενε* (*eperchomene*), “coming upon,” “supervenient.” For Christ is *ὁ ἐπερχόμενος* (*ho eperchomenos*), “the one who is coming.” II, 416 B; 422 D.

EPICITHARISMA. II, 589 A, “Like an *epicitharisma* after the play.” A proverbial expression derived from the stage, where a play was concluded by a considerable musical performance or by a concert arranged to soothe the ears.

EYEBROW (*EPISCYNIUM*). I, 1044 A, “With the eyebrow relaxed,” that is, with the brow less tightly drawn and contracted, less severe in its censure and rebuke.

[Col. 1295C] SUPERINTENDENT (*EPISTATES*), Greek *ἐπιστάτης* (*epistatēs*), an overseer and master of contests, whom the author calls the presiding officer of the contest in *Scorpiace*, chap. 6. I, 624 B, “And so your superintendent is Christ Jesus.”

EXPEND, EXHAUST (*EROGARE*). II, 13 A, “By exhausting what had been assigned to the human being.” An elegant metaphor: just as expenditure exhausts money, so the torment of fever exhausts the body’s strength. II, 807 A, “When at last he is exhausted by punishments.” I, 645 A, “Soon, by edict, the gods were expended as funeral offerings at the tombs,” that is, they were spent upon the pyre and consumed. I, 496 A, “When so many innocent persons are expended.”

DISPOSER IN MARRIAGE (*EROGATOR*). II, 1012 C, “Thus he declares that the preserver of a virgin does better than her disposer,” that is, the man who allows the virgin to marry.

ERROR (*ERROR*). I, 1264 B, “Vengeance rests with error,” that is, with the worshipers of idols. II, 648 B, “He is offended by prosperous error.”

FEEL SHAME BEFORE, RESPECT (*ERUBESCERE*). II, 247 A, “They blush before their own weapons,” that is, they are not ashamed of weapons, though they are so devoted to Venus. I, 611 A, “So that they may at least feel shame before you.” A Hellenism: *αἰσχυνθῶσί σοι* (*aischynthōsi soi*), “they may feel shame before you.” So Curtius, book V: “to blush before fortune.”

BELCH FORTH (*ERUCTARE*). I, 1312 A, “And let it belch forth from the conscience to the surface.” II, 700 A, “With a view to relieving overpopulation, they belch forth swarms of the nation into other territories.”

DRAW OUT (*ELIQUARE*), extract; to draw out thread by spinning. II, 1038 B, “As the drawing continues, it draws out the thread.” II, 700 A, “Marshes are drained.” II, 251 A, “The summit of magnitude must be drawn out into a single point.”

HELLEBORE (*ELLEBORUM*). II, 656 A, “Thus Chrysippus to the hellebore.” Chrysippus, a disciple of Cleanthes, was a man of extremely keen intellect, concerning whom there is the verse, *Εἰ μὴ γὰρ ἦν Χρύσιππος οὐκ ἦν στοά*—“For if Chrysippus had not existed, neither would the Stoa.” Valerius Maximus, book VIII, chap. 7, reports that Carneades, when about to dispute with Chrysippus, customarily purged himself beforehand with hellebore so that he might express his thought more attentively and refute Chrysippus more vigorously. Aulus Gellius says the same, book XVII, chap. 15: “When Carneades was about to write against the books of Zeno the Stoic, he purged the upper parts of his body with white hellebore.” [Col. 1296A]

CHARGE-RECORDS (*ELOGIA*). This was the name given to the names, ages, descriptions, and crimes of individual prisoners, which were written on tablets and presented to the judges when they apportioned each man’s punishment according to his charge-record. I, 496 B, “You who cancel charge-records by your sentences.” II, 620 B, “With the charge-record of superstition and malediction.” II, 342 B, “Indeed, this too is disgraceful by reason of the charge-record of transgression and the desert of condemnation.” II, 272 A, “In short, the Creator’s entire charge-record will be transferred to her,” that is, the name and title will be transcribed. II, 941 A, “It does not admit the charge of adultery.” II, 986 C, “Whether I call it adultery or fornication, there will be one charge-record: defiled flesh.”

STRUGGLE OUT, CULTIVATE (*ELUCTARI*), to work, cultivate. II, 1040 A, “Which nature rendered useful rather for working the field.”

EMANCIPATE, TRANSFER (*EMANCIPARE*): to assign someone to another through *mancipatio*, or an imaginary sale; or in any fashion to alienate and transfer into another’s legal ownership, or even to give as a gift. II, 291 C, “As though the holder of the scales for the good emancipated by God.” [Col. 1296B] See LIBRIPENS. II, 372 C, “Not thus transferred to the Creator’s Christ through the prophets.”

EMANCIPATION (*EMANCIPATIO*). II, 114 C, “Our emancipation is in the underworld.”

*Ἐμβρυοσφάκτη* (*embryosphaktē*). II, 692 A, “There is also a bronze needle by which the very slaughter is accomplished through a secret act of violence: they call it an *embryosphaktēs* in connection with the function of infanticide, certainly an instrument for killing a living infant.”

CORRECTOR (*EMENDATOR*). II, 589 A, “By Ptolemy’s correctors,” that is, those who emerged from Ptolemy’s school in order to correct it.

BEG, SCRAPE TOGETHER (*EMENDICARE*). II, 736 A, “Thus they scrape together arguments.”

EARN (*EMERERI*). II, 447 A, “The observers of the law earned healing from the destroyer of the law.”

STANDING OUT (*EMICANS*). II, 223 B, “The dry land was made more conspicuous,” that is, it could be seen more easily.

MIGRATE (*EMIGRARE*). II, 77 A, “Nor do I doubt that certain writings migrate”—used intransitively, just as Livy, book [Col. 1296C] X, said “difficult to migrate” in place of “difficult to export.” By “writings” he means tablets, codices, and documents.

RECITE (*ENARRARE*). II, 1031 B, “He recited the solemn formulas,” that is, he uttered the ceremonial words for inaugurating a colony. *Enarrare* is correct, since it embraces both elements: release from the former rites and the pronouncement of the subsequent rites.

ENDROMIS, a shaggy garment with rather long nap, used after a race or contest so that cold would not penetrate limbs wet with sweat. II, 1042 B, “Thus some fine-textured fabric forced the roughness out of the running-cloak in a composite garment.”

ENERGEMA (*ENERGEMA*), Greek *ἐνέργημα* (*energēma*), an effect that proceeds from some force and efficacy. II, 43 A, “By whose operation he was circumvented.”

CULTIVATE, TRAIN (*ERUDIRE*). II, 890 A, “It is refined into mildness of flavor,” that is, it is raised out of its rawness and bitterness. II, 788 B, “And from the blossom every fruit is developed into fruit.” II, 706 B, “Even by beasts trained contrary to nature,” that is, trained and impelled to savagery. II, 994 C, “When both bodies were presently worked smooth,” that is, when both cloths had been polished. In the ancient glosses polished fabrics are called *γεγραμμένα* (*gegrammena*), “written,” and rough ones *ἄγραφα* (*agrapha*), “unwritten.”

TRAINING (*ERUDITUS*), equivalent to *eruditio*. II, 584 A, “But faith is increased by the training afforded by this.”

RESCUE; INVESTIGATE (*ERUERE*). II, 105 B, “But rescue us from the evil one.” II, 524 B, “The laws command that evil men be sought out.” I, 276 A, “By which we draw forth these occasions,” that is, we bring them out opportunely and do not bury them under untimely repetitions, as the inexperienced do.

OUTBREAK (*ERUPTIO*), the execution of a contemplated crime. I, 458 A, “Right up to the very outbreak of impiety.”

FOOD-BEARING (*ESCATILIS*). I, 1259 A, “After the food-bearing rain of manna.” II, 247 A, “For those who have not so departed as to have become food, death is accursed.” [The transmitted wording is materially uncertain.]

HUNGER (*ESURIRE*). II, 125 A, “Having tasted martyrdoms in prison, they hunger for them,” that is, they desire to consummate the martyrdoms of which they have had only a taste from clubs or claws, having been beaten and, as it were, merely grazed on the outermost skin. II, 87 A, “Through those in whom they hungered.”

[Col. 1297A] EURIPUS. The Euripus is the part of the sea between Aulis, the harbor of Boeotia, and the island of Euboea. Livy, book XXVIII, chap. 6. Ditches and water-channels in gardens and country estates are also called *euripi*. Thus the ditches in the Circus were also called *euripi*; when suddenly filled with water from aqueducts through hidden channels, they flooded the whole arena for a mock naval battle. See Diodorus Siculus, book LXI; Seneca, *Epistle* 90; Suetonius, *Caesar* 39; Pliny, *Natural History*, book VIII, chap. 7; Salmasius on Solinus, fol. 638. II, 226 A, “And statues above the Euripus.” I, 640 A, “She—the Great Mother—therefore presides there over the Euripus.”

CARRY AWAY (*EVEHERE*). II, 1164 A, “But carry us away from evil.”

OUTCOME (*EVENTUS*). II, 40 B, “Among many outcomes there is no single result,” etc. The sense is: Since there are so many to whom one and the same matter is delivered, if they are mistaken, it cannot happen that there is one outcome—that is, that so many should err with a single error. [Col. 1297B] The result ought to have been various, just as error is various and manifold, and ought to produce a varied order in the churches’ teaching.

UPSET (*EVERSIO*). II, 30 A, “Unless, of course, someone wishes to incur an upset either of the stomach or of the brain.” This appears to be explained by the words immediately following: “And you indeed will lose nothing except your voice in the contention, and gain nothing except bile from the blasphemy.” He said “that someone should incur an upset of the stomach” in the same way as in book IV *Against Marcion*: “Every alteration arising from innovation must necessarily enter into diversity with the things of which it is an alteration.”

GROW VIGOROUS (*EVIGESCERE*). II, 717 A, “Finally, when those things—foods—are withdrawn, the soul grows vigorous.”

LOSE VIGOR (*EVIGORARI*). I, 376 B, “Although deprived of vigor by lusts and desires.” II, 1042 A, “Unless they had long been softened, deprived of vigor, and worn away.”

UNROLL, EMERGE (*EVOLVERE*). II, 840 A, “Jonah emerges from the belly of the fish.”

[Col. 1297C] VOMIT FORTH (*EVOMERE*). II, 994 A, “Once exposed, the discordance will vomit forth every error.” It will show that the blending of the colors was badly contrived. He says that wool vomits forth the colors it has absorbed.

*EX* WITH A NOUN USED ADVERBIALLY. I, 456 A, “Religious from faith,” that is, faithfully. I, 504 A, “Who renders it from faith.”

SWARM (*EXAMEN*), multitude. I, 332 A, “The entire swarm of the nation.” I, 482 A, “A Jewish swarm from Egypt.” II, 251 C, “A swarm of divinity”—concerning Valentinus’s thirty aeons.

AFTER EXAMINATION (*EXAMINATE*). II, 35 B, “It is enough for each person who believes after examination,” that is, only after he himself has undergone an examination.

ENSLAVE COMPLETELY (*EXANCILLARE*), to exhaust, as it were, in perpetual religious service. For according to Festus, *ancillare* means “to minister.” I, 376 A, “Although completely enslaved to false gods.”

DISARM (*EXARMARE*). II, 971 B, “Thus the laws have been disarmed.” I, 691 A, “The Lord thereafter unbelted every soldier by disarming Peter.” [Col. 1297D]

BECOME EXASPERATED (*EXASPERARI*). I, 400 B, “Thus they became exasperated.”

DISCHARGE, INVALIDATE (*EXAUCTORARE*). II, 324 A, “Which he himself later invalidated”—deprived of authority, weakened. II, 1007 C, “So that he might be discharged from the oath of blessing.” II, 1044 A, “Which had thus discharged itself.”

CHARM AWAY (*EXCANTARE*). I, 621 A, “Like a snake charmed or smoked out,” that is, weakened by an incantation or driven away by particular kinds of smoke.

PROCEED FORTH (*EXCEDERE*), concerning the eternal generation of the Son of God. I, 399 B, “And he did not depart from the womb but proceeded forth from it.”

RECEPTACLE (*EXCEPTACULUM*). I, 632 C, “And ears as a receptacle for slander.”

PARTIALITY, EXCEPTION (*EXCEPTIO*). II, 555 C, “But this too is partiality toward persons,” Greek *προσωπόληψις* (*prosōpolēpsia*). I, 392 A, “Nor overcome by objections.”

[Col. 1298A] BRAIN-SICK (*EXCEREBRATUS*). II, 382 B, “You have been made brain-sick by new wine.”

DIGRESSION; DEPARTURE (*EXCESSUS*). II, 44 A, “But I shall return from the digression.” II, 327 A, “And from every transgression after marriage.” I, 426 A, “Through whose fault Cybele did not previously know the departure of death,” that is, decease, death.

HYDRA (*EXCETRA*). The hydra was so named from the heads that grew out when one had been cut off. I, 1258 C, “Such, then, is this hydra of sins.”

FALL AWAY, CUT OUT (*EXCIDERE*). II, 233 A, “And here you have fallen away from your own lines.” II, 400 C, “To fall away from the master’s rule.” I, 529 B, “We who prefer to be condemned rather than fall away from God.” II, 960 C, “Whereby it is customary for him to cut the human being away from himself.”

DISLODGE (*EXCITARE*). II, 396 B, “And liable to dislodge every tooth.” What is dislodged is moved from its place. Thus he says elegantly that when a fist strikes the jaw, every tooth is dislodged. Nor is the wish of that wretched man beaten in a brawl without point: “May I be permitted to return from there with a few teeth.”

[Col. 1298B] EXCLUDE, THRUST FORTH (*EXCLUDERE*). II, 996 B, “Thus also the heretics assign the same fables to whom they wish, and hatch them most aptly, though not where they ought.” II, 247 A, “The breasts thrust forth [milk].”

CONSTRUCT OUT OF (*EXCONDERE*). II, 517 A, “You truly construct him as an invisible warrior,” that is, you compose and fashion him from those passages of the prophets.

EXTORT (*EXCRUCIARE*). I, 1297 A, “Or with the matter extorted,” that is, wrung and forced out by tortures under a judge.

SHAKE OFF (*EXCUTERE*). I, 137 C, “They shake off the king’s conditional threats,” that is, repel them from themselves.

REFUSE; BE EXEMPT; BEAT OUT (*EXCUSARE*). II, 899 A, “Whose flower even refuses human lust,” that is, rejects it. The virgin who rejoices in the flower of her virginity refuses lust, so that she does not wish it to be plucked, like that flower of Catullus, untouched and undefiled: “Unknown to the flock, bruised by no plow, which has not yet been deflowered, caught by a slender nail,” etc. Tertullian says it is credible that angels burned with desire for such girls rather than for bodies already defiled. II, 803 B, “Thus God too was permitted to beat out the gold of the flesh from the filth of clay, its substance freed from that filth.” [Col. 1298C] Someone is called *excusatus* when placed outside the operation of a law, burden, or charge—one, namely, who has shown that his case is not such that he ought to be oppressed by some burden or accusation, or classified or bound by some law. Thus the substance of the flesh appears to be called “freed from the filth of clay,” as though without a share in miry filth. Nevertheless, we believe that our Septimius had another meaning in mind. He says that both gold and flesh come from earth, but that each, when perfected by the craftsman’s hands, passes from a meaner matrix into a far different, brighter, and nobler material, so that they are no longer called earth but gold and flesh. Therefore, just as the craftsman was permitted to beat gold out of the earth and purify it by the violence of the glowing coal until at last it advanced to the most perfect excellence, so too the supreme craftsman of all, God, was permitted to beat the gold of our flesh out of clay, bake and refine it in the furnaces of divine powers, and enroll it under the classification of the noblest material, once that former classification as earth or clay had been beaten free of its filth. By *census* he means the material according to whatever classification it receives. But *excusatum* means “beaten out,” from *cudere* and *excudere*, whence *cusare* and *excusare*, just as from *quatere*, *jacere*, and *premere* we say *quassare*, *jactare*, and *pressatum*, where elsewhere we have *quassum*, *jactum*, and *pressum*. II, 884 B, “We too, as best we can, excuse the mouth with food.” I, 1246 A, “By the benefit of healing they excuse the offense against themselves.” II, 1002 B, “Why, then, do they exempt our neck from so great a yoke?”

WEAR AWAY (*EXEDURARI*), to be consumed, waste away. II, 1042 A, “Unless they had long been softened, deprived of vigor, and worn away.”

COPY (*EXEMPLARIUM*), exemplar. I, 232 C, “A copy of antiquity.” II, 264 B, “Since it is far more just to assent to some copies than to none.”

EXAMPLE, TYPE (*EXEMPLUM*), figure, image. I, 459 A, “And in another’s solemn observance they would inaugurate the type and image of their own hope.” II, 849 A, “But no example is greater than that of which it is the example.” II, 488 A, “But these things were made examples for us.” II, 871 C, “Therefore serve as an example [Col. 1299A] and preserve its mirror for the flesh.” II, 476 A, “For it is not an example but the reality.” II, 139 A, “After the example of the prophets.”

DEPRIVED (*EXEMPTUS*). II, 1031 D, “So that old age has not deprived you of preeminence.” A Tertullianic construction, *exemptus fastigium*, “deprived of preeminence,” like “Pompeii wrested away.” The sense is: Though you have been worn out by age, you have nevertheless not been cast down from supreme authority among the other cities of Africa.

FUNERAL RITES (*EXEQUIAE*). II, 706 A, “Because it undergoes a banquet as its funeral rites,” that is, because it is brought to a banquet, not to a tomb; expended on dinner, not on a funeral.

HOST, ARMY (*EXERCITUS*). I, 417 A, “As Plato describes great Jupiter in heaven accompanied by an army of gods and demons alike.” II, 1013 A, “To be armed against the army of propositions found throughout the entire document.”

EXPRESSLY (*EXERTE*), distinctly, explicitly, openly. II, 52 B, “Expressly and openly.” I, 978 A, “Just as we do not hesitate to command expressly.” I, 1290 A, “Now he no longer advises, but expressly commands.”

DRAWN, MANIFEST (*EXERTUS*), Greek *δῆλος* (*dēlos*), “manifest.” I, 462 A, “Manifest enemies.” I, [Col. 1299B] 1292 A, “The proposition has been disputed and made manifest.” II, 1006 C, “How the drawn edge of the spiritual sword, how the reins, as though invested with full right, authority, and power, are let loose in open rebuke.” In Virgil: “He went with sword enclosed.” The opposite is Cicero’s statement in *Against Catiline* I: “Yet now for the twentieth day we have allowed the edge of this authority to grow blunt.” For we possess a decree of the Senate, but one enclosed in the records, as though a sword were hidden in its sheath. So in Appius: “Teeth bared, sharpened and thrusting forth to inflict wounds.”

DRAIN, EXHAUST (*EXAURIRE*). II, 647 A, “Now that the hemlock of condemnation had been drained,” that is, once the sentence of his condemnation had been heard, as though Socrates had begun to drink death from that moment, since judges are likewise deemed to have taken a man’s life into custody from the moment they pronounce the sentence of death. II, 522 C, “But he emptied himself.” II, 799 C, “Therefore the Son of God was emptied to so great a degree of humility.”

PRODUCE, BRING FORTH (*EXHIBERE*). II, 246 B, “And he brought forth for the multitude.”

[Col. 1299C] SUPPORT, MAINTENANCE (*EXHIBITIO*). I, 668 B, “To whom we are bound for the sake of our maintenance,” that is, the provision of food. I, 677 B, “We flatter ourselves badly regarding the necessities of human maintenance if, after faith has been sealed, we say, ‘I have nothing on which to live.’” II, 87 B, “Through these common instruments of human maintenance.” II, 951 B, “Marrying for the sake of support.” II, 827 B, “Until the times of the presentation of all things,” that is, *τελειώσεως* (*teleiōseōs*), “completion.”

RELEASE (*EXIMERE*), dismiss, absolve. I, 277 A, “The guilty man must be canceled by payment of the penalty, not released.”

PUBLICATION; DESTRUCTION; OUTCOME (*EXITUS*). “Publication before the public.” II, 246 B. *Exitium*: destruction, death. II, 49 B, “Where Paul is crowned by John’s death.” I, 702 A, “In that Byzantine destruction,” that is, the destruction and overthrow of the town by Severus’s soldiers. I, 633 A, “It is established that he did not create the things he condemns for the outcome of works.” Here *exitus* does not mean “destruction,” as Pamelius thought, but outcome and consequence. The sense is: God did not create things so that evil works, which he condemns, should issue and follow from them. For they were good in themselves and, by the law of their created condition, would produce only good; but accidentally they were diverted into evil works. [Col. 1299D] II, 152 A, “The Apostles’ perception or their outcome?” He presently explains: “For they would have suffered nothing which they had not first perceived must be suffered.”

CONFESSION (*EXOMOLOGESIS*) is that by which we confess our sin to the Lord. I, 1243 B.

DEVIATE (*EXORBITARE*). II, 995 A, “They deviate from the true light of that comparison.” I, 298 A, “The functions of the elements deviate.” II, 16 C, “Who have not deviated into heresies.”

BEGINNING (*EXORDIUM*). II, 831 A, “And they place the whole harvest of Christian hope at the beginning of the age.” Rigault more correctly reads *in exodio saeculi*, “at the end of the age.” *Exodium* means departure, end. The ancient glosses give *ἐξόδιον* (*exodion*), “funeral rites.” Lucilius: “An ending worthy of the beginning, and the exodium will follow.” Juvenal, *Satire* III: “And at last he returns to the stage for the familiar exodium.” The ancient commentator says: “Among the ancients the exodiarius entered at the end of the games, and the laughter of this spectacle wiped away whatever tears and sadness the tragic emotions had produced.” Septimius therefore wittily called the end of the age an *exodium*, for then the time will arrive when, for Christian believers, the sorrow of all labors and miseries will be turned into joy of the kind Septimius describes near the end of the book *On the Spectacles*. II, 1306, “And that very black powder by which the outlines of the eyes are extended.”

DEPRIVE OF ORNAMENT (*EXORNARE*). I, 1326 A, “As though *exornata*,” that is, deprived and stripped of ornaments, like *exonerare*, “unburden,” *exarmare*, “disarm,” etc.

SPREAD OUT (*EXPANDERE*). II, 274 A, “He stretches himself out toward another’s heaven”; others read, “He stretches himself out toward a god”—that is, one who in prayer extends his arms toward heaven.

SHRINK IN TERROR (*EXPAVESCERE*). I, 472 A, “They shrank in terror at the single light of truth,” that is, as though dragged from the prison of darkness, death, and sin into the light of truth, they are afflicted and tremble throughout.

ACCOMPLISH, EQUIP (*EXPEDIRE*). I, 670 A, “They equip with the same crime,” that is, complete, furnish. II, 683 B, “Abundance obstructs wisdom; leanness frees it.”

[Col. 1300B] EXPEDITION; RELEASE (*EXPEDITIO*). II, 356 A, “Finally, it was completed in the eastern expedition,” when an expedition against the Parthians, who had occupied Mesopotamia, was undertaken anew, as Dio Xiphilinus relates. For at that time, after the Parthians suddenly withdrew, Severus toured the East, Egypt, and Judaea, and persecuted the Jews so severely that, according to Spartianus, he forbade conversion to Judaism under a severe penalty. I, 1282 B, “On that day of release, when God will release bodies from the bonds of death, and absolutely all his own people from every evil.” II, 650 B, “Because the involutions of solutions,” that is, solutions by which questions can be resolved.

UNENCUMBERED (*EXPEDITUS*). I, 364 A, “Released from Egypt,” concerning the Jews’ departure from Egypt, after they had freed themselves from injuries and intolerable burdens.

SEEK, DESIRE (*EXPETERE*). II, 1030 C, “But neither was it sought to divide the folds with a belt.” The sense is: In former times the Carthaginians neither sought nor cared to divide the folds of a garment. Others read, perhaps better, *expeditae*: “But they were unencumbered even without a belt dividing the folds.” The sense is: These tunics were unencumbered and caused no hindrance, and did not even require a belt to bind and divide their folds, or separate their stripes and ridges, in order to make them more convenient.

EXPIATION (*EXPIATUS*). II, 563 A, “The expiation of a conjoined suffering.”

PAINT, ADORN (*EXPINGERE*). I, 1317 A, “Who does not therefore wish to paint herself,” that is, adorn herself, as though with pigment.

LAY ASIDE, EXPOSE (*EXPONERE*). II, 556 B, “And she exposed the whole Enthymesis,” that is, laid it aside and cast it away from herself. In the same passage: “From whom she might borrow a pattern for exposing,” that is, for abandoning her offspring.

LAYING ASIDE (*EXPOSITIO*), deposition. II, 1046 B, “It certainly rejoices in the laying aside of no other garment than the toga.”

INSIST, CONTEND (*EXPOSTULARE*). II, 551 A, “But I insist that it is so,” that is, I also contend against them that God existed before all things; yet they deny this by assigning to God a coeternal companion, Ennoea or Sige.

PRESS OUT (*EXPRIMERE*). I, 457 A, “Who, by squeezing his throat”—that is, by strangling Antoninus Commodus.

POLISH CLEAN (*EXPUMICARE*), purge and polish, as though with pumice. [Col. 1300D] II, 570 A, “And it polishes her clean of all the injuries of suffering.”

COMPLETION (*EXPUNCTIO*). II, 24 B, “Where there is the completion of finding.” II, 686 A, “The completion of my duty and service,” that is, its discharge and performance. I, 613 A, “After the completion of life.”

COMPLETER (*EXPUNCTOR*). I, 1151 A, “With the Gospel superimposed as the completer of all preceding antiquity,” that is, its consummator.

CANCEL; COMPLETE (*EXPUNGERE*), erase. I, 276 A, D; 277 B, note (a). “The guilty man must be canceled by payment of the penalty, not released.” For in the conduct of a judicial inquiry, the exactor of punishments canceled from the tablet the name of each condemned person, together with the charge or cause cited, once the punishment had been exacted. Compare DISPUNGERE. I, 363 A, “With incense burning, to complete lust,” that is, to carry it through, consummate it. I, 391 A, “While it is being completed,” that is, while it is being fulfilled—concerning prophecies. I, 400 A, “The advent of Christ was accomplished.” I, [Col. 1301A] 453 A, “We who fulfill the vows of the upright,” that is, complete and discharge them. I, 527 A, “For completion,” that is, to perform fully and perfectly. II, 605 A, “And the abolition of the old law is shown to have been completed at its appointed times.” The old law was completed and canceled when the new one supervened. II, 524 B, “And if we have completed whatever matters had been deferred for this work,” that is, finished them and brought them into effect. II, 813 A, “The resurrection of the judges will complete [it].” I, 627 A, “Indeed, having exhausted every insult,” that is, no insult was omitted which they did not suffer. I, 663 B, “Yet it is consummated under the charge of idolatry,” that is, completed and brought to fulfillment. II, 76 A, “The bounty of the most excellent emperors was completed in the camp.” This signifies a donative. The individual soldiers, summoned from a roll or tablet, came forward, and each man’s name was canceled as he received his share. Thus, once the donative had been completed for everyone and every name canceled, the entire tablet containing the names of those to whom it had been agreed the donative should be distributed appeared canceled; and through this the emperor’s bounty [Col. 1301B] likewise appeared completed and discharged. “Of the most excellent emperors,” namely Lucius Septimius Severus the father and Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Caracalla the son, under whom Saint Jerome, in *On Illustrious Men of the Church*, attests that Tertullian flourished.

EXECUTION, PURSUIT (*EXSECUTIO*). II, 604 B, “And the enemy’s pursuit,” that is, persecution.

BLOW AWAY (*EXSUFFLARE*). I, 677 B, “He will spit at the smoking altars and blow upon them,” that is, professing himself a Christian through aversion to the idolatrous smoke.

STRUCTURE (*EXSTRUCTIO*). I, 1294 A, a heap or raised mass of hair, of which Juvenal, *Satire* VI, says, “She builds her head high.”

STAND OUT (*EXTARE*). I, 1331 B, “And we stand out among those who are submerged,” that is, we rise prominently. The submerged are those situated in the depths, who are opposed to the lofty and eminent.
