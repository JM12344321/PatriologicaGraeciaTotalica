[Col. 0077C] I say that none of the faithful knows a crown upon his head [Note: [Col. 0077C] “For the head,” MS. Wouw.] except at a time of trial of this sort. All observe the rule in this way, from catechumens to confessors and martyrs—or even deniers. You may consider whence comes the authority of this custom, which is now the chief question. Yet when it is asked why something is observed, it is meanwhile established that it is observed. Therefore an offense committed against an observance that must now be vindicated by its own title, and sufficiently authorized by the support of consensus, can appear neither nonexistent nor uncertain. [Col. 0078A] Its rationale must indeed be sought, but with the observance preserved—not for its destruction, but rather for its edification (2 Cor. 13:10), so that you may observe it all the more once you are also assured of its rationale.

But what sort of conduct is it for someone to call an observance into question precisely when he has fallen away from it, and then to ask whence the observance derived when he has ceased to keep it? Even if he wishes to appear to call it into question in order to show that he did not offend by abandoning the observance, he nevertheless offended by previously presuming the observance. For if he did not offend by accepting the crown today, he once offended by refusing it.

This discussion, therefore, will not be addressed to those to whom the question does not properly belong, but to those who, from a desire to learn, put forward not a challenge but a request for counsel. For [Col. 0078B] the matter is always being investigated, and I commend the faith that believed [Note: [Col. 0078C] “Believes,” Fran. Paris.] a thing ought to be observed before it learned why (Isa. 7:9). It is easy to demand at once: “Where is it written that we must not be crowned?” But where is it written [Note: [Col. 0078D] “But where is it written?” Here Tertullian argues wonderfully for traditions against the Calvinists and the Magdeburg Centuriators, who asserted far too boldly that this was a dogma of Montanus—an assertion that is false and pure slander. For Montanus, according to Eusebius, *History*, book V, is said to have been the first to propose certain things contrary to tradition. LE PR.] that we may be crowned? For those who demand the support of Scripture on the opposing side thereby prejudge that the support of Scripture ought also to be present for their own side [Note: [Col. 0078C] “Side’s,” Seml.]. For if it is said that one may be crowned because Scripture does not forbid it, the retort will equally be made that one may not be crowned because Scripture does not command it. What will discipline do? Will it accept both, on the ground that neither is forbidden? Or reject both, on the ground that neither is commanded? “But what is not forbidden is freely permitted.” Rather, what is not freely permitted is forbidden.

## CHAPTER IV

If you demand a law from Scripture for these and other disciplines of this sort, you will find none. Tradition will be presented to you as their originator, custom as their confirmer, and faith [Note: [Col. 0079B] “Of faith,” Rhen.] as their observer. ## CHAPTER V

But [Note: [Col. 0081C] “Custom, but,” Venet.] why is a greater rationale produced [Note: [Col. 0082C] “Required,” Fran.] for Christian observances, when [Note: [Col. 0082D] “When nature too defends them,” etc. And indeed nature precedes all laws; hence those who sin, sin against nature. LE PR.] nature too defends them [Note: [Col. 0082C] “Although it may defend,” Wouw.], nature being the first of all disciplines? I think, moreover, that the Lord of nature is our God, who fashioned the human being and arranged within him definite senses for desiring, judging, and attaining the fruits of things, employing the respective members as a kind of organ. Through these services of the outer human being [Note: [Col. 0082D] “Through these things that serve the outer human being, the fruits of divine gifts are conveyed by the soul to the senses.” Tertullian’s meaning is this: through these organs—namely, the ears, mouth, eyes, nostrils, and finally the hands—all of which serve and minister to the outer human being, that is, the body, assent or approval of natural things, which were created by God for humanity’s sake, is conveyed from the soul. RIG.], which minister to the inner human being, the fruits of divine gifts are conveyed to the soul by the senses [Note: [Col. 0082C] Venet. reads: “Through these things that serve the human being, the fruits of divine gifts are conveyed by the soul to the senses.”].

What fruit, then, comes from flowers? For the proper and certainly the chief substance of crowns is the flowers of the field. “Either scent,” you say, “or color, or both together.” What senses will perceive color and scent? Sight, I suppose, and smell. Which members have been allotted those senses? The eyes, unless I am mistaken, and the nostrils. Use flowers, therefore, by sight and [Col. 0082C] smell, whose fruit they are; use them through the eyes and nostrils, which are the organs of those senses. Their substance has been handed over to you by God; their manner of arrangement comes from the world—although even an extraordinary arrangement does not obstruct their ordinary use. Let flowers be these things for you whether inserted, interwoven, strung on thread, or woven around a rush, provided they are free and loose: objects, namely, of sight and scent.

If by chance you regard a crown as a bundle of flowers gathered into a sequence so that you may carry several at once and use them all together, then by all means put it into your bosom too, if it possesses such purity; scatter it over your couch, if it possesses such softness; and entrust it to your cup, if it possesses such innocence. Enjoy it in as many ways as you perceive it. But what taste of a flower is there upon the head [Note: [Col. 0083C] “What taste of a flower is there upon the head?” For this reason Diogenes, when anointing his feet with perfume, used to say that fragrance vanishes from the head into the air, but from the feet reaches the nostrils. RIG.]? What perception is there of a crown, except only that of a fetter—a place where neither its color is seen, nor its scent inhaled, nor its tenderness appreciated? It is as contrary to nature to seek a flower with the head as a sound with the nostril. Everything contrary to nature deserves among everyone [Note: [Col. 0083D] “Deserves among everyone the mark of a monstrosity.” These words, “among everyone,” are not found in the exemplar and indeed may elegantly be omitted. RIG.] the mark of a monstrosity, but among us also a formal charge of sacrilege against God [Note: [Col. 0083D] “A formal charge of sacrilege.” This alludes to the formal charges and inscriptions of defendants discussed in chapter 1 of *On Idolatry*. ## CHAPTER VI

## CHAPTER VII

And thus, as though by superabundance, let them also examine the remaining arguments which, in our own particular case, forbid every kind of crowning of the head. For we are also pressed by our participation in natural discipline to turn to the distinctive character of Christian discipline, which must now be defended in its entirety through the other varieties of crowns as well. These appear to have been devised for other uses, since they are constructed from other materials, lest—because they do not consist of flowers, whose use nature has marked out, as with this very military crown of laurel—they should be thought not to fall under the prohibition of our sect merely because they have escaped nature’s prior objection.

I therefore see that the matter must be treated both more carefully and more plainly, from its origins through its development and excesses. Secular literature is necessary for this purpose, for secular matters must necessarily be proved from their own documentary sources. I believe the few sources I have touched upon will suffice.

If there was any Pandora, whom Hesiod records as the first of women, hers was the first head to be crowned by the Graces, when gifts were bestowed upon her by everyone—whence the name Pandora. > Clio and Clio’s sisters did not appear to me  
> As I tended flocks in your valleys, Ascra.

From this we may be sufficiently instructed how alien we ought to judge the institution of the crowned head, introduced by those whom the world believed to be gods and thereafter administered in their honor. For if the devil, a liar from the beginning, produces the falsehood of divinity in this form too, he undoubtedly also provided in advance the persons through whom the falsehood of divinity would be enacted.
