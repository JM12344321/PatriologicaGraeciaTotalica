> “The Bears wheel together, and for that reason are called Wagons.” [LE PR.]

The north will send forth bears, the zodiac bulls and lions. He who endures these things to the end will be saved. [Col. 0143C] Are the end, then, and suffering, and killing, and the first confession also in heaven? And where is the flesh, which is necessary for all these things? Where is the body, which alone can be killed by human beings? Certain reasoning has conveyed these conclusions to us even in the manner of a game; and no one will dislodge the obstacle posed by this prescriptive argument, so as to avoid being compelled to transfer thither the whole course of persecution, the whole fixed preparation of its cause, wherever he has assigned a tribunal to confession. For confession arises from persecution, and persecution ends in confession. [Col. 0144A] Nor can things fail to follow together when they arrange both the entrance and the departure [Note: [Col. 0144C] Seml., following Rhen., deletes “which arrange”], that is, the beginning and the end.

Moreover, hatred of the name will be here; persecution breaks out here; betrayal brings one forward here; interrogation presses one here; the torture chamber rages here; and confession or denial completes this entire course upon earth. Therefore, if the other things are here, confession cannot be elsewhere; if confession is elsewhere, neither can the other things be here. But the other things are assuredly nowhere else; therefore neither is confession in heaven. Or, if they wish there to be another system of heavenly interrogation and confession, they will certainly also have to construct for it an entirely different course, distinct from that arrangement marked out in the Scriptures; and we can [Note: [Col. 0144C] Rig.: “we may be able”] say: Let them see to that, provided that this course of earthly interrogation and confession, proceeding from the material circumstances of persecution and public hostility toward the name, remains secure in the faith proper to it—[Col. 0144B] so that it is to be believed just as it is written [Note: [Col. 0144C] Seml., from Rhen., deletes “so that”], and understood just as it is heard.

Here I uphold the entire course, since the Lord himself assigns no other region of the world. For what does he add after the conclusion concerning confession and denial?

> Do not suppose [Note: [Col. 0144C] Seml., following Rhen.: “let him not suppose”] that I have come to send peace upon the earth; I have not come to send peace, but a sword—

certainly upon the earth.

> For I have come to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother, and a mother-in-law against her daughter-in-law; and a man’s enemies will be those of his own household.

For thus it comes about that

> brother will betray brother to death, and a father his son; and children will rise against their parents and cause them to die. And he who endures to the end will be saved

(Matt. 10:34ff.).

So entirely does this whole course of the Lord’s sword—sent not into heaven but upon the earth—also establish confession there, a confession that, by enduring to the end, [Col. 0144C] is destined to suffer death.

## CHAPTER XI

By the same pattern, therefore, we maintain that the other sayings also pertain to martyrdom.

> He who values even his own life more than me is not worthy of me

(cf. Luke 14);

that is, he who would rather live by denying me than die by confessing me.

> He who finds his life will lose it; but he who loses it for my sake will find it.

For the man who has denied Christ thereby finds [Note: [Col. 0144C] Fran.: “will find”] it [Note: [Col. 0144C] Seml.: “by that”; Fran.: “it”], in that he gains his [Col. 0145A] life; but he who thinks that by denial he gains it will lose it in Gehenna [Note: [Col. 0145C] Jun.: “so that”; Seml.: “to gain”]. The man who confesses and is killed, however, loses it for the present, but will find it unto eternal life. Indeed, when governors exhort people [Note: [Col. 0145C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “urge people”] to denial, they say, “Save your life, and do not lose your life.”

[Note: [Col. 0145D] **Save your life.** Similar words of the proconsul Paternus to Cyprian occur in the account of that most courageous martyr’s passion; Augustine also attacks them in his sermon on the same Cyprian. RIG.]

How else [Note: [Col. 0145C] Others: “in what manner”] would Christ speak, except in the manner in which a Christian would be dealt with [Note: [Col. 0145C] Rig., Seml.: “is dealt with”; others: “is being dealt with”]? When, moreover, he forbids his servants to prepare their answer for the tribunal, he instructs them and promises that the Holy Spirit will answer (Matt. 10:20); when he wishes a brother in prison to be visited (Matt. 25), he commands that care be shown to a confessor; and when he affirms that God will avenge his elect (Matt. 15), he consoles them in their sufferings. Even in the parable of the seed [Note: [Col. 0145C] Rig.: “of the word”] after the turf has dried up, he [Col. 0145B] depicts the heat of persecutions [Note: [Col. 0145D] Rig., from an old book: “in persecution”].

[Note: [Col. 0145D] **In the parable of the seed after the turf has dried up.** The exemplar reads: “In the parable of the word after the turf has dried up, he depicts the heat of persecutions.” RIG.]

If these things are not received as they are pronounced, then without doubt they mean something other than what they sound like: one thing will be in the words, another in their meanings, as with allegories, parables, and riddles. Whatever wind of argument, therefore, these scorpions have conceived [Note: [Col. 0145D] Jun.: “whatever poison they have conceived”], with whatever point they have thrust themselves forward, there is now a single line of response: they will be challenged to show whether the events themselves are carried out according to the Scriptures. For something different will be signified in the Scriptures only if that very thing is not found in the events. What is written must come to pass. Moreover, what is written will then come to pass if nothing else [Note: [Col. 0145D] Seml.: “in another manner”; Seml. also reads “and” for “is”] comes to pass.

But see: we are hated by all people on account of the name, just as it is written.

[Note: [Col. 0145D] **On account of the name.** This has been sufficiently discussed in the *Apology*, where we showed from ancient authorities that neither praise nor blame ought to be expected from a mere name. LE PR.]

We are also betrayed by those closest to us, just as it is written; we are brought before the authorities, interrogated, [Col. 0145C] tortured, confess, and are slaughtered, just as it is written (Matt. 10). Thus the Lord declared it. If he declared these things in some other sense, why do they not happen otherwise than [Note: [Col. 0145D] Jun.: “the things which”] he declared—that is, precisely as he declared them? Yet they do not happen otherwise than he declared. Therefore, just as they happen, so he declared them [Note: [Col. 0146C] “Therefore, just as they happen, so he declared them” is deleted by one edition; Venet. substitutes “but also just as”], and so they happen. For they could not have happened otherwise than he declared; nor would he himself have declared them otherwise than he wished them to happen. Thus the Scriptures [Note: [Col. 0146C] Seml. adds “these”] will signify nothing other than what we recognize in the events.

Or [Note: [Col. 0146C] Seml.: “But”], if the things foretold are not yet being done, how are [Col. 0146A] these things being done [Note: [Col. 0146C] Seml., following Rhen., deletes “being done”] which were not foretold? For the things now being done were not foretold, if what was foretold consists of different things and not of the things now being done. But now, because [Note: [Col. 0146C] Ciacc.: “when”] the very things in the events are the things [Note: [Col. 0146C] Seml. adds “also”] in the words, they are believed to have been spoken in another sense. What would happen if they were actually found to have occurred [Note: [Col. 0146D] Seml.: “been enacted”] differently? But this is [Note: [Col. 0146D] Seml.: “will be”] the perversity of faith: not to believe what has been proved, but to presume what has not been proved.

Against that perversity I shall also set this argument: if the things now being done just as they were written are not the very things foretold, then those other things too must not happen just as they were written, lest they too be endangered by the example of the former; for the possibility of one thing in the words and another in the events has indeed been excluded; and [Note: [Col. 0146D] Jun.: “Thus”] it follows that prophecies will not be recognized even when they have come to pass, if they are foretold otherwise than they are destined to happen. And how will things be believed that will not have been foretold, [Col. 0146B] since they will not have been foretold in the manner in which they happen? Thus heretics, by refusing to believe the things foretold as they have been proved, believe things that were not even foretold.

## CHAPTER XII

Who could have known the innermost substance of the Scriptures better than Christ’s own school? The Lord adopted them as disciples for himself, certainly to be taught everything; and he furnished them as teachers for us, certainly to teach everything. To whom would he more readily have explained the figurative form of his speech than to those to whom he revealed the image of his glory—Peter, James, and John—and afterward Paul, whom he made a participant in paradise even before his martyrdom?

[Note: [Col. 0146D] **To whom would he more readily have explained the figurative form of his speech?** That is, the mysteries and sacraments of his discourse, when its meanings withdraw from its literal words, as Tertullian himself speaks in this very work, ch. 15. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0146D] **Whom he made a participant in paradise even before his martyrdom.** That is, when he was caught up as far as the third heaven and saw things that no one can narrate or express, or even comprehend in thought. LE PR.]

Or did they too write otherwise than they understood, as teachers of deception rather than truth? Peter, at least, writes to the people of Pontus:

> For how great is the glory if you endure when punished not as wrongdoers! For this [Col. 0146C] is grace, and to this you have been called, because Christ also suffered for us, leaving you himself as an example, that you might follow in his footsteps

(1 Pet. 2).

And again:

> Beloved, do not be terrified by the burning that is taking place among you as a trial, as though something strange were happening to you. Rather, insofar as you share in Christ’s sufferings, rejoice, so that at the revelation of his glory you may also rejoice with exultation. If you are dishonored in [Note: [Col. 0146D] Seml. deletes “in”] the name of Christ, you are blessed, because glory and the Spirit of God rest [Note: [Col. 0146D] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “will rest”] upon you. Only let none of you suffer as a murderer, or thief, [Col. 0147A] or evildoer, or a meddler in another’s affairs. But if one suffers as a Christian, let him not be ashamed; rather, let him glorify the Lord in that name

(1 Pet. 4).

[Note: [Col. 0147D] **A meddler in another’s affairs.** That is, one who attends to concerns belonging to others. LE PR.]

John, moreover, exhorts us even to lay down our lives for our brothers, denying that there is fear in love:

> For perfect love casts fear outside, because fear has punishment; and he who fears is not perfect in love

(1 John 4:18).

What fear is it preferable to understand except the fear that produces denial? What love does he affirm to be perfect except that which drives out fear and inspires confession? With what punishment does he punish fear except that which the denier will incur, when he is to be killed in Gehenna [Note: [Col. 0147C] Seml.: “into Gehenna”] in both body and soul? But if John teaches that one must die for one’s brothers, how much more does he teach that one must die for the Lord—being sufficiently instructed by his own Apocalypse to offer this counsel!

For the Spirit had commanded the angel of the church of the [Col. 0147B] Smyrnaeans:

> Behold, the devil will cast [Note: [Col. 0147C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “casts”] some of your number into prison, that you may be tested for ten days. Be faithful even unto death, and I shall give you the crown of life

(Rev. 2:10).

Likewise, to the church of Pergamum he spoke concerning Antipas, the most faithful martyr, slain in the dwelling place of Satan. Likewise, to the church of Philadelphia, he promised that the man who had not denied the Lord’s name would be delivered from the final trial.

[Note: [Col. 0147D] **Had not denied the Lord’s name.** Compare Rev. 3:10 and 1 Cor. 10:13; and elsewhere, “the devil’s time is short.” SEML.]

Thereafter he promises each conqueror: now the tree of life and exemption from the second death; now hidden manna together with a white stone and an unknown name; now authority over the iron rod and the brightness of the morning star; now to be clothed in white, not to be erased from the book of life, and to become a pillar in God’s temple, inscribed with the name of God and the Lord and of the heavenly Jerusalem; now to sit with the Lord upon his throne—a privilege once denied to the sons of Zebedee.

[Col. 0147C] Who are these most blessed conquerors, if not martyrs in the proper sense? For victories belong to those to whom battles belong, and battles belong to those whose blood is shed. Meanwhile the souls of the martyrs rest peacefully beneath the altar,

[Note: [Col. 0147D] **The souls of the martyrs rest peacefully beneath the altar.** By the term “altar” the earth seems to be signified, since it served as an altar for the immolation of the martyrs. RIG.]

and confidence in vengeance nourishes their patience; clothed in robes, they lay claim to the white radiance of glory until others too complete the fellowship of their glory.

[Note: [Col. 0148D] **And confidence in vengeance nourishes their patience; clothed in robes.** We supplied these words from Agobard’s manuscripts. He says elegantly that the souls of the martyrs, laid beneath the altar in hope of resurrection, rest peacefully and, as though in a meadow of refreshment, nourish their patience with confidence in vengeance. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0147C] Others: “The souls nourish patience and, clothed in robes, are white.” Seml., omitting several words: “confidence in vengeance [nourishes] whiteness.”]

For once again an innumerable multitude is revealed, clothed in white and distinguished by the palms of victory—evidently those triumphing [Note: [Col. 0147C] Seml.: “triumphal”] over Antichrist. As one of the elders says:

> These are those who come out of that great tribulation; they have washed their garment and made it white in the blood of the Lamb

(Rev. 7:14).

For the garment of the soul is the flesh. Its filth is washed away in baptism, but its stains are [Col. 0148A] made white in martyrdom. Isaiah too promises that what is reddish [Note: [Col. 0147C] Seml.: “red”] and scarlet will become white as snow and wool (Isa. 1:18).

When great Babylon is also described as drunk with the blood of the saints, her drunkenness is undoubtedly supplied by the cups of martyrdom. What the fear of martyrdom will bring upon a person is likewise shown. For among all the rejected—indeed, before all the rest—it is said:

> But for the fearful,

and then for the others,

> their portion is in the lake of fire and sulfur.

Thus in John’s Epistle the fear that love casts outside has punishment (1 John 4:18).

## CHAPTER XIII

The apostle Paul, once a persecutor (Acts 9) who was the first to shed the Church’s blood, afterward exchanged the sword for the pen and turned the blade into a plow: Benjamin’s ravening wolf, who afterward, [Col. 0148B] according to Jacob, himself brought food [Note: [Col. 0148C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “himself also brought food”]. How highly he commends martyrdoms, which had now become desirable even to himself!

Rejoicing over the Thessalonians [Note: [Col. 0148C] Venet. omits “over”], he says:

> So that we may glory in you among the churches of God because of your endurance and faith in all the persecutions and tribulations that you endure as a demonstration of God’s just judgment, so that you may be accounted worthy of his kingdom, for which you also suffer

(2 Thess. 1:3–4).

[Note: [Col. 0148D] **You endure as a demonstration of just judgment.** You await the coming, the manifestation, of the judgment. RIG.]

Likewise, to the Romans:

> And not only so, but we even exult in tribulations, knowing [Note: [Col. 0148C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “by art”] that tribulation perfects endurance, endurance produces tested character, tested character produces hope, and hope does not disappoint

(Rom. 5:11).

And again:

> But if we are children, we are also heirs: heirs indeed of God, but coheirs with Christ, provided that we suffer with him [Note: [Col. 0148C] Others: “that we may suffer with him”], so that we may also be glorified with him. For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory that will be revealed in us [Col. 0148C]

(Rom. 8:17).

[Note: [Col. 0148C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth. read: “is to be revealed.”]

And therefore, shortly afterward:

> Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Tribulation, or distress, or hunger, or nakedness, or danger, or the sword? As it is written: For your [Note: [Col. 0148C] Agobard’s codex: “your,” feminine form] sake we are put to death all day long; we are accounted as livestock for slaughter. But in all these things we more than conquer through him who loved us. For we are persuaded that neither death nor life, nor power, nor height, nor depth, nor any other created thing will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord

(Rom. 8:35).

And when enumerating his sufferings to the Corinthians, he certainly prescribed what must be suffered:

> In labors more abundantly, in prisons very frequently, in deaths more often; five times I received from the Jews forty lashes less [Col. 0149A] one, three times I was beaten with rods, once I was stoned,

and the rest (2 Cor. 11:33).

Even if these appear to be hardships rather than martyrdoms, he nevertheless says again:

> Therefore I take pleasure in weaknesses, in insults, in necessities, in persecutions, and in distresses for Christ’s sake

(2 Cor. 12:10).

Likewise, earlier:

> We who are afflicted in everything, but not confined; who are in need, but not utterly destitute; who are [Note: [Col. 0149C] Jun.: “who also are”] driven by persecutions, but not abandoned; who are cast down, but do not perish—always carrying about in our body the dying of Christ Jesus

(2 Cor. 4:8).

> But even if our outer man is being corrupted,

he says—that is, the flesh, through the violence of persecutions—

> yet the inner man is renewed day by day—

that is, the soul, through hope in the promises.

> For our present, temporary and light tribulation produces for us, beyond all measure upon measure [Note: [Col. 0149C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “in addition, through”], an [Col. 0149B] eternal weight of glory [Note: [Col. 0149C] Others: “will produce”], while we look not at the things that are seen but at those that are not seen. For the things that are seen are temporary,

speaking of hardships,

> but the things that are not seen are eternal,

pledging the rewards [Note: [Col. 0149D] Agobard’s codex: “the things promised”].

Writing to the Thessalonians [Note: [Col. 0149D] Fran.: “Philippians”] about his chains, he declared blessed those to whom it had been granted not only to believe in Christ but also to suffer for him:

> Having the same contest which you saw in me and now have heard [Note: [Col. 0149D] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “hear”] about

(Phil. 1:29).

[Note: [Col. 0149D] **Writing to the Thessalonians about his chains.** This passage must be corrected to read “to the Philippians,” both because “about his chains” is added—the first Epistle to the Philippians was written from chains, whereas neither Epistle to the Thessalonians was—and because the scriptural testimonies that follow are found at Phil. 1:7 and following. In the first of these, Tertullian retained the Greek word *agōn*, “contest”; in the latter, he confirms the reading of the more accurate Latin codices, which have “I am poured out upon the sacrifice,” instead of the reading of others, “I emulate.” PAM.]

> For even if I am poured out as a libation upon the sacrifice, I rejoice and rejoice together with all of you; likewise, you too rejoice and rejoice together with me

(Phil. 2:17).

You see how he defines the blessedness of martyrdom, for which he establishes a solemn celebration of mutual joy. Finally, when he had come very near the fulfillment of his desire, how exultantly does he write to Timothy at its prospect [Note: [Col. 0150C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “he exults, saying to Timothy”]?

> For I am already being poured out as a libation, and [Col. 0149C] the time of my departure is at hand [Note: [Col. 0150C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “the time of lodging elsewhere presses upon me”]. I have fought the good contest, I have completed the course, I have kept the faith. The crown remains, which the Lord will give me on that day

(2 Tim. 4:6–7)—that is, the day of his suffering.

He himself had said enough earlier:

> The saying is faithful: for if we have died together with Christ, we shall also live together with him; if we endure with him, we shall also reign with him; [Col. 0150A] if we deny him, he too will deny us; if we do not believe, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself

(2 Tim. 2:11).

> Therefore do not be ashamed of the martyrdom of our Lord, nor of me, his prisoner,

because he had previously said:

> For God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power, love, and sound understanding

(2 Tim. 1:8).

For through power we suffer out of love for God, and with sound understanding when we suffer on account of our innocence. Wherever he commands endurance, what does he have in view more than sufferings? Wherever he tears people away from idolatry, what does he tear away from it in preference to martyrdom?

[Note: [Col. 0149D] **What does he tear away from it in preference to martyrdom?** Tertullian says: Wherever God tears people away from idolatry, what does he tear away from idolatry, to set it immediately in opposition to idolatry, in preference to the martyrdoms of courageous Christians? RIG.]

## CHAPTER XIV

To be sure, he admonishes the Romans to be subject to all authorities (Rom. 13:4–7), because there is no authority except from God, because it does not bear the sword without cause, and because it is God’s ministry [Note: [Col. 0150C] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “female minister”]; but it is also, he says,

> an avenger for wrath upon him who [Col. 0150B] does evil.

For he had also prefaced this:

> Rulers are not a cause of fear to good conduct, but to evil. Do you wish not to fear the authority? Do good, and you will receive praise from it. It is therefore God’s minister to you for good. But if you do evil, be afraid.

[Note: [Col. 0149D] **It is therefore God’s minister.** Agobard’s manuscript has, “It is therefore God’s minister . . .” From these traces it is apparent that the text was, “It is therefore God’s ministry,” as above. RIG.]

Thus he does not command you to be subject to the authorities as an opportunity to frustrate martyrdom, but as an inducement to live well, even with regard to them, as though they were assistants of justice and ministers of divine judgment, which even here passes preliminary judgment upon the guilty.

He then proceeds to explain how he wishes you to be subject to the authorities, commanding that you render tax to whom tax is due, and revenue to whom revenue is due—that is:

> the things that are Caesar’s to Caesar, and the things that are God’s to God

(Matt. 22:21).

But the human being belongs to God alone [Note: [Col. 0150D] Rhen., Seml., Oberth.: “God alone; the human being”]. Peter, of course, had spoken in agreement: the king is indeed to be honored, but in such a way that the king is honored when he attends to his own affairs and [Col. 0150C] remains far removed from divine honors. For father and mother too are loved [Note: [Col. 0150D] Fran., Pam.: “diligently”], provided they are not compared with God. But it will not be permissible to love even one’s own life above God.

[Note: [Col. 0150D] **Peter, of course, had spoken in agreement.** *Condixerat* means that Peter had spoken together with Paul, had said things consonant with Paul. So in the book *On the Crown*: “which the pagan faith likewise affirmed together.” RIG.]

## CHAPTER XV

Are even the apostles’ writings, then, mutable [Note: [Col. 0150D] Pam.: “well known”; Jun.: “clouded”]? [Col. 0151A]

[Note: [Col. 0150D] **The apostles’ writings are mutable.** This is the reading in Ursinus’ exemplar, which we followed in the first edition, as though Septimius had spoken of “mutable writings” which meant something other than they sounded, with one thing in the words and another in their meanings. We may now be permitted to change our judgment, since our only concern is the truth. In Agobard’s book Septimius says: “Are the apostles’ writings, then, well known?” That reading must be retained. For after proving, on the authority of the prophets and the Gospel, that martyrdoms must be courageously undergone by Christians, and that this is the Lord’s precept or command, he also proves it by citing the apostles’ writings, and adds: “Are the apostles’ writings, then, well known?” That is: Are they not known, clear, and manifest to everyone? Certainly they are. He immediately presses the point: “Let it indeed be so, that their meanings withdraw from their letters”—as if those writings were not so clear and manifest that they could admit some indulgence in interpretation. Let us come to the events themselves. Let us see what the apostles themselves, the interpreters of their own writings, did. They certainly did not hesitate to expend their lives in martyrdom, since they had no doubt about the Lord’s command. RIG.]

And are we altogether simple souls [Note: [Col. 0151C] **And are we altogether simple souls?** These are sayings—or rather, the stings of scorpions—of the sort mentioned above. RIG.], and doves only [Note: [Col. 0151C] **And doves only.** Not also serpents in prudence. He alludes to Christ’s words in Matthew: “Be prudent as serpents and simple as doves.” RIG.], who gladly wander astray [Note: [Col. 0151C] **Who gladly wander astray.** Straying through unwary simplicity and flying away from the Lord’s dwelling. RIG.]? I suppose it is from a desire to live.

Let it indeed be so, that their meanings withdraw from their literal words. Yet the sufferings that we know the apostles underwent constitute manifest teaching. This alone I understand as I run through the Acts. I seek nothing further. There are prisons, chains, scourges, stones, swords, assaults by Jews, gatherings of the nations, reports from tribunes [Note: [Col. 0151B] Jun.: variant spelling *ellogia*], audiences before kings, tribunals of proconsuls, and the name of Caesar: these require no interpreter.

That Peter is beaten [Note: [Col. 0151B] Rig.: “is raised up” (*ὑψοῦται*, “is exalted”)], that Stephen is crushed, that James is sacrificed, that Paul is torn apart—these things are written in their own blood.

[Note: [Col. 0151C] **Peter is beaten.** So Agobard’s old exemplar reads. Nevertheless, since the deaths of the apostles are designated in this passage by their kinds of punishment, we judge that Peter’s passion was described by a more expressive word and that the reading here, “is beaten,” is corrupt. We should therefore read “is raised up,” meaning that he is lifted and suspended upon the elevated and erected cross: *ὑψοῦται*, which the common version rendered “is exalted.” John 12 contains Christ’s words, indicating by what death he was to die: *ἐὰν ὑψωθῶ ἐκ τῆς γῆς*, “If I am exalted from the earth.” Sulpicius Severus: “Peter was raised upon the cross.” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0151D] **Stephen is crushed.** By the onrush of stones, by stoning. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0151D] **James is sacrificed.** The Lord’s brother, or John’s brother? Herod killed John’s brother with the sword. Luke, Acts 12: *Ἀνεῖλε δὲ Ἰάκωβον, τὸν ἀδελφὸν Κυρίου, μαχαίρᾳ*—“He killed James, the brother of the Lord, with the sword”—which in the martyrologies is understood as beheading and is entirely unsuited to sacrifice. It must therefore be said that James, the Lord’s brother, is designated here. Eusebius, Epiphanius, and Jerome report that he died after being struck in the head with a fuller’s club; but this derives from Hegesippus’ rather foolish and worthless narrative. Nevertheless, as far as the manner of death is concerned, it seems to be confirmed by these words of our Septimius: he would have said that James, the Lord’s brother, was sacrificed because he was slaughtered with a fuller’s club, as though with the mallet or club of a sacrificial attendant. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0152C] **Paul is torn apart.** His head was separated and cut from his neck. Septimius, in book IV *Against Marcion*, says that separation is the work of the sword; and Luke used *διαμερισμόν* (“division”) for what Matthew called *μάχαιραν* (“a sword”). Paul is therefore torn apart by beheading, which removes the chief part of the body. For this happens to those punished by such a penalty: they do not fall whole, nor lie there as an entire corpse. Indeed, the heads of the beheaded are generally fixed upon a stake or spear. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0152C] **Is beaten, is crushed, is sacrificed, is torn apart.** Septimius plays upon antitheses. He opposes Stephen to Peter. Peter was raised and elevated upon the cross, whereas Stephen was buried beneath a heap of stones. He then opposes James to Paul. Paul, his head severed, was divided into two parts; but James, sacrificed and dashed to the earth by the blow of a club, fell whole. # THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN AGAINST PRAXEAS

## SUMMARY

[Col. 0153B]

Praxeas, a Phrygian originating from Asia, had once been cast into prison for the sake of the faith; because of those sufferings—which were not especially worthy of mention—he had become greatly swollen with pride. Nevertheless, he had rendered distinguished service to the Church. For when the pontiff—called Eleutherius by some and Victor by others—was on the point of granting letters of communion to the Montanists, who represented themselves as catholic in the transmitted profession of faith, Praxeas, then arriving from Asia, disclosed the errors of the Montanists that had become notorious in that province, and so prevented those men from deceiving that excellent pontiff.

In the end, his own arrogance destroyed the wretched man. For while he never ceased boasting of the titles of a martyr, he shared the fate common to all the proud and became the unfortunate founder of a new sect. He acknowledged one God in such a way as to abolish every distinction of persons, daring to assert that the Father himself had assumed flesh, had been driven to the cross, and now sits at his own right hand. His followers were called Patripassians or Monarchians because they posited only a single person of the most holy Trinity.

The most excellent work that Tertullian certainly wrote after his own lapse is this treatise *Against Praxeas*, in which he undertakes the defense of the faith of the Trinity; for on this point the Montanists did not disagree with the Catholic Church. In this work Septimius explicitly employs the term “Trinity” and exposes the deception of those heretics who usurp the name “monarchy” in order to persuade the less educated that they are defending the one almighty Creator of the world, while in the meantime making a heresy out of the One.

To establish the distinction between the persons of the Father and the Son, he says that the entire question concerning the Son must be examined: whether he exists, who he is, and how he exists.

> Before all things God was alone . . . because nothing else existed outside him. Yet not even then was he alone, for he had with him his own Reason—the Word, Wisdom—which he afterward sent forth externally, and it became the outward Word.

Tertullian judges that the Word of God should be called *Sermo* (“Speech” or “Word”) only after its outward utterance, or manifestation, made in the creation of the universe, in accordance with the very ancient theologians’ phraseology and manner of speaking. He nevertheless acknowledges that the Word was with God in the beginning. For although God had not yet sent forth his Word, he nonetheless possessed him within himself, together with and in Reason itself, silently thinking and arranging within himself the things that he was soon to speak through the Word.

From this it becomes clear in what sense he said elsewhere:

> There was a time when neither sin nor the Son existed;

namely, with respect to their outward manifestation through the creation of things—especially when he said, “Let there be light.” With respect to inward generation, however, his Son, the Word and Wisdom, was always with God the Father.

Tertullian further shows that the Son is from the Father’s substance and that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son; that the Word, who was with God from all eternity and who went forth from him, as it were, to create and govern the world, is a subsistent person, yet is not different in substance from the Father; that it does not therefore follow that there are two Gods and two Lords; and that the Son, not the Father, became human, without ceasing to be God, to such a degree that the properties of human nature were found in Christ. In a word, throughout this treatise he lucidly explains the Church’s faith concerning the mysteries both of the Trinity and of the Incarnation. LUMP.
