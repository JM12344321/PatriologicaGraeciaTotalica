Καὶ νοῦς ὁρῇ, καὶ ἀκούει, τὰ δ’ ἄλλα κωφὰ καὶ τυφλά (“It is the mind that sees and hears; all else is deaf and blind”). LE PR.] The mind sees, the mind hears; everything else is deaf and blind. And so, once again, he says that the man who is wise chiefly by means of his mind possesses the highest wisdom: he neither sets sight before himself nor draws any such sense into the mind, but, in his reflection, employs the pure mind itself to apprehend whatever is pure in things, withdrawing as far as possible from the eyes and ears and, so to speak, from the entire body, because it disturbs the soul and, whenever it associates with it, does not permit the soul to possess truth and wisdom. Plato therefore established Ideas, which, since they always remain in the same condition, would provide stability for definitions and sciences. LAC.] whose objects are not present or subject to the bodily senses, but are far removed from common awareness, in secrecy, in the higher realms, and with God himself.

For Plato holds [Note: [Col. 0678D] “That there are certain invisible substances,” etc. Very many of the Fathers accept the Ideas. Those who reject them, however, reject them as they were understood by the Gnostics and the followers of Valentinus. These heretics appear to follow Plato in many matters, although I have also read the Ideas condemned by many Fathers in the sense taught by Aristotle: namely, that Plato admitted certain Ideas as separate universal natures, through participation in which individual things were produced. In this Aristotle either perpetrates or suffers an imposture, for this cannot be elicited from the Platonic writings. LE PR.] that there are certain invisible, incorporeal, supramundane, divine, and eternal substances, which he calls Ideas—that is, forms, exemplars, and causes of these natural things that are manifest and subject to the bodily senses. Those Ideas, he says, are the truths, while these things are their images. Do the heretical seeds of the Gnostics and Valentinians now begin to shine through? For from this source they seize upon the distinction between the bodily senses and the intellectual powers, and they even adapt to it the parable of the ten virgins (Matt. 25): the five foolish virgins are supposed to represent the bodily senses—foolish, of course, because they are easily deceived—whereas the wise virgins are supposed to express the mark of the intellectual powers, namely wisdom, because they attain that secret, heavenly truth established within the Pleroma—the mysteries of the heretical Ideas. Thus they also divide sense-perception: intellectual perception belongs to their spiritual seed, while sensory perception belongs to the animal seed, because the latter can in no way apprehend spiritual things. Invisible things belong to the former; visible, lowly, and temporal things, which are encountered by sense and consist in images, belong to the latter. For these reasons, therefore, we have laid down beforehand that the mind is nothing other than something supplied and constituted by the soul, and that the spirit is nothing foreign, but simply what the soul itself is through breath. Any later addition, however, must be attributed to what either God or the devil subsequently breathes into it.

And now, regarding the distinction between sensory and intellectual things, we admit no distinction other than the diversity of the objects themselves: bodily and spiritual, visible and invisible, openly disclosed and secret. The former are assigned to sense and the latter to understanding, yet both are allotted to the service of the soul, which perceives bodily things through the body just as it understands incorporeal things through the mind—with the qualification that it also perceives while it understands. For is not perceiving also understanding, and is not understanding [Col. 0679A] [Note: [Col. 0679C] Wouwer inserts “not.”] also perceiving? Or [Note: [Col. 0679C] Rhenanus and Pamelius read “But.”] what will perception be except the understanding of the thing perceived? What will understanding be except the perception of the thing understood? Why these instruments of torture for tormenting simplicity and suspending truth? Who will produce for me a perception that does not understand what it perceives, or an understanding that does not perceive what it understands, so as to prove that either can exist without [Note: [Col. 0679C] From the Codex Agobardinus.] the other?

If bodily things are perceived while incorporeal things are understood, it is the classes of objects that are different, not the dwelling places of perception and understanding—that is, not the soul and the mind. After all, by what are bodily things perceived? If by the mind, then the mind is already sensory and not merely intellectual; for while it understands, it perceives, since if it does not perceive, neither does it understand. But if bodily things are perceived by the soul, then the power of the soul is already intellectual and not merely sensory; for while it perceives, it understands, since if it does not understand, neither does it perceive.

Likewise, by what are incorporeal things understood? If by the mind, where will the soul be? If by the soul, where will the mind be? Things that are distinct ought to be absent from one another when they perform their respective functions. You might indeed suppose that the mind is absent from the soul whenever we are so occupied in mind that we do not know that we have seen or heard something, [Note: [Col. 0679C] “Because the mind was elsewhere.” Pertinent in part is this passage of Blessed Gregory of Nyssa, *Philosophy*, Book IV, chapter 3: “Or when the mind attends to other things: for example, when someone has resolved to meet a friend and, just as he is about to address him, passes him by because his mind is distracted by other things. So much so, however, would I contend that the soul itself neither saw nor heard, because it was elsewhere together with its own power—that is, the mind. For when a man loses his mind, it is the soul that loses its mind: the mind is not then wandering away, but suffers together with the soul.

[Note: [Col. 0679D] “The fall, however, belongs principally to the soul.” These words appear to be adduced from the opinion of those who distinguished the mind from the soul, as also are the words, “when the soul has departed,” etc. Yet he adapts that argument to his own opinion in the words that follow: “But since it follows,” etc. PAM.—“The fall belongs principally to the soul.” He is not speaking from the opinion of those who distinguished the mind entirely from the soul, as Pamelius supposed, but from his own position against them. LAC.] The fall, however, belongs principally to the soul. This is confirmed by the fact that, when the soul has departed, the mind is likewise no longer found in the man: thus the mind follows the soul everywhere and does not remain behind even at the end. But since the mind follows the soul and is attached to it, the understanding is likewise attached to the soul: the mind follows the soul, and the understanding is attached to the mind.

Let understanding now be superior to perception and the superior knower of mysteries, if you will. Provided that it too, like perception, is a proper power of the soul, it makes no difference to me—except when understanding is preferred to perception for the very purpose of treating it as more separate in proportion as it is declared superior. Then, after refuting the distinction, I must also blunt the claim of superiority, since it would ultimately lead [Note: [Col. 0679C] Semler and Pamelius read “which would also lead.”] to belief in a superior God. [Note: [Col. 0679D] “Belief in a superior God.” He strikes at the Marcionites and other Gnostics, who invented several gods and preferred one to another—the good god to the evil. He treats these matters extensively, however, in the books against Marcion. LAC.] [Note: [Col. 0680C] “But concerning God, on his own field, when dealing with the heretics.” He appears [Col. 0680D] to indicate his books against Marcion, which he was already contemplating and which are included below. For the first and second books deal especially with God. PAM.] But we shall contend with the heretics concerning God on the field proper to that subject. Our present title concerns the soul, and this is the place for showing that understanding must not be insidiously preferred.

For even if the things apprehended by understanding, such as spiritual things, are superior to those apprehended by sense, such as bodily things, the superiority will belong to the objects—namely, the higher things over the lower—not to understanding over sense. [Note: [Col. 0680D] “For how can understanding be preferred to sense?” Tertullian, relying artfully upon the confidence of his genius, is accustomed, in order to persuade us of what is right, to bend toward the contrary and oblique side, as those who straighten a crooked rod twist it in the opposite direction. Thus here he appears to equate sense with understanding against those who unduly debased the former and exalted the latter. Yet although, when the faculties are compared in themselves with one another, understanding excels sense, when they are referred to their first root and origin, as he has done in this chapter, neither excels: for the source and original dwelling place of both is the same thing—namely, the soul—not the soul and mind as different things, as his opponents had supposed. The proofs Tertullian adds may also be referred to this point, so that the dependence of understanding upon the senses may be an argument for the unity of their principle and root, and consequently for the equality of their origin. LAC.] For how can understanding be preferred to sense, when it is instructed by sense in the knowledge of truths? If truths are apprehended through images—that is, if invisible things are known through visible things—since the Apostle also writes to us (Rom. 1:20), “For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are seen when understood through the things that have been made”; and since Plato says for the heretics, “The face of hidden things is the things that appear,” and “This world must in every respect be a kind of image of some other world” [Note: [Col. 0680C] Francius adds “that which for itself”; Pamelius reads “that which for itself.”]—then understanding evidently employs sense as its guide, author, and primary foundation, and cannot attain truths without it. How, then, will it be superior to that through which it exists, which it needs, and to which it owes everything it apprehends?

Thus both points are established: understanding must neither be preferred to sense—for whatever is established through something else is inferior to that thing itself—nor separated from sense; for whatever exists through something else exists together with it.

## CHAPTER XIX

Nor should we pass over those who deprive the soul of understanding even for a brief period. For in this way they prepare the road for subsequently introducing understanding into the soul, just as they also introduce [Note: [Col. 0680C] Junius deletes “also.”] [Col. 0680C] the mind, from which, of course, understanding proceeds. [Note: [Col. 0680C] Francius reads: “so that the soul, namely.”] They maintain that infancy consists of the soul alone, by which it merely lives, without at the same time possessing intelligence, because not everything that lives is intelligent. Trees, after all, live without being intelligent, according to Aristotle and anyone else who attributes animal substance [Note: [Col. 0680D] “Animal substance.” He uses “animal substance” for the animal or vegetative power. PAM.] universally—a thing which, according to us, is the private possession of the human being, not merely as a work of God, as all other things are, but as the breath of God, which this alone is. We say that it is born [Note: [Col. 0681C] “With all its equipment.” As though “with everything fitted out and supplied,” “with all its endowment,” “with all that belongs to it.” For so he writes above. And if we are referred to trees, we shall embrace the example. For even while they are not yet saplings, but still merely stems and even now only shoots, there arises together with them from the planting holes the distinctive power of their soul. For a time, however, it is delayed, consolidating and maturing together with its own strength until age completes [Note: [Col. 0681C] Semler and Oberthür omit “and maturing together.”] [Note: [Col. 0681C] Pamelius and Rhenanus read “fully completes.”] the condition in which its nature performs its function. Otherwise, from where do branches immediately begin to be grafted upon them, leaves to be formed, buds to swell, little flowers to be adorned, and juices to be seasoned, unless the entire equipment of their kind lies at rest within them and, as it advances through their parts, grows larger? Therefore they derive intelligence from the same source from which they derive life, possessing from their own infancy the distinctive properties both of living and of understanding.

For I see even a vine that is still tender and immature, yet already understands its work and desires to cling to something upon which it may lean [Note: [Col. 0681C] Pamelius and Rhenanus read *innixa*, with the same sense.] and fasten itself so as to grow. Indeed, without waiting for instruction from the farmer, without a reed, [Note: [Col. 0681D] “Without a forked support” (*cervus*). I also see ivies, however young you please, immediately striving upward and, with no one leading them, hanging aloft, because they would rather advance over walls as a woven woodland than be trampled on the ground—choosing an injury voluntarily. Conversely, see how those [Note: [Col. 0681D] “For which it goes badly with a building.” Similarly, in *On the Pallium*: “It goes well from heaven.” RIG.] for which a building is harmful withdraw from it as they grow! How they flee! [Note: [Col. 0681C] Rhenanus, Pamelius, and Semler read “a refuge.”] You would perceive that the branches [Note: [Col. 0681C] Another reading is “for the branches”; Junius reads “directed.”] are directed elsewhere [Note: [Col. 0682C] The Paris edition reads the singular “directed.”], and from their separation from the wall you would understand the animation of the tree. [Note: [Col. 0681D] “It is content with smallness.” The Codex Agobardinus reads: “Self-restraint is small...” RIG.] It is content [Note: [Col. 0682C] Pamelius, Rhenanus, and Semler read “content with its own.”] with the smallness it learned from the beginning as a most provident plant, [Col. 0681C] fearing [Note: [Col. 0682C] Pamelius, Rhenanus, and Semler read “of one fearing.”] even collapse.

Why should I not argue for these forms of wisdom and knowledge in trees? Let them live, as the philosophers wish; let them understand, as the philosophers do not wish. Let even the infancy of a piece of wood possess understanding [Note: [Col. 0682C] Pamelius and Rhenanus read “Let them also understand the infancy of a piece of wood.”]—how much more the infancy of a human being, whose soul, like a kind of shoot drawn into propagation from the stock of Adam and entrusted to the female’s generative furrows with all its equipment, will bud forth in understanding as well as in sense?

I am lying if the infant, as soon as [Note: [Col. 0682C] “It greeted life with a wail.” Pliny treats these sufficiently unhappy beginnings and first conditions of human beings in the preface to Book VII. Thus that first cry is forced out by the first impulses both of the senses and of understanding. More than this: [Note: [Col. 0682D] “From its foresight of a tearful life.” What is more deserving of tears than that life which begins in wretched wailing, pain, hunger, thirst, and a thousand other hardships; which is spent amid the same and still greater hardships; and which has death as its end? LE PR.] some interpret that plaintive cry as an omen of the hardships foreseen in a tearful life, so that the infant must be regarded as possessing foreknowledge from the very entrance of birth, much less merely understanding. [Note: [Col. 0682C] Pamelius, Rhenanus, and Semler insert “not indeed.”]

Thereafter it tests its mother by scent, examines its nurse by scent, and recognizes its attendant by scent, fleeing unfamiliar breasts, rejecting unknown beds, and seeking no one except through familiarity. From where does it derive its judgment of novelty and habit if it possesses no intelligence? From where its capacity to be offended and soothed if it possesses no understanding? It would be sufficiently astonishing if infancy were naturally ensouled (*animosa*) while lacking a mind (*animus*), and naturally affectionate while lacking understanding. Yet Christ, by drawing praise from the mouths of nursing infants and little children, did not declare either childhood or infancy dull-witted: the former (Matt. 21), coming forward with its acclaim, was able to offer testimony to him; the latter (Matt. 2), slaughtered for his sake, certainly felt the violence.

## CHAPTER XX

So too says [Note: [Col. 0682D] “Seneca, often one of ours.” Because he frequently thinks in accordance with Christians. ## CHAPTER XXI

Therefore, if the nature of the soul was uniform from the beginning in Adam, before so many different dispositions arose, it is not made multiform [Note: [Col. 0684C] Rhenanus and Pamelius add “because it is uniform.”] by those many dispositions. Nor, then, is it [Note: [Col. 0684C] Rigaltius and the Venetian edition read “nor in what respect”; the Codex Agobardinus reads “nor fourfold.”] threefold, so that the Valentinian [Note: [Col. 0684D] “The Valentinian trinity.” This is a trinity of the human being, not of God; for those heretics made the human being spiritual, animal or fleshly, and earthly or material. LE PR.] trinity may still be demolished—a trinity which is itself not recognized in Adam.

For what was spiritual in him? If it was because he prophesied that great mystery concerning Christ and the Church (Gen. 2): “This is bone from my bones and flesh from my flesh; she shall be called woman. Therefore a man shall leave father and mother and shall cleave to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh”—this came upon him afterward, [Note: [Col. 0684D] “When God cast madness upon him.” Above: “He underwent the accident of spirit, for ecstasy fell upon him—the power of the Holy Spirit, which produces prophecy.” RIG.—“He cast madness upon him.” This comes from the Septuagint translators, who have ἔκστασιν (“ecstasy”) at Genesis 2. Thus the Author himself in chapter 11 called Adam’s sleep an ecstasy, and he means nothing else by “madness.” LAC.] when God cast madness upon him, the spiritual power in which prophecy consists.

If evil also appeared in him through the commission of transgression, this too must not be accounted natural, since he committed it at the serpent’s instigation (Gen. 3); it was no more natural than it was material, because we have already excluded the claim that it derived from matter. Therefore, if neither the spiritual nor what is called the material was properly present in him—for the material would have been present if the seed of evil [Note: [Col. 0684C] Semler and Oberthür read “even if.”] had come from matter—it remains that only one thing was in him, namely what is reckoned animal and natural; and this we maintain was simple and uniform in condition.

It plainly remains to ask whether that which is called natural should be believed capable of change. For the same people deny that nature is convertible, so that they may fix their trinity within the respective properties of its members: because a good tree does not bear bad fruit, nor a bad tree good fruit; and no one gathers figs from thorns or grapes from brambles. If this is so, therefore, God will be unable to raise up children of Abraham from stones (Matt. 3), or to make the offspring of vipers produce fruits of repentance; and the Apostle was mistaken when he wrote (Eph. 5), “You too were once darkness”; and (Eph. 2), “We too were once by nature children of wrath”; and (1 Cor. 6), “Such also were you, but you have been washed.”

But the holy sayings will never disagree. For a bad tree will not produce good fruit (Luke 6) if it is not grafted; and a good tree will produce bad fruit if it is not cultivated. Stones will become children of Abraham if they are formed in the faith of Abraham; and the offspring of vipers will produce the fruit of repentance if they spit out the poisons of wickedness. This will be the power of divine grace, which is certainly more powerful than nature and has within us, subject to itself, the free power of choice called αὐτεξούσιον (*autexousion*, “self-determination”). Since this power is itself both natural and mutable, in whatever direction it is turned, nature is converted. [Note: [Col. 0685C] Rhenanus and Semler add “and.”] [Note: [Col. 0685C] “That τὸ αὐτεξούσιον is present within us.” Thus free choice is named among the Fathers and Philosophers. Nazianzen calls the human being τῷ αὐτεξουσίῳ τιμηθείς, “honored with free choice.” LAC.]

We have already shown both Marcion and Hermogenes that τὸ αὐτεξούσιον (*to autexousion*, “the power of self-determination”) is naturally present within us. What, then, [Note: [Col. 0685C] Semler and Rhenanus omit “also.”] if the condition of nature must also be defined in such a way that a twofold classification is established: of things born and unborn, made and unmade? Accordingly, whatever is established as born and made will possess a nature capable of alteration, for it will be able both to be reborn and to be remade. But the unborn and unmade will remain immovable. Since this belongs to God alone—as alone unborn and unmade, and for that reason immortal and unchangeable—it is settled that the nature of all other things, as born and made, is convertible and mutable. Thus, even if a trinity were to be ascribed to the soul, it would be attributed to a change arising from accident, not to the constitution of nature.

## CHAPTER XXII

## CHAPTER XXIII

Some believe that they have descended from heaven, with as much conviction as they confidently promise that they will return there. Thus Saturninus, the disciple of the Simonian Menander, introduced the doctrine that the human being was made by angels. He asserted that at first their work, futile, feeble, and unstable, writhed on the earth like a worm because it lacked the strength to stand; that afterward, through the mercy of the supreme power, in whose image—though an image not fully perceived—it had rashly been constructed, it received a little spark of life, which awakened and raised it, endowed it with firmer vitality, and after life’s departure would carry it back to its original matrix.

Carpocrates likewise claims so much for himself from the higher realms that his disciples equate their own souls even with Christ, much less with the Apostles, and prefer them whenever they please. They suppose these souls were similarly conceived from a sublime power that despises the ruling powers of the world.

Apelles reports that souls were enticed by earthly foods from their supercelestial abodes by the fiery angel who is the God of Israel [Note: [Col. 0686C] Pamelius and Rhenanus read “of the Israelites.”] and our God, and that he thereafter fashioned sinful flesh around them.

LE PR.] through which they recognize the stories and [Note: [Col. 0686C] Perhaps “equally,” or “and those,” according to Latinius.] Milesian tales of their Aeons from the images of visible things.

I sincerely grieve [Note: [Col. 0686D] “That Plato became the provider of condiments for all heretics.” The Author often slanders ancient philosophy as though it were the storehouse and fountain of heresies, as in chapter 7 of *The Prescription*. For his too is the statement in the *Phaedo* that souls departing from here go there, and from there come here; likewise, in the *Timaeus*, that the offspring of God [Note: [Col. 0687C] Pamelius and Rhenanus read “sprouts” (*germina*).], having been entrusted with the generation of mortals, received the immortal beginning of the soul and congealed a mortal body around it; and then, that this world is the image of some other world. In order to commend all these things to belief [Note: [Col. 0687C] The Paris edition reads “he commends.”], he devised a new argument: [Note: [Col. 0687C] “And that the soul formerly associated with God in the higher realms.” I could find nothing else in Plato’s *Timaeus* pertinent here except the statement that, after the supreme God and Craftsman had established the universe, he divided the souls into a number equal to that of the stars, assigned each one to a particular star, placed them as though upon a vehicle, showed them the nature of the universe, and expounded the laws of fate to them; and that the first origin would be ordained alike for all, but that the souls, once sown into the instruments appropriate to each at their proper times—namely, bodies—were required to produce the living creature most devoted to divine worship. PAM.] namely, that the soul had formerly lived with God in the higher realms in communion with the Ideas; that it then crossed over from there to here; and that here it rehearses, from their exemplars, the things it knew before: μαθήσεις ἀναμνήσεις—that is, “acts of learning are acts of recollection.” [Note: [Col. 0687D] “That is, acts of learning are acts of recollection.” This passage was undoubtedly taken from Plato’s *Phaedo*, not from the *Timaeus*. For souls coming from there to here, he says, forget the things among which they previously existed and then, once instructed by these visible things, remember them. Since, therefore, Plato employs an argument of this kind to insinuate the doctrines that the heretics borrow from him, I shall have sufficiently beaten back the heretics if I demolish Plato’s argument.
