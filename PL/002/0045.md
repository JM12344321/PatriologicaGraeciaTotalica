“But Christ forbids divorce, saying: ‘Whoever dismisses his wife and marries another commits adultery; whoever marries a woman dismissed by her husband is likewise an adulterer.’ Thus, in this way too, he forbids divorce by making marriage to a repudiated woman unlawful. Moses, however, permits repudiation in Deuteronomy (Deut. 24:1): ‘If anyone takes a wife and lives with her, and it happens that she finds no favor with him because some indecent matter has been found in her, [Col. 0442A] he shall write a bill of repudiation, place it in her hand, and dismiss her from his house.’ Do you see the difference between the Law and the Gospel, between Moses and Christ?”

Certainly. For you have not also accepted that Gospel (Matt. 19:8) which belongs to the same truth and the same Christ, in which, while forbidding divorce, he resolves the very question concerning it: “Moses, because of the hardness of your hearts, commanded that a bill of repudiation be given; but from the beginning it was not so,” because, of course, he who had made male and female had said, “The two shall become one flesh” [Note: [Col. 0441D] “Into one flesh,” Fran.]; “therefore, shall man separate what God has joined?” [Note: [Col. 0441D] “Man does not separate,” Fran.] By this answer he both safeguarded Moses’ ordinance as his own and restored the Creator’s institution as the Creator’s Christ.

But since you must be refuted from those things which you have accepted, I shall meet you on that ground, as though he were my Christ. Did not he himself, while forbidding [Col. 0442B] divorce and yet presenting [Note: [Col. 0441D] “Attesting,” Lat.] as Father the one who joined male and female, excuse rather than destroy Moses’ ordinance? But suppose this Christ is yours, teaching what is contrary to Moses and the Creator, on condition that, if I do not show that he teaches nothing contrary, he shall be mine.

For I say that on this occasion he made the prohibition of divorce conditional: namely, if someone dismisses his wife in order to marry another. “Whoever dismisses,” he says, “his wife and marries another has committed adultery; and whoever marries a woman dismissed by her husband is likewise an adulterer”—surely for the same reason [Note: [Col. 0442D] Oberth. adds “dismissed.”] that she may not be dismissed, namely, so that another may be married. For one who marries a woman unlawfully dismissed, treating her as though she were no longer married, is an adulterer. The marriage remains in force because it was not properly dissolved. To marry while a marriage remains in force is adultery. Thus, if he conditionally forbade the dismissal of a wife, he did not forbid it altogether; [Col. 0442C] [Note: [Col. 0442D] “And what he did not forbid altogether, he permitted. It is otherwise where the cause ceases.” The exemplar reads: “He permitted altogether. Another cause submitted ceases.” From the traces of this reading, we think that the passage should be emended as follows: “And what he did not forbid altogether, he permitted in other cases, where the cause for which he prohibited it ceases.” RIG.] and what he did not forbid altogether, he permitted in other cases, where the cause for which he prohibited it ceases.

Neither, therefore, does he teach what is contrary to Moses, whose commandment he preserves in a certain case—I do not yet say confirms. Or, if you deny that Christ permits divorce under any circumstances, why do you dissolve marriages, neither joining male and female nor admitting those joined elsewhere to the sacrament of baptism and the Eucharist unless they have conspired together [Note: [Col. 0442D] Against the fruit of marriage. What he called “the fruit of wedlock” in chapter 23 means the procreation of children. We have discussed these matters elsewhere. LE PR.] against the fruit of marriage, as though against the Creator himself?

Certainly, what will [Note: [Col. 0442D] “Will do,” Wouwer codex.] a husband among you do if his wife has committed adultery? Will he keep her? Yet you know [Note: [Col. 0442D] Rhen. omits “you know.”] that even your apostle does not permit the members of Christ to be joined to a prostitute. Christ, therefore, is also the defender [Note: [Col. 0442D] The justice of divorce. Here he appears to favor the opinion of those who maintain that, even if divorce were evil in itself, nevertheless, once it was permitted through Moses, it became just and lawful. But if what follows is carefully examined, it becomes clear that it was permitted only “if an indecent matter has been found in the woman.” This is also supported by the prophecy of Malachi 2, which he soon cites according to the Septuagint: “You shall not dismiss the wife of your youth.” To this may be added what the prophet continues there: “But if, hating her, you dismiss her, says the Lord God of Israel, impiety will cover your thoughts.” PAM.] of the justice of divorce.

From this point onward [Col. 0443A] Moses is confirmed by him, since Moses forbids repudiation on the same ground as Christ, unless an indecent matter has been found in the woman. For in the Gospel of Matthew as well (Matt. 5:32), he says: “Whoever dismisses his wife, except on the ground of adultery, causes her to commit adultery”; and thus [Note: [Col. 0443D] Seml. omits “thus”; “likewise thus,” Par., Fran.] the man who marries a woman dismissed by her husband is also accounted an adulterer.

Apart, however, from the ground of adultery, not even the Creator separates what he himself has joined, since the same Moses elsewhere ordains (Deut. 22:28) that the man who [Note: [Col. 0443D] Who had brought about marriage through forcible intercourse. He means violence or violation, in which either the adulterer or the unchaste man “uses force against the female” (*biázetai tēn thēleian*, βιάζεται τὴν θηλεῖαν). LE PR.] brought about marriage through forcible intercourse could never dismiss his wife. If a marriage compelled by violence is to remain in force, how much more one entered voluntarily by agreement? So too by the authority of prophecy [Note: [Col. 0443D] “Of the prophet,” Lat.] (Mal. 2:15): “Do not dismiss the wife of your youth.”

You therefore have Christ spontaneously following [Col. 0443B] the Creator’s footsteps everywhere, both in permitting repudiation and in forbidding it. You also have him as the guardian of marriage in whichever direction you please: he does not wish marriages to be separated, and therefore forbids repudiation; nor does he wish them to be maintained with a stain, and therefore permits divorce in that case. Be ashamed that you do not join those whom even your Christ has joined. Be ashamed also that you separate them without the ground upon which even your Christ wished them to be separated.

I must now also show from what source the Lord derived this pronouncement and to what end he directed it. It will thereby be more fully established that, by the proposition concerning repudiation introduced at this point, he was not aiming to destroy Moses. For it was not introduced abruptly, since it has its root in the same mention of John.

John had rebuked Herod for having taken, contrary to the Law, the wife of his deceased brother, who had a daughter by her. [Col. 0443C] The Law permitted—indeed, commanded—this in no other case (Deut. 25) [Note: [Col. 0444D] Than if the brother died childless. In Greek this is *ápais* (ἄπαις), “childless.” LE PR.] than when a brother had died childless, so that offspring might be raised up for him by his brother and from his own stock. John had therefore been cast into prison and was later killed by that same Herod [Note: [Col. 0443D] Oberth. omits “Herod.”].

Accordingly, once mention had been made of John, the Lord—certainly following out the consequence of John’s fate—hurled against Herod figures of unlawful marriages and adultery [Note: [Col. 0443D] “A figure,” Pam.; “flashes,” Ciaccon.]. He pronounced even the man who married a woman dismissed by her husband an adulterer, thereby weighing down Herod’s impiety all the more, since Herod had married a woman dismissed by her husband no less by death than by repudiation. He had done so although his brother had a daughter by her, and therefore unlawfully on that ground alone; he acted at the prompting of lust, not of the Law, and for that reason had also killed the prophet who defended the Law.

My discussion of this point will also assist me with [Note: [Col. 0443D] Seml. inserts “with.”] the following argument concerning the rich man suffering in the underworld and the poor man resting in Abraham’s bosom. For that account too, so far as the surface of Scripture is concerned, is introduced abruptly; but so far as the intention of its meaning is concerned, it is itself connected with the mention of John’s mistreatment and with the stigmatizing [Note: [Col. 0443D] “Stigmatized,” Pam.] of the ill-deserving Herod [Note: [Col. 0444D] “Married,” Par.; “provoked,” Jun.]. It depicts the fate of each: Herod’s torments and John’s refreshment. Thus Herod might already hear: [Note: [Col. 0444D] “They have Moses and the Prophets there.” The author added “there” for greater emphasis—namely, in the synagogue of the Jews—for it is not read in the Greek, Syriac, or Latin, nor in Epiphanius, who in *Refutation* 46 likewise turns this passage against Marcion, the opponent of Moses and the Prophets. PAM.] “They have Moses and the Prophets there; let them hear them.”

But Marcion forces the passage in another direction. He holds, namely, [Note: [Col. 0444D] Oberth. inserts “that.”] that both forms of the Creator’s recompense—whether torment or refreshment—are appointed in the underworld [Col. 0444B] for those who have obeyed the Law and the Prophets, but assigns [Note: [Col. 0444D] “May assign,” Seml.] a heavenly bosom and harbor to his Christ and his god.

We shall answer him, with this very Scripture refuting his blindness, for it distinguishes Abraham’s bosom, in which the poor man rests, from the underworld [Note: [Col. 0444D] “To the underworld,” Oberth.]. The underworld is one thing, as I understand it, and Abraham’s bosom another. [Note: [Col. 0444D] For he says that a great depth, a *chásma* (χάσμα), lies between those regions; others have called it chaos. RIG.] For he says that a great depth lies between [Note: [Col. 0444D] “Between,” Seml.] those regions and prevents passage in either direction. Nor would the rich man have lifted up his eyes, and that from far away, unless he were looking upward, across the immense distance between height and depth.

It is therefore apparent to every knowledgeable person who has ever heard of the Elysian regions that there is a definite locality called Abraham’s bosom, intended to receive the souls of his children, including those from the nations. For he is the father of many nations, which are to be enrolled in Abraham’s line [Col. 0444C] through the same faith by which Abraham believed God, while under neither the yoke of the Law nor the sign of circumcision.

That region, therefore—I mean Abraham’s bosom—although not heavenly, is nevertheless higher than the underworld. It will in the meantime provide refreshment for the souls of the righteous, until the consummation of all things brings the resurrection of all to completion with the fullness of their reward. Then the heavenly promise that Marcion claims for his own [Note: [Col. 0444D] Ciaccon. adds “Christ.”] will appear, as though it had not been proclaimed by the Creator.

For this promise Christ builds his ascent into heaven, according to Amos (Amos 9:6)—for his own, of course—where the eternal place is, concerning which Isaiah says (Isa. 33:14): “Who will announce to you the eternal place?” Who, indeed, but Christ, “walking in righteousness, speaking the straight way, hating injustice and iniquity”? If, then, an eternal place is promised and an ascent into heaven is built by the Creator, who also promises that Abraham’s offspring will be like the stars of heaven—surely on account of the heavenly promise—then, without prejudice to that promise, [Note: [Col. 0445D] Why it may not be called Abraham’s bosom. We have already encountered this verb frequently above. The meaning here is: “Why can it not be called Abraham’s bosom, a temple,” etc. So too in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*: “the Holy Land, Paradise, which may also be called the land of the fathers.” RHEN.] why may not some temporary receptacle for the souls of the faithful be called Abraham’s bosom? There the image of the future is already outlined, and [Note: [Col. 0446D] A certain white robe of expectation for both judgments. “White robe” signifies hope and expectation. It was the clothing of candidates, as we noted above. RHEN.] a certain white-robed expectation [Note: [Col. 0445D] “And,” Lat.] of each judgment is held out in prospect.

It also admonishes you heretics, while you are alive, to hear Moses and the Prophets, who proclaim one God, the Creator, and one Christ who is his, and who declare that both judgments—of punishment and of eternal salvation—are vested in the one God, who kills and makes alive.

“But,” he says, “our God’s admonition from heaven did not command Moses and the Prophets to be heard, but Christ: ‘Hear him.’” Quite rightly. For by then the apostles had already heard Moses and the Prophets sufficiently: they had followed Christ by believing Moses and the Prophets. Peter could not have begun [Note: [Col. 0445D] “Could not have begun, Peter,” Ciaccon.] to say, “You are the Christ,” before he had heard and believed Moses and the Prophets, by whom alone Christ was as yet being announced.

Their faith, therefore, had deserved to be confirmed also by a heavenly voice commanding them to hear the one whom they had accordingly acknowledged: the one “evangelizing peace, evangelizing good things,” announcing the eternal place and building for them his ascent into heaven (Isa. 52:7; 33:14; Amos 9:6).

In the underworld, however, it was said of those people, [Col. 0445C] “They have Moses and the Prophets there; let them hear them” (Luke 16:29), who did not believe—or who did not fully believe—that after death the punishments of pride in riches and vaunting in pleasures had been announced by Moses and the Prophets, and decreed by that God who (Ps. 113:6) casts rulers down from their thrones and raises the needy from dunghills [Note: [Col. 0445D] “Dungheaps,” Pam.]. Thus, since the difference between the two pronouncements is appropriate to the Creator, no separation between deities is to be established, but only between the subjects themselves.

## CHAPTER XXXV

Turning in the same passage to his disciples (Luke 17), he pronounces woe upon the author of scandals: it would have been better [Note: [Col. 0445D] “Would have been,” Par.] for him if he had not been born, or if, with a millstone tied around his neck, he had been cast into the deep, than if he had scandalized one of those little ones—his disciples, of course. [Col. 0445D] Consider what punishment he threatens that man [Note: [Col. 0445D] “That man,” Lat.]. For no other will avenge the scandal given to his disciples.

Recognize him, therefore, as Judge as well, pronouncing this out of that same feeling of care for his own with which the Creator had previously said [Col. 0446A] (Zech. 2:8): “Whoever touches you touches, as it were, the pupil of my eye.”

The same sentiment belongs to the same God: he commands that a sinning brother be rebuked. Whoever does not do so has surely sinned, whether because in hatred he wishes his brother to persist in sin, or because, through partiality, he spares him. He has Leviticus (Lev. 19:17): “You shall not hate your brother in your heart; with rebuke you shall rebuke [Note: [Col. 0445D] “You shall not rebuke,” Sem.; “it is not absent,” Pam.] your neighbor”—and surely your brother as well—“and you shall not incur sin because of him.”

Nor is it surprising if he teaches in this way, since he forbids you to disregard even your brother’s livestock if you find them straying on the road (Exod. 23:4), instead requiring you to bring them back to your brother [Note: [Col. 0446D] Oberth. omits “your”]; much less may you disregard your brother himself and fail to restore him to himself.

He also commands you to grant forgiveness to a brother who sins against you, even seven times. Too little, certainly. For there is more with the Creator, who sets no limit, declaring without qualification (Lev. 19:18): [Col. 0446B] “Do not remember your brother’s malice.” Nor [Note: [Col. 0446D] “Do not,” Venet.] does he command that you grant forgiveness only to one who asks for it, but also to one who does not ask. For he does not wish you merely to pardon the offense, but to forget it.

It will be our part to understand how much interpretation the law concerning lepers (Lev. 13–14) requires with regard to the manifestations of the disease itself and the high priest’s inspection of them. Marcion’s part is to object to the Law’s tediousness, so that here too he may affirm that Christ was its rival: one who anticipated the Law’s solemn rites even in the healing of the ten lepers (Luke 17), whom, after merely commanding them to go and show themselves to the priests, he cleansed on the way, now without a touch and without a word, by silent power and sheer will.

This is as though, once Christ has been proclaimed as the healer of illnesses and diseases and proved by his effects, it were necessary to reopen the question because of the particular manner of his healings; or as though the Creator in Christ must be measured against the Law whenever he accomplished something in a manner different from that prescribed by the Law. Yet the Lord naturally works in one manner [Col. 0446C] through himself or through the Son, and in another through his servants the prophets, who were proofs of his very great [Note: [Col. 0446D] “Especially,” Rig.] strength and power. Since his own works are brighter and more powerful, it is proper that they should differ from works performed through deputies. But matters of this kind have already been discussed elsewhere, in the preceding example.

Now even if he had declared beforehand that there were many lepers in Israel in the days of the prophet Elisha, and none of them was cleansed except Naaman the Syrian (Luke 4:27), the number will certainly [Note: [Col. 0446D] Seml. adds “also.”] not establish a difference between the gods, to the destruction of the Creator who healed one and the exaltation of the god who cleansed ten. For who could doubt that more could have been healed by the one who had healed a single man than by the one who healed ten but had never previously healed even one?

Rather, by this pronouncement above all [Col. 0446D] he strikes at Israel’s distrust or pride: although there were many lepers there and a prophet was available—even after a proof of divine power had been given—no one had hastened to the God who worked through the prophets.

Therefore, [Col. 0447A] because he himself was the authentic high priest of God the Father, he inspected them according to the mystery of the Law, which signified that Christ is the true judge and remover of human stains. Yet he also commanded what belonged to the Law’s public observance: “Go, show yourselves to the priests.” Why, if he was going to cleanse them beforehand? Was it as a mocker of the Law, so that, after healing them on their way, he might show that the Law, together with its priests, amounted to nothing? Let anyone to whom Christ seems so conceited judge that for himself. More worthy interpretations, and ones more just to faith, must be given.

He therefore healed them because, having been ordered in accordance with the Law to go to the priests, they had obeyed. For it is not credible that observers of the Law should have earned healing from the Law’s destroyer. But why did he give no such command to the former leper? Because Elisha gave no such command to Naaman the Syrian either; yet Elisha did not for that reason cease to belong to the Creator.

I have answered sufficiently; but one who believes also understands something more profound. Learn, then, the reasons. The event took place in the region of Samaria, from which, at least, one of the lepers came. Samaria had broken away from Israel in the schism involving the ten [Note: [Col. 0447D] “Nine,” Oberth.] tribes, which Jeroboam, after they had been torn away through the prophet Ahijah, had settled in Samaria (1 Kings 11 and 16).

Moreover, the Samaritans had always taken pride in their mountains and the wells of their ancestors, as did the Samaritan woman in the Gospel of John (John 4), in her conversation with the Lord at the well: “Surely [Note: [Col. 0447D] “Not,” Seml.] you are not greater,” and so forth; and again: “Our ancestors worshiped on this mountain, and you say that one must worship at Jerusalem.”

Accordingly, he who had also said through Amos [Note: [Col. 0447D] “Isaiah,” Rhen.] (Amos 6:1), “Woe to those [Note: [Col. 0447D] Who trusted in Mount Samaria. He touched above on the history of that schism, in which Jeroboam separated ten tribes from the others and led them to Samaria, where he was made king. The remaining two, namely Judah and Benjamin, remained under Rehoboam. This is recounted in 1 Kings. LE PR.] who trust [Note: [Col. 0447D] “Who are trusting,” Venet.] in Mount Samaria,” now, deeming Samaria itself worthy of restoration as well, deliberately commands them to show themselves to the priests—surely those who existed nowhere except where the Temple was. He thus subjects the Samaritan to the Jew, because the salvation of both the Israelite and the Samaritan [Note: [Col. 0447D] “Samaritan woman,” Venet.] comes from the Jews.

For the whole promise to the tribe of Judah was Christ, so that they might know that in Jerusalem were both the priests and the Temple, and the mother-source of religion, and the fountain—not the well—of salvation. And therefore, when he saw that they had acknowledged that the Law must be discharged at Jerusalem [Note: [Col. 0447D] “They had acknowledged the Law,” Seml.], he healed them, since they were now to be justified by faith without the Law’s order.

Hence also, when he marveled at that one man out of the ten—the Samaritan alone [Note: [Col. 0448D] “Alone,” Wouwer.] released from obligation, mindful of divine grace—he did not command him to offer the gift prescribed by the Law. For he had already offered enough by giving glory to God, since the Lord also wished the Law to be interpreted in this way.

And yet to which God did the Samaritan give thanks, when not even the Israelite had yet learned of another God? To what other God than the one to whom all those previously healed by Christ had given thanks? Therefore he hears, “Your faith has saved you,” [Col. 0448A] because he had understood that he ought to make a true offering to Almighty God—namely, thanksgiving—at God’s true Temple and before his true high priest, Christ.

Nor can the Pharisees be thought to have questioned the Lord about the kingdom of some other God—asking when it would come—so long as no other God had been revealed by Christ. Neither can Christ be thought to have answered concerning any kingdom other than the one about which he was being questioned.

“The kingdom of God,” he says, “does not come with observation; nor do they say, ‘Behold, here,’ or ‘Behold, there.’ For behold, the kingdom of God is within you” (Luke 17:20–21). Who would not interpret “within you” as meaning “in your hand, within your power, if you hear and perform God’s commandment”?

But if God’s kingdom consists in his commandment, then set Moses over against it, [Note: [Col. 0448D] According to our antitheses. Marcion had compiled *Antitheses* in which he opposed the Law of Moses to the Gospel. Tertullian opposed other antitheses to them, in which he reconciled the old Law with the new. LE PR.] according to our antitheses, and the pronouncement is one and the same. “The commandment,” he says (Deut. 30:11–14), “is not too high, nor is it far from you. It is not in heaven, so that you should say, ‘Who will ascend [Note: [Col. 0448D] “Ascends,” Seml.] into heaven and bring [Note: [Col. 0448D] “Brings,” Oberth.] it down to us, so that, when we have heard it, we may perform it?’ Nor is it beyond the sea, so that you should say, ‘Who will cross the sea and take it for us, so that, when we have heard it, we may perform it?’ The word is near you, in your mouth, in your heart, and in your hands, that you may perform it.” This will be the meaning of: “Not here, nor there; for behold, the kingdom of God is within you.”

And lest heretical audacity argue that the Lord was answering them about the Creator’s kingdom, concerning which he was being questioned, and not about his own, what follows stands in its way. For when he says that the Son of Man must first suffer many things and be rejected before his coming, in which the kingdom will also be substantially revealed, he shows that the kingdom about which he had answered was his own—the kingdom that awaited his sufferings and rejection.

Moreover, since being rejected implies afterward being recognized, received, and exalted, he took [Note: [Col. 0448D] “Excerpted,” another reading.] even the word “rejected” itself from the passage in which both revelations of him were sung by David under the riddle of the stone (Ps. 118:22): the first dishonored, the second honored. “The stone,” he says, “which the builders rejected has become the head of the corner. This was done by the Lord.”

For it would be meaningless if we believed that God had foretold the dishonor or glory of just anyone, rather than [Note: [Col. 0448D] “Rather than that,” Seml.] thereby foreshadowing the same person whom he had also foreshadowed under the figures of the stone, the rock, and the mountain (Isa. 8:14) [Note: [Col. 0448D] “Had foreshadowed,” Oberth.].

But if he is speaking of his own coming, why does the good, gentle, and mild God compare it to the grim and dreadful days of Noah and Lot? Why does he admonish them to remember Lot’s wife, who did not despise the Creator’s command without punishment, unless he comes with judgment [Col. 0448D] to vindicate his own commandments? Even if he vindicates them as the Creator did, even if he judges me, he ought not to have fashioned my conduct through the Creator’s examples while destroying the Creator, lest it appear to be the Creator who fashions my conduct.

But if here too he is speaking not of his own coming, but of the Jewish Christ’s, let us wait even now to see whether he ever foretells anything about his own; meanwhile, let us believe him to be the one whom he proclaims everywhere.

## CHAPTER XXXVI

For when he also commands perseverance and urgency in prayer (Luke 18), he sets forth the parable of the judge who was compelled to hear the widow by the urgency and persistence of her appeals. He therefore shows that God the Judge must be prayed to, not himself—if he himself is not the Judge. But he added that God would execute vengeance for his elect. If, therefore, he will himself be the Judge, he is also the Avenger. Thus he proved the Creator to be the better God, since he shows him as the avenger of his elect, who cry to him day and night.

And yet, when he introduces the Creator’s Temple [Col. 0449B] and describes two men worshiping with different dispositions—the Pharisee in pride and the tax collector in humility—and therefore says that the one went down rejected and the other justified (Luke 18), he is teaching by what discipline one must pray. Here too he consequently appoints as the object of prayer the one from whom they would receive the corresponding outcome of that discipline of prayer: either the rejection of pride or the justification of humility.

In Christ’s teaching I find neither temple, worshipers, nor judgment belonging to another God, but only those of the Creator. [Note: [Col. 0449D] He commands that the Creator be worshiped in humility, etc. He seems to me to allude to Christ’s saying: “Everyone who humbles himself will be raised, and whoever exalts himself will be humbled”; or to the other saying in Mary’s canticle: “He has cast the powerful down from their seat” (for this is what it means to destroy) “and has exalted” (or raised) “the humble.” PAM.] He commands the Creator to be worshiped in humility, as the one who raises the humble; not in pride, as the destroyer of the proud. What other being does he show me must be worshiped? By what discipline? With what hope? No one, I think.

For we have also proved that the prayer he taught is appropriate to the Creator. It would be another matter if, as the best God, good without prompting, he did not also wish to be worshiped. But “who is best,” [Col. 0449C] he says, “except the one God?” He did not mean that, out of two gods, he had shown one to be best, but that the one and only God is best, who is uniquely best because he alone is God.

And he is certainly best who rains upon the just and unjust and causes his sun to rise upon the good and evil, sustaining, nourishing, and helping even the Marcionites.

Finally, when a certain man asked him, “Best Teacher, what must I do to possess eternal life?” he demanded to know whether he knew—that is, practiced—the Creator’s commandments, in order to testify that everlasting life is acquired through the Creator’s commandments.

When the man affirmed that he had observed all the principal commandments from his youth, Christ said: “One thing is lacking to you. Sell everything you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow me.”

Come now, Marcion, and all his fellow-wretches [Col. 0449D] and fellow-objects-of-pity among the heretics: what will you dare to say? [Col. 0450A] Did Christ annul the preceding commandments—not to kill, not to commit adultery, not to steal, not to bear false witness, and to love father and mother? Or did he both preserve those commandments and add what was lacking? Yet even this command to make gifts to the needy is diffused everywhere throughout the Law and the Prophets, so that this most boastful observer of the commandments might be exposed as one who held money far dearer.

This declaration in the Gospel therefore also remains secure (Matt. 5:17): “I did not come to dissolve the Law and the Prophets, but rather to fulfill them.” At the same time he freed all the other points from doubt, making clear that the name of God and of the Best, eternal life, treasure in heaven, and Christ himself all belong to one and the same being; for by supplementing his commandments, Christ both preserved and enlarged them.

He must also be recognized here in accordance with Micah, who says (Mic. 6:8): [Col. 0450B] “Has a man announced to you what is good, or what the Lord requires from you, other than to execute judgment, love mercy, and be ready to follow the Lord your God?”

Christ is indeed the man announcing what is good: knowledge of the Law—“You know,” he says, “the commandments”; the execution of judgment—“Sell,” he says, “what you possess”; the love [Note: [Col. 0449D] “Love,” Seml.] of mercy—“And give,” he says, “to the needy”; readiness to go [Note: [Col. 0449D] “If you are ready, he says, to go,” Oberth.] with God [Note: [Col. 0450D] “With the Lord,” Seml.]—“And come,” he says, “follow me.”

From the beginning the Jewish nation had been so carefully distinguished into tribes, peoples, families, and households that scarcely anyone’s lineage could have been unknown, [Note: [Col. 0449D] Or from the recent Augustan census-records, perhaps still extant at that time. “At that time” means when the blind man said, “Jesus, son of David.” “Extant” refers to the census registers; for, as we noted above, censuses appear to have been conducted more than once under Augustus. Septimius himself, in the book *Against the Jews*, seems uncertain whether Christ was entered in the census immediately upon his birth. RIG.] or perhaps from the recent Augustan census-records, which were still extant at that time.

Yet Marcion’s Jesus, who appeared to be a man, could not have been doubted to have been born; for precisely because he had not been born, he could have had no publicly recorded identification of his lineage. [Note: [Col. 0450D] But he had to be reckoned as some one individual among those, etc. *Unus aliqui* is used for *aliquis*, as at the beginning of the following book, *unus aliqui discipulus*, “some one disciple.” The meaning is: “He will be a son of the earth, or just anyone from the crowd, one of the many,” what the Greeks call *ek tou pollou dēmou, kai tōn pollōn heis tōn tychontōn* (ἐκ τοῦ πολλοῦ δὴμὸυ, καὶ τῶν πολλῶν εἐς τῶν τυχόντῶν), “one chance individual from the common people and the multitude.” RHEN.] Rather, he would have had to be reckoned as some one individual among those who were regarded as obscure for one reason or another.

When, therefore, the blind man heard him passing by, why did he cry out, “Jesus, son of David, have mercy on me,” unless Jesus was commonly reckoned, and not without reason, to be the son of David—that is, a member of David’s family—through his mother and brothers, who had once certainly been announced to Jesus by people personally acquainted with them?

But those walking ahead rebuked the blind man and told him to be silent. Quite rightly, because he was shouting—not because he was lying about the son of David. Otherwise, show that those who rebuked him knew that Jesus was not the son of David, so that we may believe that this was why they enjoined silence upon the blind man.

But even if you were to prove it, it would more readily be presumed [Note: [Col. 0450D] “Would presume,” Rhen.] that they were ignorant than that the Lord could have tolerated a false proclamation about himself. Yet the Lord is patient, though not one who confirms error; indeed, he is the revealer of the Creator. Thus he would not first have dispelled the cloud over that man’s blindness without dispelling his error about himself. [Col. 0450D] On the contrary, lest you bring his patience into disrepute, [Col. 0451A] or [Note: [Col. 0451D] “Lest,” Ciaccon.] attribute to him any reason for dissimulation, or deny that he is the son of David, he most plainly confirmed the blind man’s proclamation, both by rewarding him with healing and by bearing testimony to his faith. “Your faith,” he says, “has saved you.” What would you have the blind man believed? That Jesus had descended from another [Note: [Col. 0451D] “That,” Oberth.] God to overthrow [Note: [Col. 0451D] “To the love of,” Seml.] the Creator? To destroy the Law and the Prophets? That he was not the one destined to come from the root of Jesse and the fruit of David’s loins (Ps. 132:11), who was also to reward the blind? But I do not think that there were yet blind men at that time of the same sort as Marcion, so that this could have been that blind man’s faith when he believed at the cry, “Jesus, son of David.” He who both knew himself to be this and wished to be known [Note: [Col. 0451D] “Known by all,” Oberth.; “known by men,” another reading; “by all” is absent from Par.] by everyone granted outward sight as well to the man’s faith—a faith that already saw better and possessed the true light—[Col. 0451B] so that we too might learn both the rule and the reward of faith. Whoever wishes to see Jesus must believe that he is the son of David through the virgin’s census-record. Whoever does not so believe will not hear from him, “Your faith has saved you.” He will thus remain blind, falling into the antithesis, while the antithesis itself also falls. For so the blind are accustomed to lead the blind.

“For if on one occasion (2 Sam. 5), when David was recovering Zion, the blind resisted him and gave offense by preventing his admission”—thereby prefiguring the likewise blind people who would one day refuse admission to Christ, the son of David—“Christ therefore, conversely, assisted a blind man so that by this he might show that he was not the son of David; and, because of their difference in disposition, he was kind to the blind whom David had ordered to be struck. And why did he say that he had granted this on account of faith—and false faith at that?” On the contrary, the son of David answers this objection too: the antithesis must be beaten back on its own ground. [Col. 0451C] For those who had offended David were blind, whereas now a man of the same flesh [Note: [Col. 0451D] “Disfigured,” Par., Fran.] had presented himself as a suppliant to the son of David. Therefore the son of David, placated because the man had in some sense made satisfaction to him, restored his sight, together with testimony to the faith by which he had believed this very thing: that he must entreat the son of David. And yet I think it was men’s audacity that had offended David, not their physical infirmity.
