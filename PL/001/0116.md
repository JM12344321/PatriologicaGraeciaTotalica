## ARTICLE IV. The fictitious threefold class of gods devised by Varro is refuted: physical or natural; mythical or fabulous; and national, or civil and popular

[Col. 1101D]

In order to eradicate the pagans’ errors concerning their gods, Tertullian, in the second book of his *To the Nations* (Tertullian, Book II, *To the Nations*, chap. 1 ff., p. 64 ff.), refutes everything that Varro had taught about them in the sixteen books *On Divine Matters* cited by Augustine (Augustine, Book IV, *City of God*, chap. 2, p. 147). He thought that Varro should be refuted by name because he was regarded as the most acute and learned of all writers, as we have shown in the appropriate place (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, final chap., art. 3).

Varro had distinguished a threefold pagan theology, [Col. 1102A] or class of gods. The first was physical or natural, which the pagan philosophers attempted in vain to establish in their schools and in almost innumerable treatises. For these are Varro’s words as quoted by Augustine:

> The philosophers have left many books in which this is discussed: who the gods are, where they are, of what kind and character, from what time they have existed, whether they have existed from eternity, whether they are made of fire, as Heraclitus believes; of numbers, as Pythagoras holds; or of atoms, as Epicurus holds—and likewise other things which ears can more readily endure within the walls of a school than outside in the forum.

(Augustine, Book VI, *City of God*, chap. 5, p. 150.)

But these disagreements and controversies among the philosophers concerning the gods demonstrate with the greatest clarity that their opinions concerning their gods were utterly false and absurd.

[Col. 1102B] Varro called the second class of gods mythical or fabulous. “For *mythical*,” Augustine says, “is derived from fables, since [non-Latin text omitted] in Greek means a fable” (*Ibid.*). This class, however, had been invented by poets who celebrated the gods’ births, rapes, adulteries, and every other abominable deed. Nevertheless, Varro himself, as Augustine again testifies (Augustine, Book IV, chap. 32, p. 112), openly admitted that the ancestors, or ancient Romans, believed those who proclaimed the gods’ horrifying crimes and marriages, because the mass of the uneducated populace was more inclined toward these poets than toward the natural philosophers. Who, however, would not detest the very great impiety of both parties? For the peoples seriously worshipped these gods, however infamous and ridiculous. Others, wiser than they, were incited by the wicked deceits [Col. 1102C] and frauds of demons either to mislead the unwary minds of the common people in religion itself, which is the highest of all concerns, or to allow them to be misled.

The third class of theology or gods is called civil, that is, belonging to citizens and peoples: namely, those gods whom peoples had adopted for themselves according to their whim and caprice. Yet apart from the fact that this class of gods is plainly reducible to the second and fabulous class, as Augustine, whom we have already cited, teaches (Augustine, Book VI, chap. 7, p. 153), what could be more absurd than to believe in those gods whom a blind and insane populace imagined to have been enrolled among the gods after they had passed through the course of this mortal life in every kind of crime, as we have often said?

Our Tertullian [Col. 1102D] and, afterward, Augustine utterly overthrow that threefold class of gods by arguments of various weight (Augustine, Book VI, chap. 5 ff., p. 150 ff.). It is therefore unnecessary for us now to explain individually the matters examined and elucidated by so great a teacher (Augustine, Book IV, chap. 31 ff., and Book VIII, chap. 1 ff., p. 162 ff.). Moreover, if any difficulty arises in them, it has been smoothed away to the best of our ability in this and our other dissertations.

## CHAPTER XXVI. How clearly Tertullian shows from the pagans’ own literature and writings that all their gods are false

## ARTICLE I. How Tertullian demonstrates that the gods of the pagans are false from the literature through which they were trained for prudence and liberal duties, and were taught of the gods’ various battles with one another, Venus wounded, Mars long confined in chains, Jupiter freed from a monster, or weeping over Sarpedon’s fall, or rutting with desire for his sister; likewise the servitude of Apollo and Neptune, Aesculapius struck by lightning, and from the poets’ tragedies—particularly those of Lentulus and Hostilius—and other works ridiculing the Moon as male

[Col. 1103A]

Such is the nature of error that it does not remain consistent with itself for long, and even destroys itself precisely when it raises its head more boldly. It is therefore unsurprising that the pagans overthrew the divinity bestowed upon dead men in the very place where they should have established it most firmly. For nowhere should it have been explained more clearly and distinctly than in the literature by which [Col. 1103B] young men were trained, as Tertullian says, for prudence (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 14, p. 351), which, according to Cicero, consists in knowledge of things good and bad and of things neither good nor bad; and for liberal duties (Cicero, Book III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 245, line 15), which, Terence says, it is altogether proper for a freeborn and well-bred man to know (Terence, *Eunuchus*, act III, scene 2, v. 24). Yet in this literature, as our Author puts it, what mockeries do I find by which they brought the divinity of their gods into disrepute?

If anyone asks what this literature and these compositions are, we shall reply that the poems of Homer are designated by that name. For the matters which our Author relates in the same passage were drawn from that poet. But if anyone wishes to have this point confirmed by some authority, behold, [Col. 1103C] Minucius Felix is at hand. After reviewing those various mockeries of the gods from the poems of Homer, whom he calls by name, he continues as follows:

> By these and similar inventions and more pleasing falsehoods, the minds of boys are corrupted; clinging to the same fables, they grow up until they reach the strength of mature age, and miserably grow old in the same opinions.

We have, however, attempted to explain these matters more fully in the preceding dissertation on this book of Minucius (*Dissertation on Minucius*, chap. 17, art. 4).

Let us now consider, if you please, how Tertullian proves these points:

> I shall begin with your own … beginning, its source and the whole expanse; the more honor you add to him, the more you take away from your gods by magnifying the man who made sport of them.

(Tertullian, Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 575.)

These words are written in this form [Col. 1103D] in the Agobard manuscript and in Gothofredus’s edition; Gothofredus thinks that the missing words there should be supplied as follows: “I shall begin with your own study, from which comes all the material and the whole expanse.” Rigaltius, however, without giving any reason and as though certain of the true reading of Tertullian, wished the text to read: “I shall begin with your Homer himself, his wave and the whole expanse.”

But this conjecture should have been supported by at least some argument. It nevertheless appears sufficiently probable to us that Tertullian, who is speaking of pagan literature, cites a particularly famous writer of theirs, to whom the more honor the pagans paid, the more they detracted from their gods by praising him. That writer is without doubt Homer: both because, as we have seen elsewhere, [Col. 1104A] he is the oldest of all writers, and because they valued his writings so highly that, as we were just saying, young men were educated through them for prudence and liberal duties. If, however, someone more discerning demonstrates that Tertullian meant another writer, we shall gladly hear him. Yet observe, I ask you, that Homer himself is called by name in these subsequent words of our Author:

> We still remember Homer. He, I think, is the man who treated divine majesty in terms of the human condition, imbuing the gods with human accidents and passions.

(*Ibid.*)

That is, representing them as corrupted by every human emotion and vice, even the foulest.

And indeed, that leader of the Greek poets everywhere defiled his books with these disgraces of the gods. Our Author, so as not to be too lengthy, selected several examples. The first is the episode in which Homer celebrates [Col. 1104B] the gods as having met in combat with one another on behalf of the Trojans and Achaeans, like matched pairs of gladiators (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 14, p. 351); or, as the book *To the Nations* puts it: “He set them against one another, divided by their partisan favor, in something like gladiatorial pairs” (Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 575). But who does not know how often Homer sang in the *Iliad* of these battles of the gods, and, as Tertullian continues (*Ibid.*), of Venus wounded by a human arrow, Mars nearly wasted away in chains, Jupiter at one moment freed through the efforts of some monster, at another weeping over Sarpedon’s fall, and now, after the most obscene manner of swine, rutting and burning with lust for his sister and wife Juno, while recounting to her his shameful love for his mistresses?

Here, however, it should be observed in passing that Rigaltius’s edition reads simply: “Venus wounded by a human arrow.” Our manuscripts and the older printed editions, however, read:

> Venus wounded because she wished to rescue her son Aeneas, nearly killed by the same Diomedes. Mars, etc.

[Col. 1104C] Rigaltius does not explain why he removed these words, but he doubtless judged that they had been added by some copyist for the sake of explanation and later intruded into the text. For they do not occur in the book *To the Nations*, where the same things are repeated. Nor is it easy to say why the words “by the same Diomedes” were added there, since he had not previously been mentioned. Yet because these words are present in very many of the oldest manuscript copies, they ought not to have been cut away without prior notice, as we said above (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, chap. 17, art. 4). Moreover, we have indicated and explained elsewhere (and in the *Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 12, arts. 1 and 3) [Col. 1104D] the passages of Homer which Tertullian appears, with good reason, to have cited concerning that Venus, Jupiter, and Mars.

In like manner, we have also shown in the same dissertations with what great reproaches and insults other poets pursued their gods. For, not without great disgrace to them, they had imagined, as Tertullian again notes (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 14, p. 352), Apollo tending the flocks of King Admetus, Neptune contracting to perform building work for Laomedon, and Aesculapius struck by Jupiter’s thunderbolt, just as Pindar also sang.

The gods were exhibited to even greater mockery and disgrace, as Tertullian continues (Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576, and Book [Col. 1105A] II, chap. 14), and as we have also observed elsewhere (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 14, art. 1), on the stages and in the tragedies and games of the pagans. For with what abuse and insults, I ask you, were they not harassed by the performers of comedies and tragedies? Their titles alone, and their plots alone, made this sufficiently evident to everyone. Tertullian reviews a few of them (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15, p. 358), namely, Anubis the adulterer, Diana scourged, the will of the dead Jupiter, three hungry Herculeses held up to ridicule, and the male Moon. What then, you will ask? Was the Moon counted as masculine among the titles of comedies? Indeed so. For many pagans foolishly held the conviction that at least some of their gods were both male and female (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 27, arts. 3 and 4, [Col. 1105B] and the other passages cited there). Servius, commenting on the following verse of Virgil, in which he introduces Aeneas speaking thus while Ilium burns—

> I descend, and with a god as my guide, make my way  
> Through flames and foes. . . . . . .

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book II, v. 632),

makes these observations:

> According to those who say that divinities participate in both sexes. For Calvus writes:
>
> “And the mighty god Venus.”
>
> Likewise Virgil:
>
> “Nor was a god absent from the erring right hand,”
>
> although it was either Juno or Allecto. There is also in Cyprus an image of a bearded Venus.

(Servius, p. 252.)

If you desire other witnesses, Macrobius will give you several; he observes that in the cited verse of Virgil one must read “god,” not [Col. 1105C] “goddess” (Macrobius, Book III, *Saturnalia*, chap. 8, p. 329). He proves this not only by the words of Calvus already cited by Servius and by the image of the bearded Venus, but also by the testimony of Aristophanes, who calls her [non-Latin text omitted], and of Laevinus, who worshipped her whether she was male or female. He adds that Philochorus asserted that she was the same as the Moon, to whom men sacrifice in women’s clothing and women in men’s clothing.

Even closer to Tertullian’s purpose is Spartianus’s report that the emperor Caracalla came to Carrhae for the sake of the god Lunus (Spartianus, *Life of Caracalla*). There he reminds us of a point committed to memory by all the most learned and especially observed by the people of Carrhae: the man who thought the moon female would always be subject to women, whereas the man who believed it a male god [Col. 1105D] would rule his wife and suffer no feminine plots.

What wonder is it, then, if poets composed tragedies in mockery of the gods and inscribed them with ridiculous titles referring to the gods? Our Author names two of these poets, Lentulus and Hostilius (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15, p. 358, and Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576), but mention of them rarely occurs in other writers. Yet in another book Tertullian said of this same Lentulus:

> The boxer Cleomachus … now deserving to be crowned among the Novian fullers, and deservedly commemorated by the mime-writer Lentulus in the *Catinenses*.

(Tertullian, *On the Pallium*, chap. 4.)

There he expressly calls Lentulus, whom Jerome also mentions (Jerome, Book II, *Apology Against Rufinus*), a mime-writer, or author of a play [Col. 1106A] to which he had prefixed the title *Catinenses*. A little later, however, our Author cites the pronouncements of Lentulus the augur (Tertullian, *On the Pallium*, chap. 4). But this man was different from the mime-writer. There is therefore greater reason to doubt whether the latter is the mime-writing poet whom Juvenal sharply rebukes in these two verses:

> Nimble Lentulus, too, performed the *Laureolus* well—  
> In my judgment, worthy of a real cross.

(Juvenal, Satire VIII, v. 184.)

For although no one denies that the *Laureolus* was a play, some nevertheless suspect that the word “performed” indicates that it was acted by Lentulus the actor, rather than committed to writing by a poet of that name. But what if he was both actor and poet, and both wrote and performed it? Moreover, the play was not performed by only one [Col. 1106B] actor. Why, then, is Lentulus alone named, unless he had also committed it to writing?

Mention of Hostilius the mime-writer is still less frequent in the books of others. Yet, if Priscian is to be trusted, Hostilius, following the example of Ennius, published annals of the Roman people in concise language. He could therefore also have written comedies. If anyone offers us better information about him and Lentulus, he will do us a very welcome service.

## ARTICLE II. How clearly Tertullian demonstrates that the gods of the pagans are false from the scripts of actors, by whom there were represented through voice, gesture, movement, and song the Sun mourning his son, Cybele sighing after a shepherd, Jupiter’s eulogies, the judgment of Juno, Venus, and Minerva, and the disgraceful representation of Minerva and Hercules

[Col. 1106C] Tertullian passes to the actors and openly shows that their gods were treated with no less mockery by these performers than by the poets. “The actors’ scripts,” he says, “depict all their foulness” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15, p. 359), and express it through voice, facial expression, bodily movements, costume, and insignia. Who, indeed, does not know that the actors, about whom Valerius Maximus has transmitted more information (Valerius Maximus, Book II, chap. 4, § 4), were performers of tragedies and comedies who represented the characters of gods and others?

But why, someone will ask, did Tertullian not only here but also in another book say, not “actors,” but “the scripts of actors” (Tertullian, Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576)? What are these actors’ scripts? Some have thought that “sides” should be read there, but this is mere conjecture, supported by the authority of no manuscript. [Col. 1106D] What if, by the term “scripts,” he meant the things exhibited by the actors’ voices and bodily movements, but first committed to writing and letters by the poets? For, as Augustine says:

> What, in comparison with this evil, are Mercury’s thefts, Venus’s wantonness, and the rapes and obscenities of the others, which we would bring forward from books if they were not sung and danced daily in the theaters?

(Augustine, Book VII, *City of God*, chap. 26, p. 182.)

By whom were they sung and danced, if not by the actors, after they had been committed to writing by the poets?

Tertullian offers the following examples of acts performed by those actors to the insult and utmost disgrace of their gods: “The Sun mourns his son cast down from heaven” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. [Col. 1107A] 15, p. 359). Our parchment manuscripts and editions older than Rigaltius read “cast down from heaven,” doubtless referring to Phaethon, whose story is well known. Nevertheless, in the first book *To the Nations* we read: “The Sun mourns his son destroyed by lightning” (Tertullian, Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576). This applies to Aesculapius, as we observed in the preceding article. Yet because we saw that Aesculapius was already mentioned in the *Apologeticum*, it may be inferred that Phaethon is meant there and Aesculapius in the book *To the Nations*.

An actor also represented “Cybele sighing after the disdainful shepherd,” namely Attis. We have seen this story described by Minucius Felix (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, chap. 20, art. 2; and *Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 26, art. 1), and by Arnobius, who accordingly [Col. 1107B] reproaches the pagans thus:

> Does the Great Mother become calmer and milder if she sees the ancient story of Attis revived by the actors?

(Arnobius, Book VII, *Against the Pagans*.)

Earlier he had said:

> The Great Mother loved him. If as a grandson, like a grandmother, the matter is innocent; but if as the theaters proclaim, it is an infamous and destructive love.

(Arnobius, Book V.)

Tertullian continues and rebukes the pagans with these words: “You endure the eulogies of Jupiter being sung” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15, p. 359), and in the book *To the Nations*, “the eulogies being set to music” (Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576)—that is, Jupiter’s censures and disgraces being expressed in comedies by actors’ voices and gestures and by the modulation of flutes, just as they were celebrated in the *Amphitryon* and surely in very many other similar works.

“You also endure Juno, Venus, and Minerva being judged by a shepherd” (Tertullian, [Col. 1107C] *Apologeticum*, *ibid.*), namely Paris, who was raised among shepherds on Mount Ida and called a shepherd; his judgment concerning these goddesses is unknown to no one. We shall therefore add nothing except what Augustine aptly and altogether appropriately said for our purpose:

> Although the quarrels or judgments concerning several gods may be false, including that of the three goddesses who are said to have contended before Paris as judge for preeminence in beauty in order to obtain the golden apple, nevertheless, in order to appease with games the gods who delight in these charges, whether true or false, they are sung and danced amid theatrical applause.

(Augustine, Book XVIII, *City of God*, chap. 10, p. 495.)

Thus, our Author says, the majesty of the gods was violated and prostituted while the pagans not only did not blush (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15, p. 359), but even applauded, as Rigaltius’s edition reads; our [Col. 1107D] parchment manuscripts, the old editions, and the book *To the Nations*, however, read “rejoiced” (Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576).

Tertullian presses the point (*ibid.*):

> What of the fact that the image of your god clothes a most disgraceful and notorious head?

Our manuscripts and old editions include “itself”; other manuscripts read “a disgraceful head.” In the book *To the Nations*, however, we read:

> A notorious and mutilated head is clothed by the image of any god whatsoever.

That is, a disgraceful actor notorious for his crimes represents the image of any god whatsoever. He adds:

> An impure body, trained by effeminacy for that art—

that is, “a shameless female mime represents some Minerva, or a still more impudent male mime represents Hercules” (*ibid.*); that is, he exhibits the shameful deeds of either on the stage. What person [Col. 1108A] of sound mind does not see that the divinity of these gods vaunted by the pagans was plainly and utterly overthrown by these performances of the actors, by the applause and delight of the spectators, and by all those tragedies and comedies, about which we have discussed more extensively in the preceding dissertation and in the other places cited there (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 27, art. 3)?

## ARTICLE III. How greatly the pagans, assuming the person of the gods in the arena, ruined their divinity by representing Attis castrated, Hercules burning alive, Mercury testing the dead with a cautery, Jupiter’s brother conducting away the corpses of gladiators with a hammer, and others arranging adulteries and acts of procuring in the temples, by which they degraded the majesty of the gods

[Col. 1108B] Tertullian descends from the actors’ theater into the arena, as he himself puts it, and does not find the pagans any more religious there (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15, p. 361; and Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576). They distinguish three sections of the seating area—or rather, its three parts—as we learn from Cicero (Cicero, *On Old Age*, p. 412, line 37, and *On Friendship*, p. 422, line 13), Suetonius (Suetonius, Book II, § 44), Servius (Servius on Book V of the *Aeneid*, v. 340, and Book VIII, v. 635), and others. The highest was where women sat; the middle, where the people sat; and the third, where men of higher rank sat and watched the public games. Tertullian, however, like Ammianus Marcellinus (Marcellinus, Book XIX, p. 550), uses the word *cavea* to designate the amphitheater’s arena, in which gladiators fought. For in his book *To the Nations* he wrote:

> You are [Col. 1108C] more religious in the gladiators’ arena, where, over human blood and over the defilements of punishment, your gods dance in like manner, supplying plots and stories for the punishment of criminals—or else criminals are punished in the roles of the gods themselves.

(Tertullian, Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576.)

In the *Apologeticum* he writes similarly:

> In the arena, where, over human blood and over the defilements of punishment, your gods dance in like manner, supplying plots and stories to criminals, except that criminals themselves also often assume the roles of your gods.

(Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15, p. 361; and Book I, *To the Nations*, cited above.)

By these words he means that after a gladiatorial contest a pantomime, having assumed the mask and character of some god, descended into the arena and danced over the bodies of those who had been killed or were breathing their last—criminals, that is, those who, after being condemned for their crimes, were forced to fight with wild beasts. [Col. 1108D] Say, then, how beautiful and pious it was for one impersonating a god to perform the office of an executioner or to mock the corpses of condemned men killed by beasts.

No one should suppose that our Author invented or exaggerated this, for he testifies that he had often seen it with his own eyes: “We have seen on occasion,” he says (Tertullian, *ibid.*); and in the book *To the Nations*: “We have often seen Attis castrated,” that god from Pessinus, a city situated on the frontier of Greater Phrygia beneath Mount Agdistis, where Attis was said, according to Pausanias, to have been buried. Furthermore: “The man who was burning alive,” he says, “had assumed the role of Hercules” (Pausanias, Book I, p. 4). What else did he mean by these words than that in those cruel spectacles condemned and criminal men, having taken the appearance either of Attis [Col. 1109A] or of Hercules, represented Attis castrated and Hercules burned alive? We have treated both in our dissertation on the books of Arnobius (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 14, art. 2, and chap. 18, art. 1).

What more need be said? “We laughed,” Tertullian continues, “amid the sportive cruelties of the midday gladiators” (Tertullian, passage cited), whom we discussed above, at “Mercury”—a man wearing a mask of Mercury—testing with a cautery, or red-hot iron, whether some fraud lay concealed in the dead, that is, the slain gladiators, and whether they were truly dead.

“We have also seen,” he continues (*ibid.*), “Jupiter’s brother,” namely Pluto, “conducting away the corpses of the gladiators with a hammer,” or, as the book *To the Nations* reads, “conducting their funeral procession with a hammer”—namely, into the *spoliarium*, where defeated gladiators who had received a mortal wound were killed if any breath remained in them. [Col. 1109B] Hence Seneca wrote: “Do you suppose anyone is so eager for life that he would prefer to have his throat cut in the *spoliarium* rather than in the arena?” (Seneca, Epistle 94, near the end, p. 384.) Elsewhere he says more about the midday gladiators (Epistle VII, p. 172), as does Suetonius (Suetonius, *Life of Claudius*, § 34, and *Caligula*, § 26 ff.). See them, as well as Lampridius (Lampridius, *Life of the Emperor Commodus*), concerning the *spoliarium*, into which gladiators were dragged with a hook.

One is moved to wonder at certain scholars who wish Tertullian’s text here to read: “We have also seen him conduct them to Jupiter’s brother” (Johannes Argotus, *Notes on Onuphrius Panvinius, On the Games of the Circus*, chap. 2), as though he had said: “We have seen the same Mercury, who not only tested the gladiators’ corpses [Col. 1109C] with a cautery but also conducted them with a hammer to Pluto, Jupiter’s brother—that is, to the underworld.” For they do not appear to have paid sufficient attention to these words of the same Tertullian in the book *To the Nations*:

> Dis Pater, Jupiter’s brother, conducts the gladiators’ funerals with a hammer; Mercury, feathered upon his bald head and provided with a fiery wand, tests with a cautery bodies already lifeless or feigning death.

(Tertullian, Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576.)

He therefore distinguishes the offices of both: that of Mercury, who tested whether the gladiators were dead, and that of Pluto, who conducted the dead, or their funeral processions, into the *spoliarium*. For he is speaking of things customarily done at gladiatorial games. Otherwise, Mercury was said to conduct souls to Tartarus with his caduceus or wand, as Virgil sings of him:

> Then he takes his wand: with it he summons pale souls  
> From Orcus, sends others beneath gloomy Tartarus,  
> Gives sleep and takes it away, and unseals eyes in death.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, Book IV, v. 242 ff.)

[Col. 1109D] The pagans, moreover, mocked their gods not only in public spectacles and in the arena, but even in their temples, where surely, if anywhere, divine honors ought to have been paid to them. For there, Tertullian says, adulteries could be seen being arranged, acts of procuring negotiated between the altars, and lust discharged—that is, expressed and satisfied—most often in the very lodgings of temple attendants and priests, no differently than in brothels, beneath those same sacred ribbons, caps, and purple robes, while incense burned (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, [Col. 1110A] chap. 15).

Do not, I ask you, regard his testimony as suspect. For Juvenal, although a pagan, could not endure such great crimes and reproached them with the utmost indignation in these verses:

> For lately, as I recall, the shrine of Isis and the Ganymede  
> Of Peace, and the secluded palace of the imported Mother,  
> And Ceres—for in what temple does a woman not prostitute herself?

(Juvenal, Satire 9, v. 22 ff.)

Indeed, he had previously said:

> Or rather, at the sanctuary of Isis the procuress,  
> She arranges her hair.

(Satire VI, vv. 4–5.)

But we have already said more about these matters in our other dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius*, chap. 13, art. 1; and *On Lactantius*, p. 27, art. 1 and 34). Who of sound mind would not willingly agree with Tertullian, who, from [Col. 1110B] all these things explained in this and the preceding articles, correctly concludes that the pagans themselves utterly ruined and completely overthrew all the divinity of their gods, whom they wished Christians and others to acknowledge and worship?

For so many and such great insults, disgraces, and mockeries of the gods, he says, “disturb the honor of divinity”; that is, they trouble it and do not allow it to remain undisturbed and secure. Indeed, “they degrade the summits of majesty” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 15; and Book I, *To the Nations*, chap. 10). Thus the word is written in our manuscripts and in all editions published before Rigaltius. But he considered the meaning of the verb *obsoletant* wholly inappropriate and wished it corrected to *adsolant*, which, following Gothofredus, he judges to mean “they cast down to the ground and earth.” He readily admits that the first book of our Author’s *To the Nations* reads: “They cast down the summit of majesty” (Tertullian, passage cited). But what prevents Tertullian from having employed one verb in one book and another in the other?

[Col. 1110C] Whoever objects that the meaning of *obsoletant* is utterly inappropriate has surely failed to notice that the same Tertullian said elsewhere: “Most would perish again after salvation who had worn out their wedding garment” (Tertullian, *Scorpiace*, chap. 6). Does the verb not have the same sense in this book as in the *Apologeticum*? If, then, it seems utterly inappropriate, why, I ask you, did Rigaltius alter it in this book but preserve it in the other? It therefore ought not to have been changed on the testimony of the single Fulda manuscript, even if it is found there, since so many other copies of older and better character openly disagree with it. It is sufficient that *obsoletant* is a genuine word of Tertullian, which Fortunatus also used in precisely the same sense (Fortunatus, *Life of St. Albinus*, § 1): they abolish, invalidate, and render obsolete.

Our Author’s meaning, therefore, is this: when the pagans harass their gods with so many insults, reproaches, and injuries in every place, sacred and profane alike, yet nevertheless wish them to be worshipped and adored, they invalidate and destroy with one hand the divinity which with the other [Col. 1110D] they vainly endeavor to establish.

## ARTICLE IV. By what arguments Tertullian disclosed that the gods of the pagans were mocked and rejected by philosophers, particularly Socrates, Diogenes, and Varro

No one, whoever he may be, can object to us [Col. 1111A] that the pagan philosophers showed greater reverence toward their gods than did the other common people (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 14, p. 353). For nearly all were so estranged from acknowledging the true God, or from proclaiming him once acknowledged, that Tertullian thought it superfluous here even to call them by name. He appears to have passed them over here because elsewhere, as we have shown (above, chap. 5, art. 4), he had sufficiently and openly demonstrated their vain and utterly absurd opinions concerning God.

Nevertheless, in this very passage he names Socrates specifically, and not without reason. For, as we observed earlier (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, chap. 3, art. 2), he had been declared by Apollo’s oracle the wisest of all mortals. Yet although he was regarded as so wise, [Col. 1111B] “to insult the gods,” Tertullian says, “he swore by the oak, the goat, and the dog” (Tertullian, passage cited). Rigaltius wished us to be informed that the words “and the dog” are absent from the Fulda manuscript; it is surely sufficient, however, that they are found in all the others. In the book *To the Nations*, a copyist erred by writing “mistress” in place of “goat” (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, chap. 10, p. 576). Elsewhere we have related more concerning this oath of Socrates and openly shown that various writers report it in various forms, but never without contempt and injury toward the gods (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 30, art. 1).

Tertullian further adds that Socrates was condemned to death by the Athenians because he destroyed the gods, but that they were at last so moved by repentance for their unjust condemnation that, he says, “they afterward punished Socrates’ accusers” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 14, p. 354): [Col. 1111C] namely, Melitus with death and Anytus with exile; and they placed a golden statue of Socrates in the temple (Plato, *Apology of Socrates*, Vol. III, p. 390). Yet we read that Laertius recorded: [non-Latin text omitted], “they honored him with a bronze, not a golden, statue” (Laertius, Book II, *Life of Socrates*, § 43).

Casaubon and Menagius therefore wish “bronze” to be restored in Tertullian’s text, and the latter is surprised that Rigaltius made no mention of this emendation. But Rigaltius is surely less deserving of surprise here, where he follows the common reading of all the manuscripts, than in very many other places where, contrary to their manifest testimony, he altered and corrupted Tertullian’s authentic text.
