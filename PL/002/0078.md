## CHAPTER LIII

But where the soul, naked and expelled, next turns aside we shall undoubtedly pursue in due order. First, however, we shall complete what belongs to this place, lest, because we have declared the various forms [Col. 0739C] of death, someone expect us to provide the reasons for each one. These are better left to physicians, the proper judges of all lethal things or causes and of bodily conditions themselves.

Still, in order to protect the soul’s immortality at this point also, I shall interweave into the discussion [Col. 0740A] of death something about that kind of departure in which the soul slips away little by little and particle by particle. For, sustaining the appearance of failure, it is led away while it seems to be consumed, and from the gradual measure of its departure it furnishes a conjecture of annihilation.

The whole explanation, however, lies in the body and in its departure from the body. For whatever particular departure death may be, it is unquestionably the destruction either of vital materials, regions, or channels: materials, such as bile or blood; regions, such as the heart or liver; channels, such as veins or arteries. Therefore, while these are being ravaged in the body, each by the cause peculiar to its injury, even to the final destruction and severance [Note: [Col. 0739C] *Recussionem* (“striking back”) Pam. Rhen. Seml.] of the vital—that is, natural—boundaries, positions, and functions, the soul also, as its instruments, dwellings, and spaces gradually collapse, is necessarily compelled to migrate little by little [Col. 0740B], and is reduced to an image of diminution.

It is no different from the way in which even the charioteer himself is presumed to have failed when the exhaustion of his horses has denied him their strength: this judgment concerns the plight of the helpless man, not the true condition of what he undergoes. In just the same way the animate spirit, the charioteer of the body, fails on account of its failing vehicle, not on its own account: it withdraws from its work, not from its vigor; grows weak in activity, not in condition; exhausts its stability, not its substance, because it ceases to appear, not because it ceases to exist.
