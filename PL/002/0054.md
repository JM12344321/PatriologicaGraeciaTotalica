When he enumerates the variety in preaching—how some, encouraged by confidence arising from his bonds, proclaimed the word more boldly; others through envy and contention; some, indeed, from esteem for the word [Note: [Col. 0521D] “Good esteem,” Lat.]; most from love; some from rivalry; and still others from hostility preached Christ—this surely would have been the place to censure the preaching itself for a diversity of doctrine that produced so great a diversity even of dispositions. Yet, since he presents only the motives of their dispositions as diverse, and not the rules of the mysteries, he confirms that there is nevertheless one Christ and one God of Christ, whatever the purpose [Col. 0522B] with which he is preached. And therefore he says: “It matters nothing to me whether Christ is proclaimed from pretext or in truth,” because it was one and the same Christ who was proclaimed, whether from pretext or from the truth of faith. For he referred the mention of truth to the faithfulness of the preaching, not to the rule itself: the rule was indeed one, but the faith of some of the preachers was true—that is, sincere—while that of others was overly sophisticated.

Since this is so, it is evident that the Christ who was preached was the one who had always been proclaimed. For if another Christ, far removed from the Apostle’s, had been introduced, the novelty of the matter would have created a doctrinal difference. Nor would there have been lacking those who would nevertheless have interpreted the evangelical preaching as referring to the Creator’s Christ, since even today, in every place, those who hold our doctrine are more numerous than those who hold the heretical one. Therefore neither here [Note: [Col. 0521D] *So that not*, Pam.; *because neither*, Lat.; *indeed neither*, others.] would the Apostle have remained silent [Col. 0522C] about identifying and rebuking the difference. Thus, since the difference is not even censured, the novelty is not established.

The Marcionites plainly suppose that the Apostle supports them here also concerning the substance of Christ, namely, that the flesh in Christ was a phantom, when he says that, being constituted in the form of God, [Note: [Col. 0521D] “He did not consider equality with God a robbery.” If Christ’s body had been phantasmal, none [Col. 0522D] of these things would have happened to him, nor would he have undergone death. This passage proves the point at length and departs slightly from the Vulgate, for *pariari* means “to be equal,” Greek εἶναι ἴσα (“to be equal”); and “he emptied himself,” that is, “he cast himself down,” Greek ἑαυτὸν ἐκένωσε, “he made himself empty.” LE PR.] he did not consider equality with God a robbery, but emptied himself, taking the form—not the reality—of a servant; being in the likeness of a human being, but not actually human; and being found in the figure of a human being, but not in human substance, that is, not in flesh—as though figure, likeness, and form did not also belong to substance.

It is well, however, that elsewhere he also calls Christ “the image of the invisible God.” Is Christ, then, not truly God here also, because he places him in the form of God? Equally, Christ will not truly be a human being if, when constituted in the form of a human being, he was not truly human. For in both cases reality must necessarily [Col. 0522D] be excluded if form, likeness, and figure [Note: [Col. 0522D] *Cura*, Venet., erroneously.] are claimed for a phantom. But if, in the form and image in which the Son of the Father was proclaimed truly to be God [Note: [Col. 0522D] *Was prejudged*, Ober.], he was likewise, in the form and image of a human being, in which he is the Son of Man, found truly to be a human being—for the Apostle used “found” deliberately, meaning [Col. 0523A] “most certainly a human being,” since whatever is found is established as existing—so too he was found to be God through power, just as he was found to be human through flesh. For the Apostle would not have declared him subject to death if he had not been constituted in a mortal substance.

Still stronger is what he added: “Even the death of the cross.” For he would not have intensified [Note: [Col. 0523B] *Would have intensified*, Lat.] the horror of the death by extolling the virtue of submission [Note: [Col. 0523B] *Collection*, Rhen.], if he had known that submission to be imaginary in a phantom, which had mocked death rather than experienced it, and had not exercised virtue in suffering but merely played a game.

The things that he had formerly counted as gain, and which he also enumerates above—the glory of the flesh, the mark of circumcision, descent as a Hebrew from Hebrews, the title of the tribe of Benjamin, and the distinction of Pharisaic purity—he now counts as loss to himself: not God, but the dullness of the Jews. He regards these things as dung in comparison with the knowledge of Christ, not in comparison with the rejection [Col. 0523B] of God the Creator, having righteousness no longer his own, which comes from the Law, but that which comes through him, namely Christ, from God.

“Therefore,” you say, “by this distinction the Law was not from Christ’s God.” Subtle enough. Then receive an even subtler answer. For when he says, “Not that which comes from the Law, but that which comes through him,” he would not have said “through him” of anyone other than him whose Law it was.

“Our citizenship,” he says, “is in heaven.” I recognize the Creator’s ancient promise to Abraham (Gen. XXII, 17): “And I will make your seed like the stars in heaven.” Therefore also “star differs from star in glory.”

But if Christ, coming from heaven, will transform [Col. 0524A] the body of our humiliation so that it conforms to the body of his glory, then this body of ours, which is humiliated in sufferings and cast down [Note: [Col. 0523B] *In* deleted by Ciacc.] to the earth under the very law of death, will rise again. For how will he transform it [Note: [Col. 0523B] *Transformed*, Venet.; *will have transformed*, Rig.; *will be transformed*, Pam.] if it will not exist? Or, if this was said [Note: [Col. 0523C] “Those who are found in the flesh at the coming of God will have to be reckoned.” Rather, “to be changed,” which he will presently call “to be transformed.” RIG.] of those who, being found in the flesh at God’s coming, will have to be reckoned [Note: [Col. 0524B] *To be changed*, others; *to be caressed*, Lat.], [Note: [Col. 0523D] What will those who rise first do? In a similar construction Paul said, “What will those do who are baptized [Col. 0524C] for the dead?” RIG.] what will those who rise first do? Will they have nothing from which they may be transformed? Yet, he says, “Together with them we shall be caught up in the clouds to meet the Lord.” If we are taken up with them, then surely we shall also be transformed with them.

## CHAPTER XXI

*(On the Epistle to Philemon.)* This Epistle alone profited from its brevity, so as to escape Marcion’s falsifying hands. Yet I wonder, since he accepted a letter written [Col. 0524B] to one individual, why he rejected the two addressed to Timothy and the one to Titus, which were composed concerning the ordering of the Church. He wished, I suppose, to tamper even with the number of the Epistles.

Remember, reader, that we have also proved from the Apostle the matters treated earlier; and if any matters had been deferred to this work, [Note: [Col. 0524C] *Expunxerimus*: that is, “we have discharged what was owed and fulfilled it.” Thus in the preceding Book IV: “And if we are reminded,” he says, “of the promise to Moses, it will be found discharged here.” RHEN.] we have discharged them. Thus you should neither regard as superfluous the repetition here by which we have confirmed our former hope, nor consider suspicious the postponement there by which we have brought these present occasions to light. If [Note: [Col. 0524B] *But*, Fran.] you inspect the entire little work, you will judge that there is neither redundancy here nor lack of confidence there.

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN AGAINST THE VALENTINIANS

## Preface

[Col. 0523D] Tertullian (*On Prescription*, chap. 30) and Theodoret (*On Heretical Fables*, Book I) affirm that the heresy of Valentinus broke forth under Antoninus Pius, who assumed the imperial power in the year 138. Valentinus was a native of Phrebonis, on the maritime coast of Egypt, a follower of Platonic philosophy, imbued with the arts and inventions of the Greeks and—as his followers boasted (in Origen, *Dialogue Against Marcion*)—a hearer of Theodas, a disciple of the Apostle Paul. Massuet (*Dissertation* [Col. 0524D] *on Irenaeus*, no. 7) judges that Valentinus was born in the year of Christ 85 and died in 158.

Valentinus is said to have written several psalms, as well as letters cited by Clement of Alexandria in Books II and III of the *Stromata*. One of those letters is entitled *To Agathopous, the Antiochene Deacon*, as Grabe and Cave suppose. Valentinus also published homilies and a work entitled Πόθεν τὰ κακά, that is, *Whence Come Evils?* Fragments of this work survive in the aforementioned Clement, Book VI, and in [Col. 0525A] Epiphanius, Heresy XXXI. No one except the author of the final nine chapters of the book *On Prescription* reports that he wrote a gospel of his own. He defected from the Catholic Church because he was unable to obtain the episcopate for which he burned with desire, as Tertullian relates against the same Valentinus in chapter IV.

After leaving Alexandria, Valentinus came to Rome during the episcopate of Hyginus and remained there until that of Anicetus. Besides St. Irenaeus, Tertullian, Epiphanius, and Theodoret, Massuet (*ibid.*, in the same Dissertation), Natalis Alexander (*Ecclesiastical History*, Century II, chap. 2, art. 6), Beausobre (*History of the Manichaeans*, part II, Book V), Semler (*First Volume of Baumgarten’s Polemics*), Walch (*History of Heresy*, vol. I), and Ittig (*Dissertation* [Col. 0525B] *on the Heresiarchs*, sect. II, chap. 5, and the same author’s dissertation appended to sect. II, chap. 5) write at greater length about the Valentinians; and, more recently, Matter (*History of Gnosticism*, vol. II).

Although the fabulous heresy of Valentinus appears utterly absurd from its mere description, there were nevertheless some who considered Valentinus himself orthodox, such as the Abbé Faydit, who published a *Clarification of the Doctrine of the Early Centuries*. Beausobre likewise inserted much into his *History of the Manichaeans* to polish and improve Valentinus’s system (Lumper, *Critical Theological History of the Life, Writings, and Doctrine of the Holy Fathers*, part III, sect. IV, chap. 3, preliminary dissertation, art. 1).

Moreover, it is more certain than certainty itself that Tertullian, while composing his book against the Valentinians, had before his eyes the ancient and commonly circulated Latin translation of St. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, [Col. 0525C] *Books Against Heresies*. That translation, preserved down to our own time, is known to have been published either while Irenaeus himself was still alive or shortly after his death. Whoever compares the Latin Irenaeus with Tertullian will readily discover that the latter follows the former’s footsteps so closely, adheres so closely to his very words, figures of speech, and order, that he seems to have set himself the single task of condensing the same material into fewer words. He often preserves the same language, though he intermingles, according to his custom, biting remarks expressed with a certain impudence characteristic of an African temperament. Compare Irenaeus, Book I, chap. 11, no. 3, with Tertullian, chap. 23, and Irenaeus, no. 5, with Tertullian, chap. 25, etc. See Massuet, Dissertation II on Irenaeus’s books, no. 55, pp. 89–90.

We have taken care diligently to collect the many similar passages scattered here and there [Col. 0525D] and to place them in the notes, so that the reader may compare them more easily. Nor, unless I am mistaken, will it now remain doubtful to anyone that Irenaeus preceded Tertullian. Johann Salomo Semler was therefore wrong to call these matters into question and reject the books [Col. 0526A] as spurious—a man who, if anyone ever was, seems truly to have been born to cast down nearly all the monuments of venerable antiquity from their authority on his own authority. For in his *Historical Introduction to Baumgarten’s Polemics* (vol. II, p. 131), in his *Discourse on Avoiding Troublesome Sacred Zeal in Correcting Certain Opinions of Irenaeus and Tertullian* (Halle edition, 1772), and in the *Dissertation on the Variable and Uncertain Character of These Books* appended to the Halle edition of Tertullian (vol. V, pp. 245–290, Halle-Magdeburg edition, 1828), he attempted to diminish the credibility of Irenaeus and to bring his books under suspicion of νοθεία (*spuriousness*). The celebrated Walch, theologian of Göttingen, undertook their defense in the *New Commentaries of the Royal Society of Sciences of Göttingen* (vol. V, part 100, [Col. 0526B] years 1774–1775; Lumper, work cited, chap. II, art. 1, p. 218). THE EDITORS.

[Col. 0526B] Moreover, that this book against the Valentinians belongs to Tertullian is established even from the fact that in chapter XVI he expressly mentions the book *Against Hermogenes*, which is known to be his. And indeed clear witnesses that he wrote against the Valentinians are the blessed Optatus of Milevis, Book I *Against Parmenian the Donatist*, and Vincent of Lérins, in his book *On the Profane Novelty of Heresies*. A manuscript was also seen long ago by Trithemius and Politian, and it was among the first works edited by Rhenanus, who illustrated it first with scholia and then with annotations, with such care as he displayed nowhere else.

Nevertheless, the reader will easily gather that this latest recension of ours is not to be despised, from the annotations that we have added for still greater illumination, drawing upon St. Irenaeus, Book I, chapter 1 and several [Col. 0526C] following chapters—both the Greek text preserved in Epiphanius and the Latin text in its various versions—as well as three Vatican manuscript copies; and from the clearer Argument, and indeed the Catalogue of the Aeons and the diagram of their respective positions. PAMELIUS.

A more suitable occasion will certainly present itself for a fuller discussion of the immense medley of ΓΝΩΣΕΩΣ (*gnōseōs*, “of knowledge/gnosis”) in general, and of the difficult Valentinian theme in particular, when we come to discuss Irenaeus’s most excellent books *Against Heresies*. For us, therefore, according to our custom and modest capacity, let it suffice to have offered these few remarks in advance by way of preface, and to add a few items drawn from various sources, whether Pamelius or Massuet. By these, before we approach this book—admittedly obscure and almost impenetrable to understanding—the [Col. 0526D] reader may be furnished with preliminary illumination both by an interpretation of the names of Valentinus’s Aeons and by an ordered scheme of the gnosis constructed according to his hypothesis. THE EDITORS.

## Synopsis

[Col. 0535]

[Col. 0535A] Intending to write against the Valentinian heretics, Tertullian postpones direct engagement and declares that he will merely recount their errors—or rather, their fables—with ridicule.

First, he relates that the Valentinians, certainly the most numerous association among the heretics, take greater care over nothing than concealing what they preach. Chapter I.

Next, since they called Catholics “simple,” he teaches that simplicity is approved by the Scriptures. Chapter II.

However much they hide themselves in the meantime—whereas truth, by contrast, feels no shame—ordinary Christians nevertheless know all their fables and genealogies, which the Apostle condemns as endless. Consequently, merely to display them is to destroy them. Chapter III.

The origin of the Valentinians from Valentinus is also well known, [Col. 0535B] although Ptolemy, Heracleon, Secundus, Marcus the Magician, and Theotinus departed from him. Thus Valentinus is now found nowhere, and yet there are Valentinians named after him, while only a certain Axionicus at Antioch preserved his rules in their entirety. Chapter IV.

Meanwhile, Tertullian declares that his dispute is with the original authorities and that he does not rely upon fabricated materials, since contemporaries of the heresiarch himself—Justin Martyr, Miltiades, Irenaeus, and a certain Proculus—disclosed and reported them in most fully documented volumes. Chapter V.

Then, in words quoted partly at the beginning of this Summary and partly beneath it below, he promises only an exposition of their secret doctrine and indicates the method he has decided to employ. Chapter VI.

He begins with the first Ogdoad of the Valentinian Aeons, or [Col. 0535C] the double Tetrad, in which Nous (*Mind*) and Truth were born from Bythos (*Depth*) and Sige (*Silence*); Nous and Truth produced Word and Life, and from these came Human Being and Church. Chapter VII.

Then, from that second Tetrad, Word and Life poured forth a Decad of Aeons together, while Human Being and Church produced the number twelve, so that the Pleroma, or Fullness, of the thirtyfold divinity was thus completed. Chapter VIII.

Then, when all fecundity had failed, Sophia, the final Aeon, was restrained by Horos (*Limit*) so that she would not dissolve; and, from the violence of her passion, without any act of conjugal union, she brought forth an aborted offspring, Enthymesis (*Thought*), which was exposed. Chapter IX.

Meanwhile, at the entreaty of the whole kindred, Sophia was restored to her consort Theletos, but Enthymesis was banished outside the Pleroma. Chapter X.

Therefore, lest any such disturbance recur, [Col. 0535D] that Nous produced a new pair: Christ and the Holy Spirit. Chapter XI.

Then, from all the Aeons together, there was fashioned Jesus, the perfect fruit of the Pleroma, whom they also call Soter (*Savior*), Christ, and Word; and the angels were fashioned as his attendants. Chapter XII.

And thus far, says the author in jest, extends the first scene of the tragedy; now comes another performance in tragic buskins outside the Pleroma. Chapter XIII.

For Enthymesis—now bearing the new and interpretable name [Col. 0536A] Achamoth—was expelled into that void of Epicurus. Although she had already been given form by Christ, who had been sent down from above together with the Holy Spirit, she nevertheless could not reach the light of the Pleroma. Excluded by Horos, who guarded its outermost boundary, she was afflicted by grief, fear, consternation, and ignorance. Chapter XIV.

From these, they say, matter—which the Pythagoreans, Stoics, and Plato himself hold to be ungenerated—and the very elements derived their origin. Chapter XV.

She then turned to prayer; and when the Paraclete, Soter Jesus, was sent to her, she was again strengthened and cleansed of all the injuries of passion. From that conversion she produced the corporeal realities of the psychic order. Chapter XVI.

Thereafter, out of joy arising from the contemplation of Soter and of the angelic lights that had come with him, she produced the spiritual realities; and thus the trinity of kinds came into being. Chapter XVII.

Then from psychic substance was produced the Demiurge, this God of ours, king of all things. Chapter XVIII.

They are not, however, in agreement among themselves about this matter. Chapter XIX.

Next, the Demiurge, stationed outside the boundaries of the Pleroma, fashioned this world from psychic and material substances, but from incorporeal substances he fashioned the sevenfold stage of the heavens. Chapter XX.

Amid these matters Tertullian occasionally inserts the various names of Achamoth, or the second Sophia. Chapter XXI.

Indeed, they even make the devil a work of the Demiurge, calling him the Munditenens (*World-Holder*). Chapter XXII.

Then, after inserting an account of the position of the individual powers, he adds fire, fanned into all these bodies and elements. Chapter XXIII.

After this, the Demiurge set his hands to the human being and created flesh for him, not from the mud of the earth, but from that philosophical matter. Chapter XXIV.

When the Demiurge then imparted a soul to him from his own breath, spiritual seed was likewise channeled into him by Achamoth; this they called the Church. Chapter XXV.

From this Tertullian describes the three kinds of human beings, or the trinity within the human being, and the end allotted to each. Chapter XXVI.

They also teach that the Demiurge sent forth Christ, his natural Son, through the Virgin, though he was not produced from the Virgin; and that Soter descended upon him in the form of a dove and remained impassible in Christ, while the psychic Christ suffered. Chapter XXVII.

The better souls, too, were selected for the prophets, priests, and kings. Chapter XXVIII.

They establish three kinds of human beings: the choic or earthly, the psychic, and the spiritual. Chapter XXIX.

For the spiritual—whom they boast themselves to be—works are unnecessary, though they are necessary for us, insofar as we are psychics, that is, animal human beings. In this way they also evade the necessity of martyrdom. Chapter XXX.

And indeed, at the consummation the reward is allotted in such a way that Achamoth is transferred from the middle story into the Pleroma, where Soter, that composite bridegroom, receives her; while the Demiurge is transferred from his celestial Hebdomad into the empty place formerly occupied by his mother. Chapter XXXI.

The human race, however, will proceed to the following ends: souls of the choic and material class will perish; the souls of righteous psychics—that is, our souls—will be sent into the dwelling places of that intermediate region; and finally the souls of the spiritual—that is, of the Valentinians themselves—will be conducted into the palace of the Pleroma and taken as brides by the angels. Chapter XXXII.

Ptolemy’s revisers differ from Valentinus on this point: they assign two consorts to Bythos, Thought and Will, with Truth arising from Thought and Only-Begotten from Will. Chapter XXXIII.

There are also others who either assign no sex to Bythos or make him a hermaphrodite. Chapter XXXIV.

There is likewise someone who maintains that Bythos holds not the first but the last position, coming after the first Ogdoad, which was itself derived from a Tetrad, though under different names. Chapter XXXV.

[Col. 0538A] Some teach that from the father Bythos and the mother Ennoea (*Thought*) that entire Ogdoad was delivered in a single birth. Chapter XXXVI.

Likewise, another makes Monotes (*Singularity*) and Henotes (*Unity*) the first parents, from whom came Monas (*Monad*) and Henosis (*Union*), and from these the remaining Aeons. Chapter XXXVII.

Finally there is the more humane Secundus, who divides the two Tetrads into right and left, light and darkness, but holds that the defecting power, namely Achamoth, was not derived from any of the Aeons. Chapter XXXVIII.

Lastly, there is still greater disagreement about Jesus himself. Valentinus maintains that he was fashioned from the little blossoms of all the Aeons; others from only the ten or only the twelve; others from Christ and the Holy Spirit alone; and still others from some different source. Thus the doctrines of the Valentinians have now grown wild into whole forests [Col. 0538B] of Gnostics. Chapter XXXIX.

## CHAPTER I
