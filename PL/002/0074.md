Even when this doctrine is defended under the name of judgment [Note: [Col. 0705C] Under the name of judgment. Plato is repeatedly censured for asserting in the *Timaeus* that souls are thrust into the bodies of animals according to their good or evil deserts. LAC.], [Col. 0705B] namely, that human souls obtain different kinds of animals according to their life and deserts—each to be slaughtered in animals destined for killing, subdued in beasts of service, wearied in working animals, and defiled in unclean ones; and likewise to be honored, cherished, tended, and desired in the most beautiful, excellent, useful, and delicate animals—here too I shall say: If they are changed, the very souls that earned their deserts will not themselves be brought to account. The rationale of judgment will be nullified if the consciousness of deserts is lost; but consciousness of deserts will be lost if the condition of souls has been altered; and the condition of souls is altered if they have not remained the same. Equally, if they have [Note: [Col. 0705C] *Not* inserted by Wouwer.] remained the same for judgment—as even the Egyptian Mercury knew, when he said that the soul, after departing from the body, is not poured back into the soul of the universe, but remains individually determined, so that, as he says, it may render to the Father an account of the things it did [Col. 0705C] in the body—I wish, assuredly, to review the justice, gravity, majesty, and dignity of divine judgment, and to ask whether human judgment does not preside from a loftier tribunal, being richer in the honor accorded to both kinds of sentence, punishments and rewards, more severe in avenging and more generous in bestowing.

What do you suppose the soul of a murderer will become? Some animal, [Col. 0706A] I imagine, destined for slaughter and the meat-market, so that it may likewise have its throat cut because it too cut throats; likewise be flayed because it too stripped others; likewise be set out as food because it too cast to beasts those [Note: [Col. 0705C] “Defiled the mouth,” Pamelius, Rhenanus.] whom it slaughtered in woods and trackless places [Note: [Col. 0705D] “Because it too cast to beasts those whom it slaughtered in woods and trackless places.” The common reading was: “Because it too infected beasts with those whom it slaughtered obliquely in woods and fields.” Ursinus’s manuscript reads: “Because it too cast to beasts those whom.” Agobard’s copy: “Because it too made for beasts those whom it slaughtered in woods and trackless places.” “Let it likewise,” he says, “be set out as food, because it too made food for beasts of those whom it slaughtered in the woods.” RIG.]. If it is judged in this way, will not that soul carry away more comfort than punishment? It finds a funeral amid the most costly cooks [Note: [Col. 0705C] “Cooks,” Paris, Rigault, Venice.]; it is buried with Apician and gluttons’ seasonings; it is brought onto Ciceronian tables; it is carried out on the most splendid Sullan platters; it undergoes a banquet as its funeral rites [Note: [Col. 0705D] “It undergoes a banquet as its funeral rites”: it is brought to a banquet, not a tomb; it is expended upon a dinner, not a funeral. So great among human beings is the vengeance exacted for murder—so great as the very nature that is being avenged. Who would not prefer the justice of this world, which the Apostle not without reason testifies is armed with the sword (Rom. 13), and which is religious precisely when it rages on behalf of a human being [Col. 0706C]? If we also consider the recompense for other crimes—the crosses, burnings alive, sacks, hooks, and cliffs—to whom would it not be advantageous to undergo sentence under Pythagoras and Empedocles? For even those who deserve punishment by labor and servitude will surely be reincorporated in asses and mules. O divine judgments after death, more deceitful [Note: [Col. 0707C] “More beggarly,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] than human judgments—contemptible in their punishments and repellent in their favors—which neither the worst need fear nor the best desire; toward which [Note: [Col. 0708C] “And toward which,” Franeker.] the wicked rather than the holy will hasten: the former so as to escape the justice of this world more quickly, the latter so as to receive it later! God, therefore, will judge more fully [Note: [Col. 0707D] “God, therefore, will judge more fully because he judges last.” Namely, in the last and supreme judgment. ## CHAPTER XXXIV

To [Note: [Col. 0708C] *Ad*, Pamelius, Rhenanus.] the present day, indeed, no doctrine of madness of this kind has broken forth under a heretical name, one that refashions human souls into beasts. But we necessarily [Col. 0708B] introduced and excluded this form as well, because it coheres with the foregoing doctrines, so that Homer might likewise be beaten back in his peacock just as Euphorbus was in Pythagoras; and thus, once this metempsychosis or *metensomatosis*—transmigration into another body—had also been repelled, that other doctrine which supplied the heretics with something might in turn be struck down.

For Simon the Samaritan, the would-be purchaser of the Holy Spirit in the Acts of the Apostles, after being condemned by the Spirit himself to perish with his money [Note: [Col. 0708C] *In* omitted by Franeker.], wept in vain [Note: [Col. 0707D] “Wept in vain.” That is, repented in vain. Septimius says that he wept in vain. This may also be understood to mean that he is said to have wept in vain because, after coming to the Christian faith and professing, as is likely, that he renounced the magical arts and repented of his former frauds, he nevertheless later returned faithlessly to the same deceits and impostures. Turning to the assault upon truth, as though to console himself by revenge, and supported also by the powers of his art, he redeemed with the same money, for the display of some supposed power, a certain Tyrian woman named Helen from a place of public lust [Note: [Col. 0708C] “A certain Tyrian woman named Helen from a place of public lust.” Irenaeus, book I, chapter 20, called her a prostitute. PAM.], obtaining for himself a reward worthy of himself in place of the Holy Spirit [Note: [Col. 0708D] “A reward worthy of himself.” So too Agobard’s copy reads. He fashioned himself as the supreme Father [Note: [Col. 0708C] “Fashions,” Rhenanus, Semler.], but her as his own first projected Thought [Note: [Col. 0708D] “But her as his own first projected Thought.” The blessed martyr Justin speaks more clearly: “a certain first Mind or Intelligence”; Irenaeus: “the conception of his mind, the mother of all, through whom at the beginning he conceived in his mind the making of angels and archangels.” Likewise, the phrase “she, having attained the object of this purpose,” etc., supports Irenaeus’s meaning. [Col. 0709A] Having attained the object of this purpose, she had leapt forth from the Father and bounded down into the lower regions; and there, anticipating the Father’s purpose, she had generated the angelic powers, which were ignorant of the Father and were the makers of this world. Through envy [Note: [Col. 0709C] “By them through envy,” Latinus; “by them likewise in spirit,” Pamelius, Semler.] she was detained by them [Note: [Col. 0709D] “But through envy she was detained by them.” Irenaeus: “She was detained by them out of envy, because they did not wish to be thought the offspring of someone else.” From this it is perfectly clear that the readings found in the common editions and even in ancient manuscripts are corrupt: “By them likewise she was detained in spirit,” and “By them the soul was not detained in like manner.” RIG.], lest, if she departed, they should be seen to be another’s offspring. For that reason she was subjected to every indignity, so degraded that she was nowhere permitted to depart, and fell even into human form, as though to be restrained by bonds of flesh. Thus, for many ages, she was rolled through one female guise after another. She had even been that Helen who was utterly ruinous to Priam, and afterward to the eyes of Stesichorus [Note: [Col. 0709D] “And afterward to the eyes of Stesichorus.” That story is related by many writers. LE PR.]: she had blinded him because of his poem’s abuse, and afterward restored his sight because of the reparation made by his praise. Accordingly, as she migrated from body to body, she had, as her final degradation [Note: [Col. 0709C] “Finally, from adornment,” Wouwer manuscript.], stood for sale under the title of Helen, now cheaper still.

[Col. 0709B] She, therefore, was the lost sheep for whom the supreme Father—that is, Simon—had descended [Note: [Col. 0710C] “Descends,” Rigault, Venice.]. After first recovering her and carrying her back—I do not know whether upon his shoulders or between her thighs [Note: [Col. 0709D] “I do not know whether upon his shoulders or between her thighs.” Septimius’s style alludes to the parable of the Good Shepherd, punning upon Helen the prostitute. RIG.]—he then turned his attention to the salvation of human beings, as though they were to be liberated by vengeance from those angelic powers. To deceive them, he too conformed himself in like fashion; and, falsely presenting himself to human beings as a man, he played the Son in Judaea but the Father in Samaria.

O Helen, afflicted alike among poets and heretics—then infamous for adultery, now for prostitution! Except that she is rescued more gloriously from Troy than from the brothel: from Troy by a thousand ships, but from the brothel perhaps for less than a thousand denarii. Blush, Simon: you were slower in seeking her and less steadfast in bringing her back. But Menelaus immediately pursues her when lost, immediately reclaims her when taken away, and wrests her back by a ten-year [Col. 0709C] war, neither concealing himself, nor deceiving, nor quibbling. I fear that he was more truly her father, since he labored more watchfully, boldly, and persistently to recover Helen.

## CHAPTER XXXV

But metempsychosis did not construct this fable for you alone. Carpocrates too makes use of it, being likewise a magician [Note: [Col. 0709D] “Likewise a magician.” In one phrase he states what Irenaeus says at greater length about Carpocrates and his disciples, followed by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, chapter 7) and Epiphanius: “For,” he says, “they practice magical arts and incantations, as well as love potions, χαροτήσια [*charotēsia*, that is, aphrodisiacs, as Eusebius’s translators render it], *paredroi* [that is, assistants], *oneiropompoi* [that is, senders of dreams], and other malign practices—or, as Epiphanius’s translator has it, magical deceptions.” “Likewise a fornicator.” The Author adds this because, according to Irenaeus, Marcellina, who came to Rome under Anicetus and belonged to this school, led many to destruction. [Note: [Col. 0710D] “Likewise a fornicator, though possessing less of a Helen”—although he was a fornicator, he nevertheless did not have a Helen. RIG.—“Though possessing less of a Helen.” Although a fornicator, he nevertheless did not equal Helen, who, through the shamefulness of her life, was the cause of Troy’s destruction and the death brought by many calamities. LE PR.] For, in order to establish the complete overthrow of every divine and human rule, he asserted that souls are reincorporated. He maintained that this life is assigned to no one unless all the deeds that condemn it have been discharged, because nothing is regarded as evil by nature but only by opinion. Metempsychosis must therefore necessarily threaten if, in every first passage through this life, satisfaction is not made for all unlawful deeds. Crimes, evidently, are the appointed dues of life. Otherwise the soul must be summoned back as many times as it has rendered too little, remaining in arrears for its offenses until it pays the last farthing (Matt. 5), repeatedly cast into the prison of the body.

For he adapts to this the Lord’s entire allegory, which shines clearly under definite interpretations. First, it should indeed be [Col. 0710B] understood simply: for a pagan person too is our adversary, walking with us along the same road of our common life. Otherwise we would have had to depart from the world (1 Cor. 5), if it were not lawful for us to associate with them. The Lord therefore commands you to show goodwill toward such a person (Luke 6): “Love your enemies,” he says, “and pray for those who curse you,” lest, provoked by some injury arising from commercial dealings, he drag you before his judge and, once handed over to custody, you be confined until the entire debt has been paid.

Then, if the reference to the adversary is transferred to the devil by reason of the accompanying observance, you are warned to enter with him too into that agreement which is reckoned to arise from the covenant of faith. For you have covenanted to renounce him, his pomp, and his angels. This is what has been agreed between you. The friendship in question will consist in observing the pledge [Note: [Col. 0710C] “The observance,” Pamelius, Rhenanus.], so that afterward [Col. 0710C] you take back nothing of his from among those things which you renounced and returned to him, lest he accuse you before God the Judge as a defrauder and transgressor of the covenant—just as elsewhere we read that he is the accuser of the saints (Rev. 12:10), and as the very name “devil” [Note: [Col. 0710C] “Devil” omitted by Rigault, Venice.] means “informer”—and the Judge hand you over to the angel who executes the sentence, and that angel commit you to the infernal prison, from which you will not be released until even your slight offense [Col. 0711A] has been paid for by the delay of the resurrection [Note: [Col. 0710D] “Until even a slight offense has been paid for by the delay of the resurrection.” That is, in the interval before we rise again—not because the postponement of the resurrection by itself is the expiation and purgation of venial faults, but because in purgatory we are cleansed by various punishments after this life and before we rise again. Moreover, Pamelius noted that this interpretation of Tertullian supports the purgation of souls after this life from all lesser offenses, and therefore purgatory, adding other testimonies from St. Cyprian. LAC.].

What is more fitting than these meanings? What is truer than these interpretations? But, according to Carpocrates, if the soul is indebted for every crime, who must be understood as its enemy and adversary? I suppose it is the better mind, which drove it toward some degree of innocence, so that it must be forced again and again into a body until it is found guilty of no good life whatsoever. This is to recognize a good tree from evil fruits [Note: [Col. 0711C] “This is to recognize a good tree from evil fruits.” It is irony, by which he ridicules Carpocrates’ interpretation. PAM.]; that is, to recognize the doctrine of truth from the worst possible precepts.

Elijah, however, will come not after departing from life [Note: [Col. 0711D] “Elijah, however, not after departing,” etc. Irenaeus, book V, chapter 7, in his discussion of paradise in Eden, relates that this teaching about Elijah had been received from presbyters who were disciples of the Apostles: that he was translated into the paradise prepared for righteous people, into which the Apostle Paul too was carried and heard words inexpressible to us at present; that Elijah will remain there until the consummation of the age and meanwhile be preserved incorrupt. But how, then, is John Elijah [Note: [Col. 0711D] “But how, then, is John Elijah?” The blessed Augustine interprets these passages of Scripture in precisely the same way (*Tractate XIV on John*, chapter 1, and *On the Trinity*, book V, chapter 14). ## CHAPTER XXXVI

We have, I think, digressed into these questions from the point to which we must now return. We had established that the soul is sown in and from the human being himself [Note: [Col. 0711D] “We had established that the soul is sown in and from the human being himself,” etc. Tertullian established this paradox of his in chapters 25, 26, and 27, where he asserted that the soul is propagated in the womb together with the flesh, and that all souls are derived through the seed from Adam’s stock—a point which he presses again in this chapter. LAC.], and that from the beginning there is one seed, just as there is also one stock from which the entire lineage of the flesh springs. We did so, of course, because of the rival opinions of philosophers and heretics [Note: [Col. 0712C] “Because of the rival opinions of philosophers and heretics.” In chapter 23, before declaring his own opinion on the propagation of souls, he reviewed the various opinions of heretics concerning the soul’s origin, which rivaled the philosophical doctrines—especially the Platonic ones—that granted souls a heavenly origin. There he showed that Valentinus, Saturninus, Carpocrates, and Apelles were Plato’s rivals and disciples. LAC.], and because of that lethargic old discourse of Plato [Note: [Col. 0712C] “That lethargic old discourse of Plato.” He does not call Plato’s discourse “lethargic” because Plato said that acts of learning are acts of recollection, as though they proceeded from a lethargic sleep. Rather, he alludes to the ancient discourse in Plato about the reciprocal circulation of souls, concerning which chapter 28 says: “What, then, is that old discourse now found in Plato’s memory?” etc. He plays wittily upon the word, to show that this old discourse is a mere dream, without any brains, and is just as lethargic as it is old. Arnobius too used *veternosum* in the sense of “old” in book I. LAC.].

We now construct from that point the sequence of the discussions that follow. The soul, being sown in the womb together with the flesh, receives its sex together with the flesh, so that neither substance by itself is regarded as the cause of sex. For if the conceptions of the two substances admitted some interval between them, so that either the flesh or the soul was sown first, it would then be possible to ascribe the distinctive character of sex to one substance rather than the other by reason of that temporal interval between the two seeds: either the flesh would imprint sex upon the soul, or the soul upon the flesh. Apelles—not the painter, but the heretic—is relevant here [Note: [Col. 0712D] “Apelles—not the painter, but the heretic.” Ἀφέλλης (*Aphellēs*), in Latin *Apelles* and *Apella*. Horace: “Let the circumcised Jew Apella believe it.” He, perhaps, was circumcised because he was a Jew; but *Apella* is the Greek Ἀπέλλα (*Apella*).]—[the sentence continues beyond this excerpt].

Apelles placed male and female souls before their bodies, as he learned from Philumena, and therefore made the flesh, as the later substance, receive its sex from the soul. Those who introduce the soul into the flesh after birth likewise make the soul, which comes to flesh already formed, receive from the flesh a prior determination of male or female sex. But the undivided seeds of both substances and their united outpouring undergo a common outcome of sex, according to whatever lines that mysterious principle of nature may draw [Note: [Col. 0712D] “According to whatever lines it may draw.” Since he had mentioned a painter shortly before, he also borrowed this proverbial expression from painters. PAM.]. Here too the archetypal form bears witness, since the male is fashioned first in time: Adam was first; the woman somewhat later, for Eve came afterward.
