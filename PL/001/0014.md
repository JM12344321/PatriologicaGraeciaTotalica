## § 11.—From a passage of Tertullian’s *To Scapula*, compared with another in Spartianus, it is shown that this earlier harassment of the Christians began in A.D. 197.

[Col. 0109C]

We come to the second argument by which we wished to determine the beginning of that disturbance raised by the populace: the passage already quoted above, § 6, from Tertullian’s *To Scapula*, in which Severus is said “not only not to have harmed very distinguished women and men of the Christian sect, but even to have honored them with his testimony, and to have resisted the populace when it raged against the Christians.” Since Severus did not do this at a time when he had already issued an edict against the Christian religion, [Col. 0109D] as we argued there, the event must be assigned to a year in which Severus had not yet become hostile through this law. [Col. 0110A] Nor does he seem to have wished to show favor in this manner to certain very distinguished persons of this household at the time when, after defeating Albinus, he carried war against the Parthians and other peoples of the East—a war from which he did not return until after he had promulgated this same edict against the Christians. For at that time he was so entangled in wars with so many very warlike nations that it can scarcely be believed that he gave thought to matters of this kind.

But Tertullian’s words are also such that it readily appears that Severus, while not harming these particular women and most distinguished men, nevertheless harmed others from whom he wished the Christians to be distinguished. And I do not see to what time the violence inflicted upon those others can be referred except that in which Spartianus reports (*Severus*, ch. 12) that innumerable supporters of Albinus were killed, among whom were many leading men [Col. 0110B] of the state and many illustrious women; for in this matter there is even a certain verbal similarity between these writers. Indeed, I scarcely doubt that Severus assumed, as it were, the protection of these Christians and honored them with testimony intended to show that they were not among Albinus’s friends, lest the populace, under the pretext of supporting the Emperor’s cause, persecute and drag to trial these adherents of the Christian religion, against whom nothing could be charged except that they were Christians. Let it therefore be accepted as true that immediately after Albinus’s death and before Severus’s expedition against the Parthians—that is, in A.D. 197—the populace violently attacked the Christians.

## § 12.—The same is further demonstrated from the date of Tertullian’s *Apology* and of the other books that preceded it.

[Col. 0110C]

Finally, this too is no slight argument for our position: it appears that Tertullian’s *Apology* cannot have been committed to writing except before those laws of Severus—a matter about which no one who has examined this little work thoughtfully can doubt. But from what we shall argue later, it will readily be understood that this defense of the ancient Christians is later than his books *To the Martyrs*, *On the Shows*, and *On Idolatry*. In turn, I hope to establish so firmly that this vindication of the Christian religion was written at the beginning of A.D. 199 that no one endowed with even moderate judgment in these matters can doubt it. [Col. 0110D] It will therefore become apparent that the books *On the Shows* and *On Idolatry* cannot be assigned to any year other than A.D. 198.

Tertullian, however, had exhorted the martyrs to steadfastness and harmony in a separate little work before he prepared himself to write those two books just mentioned. It is therefore necessary to say that the book *To the Martyrs* was published about A.D. 197. This single fact places our position concerning the year in which this earlier harassment of the Christians began beyond all doubt. For those to whom he offered the book had already been cast into chains because of the Christian religion, and these martyrs-designate are proof that the worshippers of the gods were then inflicting violence upon the Christians. But these remarks are merely incidental; we shall now say more about these books themselves.

## § 13.—The dates of books of this kind are determined, so that *To the Martyrs* was written in A.D. 197. Hoffmann’s conjectures are rejected.

[Col. 0111A]

The first among books of this kind, therefore, is that which Tertullian addressed to the martyrs. The very subject of the book demonstrates that hostilities against the Christians had already been undertaken, for it is dedicated to martyrs-designate (compare ch. 1). It remains only to determine whether they were cast into prison before or after Severus’s edict; and for several reasons the former view pleases me more.

First, because in ch. 2 he says that the Christians were enduring the judgments not of the Emperor but of the proconsul. I do not deny that Tertullian could have said this even if the governors had been employed [Col. 0111B] by an Emperor who was waging war against the Christians by means of laws, since the governors executed those laws in the provinces. Nevertheless, from this there first arises a not unreasonable suspicion that at this time the governors themselves, rather than the Augustus, were chiefly exercising this violence.

But if this type of argument seems capable of being disputed in either direction, the controversy is undoubtedly settled by what stands at the end of our little work, where he says: “For this purpose”—namely, suffering in the cause of God—“let even the present times serve us as lessons: how many persons, and of what rank, encounter ends unforeseen in view of their birth, dignities, physical condition, and age, for a man’s sake—either at his own hands, if they have acted against him, or at the hands of his adversaries, if they have stood for him.” [Col. 0111C] That this pertains to the time when Severus was making the Roman world secure from his rivals for the empire cannot possibly be doubted. For only those who have failed to understand the passages adduced above are unaware that, once Albinus had been removed, Severus most especially punished those who had stood on the side of Niger or Albinus.

From this it is no less possible to understand how Tertullian could say (ch. 2) that those martyrs-designate, to whom he wished to bring some relief by composing our little book, did not share in the nations’ festival days and were not buffeted by the shouting at the shows. For although I am not altogether unaware that games were also presented when Severus later returned from the Parthians and the other barbarians, nevertheless the very argument [Col. 0111D] which he draws, for raising the martyrs’ spirits, from the most distinguished men who had experienced the same fate leads us to understand the games as those which we know were presented because of the victory gained over Niger, and still more over Albinus.

Moreover, I do not hesitate to claim first place for our little book among those whose date is discerned by some definite reasoning. For Tertullian not only recalls the mutual violence of the contenders for the imperial power against the adherents of the opposing side—an event which cannot be said to have occurred except in the very year in which Albinus perished, that is, A.D. 197—but also mentions the calamities undergone by the Christians as though they had only now begun; whereas all the other books whose composition was occasioned by this disturbance of the times show that the Christians [Col. 0112A] had already been harried by many tortures and torments.

Nor do I now fear that some may number this little book among those Tertullian composed after he withdrew himself from communion with the orthodox Church. For the very date of the book does not permit this, if what we said elsewhere, § 3, is true: that Tertullian did not make war upon it before A.D. 200. On this matter we see Hoffmann disagreeing with us, as usual, but employing in confirmation of his opinion an argument from which the very contrary of what he intended appears more clearly (*cited dissertation*, § 10). He supposes, namely, that the martyrs to whom Tertullian’s discourse is directed were partly orthodox and partly Montanists, and that this caused dissension to arise among them, which Tertullian attempts to remove from their minds [Col. 0112B] at the beginning of the book.

“But to unite them,” says Hoffmann, “there was need of an exhorter who wished well to both parties, such as Tertullian the Montanist, who was often very bitter against the Catholics, yet did not hate them as he did heretics.” I hear him. But in order that both parties may lay aside their hostility, it certainly is not necessary that the man who seeks to reconcile peace should favor both parties to the dispute. Rather, it is necessary that those who disagree with one another should be in agreement with the person who reconciles peace. I shall speak more plainly.

It is established from some writer whose identity I do not know, whose words are preserved by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* V.16, p. 232, Reading’s Cambridge edition, that those devoted to the purer Church so despised the Montanists that, if by chance they were cast into prison with them, they always remained separate from the Montanists and refused their fellowship. [Col. 0112C] But the same cannot be said of the Montanists, since no trace whatever of such a thing exists; indeed, Tertullian himself, even in those books which he opposed to the teachings of the general Church, carefully distinguishes the “Psychics” from heretics and affirms that the Montanists and the orthodox were not at variance over any matter touching the rule of what Christians must believe.

Assume, therefore, that Tertullian had already separated himself from the decrees of the orthodox when he composed the book. Could he then have attempted with any good hope to restore peace among the divided prisoners? I hardly think so. For those among the prisoners who called themselves associates of the orthodox Church would generally have recoiled from following the precepts of a man whose sect they vehemently detested. Conversely, if Tertullian is said to have written these things as a member of the purer Church, that hatred certainly cannot be said to have prevented the Montanists also from receiving amicably the admonitions given to them. [Col. 0112D]

Indeed, I can scarcely persuade myself that Tertullian, in commending peace to the martyrs, would have used the argument in chapter 1 that, if they pursued peace with others, they might perhaps one day be able to confer peace upon others also; for everyone who has read his book *On Modesty* (ch. 22) knows how harshly he judged that custom of issuing letters of peace.

Finally, there is no reason to dwell long on the example of the contracted gladiator found in our little book, ch. 5, no less than in the first book *To the Nations*, ch. 18. Let it indeed be granted [Col. 0113A] that our book is later than that one: who will on that account pronounce our book suspect of Montanism, unless it has already been certainly established—which we shall later convict as false—that the books *To the Nations* were composed by Tertullian after he had entered into association with the Montanists? Besides, if Hoffmann thought himself entitled to say that our little book was later than those books, we have an equal right to consider those books later than ours; and all the more because it is certain that this one appeared at the beginning of the harassment, whereas those appeared, as it were, in the very heat of the disturbance.

## § 14.—The book *On the Shows* was composed in A.D. 198. The doubts of Tillemont, Hoffmann, and Dupin are resolved.

[Col. 0113B]

There is no doubt that Tertullian published the book *On the Shows* during the same period. Even if, in establishing this, we employed no argument other than the fact that he cited that book in chapter 13 of the work he wrote *On Idolatry*, it would be very easy to understand that the former was made public at the latest in A.D. 198, since Tertullian communicated the latter to the Christians in approximately the same year.

But his very purpose in composing this book—to overthrow the opinion that Christians were permitted to attend shows—teaches that shows were being presented especially at that time. The worshippers of the gods said that Christians, by despising them, were choosing what they regarded as an easy kind of death (*On the Shows*, ch. 1). I very much doubt whether these shows can be understood as any other than those which we read were presented not only at Rome but also throughout the provinces (compare ch. 7) because of Severus’s [Col. 0113C] return and his victory over Albinus; Spartianus records such shows for this period in *Severus*, ch. 14.

For if we see Tertullian recalling games (ch. 16) held on the birthdays and festivals of kings and on occasions of public prosperity, and saying that none of them were free from the worship of the gods, under whatever name and title they might be presented, we naturally suspect what we have said—being especially led to this by his statement (ch. 30) that the governors, persecutors of the Lord’s name, would one day melt in flames fiercer than those with which they themselves had raged against the Christians; and by his statement elsewhere (ch. 26) that lions were demanded daily against the Christians and persecutions were decreed. [Col. 0113D] Thus the governors were already harassing the worshippers of Christ, whose obstinacy in rejecting the pleasure of the shows was commonly made an accusation against them (ch. 1).

We said in § 7 that one of the reasons for violating the Christians was held to be their religious scruple against attending the games, and that this very savagery against them already existed in A.D. 197. For this reason, we doubt that anyone will judge the book *On the Shows* to have been written in any year other than that one or the next.

I am not unaware that many have held the opinion that in our little book Tertullian had in view rather the Secular Games, whose commemoration we know to have been celebrated on so many coins; and Tillemont himself, the most accurate interpreter of these matters if one sets aside chronological reasoning, inclined toward this view (*Mémoires pour servir à l’Histoire Ecclésiastique des six premiers siècles*, vol. III, Paris ed., 1701, quarto, p. 210). [Col. 0114A] But we are prevented from assenting to that opinion, first, by Tertullian’s statement that the governors were the authors of the Christians’ calamities; for he thus appears to depart from his customary caution and concern to remove from Christians every suspicion that they had neglected their duty toward the Emperor. Consequently, by this very caution employed everywhere else by Tertullian, but which we see absent here, we are brought to the conclusion that, when this little book was published, Severus had not yet become a participant in this crime against the Christians, although he certainly was such in A.D. 204, when those Secular Games were presented.

Second, the very opinion of those who dissent would entail that this book was written by Tertullian after he had gone over [Col. 0114B] to the Montanist party. This is far otherwise, as will shortly appear, and is rejected by the very men who think differently.

Nor is it proper to refer this book to any time other than approximately the beginning of the year 198. For it is scarcely credible that Severus returned to Rome before May of the preceding year, since after the battle of Lugdunum (§ 10) many matters still remained to be settled by him (see Herodian’s *History*, book III, ch. 8, Boecler’s ed.). Hence it follows that even the little book *To the Martyrs*, which precedes ours, can be said to have been written only toward the end of that year. If we add to this what we shall demonstrate in its proper place—that *On Idolatry* is later than *On the Shows* and was composed in the same year—our opinion concerning the date of *On the Shows* will necessarily follow.

This same date of the book sufficiently demonstrates that [Col. 0114C] it cannot be numbered among those which Tertullian composed as an adherent of the Montanist sect; for we argued above that he joined it in A.D. 200, and the book itself bears no mark of a mind imbued with Montanist errors.

“But this little book is nevertheless later than *On the Crown*, which no one denies bears signs of Montanism.” Thus, approximately, Hoffmann in the cited dissertation, § 7. If one asks him the reason for this, he answers that our book appeared “when the peace of the Church had been destroyed, not indeed by Severus’s edicts, but by the cries of the populace and the authority of the governors.” But I hold that, when Tertullian was led by precisely this argument to apply his mind to writing *On the Shows*, he was plainly still a member of the orthodox community. [Col. 0114D]

For I see the entire force of the contrary argument to consist in the fact that, in chapter 1 of *On the Crown*, Tertullian says that the Christians had still enjoyed a good and lengthy peace. What must be answered to this will find a more convenient place later. But the claim that *On the Shows* was written after *On the Crown* is contrary to the truth. For on this point we have the wholly contrary testimony of Tertullian himself, who says in chapter 6 of the little book *On the Crown*: “We have dealt with this subject, because of our pleasure-loving brethren, in a Greek work also.” We are speaking, however, of the shows, which he regards as derived from the teaching of the devil. This very passage had already moved Allix to decide that our book *On the Shows* was earlier than the other work. Hoffmann attempts to break the force of this argument by declaring that Tertullian is speaking of a Greek little book, [Col. 0115A] and denies, for reasons he has adduced, that the same conclusion should be drawn concerning the Latin work.

I do not deny that Tertullian was speaking of a book which he had formerly composed in Greek concerning the shows; but neither can it be obscure that before that Greek work he had also discussed the same subject in Latin. For surely the man who admits that he had spoken about the shows “in Greek also” must be understood to have meant that he had previously treated that subject in another language as well. As for Hoffmann’s arguments to the contrary, I have already indicated that I shall discuss them later.

Nor should Dupin be heard, who thinks that this book, together with *On Idolatry*, was written about A.D. 202 or 203 by Tertullian when he was already inclining toward the errors of the Montanists (*dans la nouvelle* [Col. 0115B] *Bibliothèque des auteurs ecclésiastiques*, Paris ed., 1693, quarto, vol. I, pp. 91 and 108, note 1). I suspect that in this matter he was misled by the birthday of Geta Caesar, which I recall is mentioned in the *Acts of Perpetua and Felicity*, who are believed to have suffered death in that year. He thinks that this birthday was indicated by Tertullian in *On Idolatry*; but there is no trace of such a thing, at least in the passage he cited. And even if there were, the question would then arise whether Tertullian meant the anniversary of Geta Caesar’s accession or of his birth. Let it therefore remain established that *On the Shows* was published approximately at the beginning of A.D. 198.

## § 15.—The book *On Idolatry* is assigned to the same year. The conjectures of Tillemont and Hoffmann are dispelled.

[Col. 0115C]

The little book *On Idolatry* follows this one. When Tertullian wrote it, the Romans were plainly occupied in displaying the greatest joy at the happy condition of those times, and for that reason had adorned their very doorways with laurels and many lamps (compare ch. 15). That this was done in honor of the Emperor is demonstrated by the entire discussion which Tertullian adds concerning this question: whether the loyalty owed to the Emperor requires Christians to join the worshippers of the gods in various displays of rejoicing, in order to declare their joy at Caesar’s well-being.

If anyone asks to what period these things refer, I have no hesitation in saying: to the various signs of rejoicing with which the Romans applauded Severus, as it were, when he returned from the war against his rivals for the imperial power. For three times [Col. 0115D] we read in the ancient writers that something of this kind occurred under Severus’s rule: first, in A.D. 193, when Severus entered Rome to assume the imperial power after Julianus had been killed (Dio in Xiphilinus, book LXXIV, ch. 2, p. 1242 C); afterward, when he returned victorious from Gaul after Albinus had been overthrown (Herodian, book III, ch. 8; Spartianus, *Severus*, ch. 12); finally, at the decennial celebration of his reign, when he returned from the East (Dio, book LXXVI, ch. 1, p. 1272; and Herodian, book III, ch. 10). Every instance of this kind can be confirmed by many coins.

The first of these cannot have been meant by Tertullian. For, to say nothing of the fact that, while there was still a rival for the empire whom very many preferred, as Niger was after Julianus’s death, it could scarcely have been alleged against the Christians [Col. 0116A] that their failure to display loyalty toward the new Caesar was impious—as nevertheless appears from Tertullian’s entire discourse—Tertullian certainly refers to his book *On the Shows*, which we have said was not written until A.D. 198. Nor should anyone hesitate because he speaks not of the Greek book on this subject but of the Latin one. Since he wrote our little book in Latin, I do not doubt that, had he wished to indicate the Greek book *On the Shows*, he would have identified it by adding some qualifying word; and we have already said that one may argue without error from the Greek work to the Latin one as already written.

Nor does the final opinion seem admissible, which seeks to refer that public rejoicing to Severus’s decennial celebration. I do not now wish to discuss the fact that the very ὀρθοδοξία (orthodoxy) of this book, which we shall later vindicate for it, conflicts with this opinion; [Col. 0116B] yet there is some room for suspecting that public offices were still being conferred upon Christians at that time. For I doubt whether Tertullian would have discussed the question of how far such an office ought to proceed in adopting some religious custom that came at least somewhat close to the worship of the gods, if Christians were not then being admitted to magistracies. Yet I do not deny that I am somewhat doubtful whether public offices continued to be shared with Christians after Severus had issued his edict forbidding the adoption of the Christian religion.

There remains, therefore, the second type of celebration we mentioned, which clearly occurred after Severus’s return from Gaul. A new argument for this view, and certainly not a negligible one, may be drawn from [Col. 0116C] Tertullian’s recollection that, at the time when he wrote this book, astrologers had very recently been banned from the City and Italy, ch. 9. When this happened can be learned from Spartianus no less than from Tertullian himself.

First, let us hear our author, *Apology*, ch. 35: “But even those who are now daily revealed as associates or supporters of criminal factions . . . with what very recent laurels did they cover their doorposts? With what lofty and brilliant lamps did they cloud their vestibules? . . . Not in order to celebrate public joys, but to proclaim their own wishes within another’s festival . . . changing the prince’s name in their hearts. The same duties are discharged by those who consult astrologers, haruspices, augurs, and magicians concerning the lives of the Caesars.”

But in this passage of the *Apology* [Col. 0116D] Tertullian says that these signs of public joy had formerly been displayed by criminals, whereas in our book he indicates very plainly that they were being displayed at that very moment. The little book *On Idolatry* was therefore written before the *Apology*, and consequently before the beginning of A.D. 199, as will appear later.

It seems to me not unreasonable to suspect that astrologers were expelled from Rome and Italy when many people had consulted them concerning Caesar’s safety. Those who had inquired of magicians about such a matter were being condemned to death at approximately the time when Severus set out against the Parthians, that is, in A.D. 198. This is understood from a passage of Spartianus’s *Life of Severus*, ch. 15, which, because it has not been sufficiently examined by others, I append here:

“After Severus had undertaken the Parthian war, he transported his army from Brundisium and, continuing his journey, came into Syria and drove back the Parthians. [Col. 0117A] But afterward he returned to Syria, so that he might prepare himself and carry war against the Parthians. Meanwhile, at Plautianus’s instigation, he persecuted the surviving remnants of Pescennius’s party.—He also put many to death on the charge that they had consulted Chaldaeans or soothsayers concerning his safety, especially anyone whom he suspected and considered capable of ruling.”

If we compare this passage with the one we have just quoted from Tertullian, it appears that each writer, when speaking of the search for the remaining supporters of Severus’s adversaries, also defines the very time when these things occurred. This is otherwise in the account of the second matter, concerning the astrologers who had been summoned for consultation. But just as the former gives us a sure means of discovering the true date of the *Apology*, so the latter teaches us that those who had questioned magicians concerning Caesar’s safety were put to death at approximately the same time. From this, at least, [Col. 0117B] what we wished to establish is understood: in our book Tertullian was speaking of the second type of celebration under Severus.

Although, if we wished to investigate this matter more subtly and apply the principle by which experience has taught us to judge the order generally observed by Spartianus in narrating events—a principle which it would take too long to explain more fully here—we could determine that the punishments of those who employed magicians to learn the fortunes of Severus’s reign need not be regarded as later than the punishments imposed upon Niger’s surviving friends. Accordingly, since the latter occurred in A.D. 198, as we shall say in its proper place, the former may not unreasonably be assigned to the preceding year.

Since this is so, it is clear that the book *On Idolatry* belongs to A.D. 198. Here I do not think it necessary to answer [Col. 0117C] Tillemont’s contention that this book was written by Tertullian after he had himself become the founder of some new sect. This is maintained without any reason whatever and was refuted by Hoffmann in the cited dissertation, § 8, p. 31. Hoffmann agrees with us approximately concerning the arguments from which the book’s date must be elicited, yet nevertheless considers it to have been written by Tertullian as a Montanist. That opinion cannot stand if what we demonstrated above is true: that he did not attach himself to the discipline of Montanus until A.D. 200.

Nor did Hoffmann properly employ in support of that view Tertullian’s own passage (*On Idolatry*, ch. 22): “Many say that no one ought to disclose himself.” He thinks Tertullian meant to indicate that a question had already arisen between Montanists and orthodox Christians [Col. 0117D] concerning flight in persecution. Yet the very sequence of the discourse teaches that he is speaking about every form of denial of the faith, not solely about flight; and from the most ancient history of the early Christians—for example, from the cases of Ignatius and Polycarp—it appears that differing opinions on this matter existed even among Christians of the purer doctrine themselves.

But to claim that this controversy between the orthodox and the Montanists arose from the action of the soldier who refused the laurel crown, and consequently that this book was written after *On the Crown*, is conjecture, not proof. Such a mode of argument ought to be kept entirely apart from all scholarly discussion.

## § 16.—The *Apology* is demonstrated to have been composed in A.D. 199.
