The magicians, therefore, having received from those demons the power to produce certain things beyond the customary order of nature, “play,” Tertullian says, “with mountebank tricks” [Col. 0879C] (Idem, *Apologet.*, c. 23, p. 411). But what, someone will ask, are these “mountebank tricks”? Some reply that they are so called because itinerant magicians, surrounded by a gathering or circle of spectators, entertain them with tales or tricks. Yet we shall more readily be persuaded that by these words Tertullian means nothing other than magical tricks. For when he refutes Marcion’s error concerning Christ’s illusory flesh, he says: “Therefore you ought now to have brought Christ down, not from heaven, but from some mountebank gathering—that is, a gathering of magicians—and not as God apart from man, but as a man who was a magician” (Idem, *De carne Christi*, c. 5). Elsewhere, moreover: “Another kind of magic, which operates through miracles . . . . . . Hence also Simon Magus, though now a believer, since he still entertained some thought of the mountebank [Col. 0879D] sect” (Idem, *De idololatria*, c. 9). What, however, does “mountebank sect” mean there except the sect of magicians? Yet somewhere, so that we may conceal nothing, he seems to distinguish magicians from mountebanks, where he argues against heretics as follows: “There have also been noted the dealings of heretics with very many magicians, with mountebanks, with astrologers, and with philosophers—men, of course, devoted to curiosity” (Idem, at the end of *De praescriptione haereticorum*, c. 43). But do you not see that there he is merely identifying various classes of people devoted to magical arts? Is not, therefore, his meaning here in the *Apologeticum* this: magicians play at many miracles by mountebank—that is, magical—arts; in other words, they fabricate them and falsely boast that they themselves perform them, and thus make sport of and deceive people?

[Col. 0880A] Furthermore, by those detestable arts “they produce phantoms and bring the souls of the dead into false repute” (Idem, *Apologet.*, c. 23, p. 410). We have already observed that he said the phantoms of the Castors were produced by demons (above, art. 3); now, however, he asserts that the phantoms, or fictitious likenesses, of other dead persons are represented by magicians through the artifice of demons. But in his book *On the Soul* he explains this more fully in these words: “In that kind of magic which is believed to tear souls already at rest from the lower regions and exhibit them to sight, no other power is more effective in deception; plainly because both a phantom is furnished and a body is counterfeited, nor is it a great thing for him to delude the external eyes when it is very easy for him to blind the inward vision of the mind. In short, the dragons produced from the magicians’ rods appeared as bodies to Pharaoh and the Egyptians; but [Col. 0880B] the truth of Moses devoured the falsehood” (Tertullian, *De anima*, c. 57, p. 355). He therefore contends that in these magical evocations there is nothing but the fraud, deception, imposture, and falsehood of demons. Indeed, in the same place he proves this by reasoning as certain as it is evident: “Unless I am mistaken, we prove this deception of an evil spirit lurking under the identities of dead persons from actual instances as well: in exorcisms it sometimes affirms that it is some man from among the subject’s relatives, sometimes a gladiator or a beast-fighter, just as elsewhere it claims to be a god—caring for nothing more than to shut out precisely what we proclaim, lest we readily believe that all souls are consigned to the lower regions, and so that it may disturb faith in judgment and resurrection” (*Ibid.*).

But why, you will ask, are the souls of the dead then said by him to be “brought into false repute”? Some think this was said because they are subjected to disgrace and insult. But we fear [Col. 0880C] that Tertullian employs this verb in another sense, namely, to fabricate and falsely exhibit. For in the same book he proves that men’s eyes are deceived and a falsehood is presented to their sight, “which brings a perfectly uniform colonnade into false repute as narrower at its end” (*Ibid.*, c. 17); that is, it exhibits it falsely and represents it by a deception. He then adds that there is no deception of the eyes and no falsehood when the causes themselves are freed from the false representation of the objects of correct vision. No one, however, would say that the verb *infamare* is used by him there for a blemish with which the colonnade is branded. Nevertheless, if anyone proves that in the *Apologeticum* it is used in this second meaning, just as in the passages just cited it is used in the first, we shall not venture to resist altogether.

Moreover, the magicians, he says, “dash boys into the utterance of an oracle” [Col. 0880D] (Idem, *Apologet.*, c. 23, p. 411). And so indeed the reading stands in our manuscripts and in the more recent printed editions, “among, etc.” [The text appears defective here.] Less correctly, however, the earliest editions read “draw forth.” For Apuleius attests that it was the common custom and practice of his time for magicians to employ boys or slaves to deliver oracles; enchanted by them, these boys collapsed, and for that reason were “dashed down” (Apul., *Apolog.* I, p. 340).

By the same fraud, Tertullian says, they “send dreams” (Tertull., *ibid.*; *Dissertat. in Minut.*, c. 20, art. 2; *in Arnob.*, c. 18, art. I; *in Lactant.*, c. 32, art. 4), dreams undoubtedly foretelling future events. But we have said more about dreams of this kind in our earlier dissertations.

[Col. 0881A] Nor were men alone accustomed to divine by this art of the magicians—or rather of the demons, who abused their services—but also, our author says, “goats and tables” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 22, p. 404). But what, I ask you, were these tables? Were they the sacred tripods, as the pagans babbled, from which the Pythias, the priestesses of Apollo, were accustomed to deliver their oracles? Virgil certainly sang of them (Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. 3, v. 358):

> Trojan-born interpreter of the gods, who know  
> The powers of Phoebus, the tripods and laurels of Clarian Apollo, and the stars.

For Servius, commenting on these verses of the Poet, says: “The tripods were tables in the temple of Delphic Apollo, upon which the prophetesses of Phoebus prophesied” (Bulen., *De oraculis et vatibus*, c. 17 ff.). Those [Col. 0881B] tripods are known to everyone; if you desire to know more about them, you may consult Bulenger and others.

Nevertheless, because Tertullian appears there to have been speaking of things effected not through the agency of pagan priests but through that of magicians, if anyone proves that he is speaking of other tables, we shall assent without reluctance. Let him consider, however, that these words can be referred to the oracles of the gods no less than to the divining goats mentioned in the same place by Tertullian himself, which we discussed in the first volume of our *Apparatus* (tom. I, *Apparat.*, bk. I, c. 10, p. 772 ff.).

Since, moreover, Tertullian in this passage of the *Apologeticum* discusses the things done inside and outside the temples of the gods, he refutes those who objected that a distinction must be drawn according to differences of place, and that only those things customarily done in temples should be ascribed [Col. 0881C] to the gods (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 22, p. 407). “For when you pagans,” he addresses them, “raise these objections against us, you do not call them gods elsewhere”—that is, outside the temples (*Ibid.*); in other words, when you acknowledge that your gods neither exist nor act anywhere except in temples, by that very fact you declare them to be false gods. For the true God is everywhere.

Next, he says, the man who flies around the sacred towers of temples appears no differently “demented”—that is, as in Lucretius, Apuleius, and Lactantius (Lucret., bk. III, v. 396; Apuleius, *Apol.* I, p. 341; Lactant., bk. IV, *Divinae Institutiones*, c. 17, p. 441; Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 13, p. 343), insane, delirious, or out of his mind—than one who leaps across the neighboring roofs [Col. 0881D], or launches himself into the air by flying from roofs adjoining the temples. He therefore asserts that the madness is equal in the case of one who was said to have flown through the air by hurling himself from any sort of tower and in the case of another who did so from the towers of temples dedicated to false gods. I should like you to consider whether he was there referring, without naming him, to Simon Magus, whom he had mentioned earlier. Lucian, moreover, introduces Philocles asserting that he had seen a certain Babylonian who flew through the air (Lucian, *Dial. Philopseudes*, p. 831). Some add the example of a certain Hagarene trickster who, according to Nicetas Choniates, died after falling headlong while attempting to fly through the air from the highest tower of the Hippodrome. But from this example one can establish only [Col. 0882A] that it is not surprising if, in Tertullian’s time or earlier, rash tricksters attempted to fly in this manner.

He presses further that the frenzy and madness are equal in the case of one who cuts his genitals or arms and another who cuts his own throat (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 25, p. 421). The former was a Gallus or Archigallus, who was accustomed to cut his own limbs in the rites of the Mother of the Gods or of Bellona (*Dissert. in Minut.*, c. 20, art. 2, and *Dissert. in Lactant.*, c. 34, art. I). Tertullian speaks of this Archigallus a little later, while we have discussed him and the other Galli elsewhere: “And yet,” he says, “the outcome of the frenzy is comparable, and there is one and the same principle of incitement in them and in the man who cuts his own throat”—that is, who slaughters himself or drives his sword into his throat (Tertullian, [Col. 0882B] *ibid.*). In our manuscripts, however, *compara* occurs in place of *compar*; though this plainly seems corrupt, it is nevertheless not entirely without meaning of its own. Minucius Felix, as we have noted in the appropriate place, spoke somewhat differently but to the same purpose (*Dissertat. in Minuc.*, c. 20, art. 3): “The incitement of the demon is equal in them, but the manifestation of the frenzy is unequal.” For each uses the same argument against the pagans, by which they tear out their error by the roots. See the books of Cicero *On Divination* and Augustine *On the Divination of Demons*, which we cited in that dissertation.

From what has thus far been discussed, moreover, who does not see that Tertullian rightly draws this conclusion? If those things outside the usual order of nature which the gentiles attributed [Col. 0882C] to their gods were accomplished by the malignant arts, deceptions, and tricks of demons, then all these gods are false, the religion of those same gentiles is false, and that of the Christians is true. For the Christians worship the one omnipotent God, by whose power alone, superior to all others, true miracles can be performed.

## ARTICLE VI. By what argument Tertullian demonstrates that those who are thought to suffer from a god, who breathe upon altars, who conceive a divine power from the smell of burnt sacrifice, who labor by belching and pronounce oracles while panting—as well as the Virgin Caelestis, promiser of rains, and Aesculapius, who the other day supplied life to Socordius, Thenatius, and Asclepiodorus—when adjured by Christians openly answered that they were not gods but demons

[Col. 0882D] Tertullian indeed proceeds much further and, by an argument far more effective—one against which the pagans did not dare even mutter—demonstrates most evidently that all those miracles, oracles, and other things which they boasted had been performed by their gods beyond human powers were performed by the demons themselves, and consequently that those whom they called gods were in reality demons. For he proves this neither by the private authority of some man nor by reasoning devised by any human ingenuity, but by the gods’ own testimony, publicly given in the presence of any pagans whatever.

The substance of this entire argument is as follows (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 22, p. 405): by whatever demon or demons a man is driven [Col. 0883A], or whether he is agitated by their evil spirit, or the body of any man is besieged by them, those same demons are immediately expelled from all such persons solely by Christian adjuration and exorcisms, or merely by touch or breath. Yet at that time, although they were cast out unwillingly and not without the greatest disgrace, they were compelled openly and before all the pagans to confess that they were not gods, as the gentiles foolishly claimed, but mere demons, and that there was one God, whom the Christian faith worships and adores.

Since the matter is therefore thus, our Author rightly concludes from it (*Ibid.*, col. 405): “What is more manifest than this work? What proof is more trustworthy? The simplicity of truth stands in the midst.” For that public confession of the demons was either true or false and deceitful. If true, they must [Col. 0883B] undoubtedly be believed, and the pagans were obliged to confess that their gods were false and that the one whom the Christians venerate was the only true God. But if the demons were then lying, in the first place you will not easily persuade anyone of this. For everyone sometimes lies for his own honor, but never for his own disgrace and infamy. Furthermore, if they were lying and by that lie complied with the Christians, it follows with complete certainty that they possessed none of the divinity which the pagans vaunted. For the true God can neither lie nor comply with creatures to his own disgrace. Therefore that divinity is false which, as our Author says, “does something to its own disgrace for its rivals”—that is, which lies to its own disgrace for its enemies, the Christians, and by lying subjects itself to them (*Dissertat. in* [Col. 0883C] *Minut. Octav.*, c. 20, art. 3, and *in Lactant.*, c. 9, art. 2). How much force this argument possesses has been abundantly demonstrated in our preceding dissertations.

Accordingly, nothing remains to be explained except what occurs in somewhat obscure language in our Author’s text. There he boldly challenges the pagans to put to the test both this power of the Christians over demons and that public confession of demons denying that they were gods. How confidently he pronounces this you may learn from his very challenge (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 23, p. 24): “When adjured,” he says, “by Christians, if they do not confess themselves to be demons, shed the blood of that most impudent Christian on the spot”—that is, in that very place and at that very moment. [Col. 0883D] And lest anyone suspect any fraud or trickery, he demands for that test not some private place, or one suspect to the pagans, but a public place safe for them all (*Ibid.*). For he addresses them as follows: “Let someone be produced here before your tribunals who is known to be under the influence of a demon. When commanded to speak by any Christian whatsoever, that spirit will confess itself to be a demon as truly as elsewhere it falsely claims to be God.” In our manuscripts the reading is not “God,” but “Lord,” though the sense is no different.

He steadfastly asserts that this can be proved similarly not merely by this one test, but by any other test involving any person driven by a demon in any manner whatever. For he continues as follows (*Ibid.*): “Likewise let someone be brought [Col. 0884A] before the pagans’ tribunals from among those who are thought to suffer from a god, who, breathing upon altars, conceive the divine power from the smell of burnt sacrifice, who labor by belching, who pronounce oracles while panting. The Virgin Caelestis herself, promiser of rains; Aesculapius himself, revealer of medicines and, the other day, supplier of life to Socordius, Thenatius, and Asclepiodotus, who were about to die—all these, when adjured by Christians, will immediately confess themselves to be demons.”

Do you hear how many examples he advances, examples which the pagans could neither ever deny nor conceal in any way whatsoever? But because these statements, and particularly the latter ones, are covered by no small obscurity, let us endeavor to dispel it to the best of our ability. First, therefore, we shall observe that some light can be cast upon his preliminary statements by these verses of Virgil [Col. 0884B] (Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VI, v. 77 ff.):

> But the prophetess, not yet enduring Phoebus, raves wildly  
> In the vast cavern, if only she can shake the mighty god  
> From her breast; all the more he wears down  
> Her frenzied mouth, subduing her fierce heart, and shapes her by constraint.

Commenting on the first verse, Servius says: “‘Not yet enduring’: because she could not yet give full utterance to the words of the oracles.” A prophet, therefore, is said to “endure a god” when, inspired by the divine power of some god for the purpose of delivering oracles, she labored, was tormented, subdued, and constrained until she had shaken it from her breast. Indeed, prophets of this sort, as the pagans themselves said, were filled with an extraordinary frenzy.

Those whom Tertullian next calls “persons breathing upon altars” are undoubtedly *harioli*, concerning whom Donatus says in his observations on Terence (Donatus, on [Col. 0884C] Terence, *Phormio*, act 4, scene 4, v. 27): “A *hariolus* is one who prophesies under divine inspiration; he is called *fariolus* from *fata* and *fando*, since F is substituted for H, and likewise H for F, in many expressions. Or is it because they imitate aspiration in breathing out? For they are accustomed to expel the mortal soul by their breath so that they may receive the divine. Hence they also uttered a sound of this kind, *OE*, because the sound *OE* conveys the force of breathing out.” Again, Maro, whom we have already praised, sang of the Sibyl (Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VI, v. 45):

> They had come to the threshold, when the virgin said: “It is time  
> To ask the fates: the god—behold, the god!” As she spoke these words,  
> Before the doors, suddenly neither her face nor her color remained the same,  
> Nor did her hair remain arranged; but her breast panted,  
> And her fierce heart swelled with frenzy; she seemed larger,  
> And sounded no longer mortal, once she had been breathed upon  
> By the now nearer divine power of the god.

[Col. 0884D] Lucan likewise wrote of the Cumaean Sibyl as she attempted to prophesy (Lucan, bk. V, v. 91 ff.):

> Such was the Cumaean prophetess in her Euboean retreat;  
> Disdaining that her labor should serve many nations,  
> Proudly, from so vast a slaughter of destinies,  
> She selected Roman destinies with her hand; thus Phoenomoë,  
> Filled with Phoebus, labors. . . . . .  
>   
> . . . . . . . . . .  
>   
> Then for the first time foaming, frantic rage flows  
> From her mouth, with groans and murmurs clearly heard  
> Through her panting passage; then a mournful howl resounds  
> Through the vast caverns, and, the virgin now subdued,  
> Her final utterances ring out.

Accordingly, Cicero says (Cicero, *De divinatione*, bk. I, p. 274, line 35): “I believe there were also certain exhalations from the earth by which minds were inspired to pour forth oracles.” But we cited many other writers on these matters in [Col. 0885A] our dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius (*Dissertat. in Minut.*, c. 10, art. 2). Consider, I ask you, whether the following meaning of Tertullian may be gathered from them: prophets and soothsayers are thought to “suffer from a god” when, driven by him, they are tormented by heat and frenzy.

Those who “breathe upon altars,” moreover—that is, who blow and emit their breath upon the altars—conceive the *numen*, that is, the will and power of some god, from the *nidor*, that is, the odor of some thoroughly burnt victim. Next come those who, by belching—that is, by emitting belches and various panting breaths—strive to pour forth oracles and to belch them out and send them forth from their breast. But in our manuscripts and the earlier printed copies, *curantur* (“are cured”) is written in place of *conantur* (“strive”) (Tertull., *loc. cit.*). Its meaning may be that by belching and uttering an oracle they are said to be freed and cured of their labor [Col. 0885B] and torment. But the other reading appears preferable. For our author still seems to be speaking of people who are tormented and laboring, since he immediately adds: “Who pronounce oracles while panting” (*Ibid.*). The manuscripts and other editions printed before Rigault read “preface their utterances,” though with a meaning not very dissimilar. For he says that the god is then shaken out of them, as we have heard from Virgil and others, and that they pour forth oracles under the impulse of some god. But he demonstrates most clearly that all these things are done not by the operation of the true God, but, as has been said, by the deceptive tricks of demons. For when adjured by Christians, they were compelled to confess that they were demons, as we shall say again below.

Far more difficult to explain, however, are the words that follow. For who could readily say what [Col. 0885C] that “Virgin Caelestis” is whom he calls the “promiser of rains” (*Ibid.*)? Some think she is Juno, others Fortune, others Ceres, others Urania, and others Venus, because Herodotus, Pausanias, and Lucian mentioned Venus Urania, or Caelestis, and her temple (Herodot., bk. I, §§ 31 and 105; Pausanias, bk. I, pp. 14 and 17; Lucian, *dial. Mat. et musar.*, p. 973 [abbreviated title as in the source]). We, however, shall more readily be persuaded that Tertullian is there indicating Africa’s own local goddess, whom a little later he attests was worshiped in Africa (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 24, col. 416). For Augustine, certainly an eyewitness, recorded the following concerning that goddess: “In our youth we sometimes attended the spectacles and sacrilegious mockeries. We watched men seized by spirits, listened to musicians, and [Col. 0885D] delighted in the most shameful games presented to gods and goddesses—to the virgin Caelestis and Berecynthia, mother of all” (Augustine, *De civitate Dei*, bk. II, c. 4, p. 34). Again, later: “Where and when those consecrated to Caelestis heard precepts of chastity, we do not know. Yet before the very shrine where we saw that image installed, people gathered from every direction and, standing wherever each could find a place, watched with the closest attention the games being performed; their gaze alternated between the procession of prostitutes on one side and that virgin goddess being worshiped in supplication on the other, while obscenities were celebrated before her. We saw no modest mimes there, no more decorous performance; every service of obscenity was fulfilled. It was known what pleased the virgin divinity, and there was presented [Col. 0886A] what a matron, better instructed by the temple, might carry home” (*Ibid.*, c. 26, p. 56). He says still more about her elsewhere. But these passages prove more than sufficiently that among the Africans there was a temple of the virgin Caelestis, who was not only worshiped by them but, when invoked by them in a time of drought, promised rains and foretold that they would soon come. The opinion we propose is not so remote from the opinions of others that it cannot be reconciled with them. For that goddess was called by different names according to differences of place. If you desire a witness, here is Ambrose asserting it: “Her whom the Africans worship as Caelestis, the Persians as Mithras, and many as Venus, they worship under diversity of name, not diversity of divinity” (Ambrose, Epistle 18, *Against Symmachus*, § 30, p. 840).

The author then passes to Aesculapius, whom he [Col. 0886B] calls “the revealer of medicines,” and elsewhere “the one who first investigated remedies” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 23, p. 410, and *De corona*, c. 8; tom. I, *Apparat.*, p. 1192). In our earlier dissertations we showed by what reasoning the pagans ascribed the invention of medicine to him. But the clearer this point is, the greater indeed is the obscurity of the words that follow: “The other day,” he says, “supplier of life to Socordius, Thenatius, and Asclepiodotus, who were about to die” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 23, col. 24). It is astonishing how greatly these words have exercised the ingenuity of learned men. Yet this passage is certainly corrupt. For in our manuscripts, both those written by an older hand and those by a more recent one, the reading is: “The other day to those about to die” (or “to others who linger in dying”), “Socordius, Denatius, and Asclopiodotus is supplied.” In [Col. 0886C] editions older than Rigault’s: “At other times, supplier to those who linger in dying of Socordius, Denatius, and Asclepiodotus” (*Dissertat. in Arnob.*, c. 9, art. 2, and *in Lactant.*, c. 30, art. 5). Who does not see, however, that these variant readings increase the difficulty rather than remove it?

Some nevertheless suspect that these are expressions used by *agyrtae*, or tricksters, who falsely claimed to serve Aesculapius; they deem it better to pass over them in silence than to waste laughable and useless effort in explaining them. Others think they are the names of three men whose span of life Aesculapius was said to have prolonged shortly before. But this ought to be supported by at least some argument. Others, moreover, think that the three are names of herbs by which diseases could be driven away. For Pliny includes among various antidotes [Col. 0886D] *scordotin*, as he calls it, or *scordion* (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. II, c. 6, p. 395). Elsewhere he writes that this herb is useful for removing numerous diseases (Idem, bk. XXVI, c. 7, p. 451; c. 8, p. 471; and c. 10, p. 475). Yet what he says elsewhere certainly ought not to be omitted: “Against all these poisons and magical arts there will be, first, the Homeric *moly*, then *mithridation*, *scordotis*, and *centaurion*” (Idem, bk. XXV, c. 10, p. 412).

Some have thought that under the name *Asclepiodotus* Tertullian indicates *Asclepion*, about which we read the following transmitted in Pliny’s writings: “Panaces, by its very name, promises remedies for all diseases; it has numerous varieties, which are ascribed to gods. One, indeed, is surnamed *Asclepion*” (Idem, [Col. 0887A] *ibid.*, c. 4, p. 382), evidently after Aesculapius, the false god of the pagans, as its discoverer, or so named because of its efficacy.

The third name remains, and in explaining it everyone is at a complete loss. Those who read *Denatius* suppose that it denotes something customarily given to persons already at the point of death. Those who prefer to read *Thenatius* suspect that it signifies something else by which those breathing their last could be revived and, as it were, recalled from death to life. But these are mere conjectures, supported by no argument whatsoever.

Consider a little more attentively, therefore, I ask, whether these words of Julius Firmicus Maternus might cast some light on this obscure passage of our author: “Mercury, when positioned together with the sun at the horoscope, [Col. 0887B] will produce exorcists, and persons who free men suffering from an assault of demons by appropriate remedies, so that very great wealth accrues to them from those arts” (Jul. Firm., *Matheseos*, bk. III, c. 9). You plainly hear him speaking of Mercury, who was believed to cure with appropriate remedies men suffering from an assault of demons. May it not therefore be inferred from this that the remedies which Firmicus calls only generically “remedies” are called by Tertullian by their individual names, though these have been corrupted in his text—remedies by which exorcists made such by Mercury freed those afflicted by assaults of demons? For it is certain that throughout this entire passage Tertullian himself is speaking of persons driven by a demon whom certain men vainly attempted to cure by magical art. But the demon itself was immediately expelled from them when adjured by a Christian and was compelled [Col. 0887C] to disclose its name and confess the true God of the Christians. Nevertheless, if anyone suggests something better to us, we shall certainly not resist him.

Tertullian proceeds and steadfastly asserts that nothing is simpler or more trustworthy than this work of the Christians and the argument derived from it. For in it, he says, nothing can be suspected: “You will say that it is done by magic or some deception of that kind, if your eyes and ears permit you” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 23, col. 410). And thus Rigault did not hesitate to emend this passage of Tertullian without noting any variant reading. In the old editions, however, the text read: “Nothing . . . . . is done. You do not stand by what was said, if your eyes”; while in our two older manuscripts: “You say that nothing . . . . is done.” Then in other copies: “At the same time. You will not say, if your eyes,” etc. But however [Col. 0887D] difficult it may be to determine the genuine reading, it is nevertheless clear that our author’s meaning is evident. For he says that the pagans cannot either suspect or object that anything magical or deceptive is present in this Christian work, provided those same pagans are not blind and deaf through unconquered obstinacy. For they could easily see the simple manner in which the Christians acted and could also hear the demons confessing that they were not truly gods but unclean spirits, pre-condemned on account of their malice for the day of judgment together with all their worshipers and agents (*Ibid.*). Indeed, the demons themselves, while the pagans themselves listened, could not rebut, conceal, erase, or deny even the mark of their disgrace and condemnation. Therefore Tertullian [Col. 0888A] boldly challenges them to deny it sometime, if they ever can. “Let them refuse,” he says; our manuscripts read “They renounce,” while the ancient editions more correctly read “Let them renounce”—that is, though by an improper mode of expression, let them deny, reject, and disavow that they have been pre-condemned. But undoubtedly they could not deny it when, questioned and adjured by Christians, they were compelled to answer.

For how could they have denied that they were unclean spirits, when this ought to have been understood, as Tertullian says, “from their foods—blood and smoke and the stinking pyres of animals—and from the most impure tongues of the prophets themselves” (*Ibid.*)? Consult Augustine, if you please, and, if you have leisure, read what he related from Porphyry’s letter to Anebo concerning the impious and fraudulent arts of demons and [Col. 0888B] deceitful spirits (Augustine, *De civitate Dei*, bk. X, c. 11, p. 247 ff.). For there he writes that they rejoice in sacrificial odors, are enticed by victims—that is, slaughtered animals—and are compelled to do what men wish. You will also note other things there by which much of what we have thus far discussed concerning the tricks of those same demons can be confirmed and elucidated. Tertullian added that they are shown to be unclean spirits by the most impure tongues of the prophets themselves: either of those who breathed upon altars, as we said before, and were polluted by smoke and the blood of victims, or of those who were compelled by Christian adjuration to confess that the divine power they had conceived was not a god but a demon. Minucius Felix, moreover, used the word “polluted” when speaking of the statue of any false god: “It is polished,” he says, “by a polluted man” [Col. 0888C] (Minucius, *Octavius*, p. 307). And Prudentius later sang of Apollo:

> Soon, polluted, he wept for the man slain by the crooked discus.

Prudentius, *Hymn of St. Romanus*, v. 205.

## ARTICLE VII. How admirably Tertullian confirms the foregoing argument from the fact that demons were expelled in various ways from the bodies of the men whom they possessed, either by exorcists or by other Christians, whom they feared and hated and to whom they were subject; in what sense their condition is called desperate; and concerning the delay of punishment, by which they are said to possess the consolation of indulging their malignity

Tertullian presses the pagans still more sharply and makes plain to everyone how great was the Christians’ power to put demons to flight and drive them from the bodies [Col. 0888D] of possessed men, and how great their dominion over them: “For at our touch and breath,” he says, “seized by the contemplation and representation of that fire, they even depart from bodies at our command, unwillingly and in pain, and blushing in your presence” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 23, col. 410). The pagans therefore could certainly deny neither the demons’ weakness nor the Christians’ marvelous power over them by any reasoning, unless through an utterly hardened and invincible obstinacy; by that power the Christians demonstrated the truth of their religion more clearly than the noonday sun.

But someone will perhaps ask whether Tertullian is speaking there only of our exorcists. For when Cyprian writes to [Col. 0889A] Magnus about what was customarily done in his own time, he specifically attests that demons were put to flight by exorcists: “This is also done today, so that through exorcists the devil is scourged, burned, and tormented by a human voice and divine power” (Cyprian, Epistle 69 to Magnus, p. 187). “Did not Tertullian himself,” another will say, “also write these very words about the same exorcists in another book? ‘An example occurred, as the husband of the woman bears witness: she went to the theater and returned from there with a demon. And so, during the exorcism, when the unclean spirit was reproached for having dared to attack a believer, it said: “I acted consistently and most justly; I found her in my domain”’” (Tertullian, *De spectaculis*, c. 26). Did he not also say elsewhere: “With what mouth will a Christian incense-dealer—that is, one who burns incense to idols—if he passes through temples, spit and [Col. 0889B] blow upon the smoking altars for which he himself has provided? With what assurance will he exorcise his own fosterlings—that is, the demons to whom he provides his house as a storeroom? If indeed he has cast out a demon, let him not congratulate himself on his faith. For he did not cast out an enemy. He should easily have been able to obtain a favor from one whom he feeds every day” (Idem, *De idololatria*, c. 2).

Someone could indeed reply that in both those passages of Tertullian it is not exorcists who are named, but exorcisms or adjurations, which undoubtedly could be employed by all Christians. Yet no one can deny that demons were expelled from men’s bodies chiefly by exorcists, as Cyprian says, and that this was sufficient for our Author to prove the Christians’ power over those same demons.

[Col. 0889C] But he proceeds further, and in the cited passage of the *Apologeticum* he speaks generally of all Christians who cast out demons. For he unhesitatingly affirms that so great was the multitude of gentiles whom Christians freed from assaults of demons that, had this benefit been withdrawn, the Christians would have avenged themselves amply upon the gentiles for their monstrous cruelty. Here are his own words: “Who would snatch you away from those hidden enemies who everywhere ravage your minds and health—from assaults of demons, I mean—which we drive from you without payment or reward? This alone would have sufficed for our vengeance: that henceforth free possession of you should lie open to unclean spirits” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, c. 37, col. 460). Moreover, he wrote to Scapula in this manner: “And how many honorable men—for we say nothing of common people—have been cured either of demons or of illnesses?” (Idem, *Ad Scapulam*, c. 4). Who, however, will deny that these things were done not by exorcists alone but by all Christians? For the number of exorcists was then too small for them alone to have freed so great a multitude of men from assaults of demons.

Accordingly, when the pagans objected that Christians were wholly useless to the commonwealth, he refuted them thus: “At what value do you reckon those who now drive demons out of you?” (*Ibid.*, c. 43). And so indeed the text stands in our manuscripts. No less acceptable, however, is the reading of the ancient editions, “How many do you have?” and that of La Cerda’s edition, “When?” For Tertullian’s meaning is: If Christians [Col. 0890A] are useless to the commonwealth, at what value, then, do you estimate them, or at what price do you hold those who drive so many demons from you so often?
