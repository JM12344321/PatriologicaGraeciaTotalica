[Col. 0585D] 1. Now, pitiable nations, our defense desires to engage with you concerning your gods, challenging your own conscience to judge whether they are truly gods, as you wish, or falsely so, as you do not wish to know. For this is the material of human error, fashioned by its artificer, lest [Col. 0586D] ignorance of error [Note: [Col. 0585D] “Ignorance of error ...”: “be removed,” [Col. 0586D] or something similar, must be supplied. Truth is besieged by a mighty host [Note: [Col. 0587D] “Truth is besieged by a mighty host.” Since Christians could not be defeated unless they were overwhelmed by falsehood, the nations, so far as they could, suppressed the truth and defended their own cause, though it was desperate, with endless words and slanders. Whomever [Note: [Col. 0587D] “Whomever.” The exemplar reads “whatever.” RIG.] it wishes, it takes from among its very adversaries as allies and protectors for itself, and casts down that entire multitude of assailants [Note: [Col. 0587D] “Multitude of assailants.” The book of Agobard reads “assailing multitude.” We have already observed, at *Apologeticum* 25, “Greece the conqueror,” that this reading can be defended. It is against these things, therefore, that we must contend: against the institutions of the ancestors, the authority of received customs [Note: [Col. 0588D] “The authority of received customs.” Things long since accepted put forward the authority of public consensus. Accordingly, in proceeding step by step through your own commentaries, which you have taken from every kind of theology, and since among you the authority of writings is greater in matters of this kind than that of realities, I have chosen, for brevity’s sake, the works of Varro [Note: [Col. 0588D] “The works of Varro.” Lest he seem to evade the issue, and in order to bind the Gentiles by every pledge, he examines their own testimonies and the authors most highly approved among them—namely Varro, the most learned of all Romans. If I ask him who introduced the gods, he designates either philosophers, peoples, or poets. For he divided the classification of gods into three kinds: one is physical, which the philosophers discuss; another mythical, which circulates among the poets; and a third national, which the several peoples have each adopted for themselves. Therefore, since the philosophers have pieced together the physical kind by conjectures, the poets have drawn the mythical kind from fables, and the peoples have arbitrarily presumed the national kind, where is truth to be placed? In conjectures? [Col. 0587C] But their conception is uncertain. In fables? But their account is shameful. In acts of adoption? But the adoption is indiscriminate and municipal. In short, among the philosophers all is uncertain because it varies; among the poets all is unworthy because it is disgraceful; among the peoples all is indiscriminate because it is voluntary.

Now divinity, if you examine the true kind, is by definition such that it is neither inferred through uncertain arguments, nor contaminated by unworthy fables, nor judged by indiscriminate acts of adoption. For it must be held to be as [Col. 0588A] it is: certain, unimpaired, and universal, because it plainly belongs to all. Otherwise, what God am I to believe in? One whom conjecture appraised, history vaunted, or a city chose? It is far more honorable to believe in no one at all than in one who is doubtful, shameful, or adopted.

2. But the authority of the natural philosophers pleads its case as the chattel of wisdom. Certainly it is the pure wisdom of philosophers [Note: [Col. 0588D] “The pure wisdom of philosophers.” That which savors of nothing Christian and nothing divine. RIG.], whose weakness is attested by this initial diversity of opinions, arising from ignorance of the truth. But what wise man is devoid of truth, if he is ignorant of God, the father and lord of wisdom and truth itself? ... Elsewhere there is the divine pronouncement of Solomon (Prov. IX. 10; Ps. Among the Gentiles it was commonly said, “Fear first made gods in the world”.... At that time there was scarcely anyone who did not think and speak in this way. Philosophy, however, did not clearly succeed in this. For although, through its curiosity to inspect literature of every kind, it may seem to have made incursions even into the divine Scriptures, as being more ancient, and to have taken certain things from them, nevertheless ... as [Note: [Col. 0588D] “Nevertheless ...” The lacuna appears to require completion as follows: “Nevertheless, inasmuch as they believe, they prove that they either despised everything or did not believe everything.” LE PR.], they prove that they either despised everything or did not believe everything.... For elsewhere too the simplicity of truth, through over-scrupulousness ... [Note: [Col. 0587C] Havercamp, at *Apologeticum* 47: “frequently.”] wavers in faith; and thus, when the lust for glory was added, they altered it into works of their own ingenuity, through which even what they had discovered passed into uncertainty, and a flood of arguments was produced from one or two drops of truth. For when they had merely found it, they did not set it forth as they had found it, but began to dispute about its quality [Note: [Col. 0588C] Rigault: “they found—the quality”; compare *Apologeticum* 47.], its nature, and even its location: the Platonists make God the caretaker, arbiter, and judge of affairs; [Col. 0588C] the Epicureans make him idle and inactive—and, so to speak, no one at all; the Stoics place him outside the world, the Platonists within it. Since they had not admitted him completely, they could neither know nor fear him, nor therefore become wise: they had plainly strayed from the beginning of wisdom, that is, fear of God.

There survive testimonies to how thoroughly the philosophers were both ignorant and doubtful concerning Divinity. When Diogenes was asked what goes on in heaven, he said, “I have never gone up there.” [Col. 0589A] Again, when asked whether gods existed, he said, “I do not know—except that it is expedient that they should.” Thales of Miletus, when Croesus asked what he thought about the gods, requested several successive periods for deliberation and finally gave no answer. Socrates himself, as though certain, denied the existence of those gods. The same Socrates, as though certain, ordered that a cock be sacrificed to Aesculapius. And therefore, when philosophy is found to be so uncertain and inconsistent in defining God, how could it ... determine the ... of him whom it did not clearly grasp? We are speaking of the world.... For they force it into the physical kind of theology: ... they have handed down gods in such a way that Dionysius the Stoic divides the gods into three classes. One kind, he holds, is manifest, such as the sun and moon ...; another does not appear, such as Neptune; the remaining kind is said to have passed from humanity into divinity, such as Hercules and Amphiaraus. [Col. 0589B] Arcesilaus likewise presents a threefold form of divinity: Olympians, Stars, and Titans; from Heaven and Earth, and from these, through Saturn and Ops, came Neptune, Jupiter, Orcus, and the rest of the succession. Xenocrates the Academic makes two divisions: Olympians and Titans, who derive from Heaven and Earth. Most Egyptians believe in four gods: the Sun and Moon, Heaven and Earth. Democritus suspects that the gods arose together with the remaining celestial fire; Zeno holds that nature is fashioned after its likeness. Hence Varro too makes fire the soul of the world, so that fire governs everything in the world just as the soul does in us. But that is utterly vain. For “when it is in us,” he says, “we are alive; when it has departed, we die.” Therefore, when fire departs from the world in lightning flashes, the world too dies.

3. With these matters thus disposed of [Note: [Col. 0589D] “With these matters thus disposed of.” After that crowd of philosophers, who either thought most wrongly about God or abolished the gods altogether, he argues with the strongest reasoning from the very matter under inquiry—namely the origin of the gods, immortality, creation, and so forth. LE PR.], we see that this physical theology was suborned for the purpose of maintaining that the elements are gods, although it alleges that other gods were also born from them—for gods could be born only from gods. Since we shall examine these more fully in their proper place among the poets’ myths, nevertheless what must meanwhile be discussed concerning them, insofar as it pertains to the present class, and what must be drawn from the present class concerning them ... we are going to show: namely, that those said to have been born from the elements can in no way be regarded as gods. Thus it will already have been prejudged from this point that the elements are not gods, since those said to have been born from the elements are not gods. Likewise, by demonstrating that things which have come into being are not gods, we shall prepare the way for that class of their offspring, showing that those whose parents—that is, the elements—are not gods cannot rightly be defended as gods. It is a settled principle that a god is born from a god, just as no god is born from what is not a god.

Therefore, since this world contains the elements—to deal summarily with the whole before treating its parts, for whatever its condition is, the same will certainly be the condition of its elements and members—it must either have been instituted by someone, according to Plato’s humane view, or by no one, according to the hardness of Epicurus. And if it was instituted, then, since it has a beginning, it will also have an end, so that at one time it did not exist before its beginning and at some time will not exist after its end. It certainly cannot be regarded as a god when it lacks the substance [Col. 0590A] of divinity, that is, eternity, which is understood to be without beginning and end. But if it was not instituted at all and is therefore to be regarded as a god, because, like God, it admits neither a beginning nor an end of itself, how does anyone assign generation to the elements which they wish to make gods, when the Stoics deny that anything is born to God? Again, how do they wish those whom they report as born from the elements to be regarded as gods, when they deny that God is born?

Accordingly, whatever is true of the world will be ascribed to the elements—I mean heaven and earth, the stars and fire—which Varro has vainly proposed should be believed to be gods and the parents of gods, contrary to the denied generation and birth of God. Varro also declared heaven and the stars to be living beings. If that is so, they must also be mortal, in accordance with the nature of a living being; for although it is agreed that the soul is immortal, this will be permissible only for the soul itself, [Col. 0590B] not also for that to which it is attached, namely the body. No one, moreover, will deny that the body is mortal, since we both touch these things and are touched by them, and see that actual bodies fall from them. Therefore, if, once account of the soul has been set aside, the condition of bodies is mortal, the elements certainly are not gods.

And yet why do the elements seem to Varro to be living beings [Note: [Col. 0590D] “And yet why do the elements seem to be living beings?” He touches here upon Varro, who had taught that the elements were living beings because they moved. He charges him with ignorance in this matter, for Varro ought to have sought the mover and risen by the force of reason to that first principle. LE PR.]? Because they move. And lest the counterargument be proposed that many other things move—wheels, wagons, and other machines—he anticipates it of his own accord by saying that they were believed to be living beings because they moved by themselves, with no mover or driver appearing outside them, as there plainly appears to be someone who propels a wheel, turns a wagon, or regulates a machine. Therefore, unless they are living beings, they do not move by themselves. Yet in alleging that the agent does not appear, Varro shows what he ought to have sought: [Col. 0590C] namely, the artificer and arbiter of the motion. For it does not immediately follow that something does not exist merely because, since we do not see it, we do not believe it exists. On the contrary, what is not seen must be investigated all the more deeply, so that we may know the nature of what is seen. Otherwise, if only those things which appear are believed to be gods because they appear, how do you also accept as gods those who do not appear? But if those who do not exist appear to exist, why should there not also exist those who do not appear? I mean the mover of celestial things.

Let them, then, even be living beings because they move by themselves, and let them move by themselves because they are moved by no other; nevertheless they are not immediately gods because they are living beings, nor therefore because they move by themselves. Otherwise, what prevents all living beings, since they move by themselves, from being regarded as gods? ... Such folly, to be sure, is permitted to the Egyptians.

[Col. 0590D] 4. Some say that they were called gods for this reason: because θέειν (“to run forward”) and σείεσθαι (“to be moved”) mean “to run forward” and “to move.” Certainly that term is not expressive of any majesty, for it was formed from running and motion, not from the designation of divinity. But since even that one God whom we worship is called θεὸς (“God”), although neither any motion nor any running on his part is apparent—because he is not visible to anyone—it is manifest that this term ... adopted and proper, because by its very nature [Col. 0591A] it was devised for divinity.... Given the subtlety of its interpretation, it is more plausible that they were called θεοὺς (“gods”) not from running and motion, but through a loan from the title of the true God, so that those whom you had likewise fabricated as gods you might call θεοὺς (“gods”). Finally, proof of how much this is so is available in the fact that you commonly call all your gods θεοὺς (“gods”), including those in whom no function involving running or motion is discerned. Accordingly, if those who are equally θεοὺς (“gods”) are equally motionless, both the interpretation of the term and the opinion of divinity shaped from running and motion are abandoned and annulled.

But if this name is proper and simple to divinity, and no derivative interpretation of it is found in that God, then, as to the other things which you wish to be gods, show that there is also among them a partnership in the relevant quality, so that a fellowship of the name may rightly be established by community of substance. Now that ΘΕΟΣ (“GOD”), by this fact alone, [Col. 0591B] that he is not manifest, is exempt from comparison with those things which are manifest to sight and sense. But for sense it is sufficient that there is testimony proclaiming the difference between the hidden and the manifest. If the elements are openly displayed to everyone, while God, by contrast, is displayed to no one, how will you be able to bring the things you see into comparison with that aspect which you have not seen? Since, therefore, you have no means of joining them by sense ... by reason, why do you join them by a word, so that you may join them also in power?

For behold, Zeno too [Note: [Col. 0591D] “For behold, Zeno too.” He means Zeno of Citium, that distinguished philosopher who was presented by the Athenians with a golden crown and honored by them with a magnificent tomb, as Diogenes Laertius relates; this was sanctioned by public decree, and the decree was inscribed by the public scribe upon two columns. But what have I to do with physical arguments? One ought to have ascended ... from the condition of the world, not descended through uncertainties.... For Varro too recalls it, saying that belief in the divinity of the elements arose, furthermore, because absolutely nothing could be born, nourished, or advanced toward human life and the sowing of the earth without their support; indeed, neither bodies nor souls themselves [Col. 0592B] could have been adequately sustained without the balanced mixture of the elements. By this mixture the habitation of the world is furnished, bound together by the conditions of its circles, except where extreme cold or heat has denied habitation to human beings.

Accordingly, the following are believed to be gods: the sun, which enriches the day from its own resources, brings crops to maturity with its heat, ... and preserves the year through its seasons; the moon, the solace of nights and the guardian of months through its governance; likewise the stars, certain marks for noting changes of time; finally, heaven itself, beneath which are all things; earth, upon which are all things; and whatever among them acts in concert for human advantages. Nor is belief in divinity thought to belong to the elements merely because of their benefits, but also because of the contrary things which customarily occur as though from their wrath and offense: lightning bolts, hailstorms, scorching heat, pestilential winds, floods, and openings and movements of the earth. And they are rightly believed to be gods, it is said, because their nature must be honored in prosperity and feared in adversity, as being mistress, plainly, of helping and harming.

Now if people feel this way in ... intercourse, they do not likewise offer thanks or direct complaints to the things themselves by which they are helped or harmed, but to those under whose direction and power the operation of those things proceeds. For in your pleasures you do not award the prize-crown to the pipes or lyres, but to the artist who regulates the pipes and lyre by the power of harmonious skill. Likewise, when someone is in ill health, you do not remember gratitude toward the wool, antidotes, or poultices themselves, but toward the physicians through whose labor and skill the remedies are effective. Again, in adversity, those who are pierced by iron do not accuse the sword itself or [Col. 0593A] the spear, but the enemy or robber. And those whom ... [Note: [Col. 0593C] Rigault supplies: “And those whom roofs crush do not blame the tiles,” etc.] do not charge the tiles or roofing-slabs, but their age; just as shipwrecked people do not impute their disaster to rocks and waves, but to the storm. Rightly so. For it is certain that whatever happens must be ascribed not to that through which it happens, but to the one by whom it happens, because the author of the deed is the one who determined both that it should happen and through what it should happen.

And in all matters there are these three headings: what exists, that through which it exists, and the one by whom it exists; because the one who wills that something should come to be is prior, and then that which can accomplish it must be found. Thus in other matters you act rightly by considering the author. But in physical matters your rule, by which you judge wisely in other things, operates contrary to nature: you remove the supreme rank of the author and regard the things that happen, not the one by whom they happen.... Thus it comes about that you believe the powers and ... of the elements to be ... although they are forms of service and functions.

In this investigation we are not tending toward any other ... of the artificer within and the lord, but toward the service of the elements ... the works of theirs which you make into powers. But gods do not serve; therefore, those things which serve are not gods. Or let them show that such things ordinarily occur, so that freedom may be established from license for indiscriminate action, dominion from freedom, and divinity understood from dominion. For if they remember that all these things above us, according to fixed courses, lawful progressions, proper intervals, and equal alternations, occur in the likeness of a law—with times revolving and the successions of seasons being carried out—then from the very observance of their conditions, the fidelity and constancy of their operations, ... and the careful ordering of changes and the remembrance of recurring things, let them persuade you that some dominion presides over them, to which the entire business of the world appears to render obedience as it advances [Col. 0593C] toward the benefit and ... of the human race [Note: [Col. 0593D] Gothofredus: “benefit or increase”? Havercamp, at [Col. 0594C] *Apologeticum* 13: “benefit and injury”?]. For one cannot say that these elements perform and regulate these things for themselves and arrange nothing for humanity’s sake, when you defend the divinity of the elements precisely because you perceive yourself either helped or harmed by them; for if they provide for themselves, you owe them nothing.
