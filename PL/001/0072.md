PAM.], and savagery have been permitted to us, let us go to the amphitheater. If we are such as we have been taught to be, let us delight in human blood.

“It is good when the guilty are punished.” Who but a guilty person would deny this? And yet an innocent person cannot rejoice in another’s punishment, since it is more fitting for an innocent person to grieve that a human being like himself has become so guilty that he must be expended so cruelly. But who guarantees to me that it is always the guilty who are sentenced to the beasts or to some other punishment, and that innocence too is not subjected to it [Note: [Col. 0651C] “That innocence is not,” Paris.], whether through the vengeance of the judge, the weakness of the defense, or the pressure of interrogation?

How much better it is, therefore, not to know when the wicked are punished, lest I also know when the good perish—if indeed they have any taste for goodness [Note: [Col. 0652C] “Lest I also know when the good perish, if indeed they have any taste for goodness. Certainly, gladiators who are innocent come to the games,” etc. He speaks of pagans who are condemned to the beasts by the sentences of pagan magistrates. He therefore says: “But who guarantees to me that it is always the guilty who are condemned, and that the innocent are not sometimes condemned as well? How much better, therefore, not to know when the wicked are punished, lest I also know when the good perish.” But here, lest some Christian object, “Then there are good men among the pagans,” he corrects himself: “If indeed they have any taste for goodness,” he says—that is, “if there is indeed any savor of goodness in people who do not belong to Christ!” [Col. 0652D] He then adds: “Certainly, gladiators who are innocent come to the games to become victims of public pleasure.” Now he does not call them “good,” but only “innocent,” because they have not been condemned but come voluntarily to the games. RIG.]. Certainly, gladiators who are innocent come to the games to become victims of public pleasure. As for those who are condemned to the games, what sort of correction is it that, for a lesser offense, they should advance into the rank of murderers?

But I have made these replies to the pagans. Far be it from a Christian to require any longer instruction about turning away from a spectacle of this kind. Yet no one can fully describe all these things unless he is still watching them; I would rather fail to complete the account than retain the memory.

## CHAPTER XX

How vain, then—indeed, how desperate—is the argument of those who, unquestionably as an evasion intended to admit pleasure [Note: [Col. 0651C] “By an evasion,” Rig.] [Note: [Col. 0652C] “For pleasure,” Jun.], allege that no mention of abstinence from it is specifically [Note: [Col. 0652C] Fran. adds “or locally.”] laid down in the Scriptures that directly forbids a servant of God to attend gatherings of this kind.

I recently heard a novel defense [Note: [Col. 0652C] “I recently heard a novel defense by a certain lover of games.” Thus in *On Baptism*: “I have heard, with the Lord as witness, a claim of this kind,” etc. RIG.] from a certain lover of games [Note: [Col. 0652D] “A certain lover of games.” In the same way he said “your lovers of games” in *On the Soldier’s Crown*. Rhenanus correctly interprets the phrase there as referring to those Christians who still delighted in the attractions of spectacles, or in the pleasantness of games. PAM.]. “The sun,” he says, “and indeed God himself, looks down from heaven and is not contaminated.” Certainly the sun casts its rays even into a sewer [Note: [Col. 0652C] “Turned aside,” Jun., from a manuscript.] and is not defiled. Would, however, that God saw none of the crimes of human beings, so that we might all escape judgment! But he sees robberies; he sees falsehoods, adulteries, frauds, idolatries, and the spectacles themselves. Therefore we shall not look at them, lest we be seen by him who sees all things.

You compare, O man [Note: [Col. 0652C] “You should compare,” Seml.], the accused and the judge: the accused, who is guilty because he is seen; the judge, who is judge because he sees. Do we therefore devote ourselves to frenzy outside the boundaries of the Circus as well, to immodesty outside the doors of the theater, to insolence outside the stadium, and to mercilessness outside the amphitheater, merely because God has eyes even outside the vaulted chambers, tiers [Note: [Col. 0652D] “Outside the tiers.” That there were tiers in the Circus is evident from Suetonius. Hence, according to Livy and Pliny, came that law of Otho that no one possessing less than 400,000 sesterces should watch from the fourteen tiers; Pliny elsewhere calls it the Julian theatrical law. PAM.], and awnings [Note: [Col. 0652D] “And awnings.” So the Codex Agobardinus reads. This word appears to denote the coverings used in theaters [Col. 0653C] and amphitheaters for ornament and to exclude the burning rays of the sun. These were sometimes purple, a color with which the finest wool—as is likely—was dyed, such as the wool from Apulia. Martial: “Apulian fleeces of the first quality.” Hence the awnings were also called *Apularia*. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0652C] Wouw. corrects it to Ἐπαυλίας (*Epaulias*).]? We are mistaken: what God condemns is excused nowhere and never. What is always and everywhere unlawful is nowhere and never lawful. This is the integrity of truth [Note: [Col. 0653C] “The integrity of truth.” Truth ought to be incorrupt; it is also sometimes granted the name of virginity. Hence heretics are said to traffic in or corrupt the truth, τὴν ἀλήθειαν καπηλεὺειν (“to traffic in the truth”), canon 2 of the Council in Trullo, [Col. 0653D] where the canons both of the Apostles and of earlier councils, to which adherence is decreed, are enumerated. LE PR.], the fullness of discipline owed to it, the constancy of fear, and the fidelity of obedience: not to alter one’s sentence or vary one’s judgment. That which is truly good or evil cannot be something different. All things are fixed under the authority of God’s truth.
