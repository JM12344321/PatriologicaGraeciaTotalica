[Col. 1107B] We have now corrected “let the consul proceed into the city” for “into the world.” And shortly afterward, “This is not humility” for “This humility of yours.”]

Like a Gaul, be bound about its feet with soft papyrus.  
A sufficiently astonishing thing, cast down from the highest summit!  
If a consul should proceed into the city as an initiate of Isis,  
He will become an object of public laughter. Who would not laugh at you,  
Who once were consul but are now a servant of Isis?  
What shamed you in the first place does not shame you in the second:  
You allow your own character to be condemned in shameful hymns,  
While the populace answers you and the Senate assails you.  
In your own house you were formerly portrayed bearing the fasces;  
Now you bear the sistrum as well, with the face of a dog.  
This is not humility, but the mere image of humiliation.

[Col. 1106C] Those memorials will always remain in your halls.  
A public report has also reached our ears  
That you said: “Goddess, I erred; forgive me; I have returned.”  
Tell me, if you can: when you repeatedly made such requests  
And sought forgiveness, what words did she speak with you?  
You are truly out of your mind, since you follow those who are out of theirs.  
You repeat these things again, yet do not perceive that you are sinning.  
Consider what you deserve. Perhaps you would be less blameworthy  
If you knew only this and remained in this error;  
But since you have entered the portals of the law that reveres truth,  
And for a few years have had the opportunity to know God,  
Why do you retain what must be abandoned, or abandon what must be retained?  
You worship nothing while you worship everything, nor do you reconsider  
In your heart how truth differs from falsehood, and light from shadows.  
You play the philosopher, though every opinion changes you;  
[Col. 1106D] For if a popular breeze should stir your temper,  
You will become a Jew as well, and be regarded as wholly unstable.

Pardon my words: lofty wisdom gives no pleasure.  
Everything excessive falls into its opposite; heat and cold  
[Col. 1107A] Produce the same effect: this burns, and so does that;  
Thus darkness takes away sight, and so does the opposing sun;  
A freezing bath and a scalding one do equal harm;  
Food nourishes the body, and food corrupts the body;  
And anything extended beyond its limit diminishes its own power.  
Indeed, if you sit, rest provides great relief from labor;  
If you sit too long, it becomes labor. For the poet Maro  
Presented it as a punishment: “Unhappy Theseus sits [Note: [Col. 1107B] **Unhappy Theseus sits and will sit forever, etc.** This passage is Virgilian, from book VI of the *Aeneid*. I read without hesitation [below]: “And let him not impute it to any accidents.”], and will sit  
Forever.” Even what is useful always becomes harmful when prolonged.  
Long banquets harm; long fasts exhaust.  
So too an unscrupulous sect makes excessive wisdom into folly.

“My goddess taught me thus,” he said, “a pleasing moderation.”  
But you now care neither for a sect nor for moderation.  
A steadfast mind, however, is overturned by no tempest,  
And simplicity itself never devises any evil.  
Hence sincere faith will enjoy eternal faithfulness,  
[Col. 1107B] While deceit, on the contrary, will be tormented in lasting fire.

[Col. 1108A] Choose what you wish, so that you may escape the deserved expiations.  
Yet let the believer earn this pardon, I say, in this way:  
It will be a slight offense if you are unwilling to know the truth;  
It will be no slight offense if you abandon the truth after knowing it.  
But perhaps ripe old age, correcting you,  
Calls you back to better things once you have had your fill of those errors;  
For time changes evils; time digests all things.

Therefore, when mature reflection has brought you back,  
Learn to keep faith with God, lest perhaps you twice  
Fall into one and the same lapse; for that saying is truly spoken:  
He who, after striking his foot, does not know how to avoid  
The stone a second time, and carelessly injures his limbs again,  
Must blame himself, and not impute it to any accidents.

Correct [Note: [Col. 1108B] **Correct your transgression with confidence, etc.** *Fidamen*, for confidence or hope, unquestionably has a more Tertullianic than Cyprianic flavor.] your transgression with confidence; correct your mind.  
It was enough to sin once; cease to be afraid:  
He who repents of what he formerly was will not remain guilty.

# A POEM ON JONAH AND NINEVEH, WITH THE ANNOTATIONS OF FRANCIS JURETUS  
(C, G, S)

## ARGUMENT

[Col. 1107B]

This poem, never previously seen or read, was discovered in an ancient manuscript codex made available to me by P. Pithoeus, jurisconsult, a man born for the preservation of ancient authors. Two considerations show that it is a genuine work of Tertullian. First, because it is attributed to Tertullian in that same ancient codex, as is another poem, *On Sodom*, which was formerly published last under the name of Saint Cyprian, but was recently assigned conjecturally to Tertullian by Jacobus Pamelius, a man of wide reading and judgment. I shall therefore confirm in passing Pamelius’s own opinion and advise scholars no longer to doubt the true author of the poem *On Sodom*.

Second, it thoroughly savors of Tertullian’s style, as obscure and rugged as it is learned. It is regrettable that the work appears to have been copied unskillfully upon the parchment by the copyist. I have therefore presented in good faith the reading of the ancient codex together with my emendations, lest I mislead anyone, until another manuscript copy can be unearthed from which the remaining blemishes may be washed away.

As for its title, it was inscribed *On Nineveh* in the ancient codex. But because the whole narrative concerns Jonah, it seemed appropriate also to entitle it *On Jonah*. There is nevertheless reason to suspect that Tertullian rendered in verse the complete story of Jonah as it appears in the Holy Bible.

After the living tombs of Sodom and Gomorrah enduring forever,  
And the fires marked by the age-old whiteness [Note: [Col. 1107D] **Marked by the age-old whiteness of ashes.** I have thus corrected the text; in its place the ancient codex had *cinerum senios ignara* [corrupt: “ignorant of the old ages of ashes”]. My conjecture is plainly confirmed by another verse from this same Tertullian’s *On Sodom*, where it reads:

> The entire region is a pyre; hence, in the black horror of its embers,  
> There, in the ash-white waste, the ashes mark the fires.

For thus, in passing, I read from the traces of the ancient codex, which has *hinc de situ*; the commonly printed text is *hincque situ*.] of their ashes—their punishment—  
And the few things that spring up fruitlessly before the eyes of the sun;  
And after the death of the sea [Note: [Col. 1107D] **The death of the sea that was made.** He means the Dead Sea, concerning which he himself writes in the poem *On Sodom*:

> Nor does the sea live there; it is the death of the sea, a death of stillness.] likewise brought about, and after the solitary human being there,  
If human he still is, preserving his punishment in a transformed body:  
A shower from heaven had nearly struck down other fires [Note: [Col. 1108D] **Nearly other fires.** This emendation, derived from P. Pithoeus’s conjecture, is pleasing; for it comes close to the traces of the writing in the ancient codex, which has:

> *Pane alio ignes superis decesserat imbris.*],  
For the city of Nineveh had transgressed the claims of equity and justice [text uncertain]. [Note: [Col. 1108D] **Had transgressed justice.** The passage admits various conjectures, for it is doubtful whether the author wrote *justique forem* or *justi fines*, as Pithoeus likewise thinks. Immediately afterward I wrote *transgressa Ninive* (“Nineveh had transgressed”), for which the ancient codex has *transgressi Ninivitae* (“the Ninevites transgressed”).]

For what fear remains in an overturned mind? All examples  
Of punishment everywhere are empty where error holds possession.  
But the Lord—good [Note: [Col. 1108D] **But good.** The ancient codex has *at boni*.], patient with us, and slow to punish—  
The Almighty, will hurl no wrath  
Unless he first gives warning and strikes hardened hearts,  
[Col. 1108D] Stirring the prophet, prescient but confined in mind. [Note: [Col. 1108D] **In mind, the prophet.** Perhaps *prophetas* (“prophets”), so that it may be joined with *praesagos* (“prescient”).]

For because of the Ninevites’ deserts the Lord had commanded Jonah  
To foretell their destruction; but Jonah knew that he  
Was ready to spare the submissive and remit the penalty owed [Note: [Col. 1109B] **To remit the penalty owed.** I have thus emended it, since the ancient codex corruptly had *debitam cedere poenam*. He used *cedere* in the sense of “remit.”]  
To suppliants, and readily inclined to goodness; therefore he declined to undertake [Note: [Col. 1109B] **He declined to undertake.** That is, Jonah refused to fulfill the duty of warning. Prudentius excuses Jonah in precisely the same manner in hymn 7 of the *Cathemerinon*, where this story is described:

> The gentle avenger summons Jonah the prophet  
> To go as herald of the punishment at hand;  
> But because he knew that the threatening Judge  
> Preferred to preserve rather than to strike and punish,  
> He secretly turned his hidden figure toward Tarshish.

Other writers, however, say that Jonah did this out of contempt. Saint Jerome, *Commentary on Jonah*: “He was conscious of his flight and of the sin by which he had neglected the Lord’s commands.” Saint [Col. 1109C] Gregory, book VI on Job, chap. 13: “There, carried by the beast, he arrived at the place to which he had disdained to go willingly. Behold: the storm finds God’s fugitive, the lot accuses him, the sea receives him, the beast confines him; and because he struggles to obey his author, he is carried as a culprit in his own prison to the place where he had been sent.” Isidore, *On the Life and Death of the Saints*, chap. 45: “Jonah is sent to preach to the nations; when sent, he disdains; disdaining, he flees; fleeing, he sleeps,” etc.],  
Lest his threats prove empty when peace intervened.

Flight was soon his plan—if indeed any power exists  
To avoid God and escape the Lord’s right hand,  
Beneath which [Note: [Col. 1109C] **Beneath which.** The ancient codex had “above.”] the whole trembling world is held in check.  
Yet the prophet believed that what he did sprang from a holy heart. [Note: [Col. 1109C] **Yet he believed.** The exemplar is again corrupt, having *an rati*. Immediately afterward I restored “from the heart,” for which the ancient codex has “from the body.” The author removes blame from the prophet so as to excuse his deed.]

On the lip of the shore [Note: [Col. 1109C] **On the lip of the shore.** Sisenna uses a similar expression in Nonius Marcellus: “and he stops at the uppermost lip of the river.” Marcus Tullius similarly spoke of the “mouth of the harbor,” for he writes thus in *Against Verres*, on the punishments: “For at the very entrance and mouth of the harbor.”], renowned for its safe harbor,  
Is a city; the Cilician coast lies opposite Joppa [text uncertain].  
From there, then, Jonah hastily boards a ship for Tarshish. [Note: [Col. 1109C] **Tarshish.** The ancient codex has: “He hastily boards a vessel for Tharros.”]

God tolerates his deeds; nor is it at all surprising  
If, fleeing the Lord on land, he finds him upon the waters.  
For suddenly a tiny cloud had stained the air,  
Driven from the sulfurous seed of the wind’s bellows, [Note: [Col. 1109C] **Driven by the wind.** I think I have rightly corrected it thus; [Col. 1109D] the ancient codex has *conotia venti*.]  
And little by little the mass cohered together with the sun  
[Col. 1109B] And enclosed the defrauded day [Note: [Col. 1109D] **The defrauded day.** He used this for “the day that was taken away.”] within a marching host of darkness.

[Col. 1110A] The sea becomes a mirror of the sky; a black circuit stains  
The waters; the upper air plunges into darkness, and the sea rises.  
In vain the clouds touch the midst of the waves,  
Which the triumph of all the winds mingles in a whirlwind.  
A manifold fury gnashed its teeth against the fugitive Jonah.  
One ship became the object of contention between sky and sea.  
It is battered from this side and that; all its timber trembles beneath the blow,  
And beneath the wave-breaking [Note: [Col. 1109D] **Wave-breaking.** An alternative reading of the ancient codex is *fluctivago* (“wave-wandering”).] shock the coil of the keel is shaken.

The creaking yard trembles, [Note: [Col. 1109D] **The creaking yard.** I shall not say here that the last syllable in *antenna* is lengthened because of the penthemimeral caesura; rather, I advise that one should observe the custom of the ancients, who made a vowel long when two consonants followed it. Yet modern scholars neither understood nor perceived this custom and have frequently corrupted passages in authors contrary to the authority and agreement of all the ancient codices. I demonstrated this with cited and restored testimonies in my annotations on Paulinus, Sedulius, Marius Victor, Juvencus, and others. I shall add only the words of the very ancient grammarian Terentianus Maurus, in his chapter on heroic verse:

> Two sonorous consonants can establish a spondee;  
> Whether the examples I follow are wrong or right, I shall prove.  
> Whoever desires to know, or whoever takes care to write:  
> One should say *antestare* when I speak and divide the word—  
> Nature created the poet Stesichorus before.] the *labor* shudders from above, [Note: [Col. 1110B] **The *labor* shudders from above.** P. Pithoeus suspects that the word *labor* here signifies a kind of standard or sail. Gregory of Nazianzus, in his *Oration against Julian*, indeed says that a standard is called *labor* by the Latins because it has the power to release people from their labors. In the Theodosian Code, book VI, title 25, *On the Commanders of the Labores*, it reads: “The commanders of the *labores* are promoted through our exertions and military service.” In the Justinianic Code as well, book XII, title 18, all the manuscripts most consistently give the title *On the Commanders of the Labores*. This reading nevertheless seemed so unfamiliar to some that Accursius notes that in his age certain persons absurdly substituted *labiorum* (“of the lips”) for *laborum*. Others now think that *labarorum* should instead be read, following the example of Emperor Constantine, who called the famous standard he used in war the *labarum*. I think that we should not rashly depart from the reading of the ancient codices.]  
And even the mast itself bends, fearing that it will break.

Meanwhile the sailors’ twofold cry [Note: [Col. 1110C] **Twofold cry.** Tertullian shortened the first syllable in *clamor*, and he was not alone among the ancients. For Paulinus, in his books on the life of Martin, used the same license. . . . Cries, moreover, have a place in a storm at sea. Ovid, book II of the *Metamorphoses*:

> The men resound with shouting, the rigging with shrieking,  
> And the heavy wave with the assault of the waters.

But Tertullian himself shortly afterward adds:

> Wretched voices arise at every breaking of the sea.] tries every measure  
For ship and life: to clear the obstructing coils, [Note: [Col. 1110C] **Life, coils.** The ancient codex had “lives, coils.” Isidore (*Etymologies*, book IX, chap. 3) says that *spirae* are ropes used by sailors in storms, named from *spartum*.]  
To loop the *mithra*, [Note: [Col. 1110C] **To loop the *mithra*.** This most felicitous restoration of the passage is owed to both the acumen and judgment of P. Pithoeus. The ancient codex corruptly had *oblaquearem vibram*. Isidore, *Etymologies*, book XIX, chap. 4, writes that the *mithra* is the rope [Col. 1110D] by which the middle of a ship is bound. It is a rare word, selected elsewhere by few writers.] to tighten the tillers’ strained pull,  
Or to drive the resisting circuits onward with the breast. [Note: [Col. 1110D] **With the breast, the circuits.** The ancient codex corruptly had *nigyros*, and perhaps the whole passage would more correctly be read as follows: “Or to drive the struggling circuits of the sail onward with the breast.”]  
One group in turn makes the bilge, a portion of the inner sea, reek. [Note: [Col. 1110D] **To make the bilge reek.** I think the author used this verb actively, and that “to make the bilge reek” is equivalent to “to cause the bilge to exhale a foul odor.” I therefore understand this passage as referring to the ship’s bilge, which smells worse when the sediment of stagnant water is disturbed and pumped out. Paulinus, in his poem to Cytherius:

> Where the boat had drawn much water through its cracks  
> Into the foul-smelling pool of the bilge.

Quintilian, book VIII, observes that Cicero spoke of “the bilge of the commonwealth” to signify the vileness of men; Tertullian calls it a part of the inner sea because bilge water comes from the sea.]

[Col. 1110B] Then all seize their precious merchandise [Note: [Col. 1110D] **They seize their precious merchandise.** The ancient codex had *Egregiae tunc repiunt*.] and cargo  
[Col. 1111A] And hurl it overboard, striving to overcome peril by losses.  
Wretched voices arise at every breaking of the sea,  
And they spread their hands toward the images of nonexistent gods, [Note: [Col. 1111B] **Nonexistent.** In the ancient codex, a somewhat later hand had written *multorum* (“many”) above this word. But he rightly calls the deities “nonexistent,” because the gods of the nations are in reality nothing, or because they are of no use or assistance. Hence that expression among the ancient comic poets: “I am nothing.” And Paulinus:

> I was nothing, and through God’s action  
> I was born that I might exist.]  
Whom the force of sea and sky does not fear; no less does it sink those gods, [Note: [Col. 1111C] **No less those gods.** I emend thus; in its place the ancient codex has *aut munus illos puppibus abstruso Siratos*. I do not doubt that these words allude to the custom of the ancients, who carried gods aboard their ships as tutelary deities. Virgil:

> The mighty gods upon the stern.

> The ship shone with its laurel-crowned Apollo.  
> (*Aeneid*, book X)

Ovid, *Tristia*, book I:

> A wave no lower than the mountain leaps  
> Upon the prow and curving stern and lashes the painted gods.

Authors call them *tutelae* (“tutelary powers”) almost as a proper name. Silius Italicus:

> And the divine tutelary powers float away.

[Col. 1111D] Seneca, Epistle 76, alludes to this when he says: “A ship is called good,” etc., “not one whose tutelary deity is carved in ivory.” In imitation of this, they also say that even today the Venetians raise Saint Mark upon the stern as the tutelary deity of their city.]  
Thrust against the sterns, beneath the angry whirlwind.

Unaware of these things, the guilty man himself, beneath the hollow vault of the stern,  
Snored out sleep through his distended nostrils as he lay there, [Note: [Col. 1111D] **As he lay there.** Philippus Robertus, jurisconsult, prefers *stertentem* (“snoring”). Indeed, where Jonah 1 in the Vulgate has, “and he slept in a heavy slumber,” Saint Jerome translates from the Septuagint, “and he slept and snored.” Saint Ambrose likewise writes on Psalm 43: “We read of Jonah, who slept in the belly of the ship and snored.” Jerome himself also says in his *Commentary on Jonah*: “As though secure, he slept and gave forth the sound of snoring in the deepest sleep.”]  
Already then in his sleep prefiguring the Lord. [Note: [Col. 1111D] **Prefiguring the Lord.** The ancient codex had “forming himself”; the passage refers to Matthew 8, where Christ sleeps in the boat and is awakened by his followers.]

At once the man who directs the duties of the wave-cleaving prow, [Note: [Col. 1111D] **Who directs the duties of the wave-cleaving prow.** If my conjecture has any truth, I have not badly emended in this way the extremely corrupt reading of the ancient codex, which has: *Undis ita qui cogit moenia* [Col. 1112B] *prorae*—unless one prefers *undisicae*. The change from *undisis ita* to *undisica* is easy. But the word *moenia* may also be retained, for Pompeius Festus notes that the ancients used *moenia* for “duties.”]  
Found him lulled by gentle peace, stubborn in his rest, [Note: [Col. 1112C] **Stubborn in his rest.** Sosipater Charisius, book I, says: “*Quies* forms the accusative *quietem*; *requies* does not form the accusative *requietem* but *requiem*, although Cicero used *requietem* in writing to Hostilius.” Charisius repeats this elsewhere: “Cicero declined it differently, following the numerical pattern, for he said *requietem*.” He calls Jonah *superbus* because he persisted stubbornly in sleep, as though scorning to be moved by the shouting of the endangered sailors, excessively secure.]  
And pressed him, shaking him: “Why,” he said, [Note: [Col. 1112C] **Why, he said.** The ancient codex corruptly had, “What do they say of the danger in this?”] “in this danger

In so great a whirlwind? Does not the wave cover us? [Note: [Col. 1112C] **Us, the wave.** I have added the word *nos* (“us”), which seems to be required.] Our only hope lies in the gods.  
[Col. 1111B] You too, whoever your God may be, utter your vows and prayers.”

Then they resolved to learn by lot who was responsible for the offense,  
Who was the cause of the storm; nor did the lot speak falsely: Jonah. [Note: [Col. 1112C] **Then who.** The ancient codex was extremely corrupt here, having *Indicit exin quis*.]  
Then they ask: “Who are you among men, and whence? What is your occupation?

The word *clue*, not unknown among the ancients, I have restored in other authors where the common text had rashly corrupted it. I shall therefore not repeat that discussion.] He confesses himself a greatly God-fearing and faithful servant  
Of God, who raised the heavens, [Note: [Col. 1112D] **Who raised the heights.** The words in the ancient codex were redundant, for I found it written thus: “Who raised heaven on high.” The verse appeared to have been corrupted by a gloss; we know that *altum* is used for heaven, as in Virgil:

> And he sends Maia’s son down from heaven on high.

Lactantius, book II, chap. 1: “They cast down their eyes and fix them upon the ground.”]  
Who established the earth and poured forth the whole sea as one body [text uncertain].  
He reveals that he is fleeing from him, and reveals the reasons.

They stiffened with fear. “What guilt, then, is ours because of you? [Note: [Col. 1112D] **What is ours?** The ancient codex has *quos* or *quod*.]  
What is now to happen? By what deed shall we appease the waters?”  
[Col. 1112A] For the savage straits swelled ever more violently.

“I am that storm; I am the whole madness of the world.  
Against me,” he said, “the sky collapses upon you, and the sea rises;  
For me the land is far away, death close at hand, and there is no hope from God.  
Come, cast the guilty man headlong; lighten the ship [Note: [Col. 1112D] **And lighten the ship—one.** The ancient codex incorrectly has, “lightening the ship, one man.”]  
And throw this single great burden into the sea; I go willingly.”

But they strove in vain to turn their course  
Back toward safety, for the tiller would not allow itself to be turned,  
Nor would the rigid beam of the yard consent to be shifted.

At last they appealed to the Lord: “Do not consign us to the gulf of death  
For the sake of one soul; do not will that we be spattered  
With the blood of a righteous man, [Note: [Col. 1112D] **With the blood of a righteous man.** I read thus, for which the ancient codex has “a righteous man”; this is confirmed by the biblical text.] if your right hand guides events thus.”

And now, rising there from the deepest gulf, a sea monster  
Unrolled from its shells the scaly train of its body  
And drove its own waves through the shaken marble of the sea,  
[Col. 1112B] Of its own accord [Note: [Col. 1112D] **Of its own accord.** To fill the lacuna in the ancient codex, I added *sua* (“its own”), unless *sponte novam* is preferred.] seizing the prey whom the ship, from its height,  
Hurled down; it swallowed him in its muddy jaws  
And received the living meal into its long belly,  
And with the man devoured the frenzy of sky and sea.

The wave of the sea lies flat; the shadow of the upper air dissolves.  
On this side the waves, on that the winds, become friendly again;  
And as the tranquil keel marks out a safe course,  
White tracks blossom in the blue furrow.

Then the sailors, rejoicing in reverent fear of the Lord,  
Offer sacrifices of thanksgiving * * * * * * *

The prophet Jonah too sails on, taken aboard another  
[Col. 1113A] Vessel, cleaving the current beneath the deepest waves,  
Sailing within the entrails, a soul breathed into a wild creature,  
Confined yet unstained by the waters, [Note: [Col. 1113A] **Unstained by the waters.** It seemed possible to read “untouched,” which says less. Paulinus, in his poem to Cytherius, speaks of the same Jonah:

> Having crossed the sea, untouched by the waves, he floats.

Sedulius likewise writes of him:

> . . . swallowed and devoured by the sea monster,

Half-eaten . . .”; unless, with a change of order, one prefers: “Outside the sea, and amid the half-eaten . . .,” so that the verses cohere. > For, free amid the waves, within the sea he was outside the sea.]

[Col. 1114A] Amid half-eaten fleets and bodies dissolved by decay,  
Digested, now learning of his own—or the prophet’s . . . [Note: [Col. 1114A] **Or the pro . . .** Robertus reads “or the prophet’s.” What if it should rather be “or alive”? These are indeed all the suggestions I have been able to offer toward emending this most corrupt exemplar.]—death.

For because he was one day to become a sign of the Lord,  
He was made not for destruction, but for the glory of heaven.

# ON THE TREE OF LIFE  
(C, G, S)

[Col. 1113B] There is a place at the center of the whole world we behold,  
Which the Jews call Golgotha in their ancestral tongue.  
Here, I remember, a tree cut from barren oak  
Was planted and produced health-giving fruits.  
It did not, however, offer them to the cultivators who had planted it;  
Foreigners possessed its blessed fruits.

This form of tree rises from a single trunk  
And soon extends its arms into twin branches,  
As yards filled with heavy canvas stretch out,  
Or as yokes stand at the plow with the young oxen separated.

The earth received him whom this tree first bore, fallen  
From mature seed; soon thereafter—wondrous to tell—  
The third dawn raised up that branch again,  
Terrible to earth and heaven and blessed with life-giving fruit.

[Col. 1113C] And when strengthened for twice twenty days,  
It grew immeasurably, touched heaven  
With its highest crown, and at last hid its holy head on high.  
Yet under its enormous weight it brought forth twelve branches  
And stretched them out, spreading across the whole world,  
So that they might offer everlasting nourishment and life  
To all nations and teach that death itself can die.

Then, when fifty days had also been fulfilled,  
It brought down from its highest crown into the branches  
A draught of divine nectar—the breath of the heavenly Spirit.  
The leaves, heavy with sweet dew, dripped on every side. [Note: [Col. 1113D] Oberth reads *frontes* (“foreheads”).]

Behold: beneath the vast shade of the branches’ covering  
There was a spring, serene because disturbed by no accident,  
Its limpid waters free of mud; around it the grasses  
Poured forth joyful colors from flowers of every kind.

[Col. 1113D] Innumerable nations and peoples gathered around it.  
How diverse they were in race, sex, age, and rank:  
Unmarried and married women together, widows and daughters-in-law,  
Infants and children, men and youths and old men!

Here, when they saw the branches bending low  
With fruits of many kinds, they rejoiced to touch with eager hands  
[Col. 1114B] The fruits drenched in heavenly nectar.  
Yet they could not pluck them with eager palms  
Until they had worn away the filthy, muddy traces  
Of their former path and washed their bodies in the holy spring.

Therefore, after walking for a long time upon the soft grass around it,  
They receive the fruits hanging from the lofty tree.  
Then, if any of them feed upon the husks fallen from the branches  
And upon the sweet leaves dripping with abundant nectar,  
They desire to take the true fruits.

Therefore, when their mouths have received the heavenly flavor,  
They transform their spirits and begin to destroy  
Their covetous minds and, through the sweet sensation, to recognize the human being.

We have seen that this unfamiliar flavor turned the stomachs of many,  
And that the venom of gall, disturbed by the honey,  
[Col. 1114C] Rejected the good flavors in a troubled state of mind;  
Or, after greedily consuming it, they did not love it,  
And at last vomited up the flavor they had long and badly drunk.

Many, indeed, after their minds had been renewed, often restored  
Their sickened spirits; and they who denied that they could do so  
Had washed themselves and received the fruit of their labor.

Many others, though they had dared to touch the holy springs,  
Suddenly broke away again and, falling backward,  
Rolled mingled in the same filth and mud.

Many, however, bearing it within a good heart, receive it  
With their whole spirit and store it deep within their inward parts.

Therefore, for those who can approach the sacred springs,  
The seventh dawn brings them to the longed-for waters [Note: [Col. 1114D] Oberth reads *optata* (“longed-for”).]  
And immerses their fasting limbs in the liquid fountains.

Only thus do they put aside the filth of the mind  
And the stain of their former life; [Col. 1114D] they bring back from death  
Their pure and radiant souls, destined for heaven’s threshold.

From there runs the path to the branches and the sweet fruits of salvation;  
Thence runs the path to heaven through the branches of the lofty tree.

This is the tree of life for all who believe. Amen.

## Second Appendix

[Col. 1115] # JOSEPH MARIA SUARESIUS OF AVIGNON SENDS GREETINGS TO THE CANDID READER

[Col. 1115A] Candid Reader,

Easy access to the Vatican Library lies open to me through the kindness of the Most Eminent Cardinal Francesco Barberini, its librarian and my patron, who could truly be called a living library, and through the goodwill of its custodian Felice Contelori, a man furnished with every branch of learning.

While I was examining its individual holdings, I most opportunely came upon a very ancient parchment codex, written in Lombardic script, which presents the appearance of being six or seven hundred years old. After I had gone through Bede’s *Chronicle* and the other works it contained, I found at its end a fragment of Tertullian’s books *On the Detestable Gods of the Nations*. Its final verses had been erased, and the fragment is devoted solely to demolishing the divinity of Jupiter.

After I had read it once and then again, and had subsequently compared its contents with what [Col. 1115B] the same Tertullian writes about Jupiter in his books *Against the Nations* and in the [Col. 1116A] *Apologeticum*, I found its style wholly distinct, yet both I and the friends to whom I showed it recognized it as pure, unadulterated Tertullian; all agreed.

I therefore did not wish this work, although only a fragment, to remain hidden any longer, and now issue it for common and public use under the auspices of Pope Urban VIII. While he sits most happily in the Chair of Peter, not only the Muses, the more refined branches of literature, and the disciplines—while poets and historians everywhere pour and sweat forth their works—grow green again as though their youth had been restored; but orthodox religion too raises its head and flourishes again throughout the whole world, as the works of the holy Fathers emerge everywhere into the light.

I pray God that he may will to preserve both Urban himself and the Most Eminent Cardinal Francesco Barberini—Hercules to so great an Atlas—for a very long time, safe and well, to the glory of his Church. You, Reader, join in that prayer; and receive and enjoy this little gift of mine with gratitude.

[Col. 1115] # A FRAGMENT FROM TERTULLIAN’S BOOKS ON THE DETESTABLE GODS OF THE NATIONS, UNEARTHED FROM THE VATICAN LIBRARY BY JOSEPH MARIA SUARESIUS OF AVIGNON  
(C, G, S)

[Col. 1115B] Such blindness has seized the human race that people call their enemy “Lord,” proclaim the robber of their goods to be their benefactor, and give him thanks. They therefore call those beings by human names, not by their own. They do not know their names, because they understand that they are demons; but they read the histories of ancient kings and, although they see that the conduct of those kings was mortal, honor them with a deifying title.

Jupiter, as they call him and whom they consider the supreme God, was born almost three thousand years after the creation of the world. He was born in Greece from Saturn and Ops. To prevent his father from killing him—or, if one may say so, from giving birth to him a second time—he was carried to Crete and, on his mother’s advice, was reared in the Idaean cave. He was concealed by native Cretans [Note: [Col. 1115D] **Cretans.** Perhaps “Curetes.” SUAR.] who clashed their weapons. He sucked a she-goat’s udders and wore as clothing the hide flayed from her—namely, from his own nurse, whom he himself had killed. Yet he sewed onto it three golden curls, each worth the price of a hundred oxen, as Homer, their own authority, records—if it is fair to believe him.

When Jupiter reached maturity, he waged war against his father for many [Col. 1115D] years, defeated him, seized his house through parricide, violated his virgin sisters, chose one of them in marriage, and drove his father into exile by force of arms. The rest of his deeds have also been recorded. From other men’s marriages, or from virgins he had violated, he begot sons born of adultery; he defiled freeborn boys; and by tyrannical [Col. 1116B] and royal power oppressed peoples without law.

His father—whom those in error suppose to have been the primordial god—did not know that Jupiter was being concealed on the island of Crete. The son likewise—whom they believe to be the more powerful god—did not know that the father he had driven away lay hidden in Italy. If he was in heaven, why did he not see what was happening in Italy? Italy is not some corner of the earth. Indeed, if he had been a god, nothing ought to have been hidden from him.

That he nevertheless remained hidden there, and that the Italians called him [Col. 1116C] Saturn, has been plainly established: from his concealment the western Latin tongue has been called *Latina* down to the present day, as their own authority Virgil also records: [Note: [Col. 1115D]
