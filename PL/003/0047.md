We have Minucius himself, however, as witness that from early youth until he was already well into adulthood he had been bound by all the superstitions of the pagans, and that at last, having renounced them, he embraced the Christian faith. For he committed the following to writing about himself and Octavius: “He alone was privy to my affections and himself my companion in errors; and when, after the mist had been dispelled, I emerged from the depths of darkness into the light of wisdom and truth, he did not reject me as a companion but—what is more glorious—ran ahead of me.” Caecilius, moreover, addresses him thus from the very beginning of his speech [Col. 0382A]: “For you, Marcus my brother, there can be no uncertainty concerning the very matter we are now investigating”—that is, the truth of the pagan and Christian religions—“since, having carefully experienced both ways of life, you rejected the one and approved the other.” Indeed, Octavius, speaking perhaps not so much about himself, as we have said, as about Minucius Felix, again expresses himself in this manner: “How unjust it is to pass judgment upon what is unknown”—namely, the Christians—“and unexamined, as you do, believe us who ourselves repent of having done so. For we too were once the same, and, while still blind and dull-witted, held the same opinions as you: that Christians worshipped monsters, devoured infants, and mingled in incestuous banquets. Nor did we understand that these tales were always being bandied about by those men and [Col. 0382B] were never either investigated or proved. . . . We thought that they ought not even to be heard at all. Sometimes, even while taking pity on them, we raged against them all the more cruelly, torturing those who confessed in order to make them deny—ostensibly so that they might not perish—subjecting them to a perverse interrogation designed not to elicit the truth but to compel a lie. And if anyone weaker, crushed and overcome by suffering, denied that he was a Christian, we favored him, as though by abjuring the name he had now cleansed all his deeds through that denial. Do you recognize that we once thought and acted exactly as you now think and act?”

But someone may perhaps further ask us whether Minucius Felix, after professing the Christian faith, still devoted himself to discharging the office of advocate. Let him, I beg, attend to what [Col. 0382C] we observed shortly before: Minucius came with Caecilius and Octavius to Ostia, where the debate presented in the book under discussion was held, at the time when “the vintage holidays had relaxed his judicial duties.” Yet at that time, as we have shown from the words of Caecilius cited shortly before, Minucius professed the Christian religion. Therefore, when Caecilius had worshipped an image of Serapis that they encountered, Octavius immediately assailed Minucius in these words: “It is not the conduct of a good man, Marcus my brother, to abandon in such blindness of common ignorance a man who cleaves to your side at home and abroad, so that in the full light of day you allow him to stumble against stones . . . when you know that the disgrace arising from this error reflects no less upon you than upon him.” There, as you see, Octavius rebukes Minucius precisely because, although then professing the Christian religion, he had allowed the image of Serapis to be worshipped by Caecilius, his inseparable companion, during the journey he was making to the shore at Ostia. [Col. 0382D]

Nor should this seem surprising to anyone. For Christians at that time were not accustomed to resign their posts, dignities, or other offices together with the pagan religion. Rather, as we have said and shall explain more fully below, they were then dispersed throughout the whole world and intermingled with pagans, and were accustomed to conduct every kind of duty and business with them, excepting only the worship of the gods. Hence Tertullian says (Tertull., *Apologeticum*, ch. 37, p. 33): “We are but of yesterday, and we have filled everything that is yours: cities, islands, fortresses, municipalities, [Col. 0383A] meeting places, the camps themselves, tribes, decuries, the palace, the senate, and the forum; we have left you only the temples.” And below (the same, ibid., ch. 42): “And so we inhabit this world together with you, not without the forum, not without the meat market, baths, shops, workshops, inns, your markets, and other commercial dealings. We too sail with you, serve as soldiers with you, till the land, and trade; likewise, we combine our crafts with yours and make our labor available for your use. How we can appear unprofitable for your business, when we live both with you and by means of you, I do not know. But I do not attend your rites, etc.”

How piously and holily Minucius lived afterward is taught by Eucherius, bishop of Lyons, in these words (Eucher. Lugdun., Epistle to Valerian): “And when shall I unroll from that volume of harmonious eloquence the most illustrious Firmianus, Minucius, Cyprian, Hilary, John, and Ambrose? They had said, I believe: ‘What is this? The unlearned arise and seize heaven, while behold where we, with all our learning, wallow—in error and blood.’ They had said this, and therefore they afterward took the kingdom by force.”

## ARTICLE III. Who Caecilius and Octavius Were, Who Are Introduced as Disputants in This Book

Since this book, as we have seen, contains nothing other than a dialogue between Caecilius and Octavius debating the pagan and Christian religions, someone will doubtless ask us who these men were. But [Col. 0383C] we shall candidly reply that nothing more certain can be asserted about them than what may be drawn from this book. Caecilius, who pleads the pagans’ case, is also called Natalis there by Octavius: “Nor shall I conceal,” he says, “that at first my dear Natalis’s judgment wavered—erring, wandering, and unstable. . . . But in my Natalis I neither desire nor believe there to be cunning; subtle sophistication is far removed from his simplicity.” From these words we further gather that he was a good, upright, sincere man and a friend of unadorned truth.

Nor can anyone doubt that he was initially enslaved to the religion of the pagans, which in this book he strives in good faith and with all the powers of his intellect to defend. Indeed, when he understood that Octavius was striking at him through Minucius because, in the pagan manner, as has been said, he had saluted an image of Serapis, he was seized by such pain that he burst out in these words [Col. 0383D]: “For some time now the speech of our Octavius has sharply tormented and gnawed at me: in attacking you, he rebuked you for negligence so that, indirectly, he might accuse me more gravely of ignorance.” Finally, such were his integrity and candor, and such the sincerity of his mind, that after listening most attentively to Octavius’s speech, he immediately confessed himself vanquished on both points, abjured his former errors, and embraced the Christian faith.

Some think it can be gathered that his native city was Cirta—that is, that he originated from Cirta, the most famous town in Numidia—from the words in which he calls Fronto the orator “our man of Cirta”: “The speech of our fellow citizen of Cirta also bears witness to this.” Because Octavius knew perfectly well that this was true, he replied to him thus [Col. 0384A]: “Thus concerning this matter your Fronto too did not give testimony as one affirming a fact, but hurled abuse as an orator.” Nevertheless, since Caecilius could have called Fronto “his” for some other reason, let others judge how much weight this argument possesses.

Whatever may be decided about his native country, we do know that he lived for a long time in Rome and in the same house as Minucius. So close a bond of friendship existed between them there that Caecilius never left Minucius’s side. For Octavius openly declares that he was “a man who cleaved to the side” of Minucius Felix “at home and abroad.” Therefore, when Caecilius had worshipped an image of Serapis, Octavius immediately rebuked Minucius [Col. 0384B], saying: “You should know that the disgrace arising from this error reflects no less upon you than upon him.”

Moreover, he appears to have been a philosopher and a follower of the sect of the Academics, who asserted that everything was doubtful and uncertain and that they themselves knew nothing. In his speech he plays no other part than that of an Academic of this kind, as is most manifest to everyone not only from its beginning but also from its conclusion. What of the fact that in the same speech he endeavors to prove that it is entirely uncertain and has thus far been ascertained neither by himself nor by any human being whether there is one god or several, and that it is therefore better, in a matter so doubtful, to follow the traditions concerning those gods and their worship received from one’s ancestors? But when the light of truth shone upon him, he immediately rejected his former errors, as we have seen, [Col. 0384C] acknowledged and believed in one God, and professed the Christian religion.

Some have thought that it could be established from the writings of Lampridius that this Caecilius was an African by nationality, a rhetorician by profession, and the teacher of Diadumenianus, the son of Macrinus. But this opinion rests upon absolutely no reasoning, not even plausible reasoning—indeed, not even upon the name itself. For those who have been permitted to read the manuscript codices of Lampridius assert that in all of them the teacher of Diadumenianus is called neither Caecilius nor Caecilianus, but Caelianus. As for the suspicion of some others that he was the Natalis who, under the reign of Severus, confessed Christ but afterward defected to the Theodotian heretics, they plainly rely upon mere and utterly feeble conjecture—namely, only a similarity of name. [Col. 0384D]

Those, however, are thought to come closer to the truth who judge him to be the man who returned to Africa, was afterward ordained a presbyter, and brought Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, to the Christian religion itself. For Pontius the Deacon relates the following about him in his *Life of Cyprian* (Pont., *Vita Cypriani*): “He also had living with him one of our people, Caecilius, a righteous man of praiseworthy memory and at that time a presbyter both in age and rank, who had corrected him from secular error and brought him to the knowledge of the true divinity. He loved this man with every honor and all respect, receiving him with dutiful veneration, no longer as a friend equal to his own soul, but as the parent of his new life.” [Col. 0385A] In remembrance of so great a benefit, Cyprian doubtless assumed the additional name Caecilius. For what else, I ask, do these words of Jerome mean (Jerome, *De Scriptoribus Ecclesiasticis*, ch. 67): “Thereafter, at the persuasion of the presbyter Caecilius—from whom he also obtained his additional name—he became a Christian”?

Caecilius, in turn, treated Cyprian with such love and honor that, as Pontius further attests, when dying he entrusted his wife and children to him. “Won over by Cyprian’s devoted services,” says Pontius (Pont., *Vita Cypriani*), “he was deservedly moved to such immeasurable affection that, when departing from the world and with his summons now close at hand, he entrusted to him his wife and children; and the man whom he had made a sharer in the fellowship of the sect, he afterward made the heir of his familial duty.” They consider it not unreasonable to confirm that this was the Caecilius under discussion [Col. 0385B] from the fact that he and Cyprian belonged to the same period and, apparently, had the same native country, to which Caecilius had returned after professing the name of Christ. Added to this is the fact that Cyprian copied verbatim several passages from this book, which we shall discuss below. Yet even if you prove that nothing in these considerations conflicts with this opinion, you will nevertheless never persuade anyone that anything altogether certain and indubitable can be concluded from them.

Octavius, who is also called Januarius by Caecilius, was, as Minucius says, an excellent and holy man and his closest friend. Certainly each burned with such love for the other that “you would have believed,” says Minucius himself, “that a single mind had been divided between two men.” [Col. 0385C] Both, as we have noted, had also been advocates in legal cases and pagans, and both together, after abjuring the impious worship of the pagans, were initiated into the Christian religion. At that time Octavius was Minucius’s most faithful *contubernalis*, as Minucius himself calls him—that is, he lived with him in Rome. Afterward he returned to his native country, married there, and had several children. But while they were still infants, merely attempting half-formed words, he left them and came again to Rome on business and to visit Minucius, who received him with the greatest joy. His piety and holiness are sufficiently revealed by the fervor and zeal of mind with which he was carried against pagan superstitions, rebuking Caecilius, and even Minucius himself, so sharply because Caecilius had worshipped an image of Serapis, as we have seen. [Col. 0385D] We know, indeed, that in Cyprian’s time there were certain bishops in Africa, one of whom was called Natalis and the other Januarius. But whoever says that they were the same men who debate in this book of Minucius will be guessing at will, supported by absolutely no reasoning, however slight.

At the beginning of this book, moreover, Minucius seems to indicate that Octavius had departed this life when he committed to writing the latter’s dialogue with Caecilius. For there he explains how dear the memory of Octavius was to him and how deeply Octavius was lodged in his innermost feelings, and immediately adds: “Nor without reason did that excellent and holy man, in departing, leave us an immeasurable longing for him.” [Col. 0386A] Nevertheless, because he uses the simple word “departing,” it does not seem so easy for us to say whether it should be understood of Octavius’s death or of his departure from Minucius and Rome. We are not unaware, however, that certain learned men explain these words in the former sense, doubtless because Minucius added: “He left us an immeasurable longing for him.” Yet whether he could not have said this of a man most closely bound to him and his dearest friend—with whom he shared one mind and soul, and who had now been separated from him by a long distance over land—is for you, learned reader, to judge.

However the matter may stand, there are some who suspect that Octavius and Caecilius were fictitious, assumed personae introduced as disputants by our Minucius in this book. [Col. 0386B] Yet these men too offer absolutely no, not even the slightest, confirmation of their conjecture. We are well aware that various writers—from whom our author doubtless borrowed this dialogical form—quite often introduce invented figures of this kind in their books. But they generally alert their readers to this fiction in explicit words, or it is easily understood from their discourse. In our author’s book, by contrast, nothing whatever occurs that would lead us to believe either that real men did not conduct the debate or that all the events narrated are not true. Moreover, whatever one may say on this matter, Minucius’s arguments against pagan rites and in defense of the Christian religion will always remain intact and carry the same weight.

# CHAPTER II. ON THE DATE, STYLE, TITLE, SUBJECT, DIVISIONS, MANUSCRIPT CODICES, AND EDITIONS OF THIS BOOK; ON THE OBSERVATIONS OF VARIOUS SCHOLARS UPON IT; ON CORRUPT PASSAGES; AND ON WHAT MINUCIUS DREW FROM OTHERS AND OTHERS FROM HIM

[Col. 0386C]

## ARTICLE I. At What Time This Book Was Published

The time at which Minucius committed the conversation between Caecilius and Octavius to writing and fashioned it into the form of a book that it now displays is disputed among the most learned writers of the present age. Some believe that Minucius wrote this book around the year of Christ 160. [Col. 0386D] But they are led by no other reason than that they have persuaded themselves that the orator Fronto of Cirta, whom they call the teacher of the emperor Marcus Aurelius, is cited in this book. Yet it is entirely uncertain whether the Fronto praised by Minucius was really Aurelius’s tutor. Furthermore, what prevents Fronto, though previously deceased, from having been cited afterward by our author? Finally, in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers* (Jerome, *Catalog.*, §§ 53 and 58 [OCR: “584”]), Jerome assigns to Tertullian—whom he, like others, reports to have flourished under the emperors Severus and Caracalla—a place considerably earlier than Minucius.

Some, on the contrary, have thought that Minucius was later [Col. 0387A] than Cyprian, who ended his life in martyrdom in the year of Christ 258. For Jerome, they say, in his *Apology against Jovinian*, or *Epistle to Pammachius*, named our Minucius after that same Cyprian. From this they think two things can be established: first, that when Minucius discusses the persecution of Christians he indicates the persecution stirred up by the emperor Decius; and second, that in this book Minucius excerpted many things from Cyprian’s treatises.

But this entire argument rests solely upon Jerome’s *Apology*. There, however, when naming various writers, he does not follow chronological order. Once the foundation of this opinion has been overturned, therefore, the opinion itself must necessarily collapse as well. Nor does Minucius seem to speak of the Decian persecution [Col. 0387B], but of another and earlier one. Cyprian also undoubtedly excerpted his material from Minucius rather than Minucius from him.

For this reason, others judge that this book of our Minucius was published at the beginning of the Church’s third century, when, after the edicts issued by the emperor Severus, the pagans were raging against Christians. For in the *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers* (the same, *Catalog. Script. Eccles.*, §§ 58 and 59), where Jerome observes chronological order more carefully, he placed our Minucius immediately before Caius, as in all printed editions of that *Catalogue*, or immediately after him, as in our very ancient Saint-Germain codex, copied in Lombardic script a thousand years ago. But in all the codices of that work, whether printed or unprinted, Cyprian is placed considerably after both of them [Col. 0387C]—namely, after seven other writers. May one not infer from this that Minucius and Caius were contemporaries and older than Cyprian?

Now “Caius, under Zephyrinus, bishop of the city of Rome,” says Jerome, “that is, under Antoninus, the son of Severus, held a very notable disputation against Proculus, an adherent of Montanus.” Eusebius had already said more than once that this Caius lived during the pontificate of Zephyrinus (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, bk. II, ch. 25, and bk. VI, ch. 20). Zephyrinus is reported to have presided over the Roman Church from the year 201 or 202. The son of Severus, or Caracalla, whom Jerome mentions, ruled after his father Severus from 211 to 217. May it not therefore be concluded that the book of our Minucius was brought to light [Col. 0387D] under the rule of Caracalla, while the pagans were still committing outrages against Christians?

But learned men object that Caecilius reproached Octavius with the fact that Christians then had no temples, and that Octavius certainly did not deny it. Yet Origen, they say, in his commentaries on Matthew—which are reported to have been composed during the reign of Philip, that is, around the year 244—has testified to us that Christian churches had been burned (Origen, *Tractate on Matthew*, ch. 28). They had therefore previously been erected in public places, but not long before, and only when Alexander Severus ruled the Romans, around the year 222. For he, according to Lampridius, “wished to build a temple to Christ and receive him among the gods, something that Hadrian also is reported to have contemplated, since he had ordered temples without images to be constructed in every city” (Lampridius, *Life of Alexander Severus*, after the middle). [Col. 0388A] The Christians, therefore, doubtless did not lose the opportunity Alexander offered them to construct churches. But why, I ask, would they have failed to use a similar opportunity previously granted them by Hadrian, as Lampridius relates in the same passage? This argument therefore wavers, and absolutely nothing certain can be established from it.

They nevertheless press the point that Caecilius boldly asserted that Christians were people from the very dregs of the populace and were everywhere oppressed by the greatest misery and poverty. But this, they say, could not have been said except shortly before the reign of Alexander Severus, whose palace was filled with Christians—people who doubtless did not live in the greatest misery and [Col. 0388B] poverty.

But this argument certainly has no greater weight or force. For we have already heard Tertullian saying that the cities, fortresses, forum, senate, and palace had been filled with Christians, who had left nothing to the pagans except the temples of their false gods. Who will deny that these Christians, like the pagans, were not all of the lowest rank but belonged to every social condition?

Indeed, what of the fact that our Minucius himself, after professing the Christian religion, was of no lower status than before, but was, as we observed above, a distinguished advocate in legal causes? Although Caecilius and others as well, as we shall explain below, said that many Christians were poor and needy, they nevertheless do not deny that a considerable number of them abounded [Col. 0388C] in great wealth and resources. Hence Octavius replies to Caecilius, when the latter reproaches Christians with their poverty, obscurity, and lowliness: “We do not therefore consist of the lowest populace merely because we reject your offices and purple robes”—that is, when those things were contrary to Christian holiness.

“What then?” someone will ask. “Can nothing certain be determined about the date of this book?” We shall reply that Minucius rather clearly denies that, when he wrote these things, Christians had built public churches or, as he himself puts it, that there were any public temples in which they could assemble openly. From this it may not unreasonably be inferred that this book was published before Alexander Severus obtained the Roman Empire, since his palace, as we have said, was full of Christians and he himself had resolved to construct a temple [Col. 0388D] to their God.

The particular time at which that book was published can, in our judgment, be ascertained with greater certainty from no other source than Jerome’s aforementioned *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*. For even if it were proved that not absolutely all the writers mentioned by him are there enumerated in exact chronological order, nevertheless more writers are listed from Tertullian to our Minucius, and from that same Minucius to Cyprian, than would permit one unreasonably to refuse the conclusion that this book was published by Minucius after Tertullian’s writings on the same subject and before Cyprian’s (Jerome, *Catalog. Script. Eccles.*, § 53, p. 115; § 58, p. 117; and § 67, p. 119). Now Tertullian brought his *Apologeticum* [Col. 0389A], which must be discussed below, to light around the year 200, while Cyprian is reported to have written his work *To Demetrianus* around the year 257. Minucius must therefore be said to have made this book public sometime between those years.

Furthermore, if he really asserts, as we were saying shortly before, that there were then no public Christian churches, it will further follow that this book appeared before the year 220, when Alexander Severus was raised to imperial power. If all these points, moreover, are reconciled with one another, may it not be said with greater probability that this book was published by Minucius around the year of Christ 217, when Caracalla was killed, or during the reign of Elagabalus, while Christians were harassed with impunity by pagans amid the various upheavals of the Roman Empire? Nevertheless, we shall not begrudge anyone more expert who may suggest something better. [Col. 0389B]

## ARTICLE II. In Whose Words and in What Style This Book Was Composed; What Its Title Is; What Aim Minucius Felix Had in It; and Whether He Pleaded the Christians’ Case Fully and Perfectly

Although Minucius Felix most openly declares that the book he published arose from the debate instituted, as was said above, between Caecilius and Octavius, no one should doubt that he composed it not so much in their words as in his own. For what they said was all the more difficult for him to retain in memory because he attests that he reduced it to the book form we now see not immediately, but after some time had elapsed and after Octavius had departed from him. [Col. 0389C] Moreover, the style of this entire book is plainly uniform. Who could easily be persuaded that Caecilius and Octavius, debating extemporaneously, spoke in precisely the same manner? In addition, the mode of expression and manner of speaking are the same in the preface placed before this debate by Minucius himself as throughout the whole book. Those who consider Caecilius and Octavius to have been assumed personae will concede far more readily that the book was composed by Minucius.

Furthermore, all confess with remarkable consensus and in a single voice that Minucius’s manner of expression in this dialogue is dignified, acute, elegant, polished, vigorous, vehement, compact, and concise, and everywhere filled throughout with excellent and weighty reflections. Certain elegant and harmonious descriptions also occur in it, especially pleasing through their very brevity—above all that universally celebrated description of the game in which boys amuse themselves by skipping shells over the sea. [Col. 0389D]

There is also no one who does not see that this book is divided into two parts, the first of which comprises Caecilius’s speech for the pagans and the second Octavius’s speech for the Christians. But everything in it has always been written continuously and without any divisions in the discourse, both in the manuscript codex and in the printed editions. In only the latest edition were certain signs or numbers for dividing the text into chapters placed in the margins. [Col. 0390A] But these divisions often do not appear sufficiently accurate. For the greater convenience of readers, however, the entire book can easily be distinguished into various chapters.

The true and genuine title of this book is *Octavius*, which the highly authoritative witnesses cited by us, Lactantius and Jerome, explicitly declare was imposed upon it by Minucius himself. In giving it this title, Minucius imitated Plato, Cicero, and others, who prefixed to books of this kind, composed in dialogue form, the name of the person who plays the principal role in them. Octavius is represented as such a person in this book: he triumphed over his opponent, namely Caecilius, and finally brought him to acknowledge as true the Christian religion that he had attacked [Col. 0390B] and immediately to embrace it.

Minucius’s only aim and purpose throughout this book was to demonstrate the falsity of the pagan religion and the truth of the Christian. But because this demonstration can be made by various arguments and in different ways, Minucius undertook to prove only the following: that the gods of the pagans were false and fictitious, and their forms of worship altogether impious and absurd; that there was only one God, present everywhere and exercising providence over all collectively and each individually; that the morals and assemblies of Christians were holy and undefiled; and that the pagans themselves were guilty of the crimes of which they falsely accused the Christians, whom, against all justice and divine law, they tortured and slaughtered with every most dreadful kind of punishment merely on account of their name. [Col. 0390C] He proves these things most invincibly either by arguments, as they say, *ad hominem*—that is, by the testimony of the pagans’ own writers—or by others drawn from natural reason and equity themselves.

“But if the matter stands thus,” someone will ask, “for what reason does Lactantius say that our Minucius was one of those defenders of the Christian religion who ‘were unequal to its defense’?” (Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, bk. V, ch. 1). Why does he also unhesitatingly assert that Minucius’s work reveals “how capable a champion of the truth he would have been, had he devoted himself wholly to that pursuit”? Do these words not indicate clearly enough that Minucius treated this subject either very superficially or only for the sake of relaxing and refreshing his mind? [Col. 0390D] By no means. For the debate was conducted by him with such seriousness and zeal that Caecilius openly professed himself vanquished by it alone and compelled to abjure the errors of the pagans and embrace the Christian religion.

Lactantius therefore seems to have meant nothing other than that the defense of the Christian religion was not presented in this book of Minucius as whole, complete, and perfected and finished in every aspect possible. For, as we have already remarked and as all readily acknowledge, the truth of the Christian religion can be demonstrated by various arguments, each of which is independently sufficient. No one, therefore, could ever justly and deservedly fault Minucius for selecting, from among numerous arguments for utterly overthrowing pagan errors, repelling their utterly false accusations against Christians, and establishing the truth of the Christian religion, those by which these things are demonstrated more than sufficiently. [Col. 0391A] Thus certain by no means undistinguished defenders of the Christian religion demonstrated its truth from the fulfillment of the oracles of the divine prophets; others from the miracles of Christ and his disciples; and still others by other arguments that there is no need to enumerate here. Yet who has ever dared to say that they did not duly discharge their office as defenders?

## ARTICLE III. On the Manuscript Codices and Various Editions of This Book

[Col. 0391B] For a long time now only a single manuscript codex of this book has remained to us, copied eight hundred years ago in an elegant hand, certainly, but by an unskilled scribe. It is presented under Arnobius’s name after the seven books of his work *Against the Nations*, with this title: “The seventh book of Arnobius ends; the eighth book auspiciously begins.” The Supreme Pontiff gave this codex, discovered at Rome in the Vatican Library, together with a copy of that first edition, as a gift to Francis I, king of France. It is now preserved in the Royal Library at Paris, formerly catalogued under number 1344 and now under number 3975.

Deceived by the false title of this codex, Faustus Sabaeus of Brescia caused it to be printed in 1542 under the name of Arnobius and as his eighth book. [Col. 0391C] Three years later, that is, in 1545, Sigismund Gelenius made public at Basel a new edition of this book, under the same title and name, from the press of Froben.

The same error afterward misled those by whom this book was again printed and circulated in Germany in the same manner.

Thus this book, wrongly ascribed to Arnobius, circulated for so long without anyone noticing the error in various parts of the world—Italy, France, and Germany. This error had so thoroughly taken possession even of all other and more learned men that Erasmus himself, well practiced in reading the books of the holy Fathers of the Church, asserted that the true *Octavius* of Minucius no longer survived. But if he had read both this work of our author [Col. 0391D] and the other books of Arnobius with a more attentive mind, it would have been no less easy and ready for him than it once was for Nepotian, as Jerome attests (Jerome, Epistle 35, to Heliodorus, p. 271), to distinguish the utterly different styles of expression of the two authors. Indeed, what could have been easier for Erasmus and all the rest than, upon examining this book, immediately to perceive that the books of Arnobius were not composed in dialogue form, as this *Octavius* of Minucius was, and were written in a very different style?

Franciscus Balduinus, however, understood this perfectly well and, for this very reason, relying upon the authority of Lactantius and Jerome, whom we cited above, restored this *Octavius* to its genuine author, Minucius Felix. Accordingly, in 1560 he caused it to be printed at Heidelberg by Ludovicus Lucius [Col. 0392A], bearing the name of Minucius himself.

Thirteen years later, however—namely, in 1583—a new edition of this book together with the seven books of Arnobius was prepared at Rome by Fulvius Ursinus. In it, *Octavius* was separated from those books in such a way as to suggest that Ursinus had been the first of all to do this and that no one had previously thought of separating this work of Minucius from those of Arnobius. But if he had seen Balduinus’s edition, one may quite reasonably find his good faith wanting; if he had not known it, his diligence.

Thereafter, however, the book was published by all others under the name of Minucius. For subsequently it appeared at Basel from the Froben press in 1603, through the study and care of Johannes a Wower. He informs us [Col. 0392B] that he corrected the errors of Minucius’s text from the edition of Fulvius Ursinus and from another volume, in whose margin Petrus Daniel had collected variant readings from the Royal manuscript and from other books.

At Hamburg, moreover, this same book of our Minucius was published together with the seven books of Arnobius, once and again, in 1610 and 1612, by Gebhart Elmenhorst.

In like manner, another edition of it was prepared at Paris in 1613 by Desiderius Heraldus and brought before the public, but without any observations on the *Octavius*, as will presently be stated.

Ouzelius informs us of another edition of this book, issued from the press of the Oxford printers but differing little from the others.

[Col. 0392C] Afterward, Nicolaus Rigaltius once again brought it before the public at Paris, from the press of the widow of Mathurin Dupuis, revised according to the authority of the Royal codex and, as he himself says, corrected and emended in various places. Indeed, in this Royal manuscript codex we found Rigaltius’s marks, by which he separated and corrected Minucius’s words, and various punctuation marks by which he believed clauses and phrases ought to be distinguished. From this edition the same book was afterward printed at Amsterdam in 1645, and among the works of Cyprian in 1666. But the edition prepared by Rigaltius could not escape the censure of many.

For although Jacobus Ouzelius extols it with considerable praise, he nevertheless published another edition, which he considered better, while still a young man, at Leiden in 1652, through Johannes le Maire. [Col. 0392D] In its preface he informs the reader that he retained the author’s words where others had correctly emended them from the Royal codex, but elsewhere preferred the commonly received reading. He inserted his own conjectures—which, as he says, were entirely certain—into Minucius’s text only once or twice, consigning the rest to his annotations. Finally, he attests that he altered a very great deal in the faulty punctuation and division of the words.
