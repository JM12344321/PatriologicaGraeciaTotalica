And indeed, how many accounts or histories—ancient though they may be—of holy martyrs, apostles, or any other distinguished men are, chiefly on this very ground, either called into doubt or rejected outright and dismissed as fables! How many ancient monuments, [Col. 0099B] on this same ground, are denied to the authors whose names they bear! Thus those who number the Epistles of Barnabas, Clement, or Ignatius among spurious productions rely chiefly upon this consideration: that in them the teaching of an apostolic man, and likewise the force and majesty of the apostolic spirit, are lacking. Those who hiss down and reject the visions of Hermas—although they were so highly esteemed by the ancients that they were almost admitted by them into the canon of Scripture—are guided almost by this consideration alone: namely, that in their judgment they contain many things insufficiently worthy of a disciple of the Apostles and altogether foreign to apostolic teaching; and that, to use Basnage’s words, the visions presented to Hermas are not accompanied by sufficient gravity and propriety.

Yet no critic has hitherto advanced to such a degree of audacity [Col. 0099C] that, because of certain blemishes, errors, or even harmful points of doctrine which he seemed to himself to detect in these or similar monuments, he doubted the sound faith and orthodoxy of those to whom even the most ancient Fathers attributed such works. They preferred, that is, to take away from the Fathers in question those things which they judged unworthy of their spirit, rather than to pronounce anything unworthy concerning their piety and religion; and to judge books or visions attributed to them in accordance with their attested virtue, rather than to endanger their reputation and honor because of those books or visions. Nor did they therefore imbibe an adverse opinion of certain holy men because they judged the writings attributed to them to be depraved; rather, they refused to attribute what they judged depraved to men concerning whom [Col. 0099D] it was not lawful to imbibe an adverse opinion. Since, therefore, it would be impious and supremely rash to raise a controversy concerning the religion and right faith of Perpetua and her fellow martyrs—approved throughout all the centuries since their martyrdom by the judgment of the universal Church and the unanimous agreement of the ancient Fathers—if Basnage found anything offensive in their Acts which, in his judgment, did not befit Catholic martyrs, he ought rather to have rejected all of it as spurious than to have pronounced anything unworthy concerning the holy martyrs.

### III

Nor indeed, had Basnage wished to follow the path opened by Augustine, would arguments have been lacking to him—given the intellectual power and critical acumen in which he excelled—by which he could have supported this opinion in some fashion [Col. 0100A] and made it plausible. For, first, no one would readily persuade himself that an African woman was so devoted to Greek literature that, in so short a composition, she could not restrain herself from repeatedly employing Greek expressions. I am well aware that very many Greek words passed even into the common and popular usage of the Latin language. But the words Perpetua employs in describing her visions are not of that kind; indeed, they are almost entirely unusual and scarcely, if ever, occur in Latin authors.

Thus, in Chapter I, §3: “Welcome, *tegnon*.” Here Ruinart observes that τέκνον (*teknon*, “child”), though more rarely, nevertheless sometimes denotes a daughter. But I should like him to have produced a Latin author who wrote τέκνον in the sense of “daughter.”

Chapter II, §3: “This was shown to me in an *oroma*.” And Chapter III, §2: [Col. 0100B] “I see in an *oroma*.” On these passages the distinguished Lucas Holstenius makes the following annotation:

> The matter requires that *horamate* be restored, unless this word was thus corrupted in the popular usage of the Africans. Now ὅραμα (*horama*) is a Greek word meaning a sight or vision. It is used at Acts 12:9. Latin writers employed it more rarely; nevertheless Aldhelm, *On Virginity*, chapter 3, where he speaks of Saint John the Evangelist, says that he was “caught up in an *horoma* on Patmos.” There Meursius rightly substituted *oramate* in his *Mantissa critica*—and so I find it printed in the *Library of the Holy Fathers*—although Suvartius censured him without cause in Book III, chapter 9 of his *Analecta* and vainly tried to persuade readers that *chroma* should be restored there. I also find these words in Alcuin, Book I, chapter 2 of the *Life of Saint Willibrord*: “His mother, in the repose of the dead of night, saw a heavenly [Col. 0100C] *horama* in her dreams.”

What this most learned man says—that Latin writers used this word rather rarely—is manifestly true. But what he seems to suggest, that it was used popularly among the Africans, I should not readily concede. It occurs neither in Tertullian, a writer of the same period, although he was exceedingly fond of exotic expressions of this kind, especially Greek ones, and very frequently discusses visions and revelations; nor in writers nearly contemporary with him, Cyprian and the deacon Pontius, although they too occasionally recount various visions; nor again in Optatus of Milevis, Augustine, Fulgentius, or Victor of Vita, although their style is not so refined and so utterly removed from popular usage.

In the same Chapter III, §2, another word with a Greek meaning occurs: “I see an Egyptian rolling about in the *apha*.” On this passage the aforementioned Holstenius says: [Col. 0100D]

> *Apha* means dust, by Greek signification; for it is the Greek noun ἁφή (*haphē*) written in Latin letters. Wrestlers preparing to compete customarily sprinkled their oiled bodies with dust, or rolled about in it as it lay on the ground. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9, speaks of two wrestlers supplying one another with dust for this purpose:
>
> > He scoops up dust in hollow palms and sprinkles me,  
> > And in his turn grows yellow beneath the cast of tawny sand.
>
> Martial, Book VII, Epigram 66, calls the sand from which the wrestlers’ bodies had just grown yellow *Haphe* in this verse:
>
> > And *Haphe* grows yellow. . . .

I do not know, however, whether the word occurs in any Latin [Col. 0101A] author apart from Martial; and perhaps the poet employed it for metrical reasons.

Likewise, in Chapter II, §3, another Greek noun is read shortly afterward: “And between me and him there was a great *diadema*.” Holstenius also has the following on this word in his notes:

> I do not deny that, if conjecture were to be allowed, it might seem possible, not without foundation, to restore *diastema* in place of *diadema*. For it may be understood from L. Frontinus, in his book *On the Qualities of Lands*, that the word *diastema* was frequently used by Latin authors in that century, where he writes as follows: “If three circles are equal and connected with one another at equal intervals (*diastemata*).” And Hyginus, *On the Establishment of Boundaries*: “What of the fact that even in that very region the position of the sun cannot be correctly ascertained unless the surveying instrument is placed at equal intervals from sunrise and sunset?” Likewise: “The earth is a point of heaven, and beneath the sun the atmosphere is suspended across a vast [Col. 0101B] interval (*diastema*).”

Peter Poussines, however, adds the following concerning this most learned man’s observation:

> Holstenius’s remarks here concerning the conjecture by which he suspects that *diastema* should be restored in place of *diadema* seem open to this objection: the passages he cites from the land-surveyors Frontinus and Hyginus do not establish with sufficient certainty that Saint Perpetua used here a word proper to the art of surveying and therefore foreign to common usage, especially that of women in Africa.

Accordingly, on the basis of the consistent and explicit reading of the manuscript codex, he wishes the word *diadema* to be retained and to signify in this passage nearly the same thing as the term *Limbo*. But the learned and subtle arguments he advances in favor of this interpretation do not show that this was at that time a customary and popular meaning of the word, as the term *Limbo* is now among the Scholastics, or consequently that it would suit an African woman better than *diastema*. For although Tertullian, Perpetua’s contemporary and a resident of Carthage, and afterward Saint Augustine, frequently named these hidden receptacles of souls, they never called them *diademata*: although the former, as has been said, was exceedingly fond of exotic and foreign words, and the latter of popular ones. [Col. 0101C]

Who, then, would readily persuade himself that a woman hastening toward martyrdom affected Greek learning, or that amid the shadows and confinement of prison, while committing this brief record to writing, she searched out unusual expressions rather than employing those in familiar use, as they spontaneously and immediately presented themselves to her mind? Saturus too is fond of Greek letters, so much so that in the brief composition in which he explains his vision he could not refrain from Greek expressions: “And we heard the united voice of those saying, *Agios, agios, agios* [ἅγιος, ἅγιος, ἅγιος: ‘Holy, holy, holy’], without ceasing.” [Col. 0101D]

### IV

Second, if we give credence to the editor of the Acts, the Acts do not have a single author but were compiled from the records of three writers: namely Perpetua, Saturus, and the compiler of the Acts themselves, who both added the preface and committed to writing what was done on the very day of the spectacle. But if we bring our eyes closer and read these things neither carelessly nor drowsily, but with an attentive mind and with the concentration they deserve, they immediately seem to present one and the same author. Similar words, [Col. 0102A] similar phrases, and similar turns of expression occur throughout; one and the same style is detected in every part.

The author of an epistle written from Bourges to Henri Valois attributed this work to Tertullian. To prove this, after comparing many passages of the Acts with other works of Tertullian, he showed that precisely the same style, the same formulas of speech, and often even the same words are found in both. But these formulas of speech, these phrases, these words, and finally this style—whatever exactly makes it Tertullianic—are observed not only in those passages which the author admits that he himself added, but also in those which he presents as the words of Perpetua and Saturus.

Basnage, however, denies the aforementioned work to Tertullian on the following grounds: none of the ancients attributes it to Tertullian; furthermore, its style is lower than Tertullian’s [Col. 0102B] and does not flash with equal fire; finally, Tertullian himself praises the Acts of Perpetua while suppressing his own name, although he discloses it when mentioning his own works. There is not much weight in the first and last of these arguments.

To the first one might easily reply, first, that it is an argument from silence and for that reason does not press very strongly. Second, that this work was not numbered among the works of Tertullian because he was regarded as the compiler rather than the author of the Acts. It would likewise be easy to evade the final argument by saying, first, that Tertullian does not cite the Acts but mentions Perpetua’s vision, which he could have received from some source other than the Acts. Next, when he wrote the book *On the Soul*, perhaps he had not yet published the Acts but was keeping them by him to be made public at the proper time. [Col. 0102C]

The second argument therefore has more weight. For it cannot be denied that the Acts employ a lower style of expression and do not stride in Tertullian’s tragic buskin. Yet it is not only Perpetua and Saturus who creep along in a lower style, but also the editor of the Acts himself. Accordingly, just as Saturus and Perpetua have certain phrases and words similar to Tertullian’s words and phrases no less than does the editor of their Passion, so the latter pursues a lower manner of expression no less than Perpetua and Saturus do. And if, because of a certain similarity, the author of the epistle suspected that the editor of the Acts might himself have been Tertullian, there is much greater reason—because virtually one and the same character of discourse appears throughout—to suspect that the Acts may have proceeded from the hand of one and the same author.

I should not like these things to be taken as though I placed great reliance upon conjectures of this kind, [Col. 0102D] or as though I truly intended to cast suspicion upon the authenticity (γνησιότης) of the Acts. My purpose was rather to show by what arguments the opinion could be supported of someone who thought he detected in Perpetua’s visions and revelations something unworthy either of the Holy Spirit or of a martyr in Catholic communion, and who therefore wished to reject all of them as Montanist inventions and fables fabricated by idle and superstitious men. This would certainly be less audacious than expunging the holy martyrs themselves from the diptychs of the Catholics and enrolling them among the Montanists.

### V

Nor, if necessary, would I be deterred from this undertaking and from pursuing this course either by the authority of Tertullian or by the very antiquity [Col. 0103A] of the Acts. Certainly not by Tertullian’s authority. For although he is believed to have had the Acts in his hands and to have praised them as genuine records of unquestionable credibility, this, as I have said, is nevertheless not established, since he could have received the vision of Perpetua which he mentions from another source. Furthermore, not much deference should be paid to this writer’s authority in the present controversy, since he possessed no standard or judgment in matters of this kind: he allowed himself to be most disgracefully deceived by deranged and fanatical little women, and believed that the soul was airy in color and altogether human in shape because a certain false prophetess from the Montanist sect had reported to him that it was furnished with such colors and outlines.

[Col. 0103B] Even if the Acts of the holy martyrs were committed to writing and commended to remote posterity, as is said, by a contemporary author—as he himself testifies in the preface—it would still not follow that everything pertaining to the visions must be received with eager ears and without applying any criterion. How easily, in matters of this kind, may even good men be deceived, and ill-employed men abuse both their leisure and the credulity of others! The author, being, as we have seen, a Montanist, could have fabricated at will everything he reports concerning the visions of Perpetua and Saturus. The events narrated took place in the deepest recesses and hidden chambers of the prison. What passed between God and the martyrs was known only to God himself and to the martyrs, who had already departed. Who, then, could guarantee that the things which to Basnage smell of the Montanists’ workshop [Col. 0103C] must truly be attributed to the martyrs, rather than rejected as the ravings of an idle man devoted to a most deluded sect?

The example of the Donatist author who committed to a written record the Acts of Marculus—a pseudo-martyr of that lost and schismatic faction—would make us all the more inclined to believe this of a Montanist man. For although that writer too was a contemporary, how many things did he fabricate with astonishing license and thrust upon his readers in those Acts with much greater impudence! Who would not reject as the inventions of a supremely audacious mind the revelations he reports concerning this hypocrite, although they would not have been altogether unworthy of a Catholic martyr? With the same liberty, therefore, with which we deny credibility to a Donatist author in what he narrates concerning the visions of Marculus, because they do not befit a heretical man, [Col. 0103D] we could treat a Montanist writer in the same way if any visions of Perpetua and Saturus reported by him did not befit Catholic martyrs.

I certainly would not wish to deny that Saturus and Perpetua were made illustrious by heavenly revelations. But whether they were precisely the revelations which the Montanist writer made public could deservedly be called into controversy, if necessary. Some of those who have wielded the pen against the Ignatian Epistles do not deny that certain letters were written by the holy martyr. Indeed, they even freely admit that these were collected by Polycarp, and that the holy bishop appended a collection (συναγωγή) of them to his own Epistle to the Philippians. But they do not admit that the letters we now possess were either written by Ignatius or [Col. 0104A] published by Polycarp, because in their judgment they contain many things which genuine letters written by Ignatius could not possibly have contained.

By this reply Daillé [Note: [Col. 0104D] In Pearson, *Vindiciae*, Part I, chap. 5.] attempts to weaken the force of Polycarp’s testimony; he even grants that the letters fabricated by an impostor were in some respects similar to the genuine Epistles of Ignatius praised by Polycarp. Basnage, like Daillé, denies to Saint Ignatius the letters now circulated under his name because they contain many things which genuine letters written by Ignatius could not possibly have contained. Why, then, did he not pursue the same course when Perpetua’s revelations were at issue? Would it not have been less rash to admit that Perpetua had indeed been refreshed in prison by heavenly visions, but to deny that they were the same as those published by the Montanist editor, [Col. 0104B] because they contain many things which revelations imparted to Catholic martyrs could not possibly have contained? Would that, I ask, not have been less rash than arraigning Catholic martyrs on a charge of Montanism? He could also readily have granted that the visions of Perpetua and Saturus which he believed had been fabricated by an impostor were in some respects similar to those then in common circulation and attributed to them, yet not altogether identical with them, because to his exceedingly discerning nostrils the extant visions smell of the Montanists’ workshop.

Finally, according to the hypothesis I am pursuing in this chapter—namely, that the Acts of Perpetua and Felicitas truly do smell of Montanism—one could also readily grant that Perpetua and Saturus wrote down their revelations in prison, but that, when by some chance, before they were made public, [Col. 0104C] they came into the hands of a man entangled in Montanus’s superstitions, he interpolated them at will and, wherever it pleased him, polluted them with heretical inventions. Whatever more expert critics thought savored of Montanism would therefore have to be charged against the interpolator, not against the holy martyrs.

# CHAPTER IV

## A Discussion of the Abundance and Profusion of Visions and Other Heavenly Gifts During the Earlier Centuries of the Church. It Is Demonstrated That the Ancient Christians, Including Those Universally Regarded as Genuine Martyrs of Christ, Employed These Divine Charisms of the Holy Spirit with No Less Confidence Than Perpetua Did

[Col. 0104D]

I. Nothing actually occurs in the Acts of our martyrs that smells of Montanus’s workshop.  
II. Saint Justin Martyr compares the prophets of his time with those of old. From this it is concluded that they employed the prophetic spirit with no less confidence than the ancient prophets.  
III. On the beginnings of the Montanists. None of the most ancient writers regarded the Montanists as false prophets on the ground that they employed the revelations of the Holy Spirit as though at will.  
IV. A notable testimony of Saint Irenaeus concerning the variety and abundance of charisms during this period.  
V. How readily the power of the Holy Spirit was available to the ancient Christians for restraining and driving away demons.  
VI. The memorable example of Potamiaena, no less remarkable than that of Perpetua.  
VII. From Cyprian’s Epistles and from the deacon Pontius it is shown how greatly Cyprian’s life abounded in visions and revelations.  
VIII. The Acts of other martyrs are filled with the same gifts of the Holy Spirit.  
IX. In the age of Perpetua and her fellow martyrs the fountain of charisms overflowed more abundantly than in the time of Cyprian. [Col. 0105A]

### I

Since, as I have repeatedly said, I do not wish the matters I presented in the preceding chapter to be understood as though I intended to call the authenticity (γνησιότης) of the Acts into controversy or to bring their editor, although a Montanist, under suspicion of fraud, [Col. 0105B] we must now undertake another course, likewise with Augustine as our guide, and labor to remove every suspicion of Montanism from Perpetua’s visions, even as they are reported in the Acts.

The authority of the Church requires us to deny that the Acts are polluted by any Montanist inventions or that Perpetua’s revelations contain anything that smells of the school and workshop of Montanus; for the Church approved the public reading of these Acts, not yesterday or the day before, but from ancient times, as is evident from Augustine. [Note: [Col. 0105D] *Exposition on Psalm* 47.] The authority of the Fathers likewise requires this, since they detected nothing foreign to the faith, nothing improper, and finally nothing smelling of the Montanists’ workshop in those very revelations of Perpetua upon which suspicion is now most rashly cast. [Col. 0105C]

The Fathers’ noses were not so dull when they discerned matters of faith that they could not immediately distinguish the good fragrance of Christ and the perfume of heavenly charisms from the stench of heretical fables and absurdities. Not only can no heretical martyr be shown whom the Fathers venerated as Catholic, but neither can there be shown any Acts of Catholic martyrs polluted by heretical fables which they wished to be read publicly in the Church for the edification of the faithful. Like good shepherds, they were exceedingly careful to turn their sheep away from harmful and useless plants. Nor do we believe that a fraud unnoticed by anyone throughout so many centuries has at last been scented out by Basnage.

We must therefore strive [Col. 0105D] to wipe away the stains of heretical depravity cast upon these ancient monuments, approved by the judgment of the ancient Fathers, and thereby also to repel the odium of this crime which has been raised against the holy martyrs. I am confident that I shall easily accomplish this by calling each point back to examination and weighing according to the stricter rules of criticism everything that led Basnage into this perverse opinion concerning the holy martyrs.

First, as we have seen, among his arguments against the martyrs which are intrinsic and drawn from the Acts themselves and from Perpetua’s words, he repeats one from what Perpetua narrates at the beginning of Chapter IV in the following words:

> Then my brother said to me: “Lady sister, you are now in great dignity, so great that you may request a vision and it may be shown to you whether it will be martyrdom or release.” And I, who knew that I conversed with the Lord, from whom I had experienced such great benefits, confidently promised him, saying: “Tomorrow I shall report it to you.” [Col. 0106A]

Basnage says that Perpetua conducts herself in such a way as though she had the Holy Spirit at her command, like a kind of necklace enclosed in a casket, to make use of his illuminations at will whenever she wished. After quoting the preceding words, he adds: “A marvelous confidence, and worthy only of the Montanist household!”

I could easily extricate myself from this argument if I were to bring forward innumerable examples of similar, if not even greater, confidence, amassed not only from the histories of more recent saints but also from Theodoret’s *Religious History*, Gregory’s *Dialogues*, John Moschus’s *Spiritual Meadow*, and other such works of the fifth, sixth, and subsequent centuries. [Col. 0106B] But since my dispute is with men of the sort who rashly deny all credibility to records of that kind, I shall leave these aside for the present and turn to other examples and testimonies which only those would dare oppose who either hold the Christian religion in derision or practice no religion at all.

No one, however, should expect me to gather here every example of heavenly visions, prophetic revelations, and other divine charisms by which the Church was so greatly adorned during its first three centuries. For although this would greatly assist us in understanding whence such confidence entered Perpetua’s mind—namely, from the supreme abundance of heavenly gifts in those times and from the almost daily and common exercise of divine charisms, available to any righteous person—nevertheless, because Henry Dodwell, a theologian of no mean standing among Protestants, composed a lengthy and learned dissertation *On Visions* in connection with Cyprian’s Eighth Epistle, [Col. 0106C] in which, beginning with the apostolic Fathers and continuing down to Cyprian, he defended and vindicated against their critics every passage in which the ancient Fathers discuss visions—against those, as he says, “who refer to Montanus and the Montanists whatever they read concerning visions in the authors of that age”—I shall therefore refrain from that labor at present. I shall merely select both testimonies from the writings of the ancient Fathers and examples from the authentic Acts of the martyrs and approved histories which appear to approach my purpose more closely, and from which it may readily be understood that the Montanist household did not possess as its peculiar property that marvelous confidence of Perpetua by which she promised her brother a revelation of the future event. [Col. 0106D]

### II

Certain passages of Justin Martyr’s *Dialogue with Trypho* should be noted first of all. In that work, as Eusebius observes, [Note: [Col. 0106D] Γράφει δὲ καὶ ὡς ὅτι μέχρι καὶ αὐτοῦ χαρίσματα προφητικὰ διέλαμπεν ἐπὶ τῆς ἐκκλησίας: “He also writes that prophetic gifts continued to shine forth in the Church even down to his own time.” Book IV of the *History*, chap. 18.] “he writes that the gifts of prophecy still flourished in the Church in his own age.” But by producing the testimony of the holy martyr, we shall ascertain and establish more clearly how they flourished. Thus [Col. 0107A] he says: [Note: [Col. 0107D] Παρὰ γὰρ ἡμῖν καὶ μέχρι νῦν προφητικά χαρίσματα ἔστιν· ἐξ οὗ καὶ αὐτοὶ συνιέναι ὀφείλετε, ὅτι τὰ πάλαι ἐν τῷ γένει ὑμῶν ὄντα εἰς ἡμᾶς μετετέθη: “For among us prophetic gifts exist even to the present day; from this you yourselves ought to understand that those which formerly existed among your nation have been transferred to us.”]

> For among us prophetic gifts continue to exist even to the present day; from this you too ought to understand that those which formerly existed among your nation have been transferred to us.

And below, just as he teaches that it was evident and before everyone’s eyes that no prophet then remained among the Jewish nation, so it was equally plain and manifest to everyone that among the Christians there were in truth not only men but also women inspired by the spirit of prophecy, in accordance with Joel’s prediction: [Note: [Col. 0107D] Μηκέτι ἐν τῷ γένει ὑμῶν κατὰ τὸ παλαιὸν ἔθος προφήτας γενήσεσθαι, ὅπερ καὶ ὄψει ὑμῖν ἰδεῖν ἔστι· μετ’ ἐκεῖνον γὰρ οὐδεὶς ὅλως προφήτης παρ’ ὑμῖν γεγένηται: “Prophets would no longer arise among your nation according to the ancient custom, as you yourselves can see with your own eyes; for after him no prophet at all has arisen among you.”]

> Thus there would no longer be prophets among your nation according to the ancient custom, as you yourselves can see with your own eyes. For after him no prophet at all has arisen among you.

And a little later: [Note: [Col. 0107D] Καὶ παρ’ ἡμῖν ἔστιν ἰδεῖν καὶ θηλείας καὶ ἄρσενας χαρίσματα ἀπὸ τοῦ Πνεύματος τοῦ Θεοῦ ἔχοντας: “And among us one may see both females and males possessing gifts from the Spirit of God.”]

> Among us one may see both women and men possessing gifts from the Spirit of God.

By this testimony of Justin Martyr, [Col. 0107B] Basnage is first of all convicted of error when he says [Note: [Col. 0107D] Under the year of the Lord 203, no. V.] that in the New Testament it is reported only of the daughters of Philip the Evangelist that, moved by the divine power, they uttered oracles. For Saint Justin states that the gift of prophecy was common and shared by men and women alike. Although it also seems hardly probable that Philip’s daughters, despite having grown old in Asia, as Polycrates writes to Victor, [Note: [Col. 0107D] In Eusebius, Book V of the *Ecclesiastical History*, chap. 14.] survived until that time.

Asterius Urbanus also numbers among the Catholic prophets of the New Testament—whom he says had no connection with the Montanists, μηδὲν αὐτοῖς προσήκοντας (“having nothing to do with them”)—not only Philip’s daughters but also a certain Ammia of Philadelphia. The Montanists boasted that [Note: [Col. 0107D] Μετὰ Κάδρατον καὶ τὴν ἐν Φιλαδελφείᾳ Ἀμμίαν, αἱ περὶ Μοντανὸν διεδέξαντο γυναῖκές τὸ προφητικὸν χάρισμα: “After Quadratus and Ammia in Philadelphia, the women associated with Montanus succeeded to the prophetic gift.” In Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, chap. 17.] “after Quadratus and Ammia of Philadelphia, those women of Montanus succeeded [Col. 0107C] to the grace of prophecy.”

Justin therefore teaches that the prophetic gifts by which the synagogue had formerly been made illustrious were transferred to these prophets—and prophetesses too—of the New Testament, and this with such clarity and evidence that he calls the Jews themselves as witnesses to the fact. This is surely evidence that the spirit of prophecy poured itself upon these new prophets with no less abundance and profusion than upon the ancient prophets.

But did not the prophets of the Old Testament conduct themselves in such a way as though they had the Holy Spirit at their command, like a kind of necklace enclosed within their breast as in a casket, to make use of his illuminations at will? When questioned by the people about future events, did they not promise, with precisely the same confidence as Perpetua, that they would report the answer to them the next day or after a brief interval? [Col. 0107D]

What wonder is it, then, if we say that such confidence was so familiar to the prophets of the New Testament—such as Agabus, Silas, Quadratus, and Polycarp—and to prophetesses such as Philip’s daughters, Ammia [Col. 0108A] of Philadelphia, and later Perpetua, that when questioned by Christians they replied: “Tomorrow we shall report it to you”? Those men and women of old do not appear to have borne the title of prophet or prophetess because they uttered prophetic oracles only once or twice, but because they employed the prophetic spirit familiarly and, I might almost say, at will. Otherwise, when prophetic gifts formerly overflowed in the Church in such abundance, why were only certain persons—and indeed no very great number—called prophets κατ’ ἐξοχήν (“preeminently”), such as those whom Asterius lists in the frequently cited passage of Eusebius? And why was Polycarp called the διδάσκαλος ἀποστολικὸς καὶ προφητικός (“apostolic and prophetic teacher”) of the Church of Smyrna? [Note: [Col. 0108D] The Epistle concerning his martyrdom, in Eusebius, Book IV of the *Ecclesiastical History*, chap. 15.]

### III

The beginnings of the Montanist [Col. 0108B] faction appear to be placed not long after the martyrdom of Justin, if, as Ruinart [Note: [Col. 0108D] Prefatory Notice to the Acts of Saint Justin Martyr, no. 12.] and others maintain, the holy martyr died in the year 167 or even later. For by the year 177, in which the martyrs of Lyons suffered, the foundations of that abominable sect had already been laid, as Eusebius reports.

At first, as is evident from Eusebius himself, their fortunes varied. Some acclaimed them as true prophets; others remained uncertain what judgment to form concerning them, so much so that the Asians asked by letter for the opinion of other churches, and especially of the Gallican Church, concerning this new prophecy. Eusebius states that the reason they were not immediately rejected by everyone was that, since charisms of divine grace still abounded in the Church, it did not seem extraordinary that those too who outwardly pursued an austere manner of life [Col. 0108C] should abound in heavenly charisms:

> When Montanus, says Eusebius, [Note: [Col. 0108D] Τῶν δ’ ἀμφὶ τὸν Μοντανὸν καὶ Ἀλκιβιάδην καὶ Θεόδοτον, περὶ τὴν Φρυγίαν ἄρτι τότε πρῶτον τὴν περὶ τοῦ προφητεύειν ὑπόληψιν παρὰ πολλοῖς ἐκφερομένων· πλεῖσται γὰρ οὖν καὶ ἄλλαι παραδοξοποιΐαι τοῦ θείου χαρίσματος εἰσέτι τότε κατὰ διαφόρους ἐκκλησίας ἐκτελούμεναι, πίστιν παρὰ πολλοῖς τοῦ κἀκείνους προφητεύειν παρεῖχον· καὶ δὴ διαφωνίας ὑπαρχούσης περὶ τῶν δεδηλωμένων, αὖθις οἱ κατὰ τὴν Γαλλίαν ἀδελφοὶ, τὴν ἰδίαν κρίσιν καὶ περὶ τούτων εὐλαβῆ καὶ ὀρθοδοξοτάτην ὑποτάττουσιν· ἐκθέμενοι καὶ τῶν παρ’ αὐτοῖς τελειωθέντων μαρτύρων διαφόρους ἐπιστολάς· ἃς ἐν δεσμοῖς ἔτι ὑπάρχοντες, τοῖς ἐπ’ Ἀσίας καὶ Φρυγίας ἀδελφοῖς διεχάραξαν, οὐ μὴν ἀλλὰ καὶ Ἐλευθέρῳ τῷ τότε Ῥωμαίων ἐπισκόπῳ, τῆς τῶν ἐκκλησιῶν εἰρήνης ἕνεκα πρεσβεύοντες: “When those associated with Montanus, Alcibiades, and Theodotus were then for the first time gaining among many people in Phrygia the reputation of prophesying—for very many other miraculous works of the divine gift were still being performed at that time throughout various churches, thereby persuading many that these men too prophesied—and when disagreement had arisen concerning those who have been named, the brethren in Gaul once again appended their own reverent and thoroughly orthodox judgment concerning these matters, setting forth various letters of the martyrs who had been perfected among them. While still in bonds, those martyrs had written these letters to the brethren in Asia and Phrygia and also to Eleutherus, then bishop of the Romans, acting as ambassadors for the peace of the churches.” Book V, chap. 3.] and Alcibiades and Theodotus had then first begun to be acclaimed in Phrygia, in popular opinion, as prophets—for many miracles of divine grace were still customarily performed in numerous churches, persuading many that they too prophesied—and when dissension arose on account of these men, the brethren in Gaul once again appended to the same letter their own private judgment concerning these matters, a judgment especially reverent and consonant with right faith. They also produced various letters of the martyrs who had been put to death among them, which those martyrs, while still in bonds, had written partly to the brethren living in Asia and Phrygia and partly to Eleutherus, bishop of the city of Rome, acting as ambassadors for the peace of the churches. [Col. 0109A]

Thus Eusebius concerning the beginnings of the Montanists. From his words everyone may readily understand that such was the abundance of divine charisms in the Church during this period that no firm inference could be drawn that certain persons must be struck from the register of prophets merely because they employed the prophetic spirit with great confidence.

In chapters 16 and following of the same book, Eusebius mentions several writers of that age who attacked the Montanists in weighty treatises, including Apollinaris of Hierapolis, Asterius Urbanus, Miltiades, Apollonius, Serapion, Aelius, and Publius Julius, bishop of the colony of Debeltum in Thrace. From some of them he quotes fairly lengthy passages (ῥήσεις), in which one may see by what kind of signs those ancient Fathers detected the spurious spirit of Montanus. Besides [Col. 0109B] their depraved morals and the falsity of their predictions, they especially objected to these new prophets—as we saw above—that, like demoniacs, suddenly struck by a kind of frenzy and displacement of mind, they uttered mad, untimely, and alien things, contrary to the custom and institution of the Church handed down by the ancestors and thereafter propagated in an unbroken succession.

Nowhere, however, do we read that they charged the Montanists with what Basnage charges against Perpetua, for which he consequently represents her as a Montanist: that they awaited heavenly revelations with great confidence. The learned man will certainly be unable to produce any testimony of the ancient Fathers demonstrating that the great confidence which he finds so remarkable in Perpetua was peculiar to the Montanist household. With what confidence, then—not to say [Col. 0109C] rashness and audacity—did he choose to make this evidence of a Montanist spirit in that most holy woman?
