## Source locus 1

“And he called Moses.” The book is called *Leviticus* because it contains God’s ordinances about the priesthood, sacrifices, and related matters. Exodus too contains divine legislation; but Leviticus also tells how the anointed priests were consecrated, how the tabernacle was constructed and fitted together harmoniously, and many further matters. Its inclusion of other material is no surprise: the title *Leviticus* identifies its principal subject.

<!-- source-locus: 2 -->
## Source locus 2

Of the same author. People who fail to understand the teaching about sacrifices despise it as inferior legislation with nothing worthy of the Creator; or they impiously pass judgment on the Creator himself as though he legislated trivialities. But one who grasps David’s “Uncover my eyes, that I may perceive the wonders from your law,” together with the Apostle’s “The law is spiritual,” can discern divine lessons hidden in these matters.

<!-- source-locus: 3 -->
## Source locus 3

“If any person among you brings gifts to the Lord.” Scripture commonly calls offerings of living creatures “gifts,” while it calls offerings of inanimate things “sacrifices,” as in Genesis: “God looked upon Abel and his gifts, but upon Cain and his sacrifices he did not look.” This distinction should also be noted here. For living offerings it says, “A person among you, if he brings gifts”; for inanimate offerings, “if a soul [brings].” The soul is the inanimate element: thus only the rational part is left from the irrational creature, while, when fire has overcome the inanimate offering, only the living part remains.

<!-- source-locus: 4 -->
## Source locus 4

“They shall pour the blood on the altar.” The altar for whole burnt offerings is called the altar of offering; the altar of incense is the inner altar.

<!-- source-locus: 5 -->
## Source locus 5

Of the same author. The Jews had become accustomed in Egypt to sacrifices and delighted in them, as their making of the calf in the wilderness also shows. God therefore allowed them to offer such sacrifices to him, using this permission to restrain the disorderly impulses of polytheism and keep them from sacrificing to demons.

<!-- source-locus: 6 -->
## Source locus 6

“If one soul sins involuntarily.” An involuntary act is whatever evil someone does without intending it. Scripture calls such an unknowing act a sin. Blessed Paul likewise mentions other matters, saying: “The law had a shadow of the things to come.”

<!-- source-locus: 7 -->
## Source locus 7

Of the same. “If a soul sins involuntarily,” it says, “and does not do from all the commandments of the Lord” [Source text: the syntax of κατὰ φύσιν ἔχει is uncertain]. This belongs to the nature of the case. Perhaps some ordinances of the Lord are not to be carried out, as in: “I gave them ordinances that were not good.” A soul therefore sins unwittingly when, under a pretext of piety, it keeps what ought not to be kept. It needs a sacrifice for forgiveness; the person who acts deliberately does not need one—like the man who became a Jew to gain Jews and circumcised Timothy.

<!-- source-locus: 8 -->
## Source locus 8

“That soul which touches any unclean thing, or a dead thing.” A “dead thing” means a person dead to God, with whom one should not even eat; contact with him makes one unclean. What the devil, our adversary, has hunted down is also unclean. “Unclean cattle” may represent people who come to the Word in a beast-like way, who sin unto death, and who have not become worthy to be saved even alongside cattle, as Scripture says: “You will save human beings and cattle, Lord.”

<!-- source-locus: 9 -->
## Source locus 9

“Everyone who touches them shall be sanctified.” Some understand “shall be sanctified” here to mean “shall be defiled.” This is, I think, not because of the sacrifice itself but because of the presumption of the one who touches it. Haggai says: “Whoever touches [it] shall be defiled.”

<!-- source-locus: 10 -->
## Source locus 10

Of the same. Sacrifices served the priests chiefly as a means of support and as a shadow of piety. Sacrifice also has a twofold benefit: honour is offered to God, and what a person sacrifices is something he will neither worship nor imagine to be God.

<!-- source-locus: 11 -->
## Source locus 11

“And you shall certainly not die.” The passage seems to threaten priests with death if they drink wine or strong drink when entering the tabernacle. Bishops who pray for the people must avoid not only wine-drunkenness but the intoxication that comes from worldly matters and human reasonings. The prohibition is not based on the idea that drinking is intrinsically wrong; rather, it takes account of Israel’s lack of self-control, for when they partook, they went to excess. When entering holy things, they must keep the soul’s discernment sober and their remembrance of God fine and clear.

<!-- source-locus: 12 -->
## Source locus 12

“Why did you not eat the sin-offering in a holy place?” The point is that, unless the priests eat it, the sacrifice remains incomplete and the sin remains. Blood had to be brought in, and they themselves had to eat.

<!-- source-locus: 13 -->
## Source locus 13

“If today they have offered their sin-offerings.” The death of those who offended God in the affair of Nadab and Abihu occurred precisely when they ought to have eaten the people’s sin-offering, so that its sins might be forgiven. How then, after being exposed today as sinners, can we obtain forgiveness by eating it? The priest must first make expiation for himself [Source text: final verb damaged].

<!-- source-locus: 14 -->
## Source locus 14

“These are the cattle that you shall eat.” Wild beasts are never clean: the law says that everything which walks on its hands among wild beasts is unclean. More generally, such beasts may represent the most savage people, of whom the Prophet says, “When evildoers drew near against me to eat my flesh,” and what follows.

<!-- source-locus: 15 -->
## Source locus 15

“Every beast that divides the hoof.” The cloven-hoofed creature represents the person who lives piously in this age while hastening toward the coming age. In dividing the hoof and trimming the claws, he removes what is superfluous and dead from his hands—that is, his deeds—and from his feet—that is, his way of life. And in rumination he does not merely store up spiritual nourishment, but remembers and meditates on it continually.

<!-- source-locus: 16 -->
## Source locus 16

“This is the law concerning cattle, birds, and every soul.” The passage says much about clean and unclean things. Its first lesson trains the soul to holiness. If something natural, made under God’s ordinance, can be unclean, how much more can what comes from a wicked choice rather than God’s ordinance be unclean? If anyone regards as unclean something whose maker is God, how much more what opposes God’s will and does what he forbade?

<!-- source-locus: 17 -->
## Source locus 17

“If a scar arises in the skin of a person’s flesh” [Source text: the opening quotation is badly distorted]. This refers to blemishes of the soul. Just as a physical wound can leave a scar after treatment, so a soul wounded by sin can retain, even after healing, something like a scar. One must understand how it is purified—or not purified.

<!-- source-locus: 18 -->
## Source locus 18

“If a man or woman develops a leprous lesion” [source text: `ἀρἡ`, probably `ἁφή`]. One should note the settings in which the text speaks of human beings: “If something arises in someone in the skin of his flesh,” or “if a man or woman develops a leprous lesion”; and those in which it speaks instead of flesh: “If flesh has an ulcer in its skin,” or “If flesh has a burn of fire in its skin.” These are wounds.

<!-- source-locus: 19 -->
## Source locus 19

“His garments shall be loosened.” This implies that a person whose soul is leprous must not hide or cover his sins. Just as the leper’s garments are loosened, uncovering the ugliness they had concealed, so he is to be exposed to all, not made into a whitewashed tomb. Covering the mouth means having no freedom to open it; leaving the camp means no longer dwelling with the assemblies of the saints.

<!-- source-locus: 20 -->
## Source locus 20

“They shall slaughter one little bird into an earthen vessel.” The bird is sacrificed in an earthen vessel into which living water has first been put, so that cleansing may be accomplished by water and blood—the water and blood that came from the Saviour’s side. The cedar wood signifies the wood of our salvation; the twisted scarlet thread typifies the precious blood by which the whole world is cleansed. The hyssop signifies, I take it, the warmth of the Spirit, and the scarlet cord the Word’s union with flesh.

<!-- source-locus: 21 -->
## Source locus 21

“He shall shave his head and beard.” Shaving the head concerns the primary and most fundamental doctrines. Shaving the beard, the symbol of manhood, means putting away a man’s sins. Shaving the eyebrows means laying aside every conceit and, if one may call it so, every raised-brow attitude. He says that these things, which have grown upon and sprung up from the soul and are called “hairs,” symbolize deadness.

<!-- source-locus: 22 -->
## Source locus 22

“He shall take two unblemished year-old lambs.” The offerings are divided into three: one brings release from former sins; one is offered whole to God; and one is offered on behalf of the person being cleansed for what he has done. Perhaps the three tenths of fine flour correspond to these: one portion accompanies the lamb being slaughtered, a second the other lamb, and a third the sheep. At the fifth cleansing there is no ordinary meal but fine flour, the material of pure bread, mingled with oil. The oil is used because it feeds a light, serving the need of light rather than fire. There is also, separately from the oil mixed into the flour, a cotyle of oil offered through the priest. The wording “the priest who cleanses shall set the person being cleansed and these things before the Lord” seems especially to require the presence of a priest.

<!-- source-locus: 23 -->
## Source locus 23

“And [Source text: the clause about the householder is damaged] shall come concerning his house and report to the priest.” The lawgiver does not mean that someone goes to the priest to accuse his own house. Rather, he goes as someone who asks a physician for a remedy to what has happened. The synagogue of the Jews, then, has become leprous. The reporting figure is the choir of holy prophets. They report the sufferings caused by the Jews’ reckless folly to the great high priest, before indignation arises—not out of hatred or hostility, but in reverence toward the Benefactor. They acknowledge the Jews’ transgressions while still asking that they receive mercy.
