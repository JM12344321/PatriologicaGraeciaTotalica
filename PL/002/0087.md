[Note: [Col. 0826D] “Even now subject to fevers and ulcers.” The sense is this: because the heretics babbled that the resurrection had already occurred through spiritual life and through a kind of withdrawal and separation from the body, which they called a tomb, when the soul asserted and freed itself from the body’s laws and, as it were, its weight—for they were not speaking merely of departure from the body, which occurs through natural death—the Author cleverly and ironically says: Very well, let the awakened heretic come forth from the tomb of the body; but how is he still subject to fevers and ulcers, which prove that he bears the body’s burden? ## CHAPTER XXIII

The Apostle, writing to the Colossians (Col. I, 29), does indeed teach that we were once dead, estranged and hostile to the Lord in our understanding, while we lived in the most wicked works; and then that we were buried with Christ in baptism and raised together with him through faith in the effective power of God, who raised him from the dead: “And when you were dead in your transgressions and in the uncircumcision of your flesh, he made you alive with him, forgiving you all transgressions.” And again: “If you died with Christ to the elements of the world, why do you pass judgment as though living in the world?”

[Note: [Col. 0825C] The editors conjecture the singular, “understanding.”]

[Note: [Col. 0825C] Others read “raised.”]

[Note: [Col. 0825C] Others read “uncircumcision.”]

[Note: [Col. 0825C] Francius and Pamelius read “as indeed”; Junius, “why as.”]

But while he thus makes us spiritually dead, yet acknowledges that at some time we shall also die bodily; and therefore, while likewise reckoning us spiritually raised, he equally does not deny that we shall also rise bodily. Finally, he says: “If you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, sitting at God’s right hand; set your mind on the things above, not on those below.” Thus he shows that it is in mind that we rise, since as yet it is by the mind alone that we can reach heavenly things. We would neither seek nor set our minds upon them if we already possessed them.

[Note: [Col. 0825D] Venice reads “we shall seek.”]

He also adds: “For you are dead”—that is, to transgressions, not to yourselves—“and your life is hidden with Christ in God.” What is hidden, therefore, has not yet been grasped.

[Note: [Col. 0826C] Semler and Rhenanus read “to us.”]

[Note: [Col. 0826C] Paris reads “our.”]

So too John: “And,” he says, “it has not yet been manifested what we shall be. We know that, if he manifests himself, we shall be like him.” So far are we from already being what we do not know—for surely, if we already were it, we would know. Thus during this interval there is a contemplation of hope through faith, not its manifestation or possession, but its expectation.

[Note: [Col. 0826C] Rhenanus and Semler read “it will be judged.”]

While he himself remains in suspense toward this, he writes to the Philippians (Phil. What voice of the archangel, what trumpet of God, has already been heard—unless perhaps in the chambers of the heretics? For even if the evangelical word may be called the trumpet of God, which has already summoned them, still they must either already be bodily dead in order to have risen—and then how are they alive?—or have been caught up into the clouds—and then how are they here? Truly most wretched, as the Apostle declared, they will be found to have hoped in this life alone, and are to be excluded because they seize beforehand what is promised after it, having missed the truth no less than Phygelus and Hermogenes.

[Note: [Col. 0827D] “For unless the apostasy comes first”—namely, the apostasy of this empire. Paul’s statement in the First Epistle to the Corinthians VII is: “The time that remains has been shortened” (*ho kairos synestalmenos to loipon estin*, ὁ καιρὸς συνεσταλμένος τὸ λοιπὸν ἐστιν). From this Tertullian translated his expression, “The time has been gathered in.” Nor does Peter mean anything else when, in his later epistle, he affirms that the day of the Lord—which will make present to the saints the joys promised them—is not delayed.

But the interpretation of this admonition held the minds of some among the Christian common people of Thessalonica in such uncertainty that, as though already wearied by waiting, they began to call their hopes empty, to despair of the Lord’s advent, and thus to lend an ear to impostures urging them toward apostasy. Who would not marvel that people formed by the discourses of the Apostle himself as he administered the divine commandments should prove so frivolous as not to retain what they had properly understood?

Paul therefore comes to the aid of those dangerously troubled and asks that they not allow their faith to be undermined by rash fickleness, but remember what he had discussed with them in person concerning the end of the age and the day of the final judgment. That day of the Lord would not be present until first the departure occurred, and immediately thereafter the man of sin, the man of perdition, was made manifest. The Apostle therefore says that they had no reason to be shaken as though deceived by false promises, for two things would precede it: the apostasy and that man of sin.

That Paul had spoken at length about these two matters when he came to Thessalonica can be inferred from his rebuking them as forgetful and insufficiently retentive. He then mentions darkly and in riddles the matters he had treated more openly among them. “For,” he says, “the mystery of iniquity is already at work; only let him who now holds continue to hold, until he is removed from the midst.”

He had said above that the advent of the Lord’s kingdom had been ordered in such a way that first the apostasy must precede it, and then the man of sin. He says that the mystery of iniquity was already bringing this man of sin into operation even then, indicating impostors, false brothers, and perverse Christians, who were furtively and secretly proceeding to choke the apostolic seed with sterile wild oats and ill-fated darnel. These were indeed the cradle of Antichrist, already creeping in behind the apostles’ footsteps, but destined not to ravage openly until the apostasy had first occurred.

“Only let him who holds continue to hold, until he departs from the midst.” The Apostle surely would not have wrapped these matters up so carefully unless he meant something whose more explicit declaration might have harmed either himself or even the Thessalonian church. He therefore sought darkness a second and a third time. For after saying that the day of the Lord would not come unless the apostasy came first, he added, “You know what restrains.” Finally he poured yet more obscurity over it: *monon ho katechōn arti, heōs ek mesou genētai* (μόνον ὃ κατέχων ἄρτι, ἕως ἐκ μέσου γένηται), which the old translator, so far as he was able, plunged into still darker concealment: “Only let him who holds continue to hold, until he is removed from the midst.”

These things may indeed have remained unknown to those from whom it was conducive to Christian peace that they be concealed. I would by no means believe, however, that they were obscure to the Thessalonians, with whom the Apostle had personally discussed the matter. Nor were they obscure to the Christians immediately succeeding the apostolic tradition, from whom Tertullian appears to have received the interpretation which, in his book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, he inserts among the Apostle’s words in the clearest light at last.

He reports the very words showing that the day of the Lord was not imminent and would not come “unless the apostasy comes first.” “Of this empire, of course,” he says, plainly signifying the Roman Empire, “and the man of transgression is revealed, that is, Antichrist, the son of perdition, who opposes and exalts himself above everything called God or religion, so that he sits in God’s temple, declaring himself to be God. Do you not remember that, when I was with you, I told you these things? And now you know what restrains him, so that he may be revealed in his own time. For the mystery of iniquity is already at work; only let him who now holds continue to hold, until he is removed from the midst. Who but the Roman state, whose departure, when it has been dispersed among ten kings, will bring Antichrist upon us, and then the lawless one will be revealed?”

We observe that the interpretation of so great a man, one so close to the times of the apostles, was confirmed as most appropriate to the matter under discussion by the common agreement of all later writers except Augustine and Theodoret. Augustine, indeed, in Book XX of *The City of God*, admits that he is altogether ignorant of what the Apostle meant. Yet immediately afterward he adds, in the clearest terms, the “suspicion,” as he calls it, of those who believed that Paul had been unwilling to write it openly lest he incur the accusation that he wished evil upon the Roman Empire, which was expected to be eternal.

Chrysostom certainly judged this suspicion—or rather opinion—so consonant with the truth that, neglecting the other interpretations, he handed down this one alone to his church as entirely certain, praising the counsel of the most holy Apostle in avoiding hatreds which he would have incurred not only without any benefit to the Christian cause, but at the greatest danger to it. For had the letter once been intercepted, and the prophecy of an apostasy destructive to the Roman Empire discovered, who would not immediately have crushed the Apostle himself as leader and standard-bearer, and indeed all the faithful as accomplices in a most criminal conspiracy?

As for Theodoret, I marvel that, after rejecting so suitable an interpretation, he follows one utterly insipid: namely, that the words “you know what restrains” and “only let him who holds continue to hold” signify the decrees of God forbidding Antichrist’s assault until the Gospel has been preached throughout the whole world. For what prevented this from being written in the plainest possible terms? Why did it have to be so heavily shadowed by such studied obscurity?

Let us therefore be content with our Tertullian, to whose opinion Augustine himself finally appears to yield. “Yet,” he says, “the Apostle’s statement, ‘Only let him who now holds continue to hold,’ is not unreasonably believed to have been spoken of the Roman Empire itself, as though it said: ‘Only let him who now rules continue to rule until he is removed from the midst,’ that is, taken away from the midst.” Jerome explained this opinion so accurately and copiously in his *Epistle to the Philippians* and Question XI *To Algasia* that he left no room for doubt. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0829A] Rhenanus reads “who.”]

[Note: [Col. 0829A] Rhenanus and Semler read “holds.”]

[Note: [Col. 0830C] “Only let him who now holds continue to hold.” Lampridius relates that, by a similar riddle, a prophet promised Alexander Severus the imperial power: “The imperial power awaits you—the power which holds imperial power.” From this, he says, it was understood that Alexander would become ruler of the Roman Empire; for where is imperial power except among the Romans—the power which holds imperial power? ## CHAPTER XXV

In the Apocalypse of John, too, the sequence of the times is set forth—a sequence which even the souls of the martyrs beneath the altar, as they demand vengeance and judgment, have learned to await: first, the world must drink its plagues from the angels’ bowls; that prostituted city must receive its deserved end from the ten kings; and the beast, Antichrist, together with his false prophet, must wage war against the Church of God. Then, after the devil has meanwhile been confined in the abyss, the privilege of the first resurrection is to be dispensed from the thrones; and afterward, once the fire has also been given, the verdict of the universal resurrection is to be adjudged from the books.

[Note: [Col. 0830D] “In the Apocalypse of John.” He indicates chapter 20. From this very many took occasion to embrace the opinion of the Chiliasts, or Millenarians. They believed that the righteous would remain upon the earth for a thousand years, so as to receive a hundredfold return for what they had abandoned for Christ, as the Lord had promised. There were very many heretics of this kind in antiquity, among whom Cerinthus was conspicuous. Tertullian nevertheless differs from him, although he appears to have imbibed the Chiliasts’ error. He shared this with certain ancient Fathers—I mean Papias, bishop of Hierapolis, and blessed Justin in his *Dialogue Against Trypho*. Eusebius teaches what authority Justin possessed, Book IV of the *History*, chapter 17: *houtōsi de spoudēs einai axioi kai tois palaiois edokoun hoi tandros logoi, hōs ton Eirēnion apomnēmoneuein autou phōnas* (οὑτωσὶ δὲ σπουδῆς εἶναι ἄξιοι καὶ τοῖς παλαιοῖς ἐδόκουν οἱ τἀνδρὸς λόγοι, ὡς τὸν Εἰρήνιον ἀπομνημονεύειν αὐτοῦ φωνάς): “The writings of this man Justin were considered so worthy of attention by the ancients that Irenaeus quotes his words.” After Tertullian, others followed the error; Tertullian himself, however, is excused by blessed Augustine in chapter 7 of Book XX of *The City of God*. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0830A] “John” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0831D] “Together with his false prophet.” I would say that Satan is meant, were it not immediately to follow, “and then, after the devil has meanwhile been confined in the abyss.” It remains, therefore, for us to say that the one meant is the lawless agent who presides over Antichristian fury in the age. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0831C] Rhenanus and Semler read “bound.”]

[Note: [Col. 0831D] “From the thrones.” From the thrones in Apocalypse XX. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0831C] Pamelius and Francius read “to the fire.”]

[Note: [Col. 0831D] “From the books”: *tōn gegrammenōn en tois bibliois* (τῶν γεγραμμένων ἐν τοῖς βιβλίοις), “of the things written in the books.” Ibid. RIG.]

Since, therefore, the Scriptures mark out both the condition of the final times and place the whole fruit of Christian hope at the *exodium*—the conclusion—of the age, it is evident either that everything whatsoever promised to us by God is then fulfilled, in which case what the heretics claim to possess already here is void; or, if the knowledge of the mystery is also a resurrection, that resurrection which is proclaimed for the end is certainly still believed intact. It follows that, by the very fact that the present resurrection is claimed as spiritual, the other is prejudged to be bodily. For if no resurrection were proclaimed for that time, this present resurrection alone, and as solely spiritual, would rightly be claimed. But since a resurrection is also declared for the final time, it is recognized as bodily, because a spiritual resurrection is not proclaimed for that time as well.

[Note: [Col. 0831D] “They place the whole fruit of Christian hope at the *exodium* of the age.” That is, at the end of the age. “*Exodium*: departure, end,” say the ancient glosses; Greek *exodion* (ἐξόδιον), “funeral rites.” Lucilius:

> First will come an ending worthy of him, and then the *exodium*.

Juvenal, Satire III:

> …and at last he returns to the stage for the familiar  
> *Exodium*….

According to an ancient interpreter, the *exodiarius* among the ancients entered at the end of the games, and the laughter of this spectacle wiped away whatever tears and sadness the tragic emotions had produced. Septimius therefore cleverly called the end of the age an *exodium*. For then the time will be present when, for faithful Christians, the sadness of all their labors and afflictions will be turned into joy of the kind Septimius describes near the end of his book *On the Spectacles*. RIG.]

For why would a resurrection of the same kind—that is, a spiritual one—be proclaimed a second time, when it ought either to be accomplished now, without any distinction of times, or then, at the complete closure of the times? Thus it is all the more fitting for us also to defend a spiritual resurrection from the entrance of faith, since we acknowledge its fullness at the departure of the age.
