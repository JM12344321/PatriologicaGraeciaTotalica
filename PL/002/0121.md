[Col. 1154B] **At the Pelusian games.** Pelusium, now Damietta, is situated at the easternmost mouth of the Nile. It was once a noble city, reportedly founded by Peleus, Achilles’ father, after he had been ordered to purify himself in a nearby lake because he had killed his brother Phocus, and had thereby recovered his sanity from the Furies by whom he was tormented on account of that murder. There, in honor of Diana—who in the language of the Egyptians, and hence the city itself under another name, was called Bubastis—games once very famous were held annually, as Herodotus again recounts at length in *Euterpe*, ch. 49. I am nevertheless aware that our Vulgate translator, at Ezek. 30:16–17, distinguishes Bubastis from Pelusium: “Like a woman in labor, (Sin) PELUSIUM shall be in pain; the young men of (Phibeseth) BUBASTIS shall fall by the sword.”

**They are dipped.** These games, without doubt, like all others of their kind, were accompanied by sacrifices, preceded, of course, by washings and purifications.

**They presume.** Just as philosophers who once enjoyed no small [Col. 1154C] authority—and especially Thales of Miletus among them—believed that the generation of all things proceeded from Water as its first principle, so perjurers, already nearly dead, as it were, because the vengeance of the angry gods threatened them everywhere and at every moment, imagined that through washings of this kind—which, as they falsely believed, expiated those crimes—they obtained a kind of immunity from punishment, returned to a new life and its security, and were, as it were, regenerated. Since, therefore, perjury was extremely common among the Greeks, before the games they were particularly anxious to propitiate the gods beforehand by washings of this kind, lest they should disgracefully succumb during the games, as though these constituted a public punishment for their perjuries.

**He purified himself.** Many examples of this practice survive, especially among the Greeks. Thus, in Herodotus, bk. I, ch. 7, Adrastus, guilty of fratricide, was purified by Croesus, king of the Lydians. They also relate that Apollo and Diana, after Python had been slain, came to Aegialea to purify themselves, as Pausanias records in *Corinth*, ch. 51. The same Pausanias, bk. I, ch. 74, relates: “Before Orestes had washed away through expiations the stain of his mother’s blood, none of the Troezenians was willing to receive him beneath his roof,” etc. But, if we believe Virgil, this [Col. 1154D] rite was already practiced among the Trojans; for Aeneas thus speaks to Anchises, *Aeneid* II, v. 717:

> You, father, take the sacred objects in your hand, and our ancestral Penates.  
> For me, departing from so great a war and from recent slaughter,  
> It would be sacrilege to touch them until in a living stream  
> I have washed myself . . .

And Seneca, *Hercules Furens*, Act IV, Scene I, v. 918:

> . . . Son, first purify your hands,  
> Dripping with bloody and hostile slaughter.

Ovid, indeed, himself mocks this custom of the gentiles in *Fasti* II:

> Ah, all too credulous are you who think that grievous crimes of blood  
> Can be removed by river-water.

Yet it is established that at that time, under the name of expiations and purifications, nothing but washings performed with water were ordinarily understood.

[Col. 1155A] A singular example, moreover, appears in Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities*, bk. V, ch. 58. When, by decree of the Senate, the conspirators had been publicly and lawfully put to death at Rome, a further decree of the Senate was then enacted that the entire city should be purified, because necessity had compelled it to become responsible for the killing of citizens: for it was unlawful to approach the sacred rites and sacrifice victims before the deed had been expiated and the pollution removed by purifications.

**Water in its natural state**, that is, natural water left to its own nature.

**Of purification.** That is, of a purification to be performed under favorable auspices, and to be undertaken successfully and with heaven itself assenting. I therefore do not see why, with Junius, one should read *auspicii et mundationis*, “of auspices and purification.”

**To be medicated**, that is, to be imbued with medicinal power. Yet I think it should be understood in another sense, one that is more common and better here, as in *Aeneid* XI:

> But it had no power to heal (*medicari*) the wound  
> Of the Dardanian spear.

[Col. 1155B] **The rival of God**, namely, the Devil.

**By a sacrament**, or mystery, and even apart from the use and application of water in the washings thus far described.

**They know.** Others read *they are*; not badly, though *they know* seems to me more elegant and should therefore be retained.

**And whatever springs.** Springs bursting from caves or other shady places were believed to possess something divine—or, rather, some diabolical power; hence blind paganism ordinarily bestowed divine honor upon them. Seneca says of these in Epistle 41: “We venerate the sources of great rivers; the sudden eruption of an abundant stream from concealment has its altars; springs of warm water are worshiped, and either their darkness or their immense depth has made certain pools sacred.” At Rome there was also a spring sacred to the nymph Egeria at the foot of the Aventine Hill, of which Livy, bk. I, ch. 19, relates: “There was a grove whose center a perennial stream watered from a dark cave; because Numa very frequently withdrew there without witnesses . . . he consecrated the grove to the Camenae.” Plutarch adds [Col. 1155C] in his *Numa* that Numa placed the Vestal Virgins in charge of this spring, etc., as we reported above in the note **Temples**.

Accordingly, springs of this kind, because of some dreadful majesty—I know not what—of the places from which they welled forth, were altogether numbered among the gods; and in their honor particular sacred observances were established, such as the Fontinalia at Rome, customarily celebrated on October 3, and many others elsewhere.

Nor did they believe that a certain hidden divinity inhered only in beneficial springs, but also in harmful ones, which had to be propitiated through divine worship so that their destructive power—though it was in fact implanted in them by nature—might be averted. Of this kind were the waters of the Alcyonian marsh, mentioned by Pausanias toward the end of his *Corinth*, which dragged swimmers down to the very bottom; likewise the springs of the river Acidans, arising from Lapitha, a mountain of Arcadia, and exhaling so foul an odor that their very breath was sufficient to kill human beings, according to the same Pausanias in *Elis* I. I pass over many other such springs discussed by [Col. 1155D] Polemon in his book *On the Marvels of Sicily*, as well as by the author of the book *On Marvels*, commonly believed to be Aristotle, by Pliny, bk. XXXI, Solinus, ch. 31, and others.

**Streams.** “A stream,” Festus says, “is commonly the name for a slender flow of water produced not by expectation or design, but by its own natural impulse.” Others employ the word with other meanings.

**Pools.** *Piscinae* designated not only ἰχθυοτροφεῖα (*ichthyotropheia*), or fishponds, but also collections of cold water, gathered for swimming or pleasurable bathing into a fairly large place enclosed on every side, like a kind of artificial lake, even though otherwise devoid of fish. Thus Lampridius says of the emperor Alexander in his *Life*: “After being anointed, he bathed in such a way that he never, or only rarely, used hot baths, but always a pool”; and Victor, bishop of Tunnuna, says in his *Chronicle* of Arrianus Olympius: “He impiously ended his life in a pool of cold water.” Of this kind, too, was the Sheep Pool discussed at John 5 and the swimming pool of Siloam in John 9; we shall discuss them below.

[Col. 1156A] Furthermore, Tertullian himself testifies in *On the Shows*, ch. 8, that baths and the pools that formed one part of them and were nothing other than cold baths were consecrated to gods, or evil demons and idols: “The streets, the forum, the baths, the stables, and even our own houses are not entirely free from idols. Satan and his angels have filled the whole age”—that is, the world. Indeed, in *On Idolatry*, ch. 15, he also testifies: “We see even doors worshiped in the baths. . . . Whatever you do to a door, you will have done to an idol.” Thus, among the other filth of idols, the gentiles also had household gods of baths and doorways, and I know not what other filthy divinities, under whose most shameful auspices they might wash—or, more truly, be defiled with far fouler filth.

**Euripi.** *Euripus* was the particular name of that turbulent strait between Euboea and Attica which reverses itself seven times during the day and as many times during the night, alternately spewing out its waters and swallowing them again. After Aristotle had unsuccessfully investigated its causes, he is said to have wasted away from grief or, as others relate—though with little truth—to have thrown himself headlong into it. Subsequently, [Col. 1156B] this designation began to be extended to “trenches and channels with water,” as Lipsius says on Seneca, Epistle 83, such as were customarily found in gardens and praetorian residences. Indeed, water-basins generally into which aqueducts discharged were called εὔριποι (*euripoi*), either παρὰ τοῦ εὐριπίζεσθαι, “from being swept along like a Euripus,” because through them the waters readily rushed down to that place and were driven about in turbulent whirlpools, or, more truly, because they flowed around a certain portion of land as though it were an island. Pausanias says of these in *Laconia*, ch. 96: “The field in which the youths join battle is surrounded on all sides by a euripus, just as an island is by the sea.” Dionysius of Halicarnassus, bk. III, ch. 91, describes the Circus Maximus built by Tarquin as follows: εὔριπος εἰς ὑποδοχὴν ὕδατος ὀρώρυκται, βάθος τε καὶ πλάτος δεκάπους, that is: “A euripus has been dug around it to receive the waters, ten feet in depth and likewise in width.” Suetonius also says of this in *Julius*, ch. 39: “At the Circensian games, after the Circus area had been extended on either side and a euripus added around its circuit, young men of the highest nobility raced four-horse and two-horse chariots and trick-riding horses.” [Col. 1156C] Naval games were also presented there, water being introduced into the Circus itself through euripi—for larger channels and aqueducts were also so called. Indeed, the extravagance of certain emperors had grown to such an extent that Spartianus records of Heliogabalus: “He is said to have presented naval Circensian games in euripi filled with wine.” Moreover, these euripi too were consecrated to certain gods, as Tertullian testifies in *On the Shows*, ch. 10: “She therefore”—namely, the Great Mother, Ops, Tellus, or Cybele—“presides there over the euripus.”

**Cisterns.** This is the name given to underground receptacles for rainwater; Festus thinks that they are so called because they lie *cis*, or beneath, the ground.

**Wells.** Wells are deep receptacles of living water that continually wells up from certain hidden veins. The Aeolians formerly called them ποτήρια (*potēria*), ἀπὸ τοῦ ποτοῦ, “from drink”; hence some maintain that our *putei* were named from *potus*, “drink,” as though they were *potei*, that is, things supplying us with a most wholesome drink. Of this kind was the well in Homer, *Odyssey* X, from which Ulysses was to [Col. 1156D] summon from the underworld the shade of the seer Tiresias.

**To seize.** Namely, those who, as St. Justin Martyr says in *Apology* II, are λαμβανόμενοι καὶ ῥιπτούμενοι by the shades—or spirits—of the dead, “seized and hurled down,” and whom everyone calls δαιμονιολήπτους (*daimoniolēptous*), “demon-possessed,” *arreptitii*, “seized,” and μαινομένους (*mainomenous*), “raging mad.”

**Of a harmful spirit.** Certain particular spirits were assigned by the gentiles to all these springs, streams, pools, euripi, cisterns, wells, etc. Since demons appeared there in female form, they were also worshiped in this form and were believed to inhabit those caves watered by the outflow of springs. Thus Virgil, in *Aeneid* I, calls that Libyan

> . . . cave beneath overhanging rocks

the dwelling of the Nymphs. Pausanias likewise mentions in his *Boeotia* a particular cave belonging to the Cithaeronian Nymphs. Describing the Corycian cave in his *Phocis*, [Col. 1157A] he says: “Many streams flow here and there from living springs. The inhabitants of Parnassus regard it as sacred to the Corycian Nymphs.” Likewise, as the same author again testifies in his *Boeotia*, the Libethrian Nymphs were worshiped at the Libethrian spring. Other springs also had their own altars, shrines, or even temples consecrated to the Nymphs; these too were called *Nymphaea*, such as the one above the spring of the river Cytherus mentioned by Pausanias in *Elis* II. More particularly, the Nymphs presiding over rivers, pools, and wells were called Naiads, Ephydriads, and so forth. Comatas, too, in Theocritus, *Idyll* 5, swears by the Nymphs λιμνάδας (*limnadas*), “of the marshes.” And just as Orpheus calls the Nymphs daughters of Oceanus and Tethys, so Virgil, *Aeneid* VIII, considered them the mothers of rivers, because rivers arose from the sea:

> Nymphs, Laurentian Nymphs, from whom the rivers take their birth.

These Nymphs, moreover, were by no means always regarded as benevolent divinities, but had to be appeased with sacrifices lest they cause harm. [Col. 1157B] Hence in Theocritus, *Idyll* XIII, they were δειναὶ θεαὶ ἀγρώταις, “dread divinities to country folk,” as we shall presently see. Meanwhile, should anyone desire further information, he will find it in Natalis Comes, *Mythology*, bk. V, ch. 2, and also in our most learned Montfaucon, *L’Antiquité expliquée*, vol. I, part II, bk. IV, ch. 7.

**Half-dead.** Laurentius Valla, bk. IV, ch. 27, calls those *enecti* who have not already been killed and died but have very nearly reached that condition. Hence, when our author in *On Fasting*, ch. 7, mentions that severe fast of the Ninevites, during which even the cattle were *enecta*, Junius rightly explains these words as referring to beasts not already dead, but so emaciated by hunger through this fast—which, according to Jonah 3:7, was extended even to the cattle by decree of the king of Nineveh—that they were now nearer death than life; and he says that this is the proper meaning of the verb *enecare*.

**Driven mad by the Nymphs.** Daniel Heinsius, in his *Readings on Theocritus*, *Idyll* V, says as follows: “Whenever [Col. 1157C] antiquity mentioned the Nymphs, it prayed to be spared their anger, that is, madness or βλεποδαιμονία (*blepodaimonia*, ‘demon-sight’; for those to whom those goddesses appeared were believed to have lost their mental balance). The same thing was also believed to happen to seers, who were called νυμφολήπτους (*nympholēptous*, ‘seized by Nymphs’); our Latin authors, however, ordinarily call them not *nymphati* but *lymphati*.” Hence our author also says in *On the Soul*, ch. 50: “The demonic spring at Colophon makes people *lymphatici*,” or frenzied. On this see Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, bk. II, ch. 5.

**Hydrophobic.** They are called ὑδροφόβοι (*hydrophoboi*), παρὰ τὸ φοβεῖσθαι τὸ ὕδωρ, “from fearing water.” This happens especially to persons bitten by a rabid dog; their most pitiable condition is to be tormented by an extreme fear of water and simultaneously by an equal thirst. For the dreadful effects of this disease and other relevant matters, see Kircher, *Mundus Subterraneus*, bk. IX, section II, ch. 5. The gentiles formerly attributed all these things to evil spirits inhabiting the water.

**Profane.** In Holy Scripture the holy and the profane are customarily opposed; thus a person is here called profane [Col. 1157D] who has nothing whatever in common with holiness.

**Pool.** In the Greek text the word is κολυμβήθρα (*kolymbēthra*), by which *piscina*, in the sense discussed above in the note **Sprinkling**, signifies a bath or swimming pool.

**Bethsaida.** The Greek text of John 5:2 reads: Ἔστι δὲ ἐν Ἰεροσολύμοις ἐπὶ τῇ πφοβατικῇ κολυμβήθρᾳ, ἡ ἐπιλεγομένη ἑβραϊστὶ Βηθεσδὰ, πέντε στοὰς ἔχουσα, κ.τ.λ. [read contextually: προβατικῇ], “Now there is in Jerusalem, by the Sheep Pool, the one called in Hebrew Bethesda, having five porticoes,” etc. Our Vulgate renders this: “Now there is at Jerusalem a Sheep Pool, which in Hebrew is surnamed Bethsaida, having five porticoes,” etc. Here it should be noted that this pool is called *probatica* not from *probare*, “to test,” but ἀπὸ τῶν προβάτων, “from sheep”; for τὸ πρόβατον means “sheep” in Greek. Thus *probatica* here is equivalent to “ovine” or “belonging to sheep.” It was called the Sheep Pool because sheep and other animals intended for sacrifice were customarily washed in it, or because the entrails of animals already sacrificed were cleansed there. Others say that it was not the pool itself but the gate beside which it stood that was called the Sheep Gate—for in the Greek one reads ἐν, “in,” or, as other [Col. 1158A] manuscripts have it, ἐπὶ τῇ προβατικῇ, “at,” or rather “by, the Sheep [Gate],” perhaps the same one that at 2 Esdras 3:1 is called the Gate of the Flock. But these observations are offered ὡς ἐν παρόδῳ, “in passing.”

Moreover, this pool, which in the Greek text is called Bethesda—“house of mercy”—and in Latin manuscripts Bethsaida—“house of fishing”—may have been so called because it was once a fishpond.

**Stirred.** Some believed this angel to have been the archangel Raphael, whose name is interpreted “Medicine of GOD”: just as he had once been sent by GOD particularly to deliver Tobit from his blindness, so he was appointed to cure the more serious infirmities of other people as well. Whoever he was, one must suppose that he did not descend in any visible form, but merely imbued the water, by a singular command of GOD, with a medicinal power impressed upon it, as it were, in passing, and thus produced a visible sign of his presence.

**They precede.** A figure must always be better known [Col. 1158B] and better accommodated to comprehension. Hence, to prefigure spiritual things, we ordinarily choose bodily or sensible signs, through which we may, as it were, be led by the hand toward an understanding of spiritual realities and higher mysteries that would otherwise exceed all human comprehension.

Moreover, among the other Holy Fathers, St. Ambrose especially asserts in *On the Holy Spirit*, bk. I, ch. 7, that this pool was a figure of our BAPTISM:

> You also have it in the Gospel that at the appointed time an Angel descended into the swimming pool, and the water was stirred, and whoever first descended into the pool was made well. What did the Angel in this type announce except the descent of the Holy Spirit, which was to occur in our times and, when invoked by priestly prayers, consecrate the WATERS—those of Baptism, of course? That Angel, therefore, was the messenger of the Holy Spirit, because through spiritual grace medicine was to be brought to the weaknesses of our soul and mind.

And in *On Those Who Are Initiated into the Mysteries*, ch. 4, he says no less elegantly:

> For them an Angel descended into that pool; [Col. 1158C] for you, the Holy Spirit. For them a creature was stirred; for you, CHRIST himself, the LORD of creation, is at work. Then one person was cured; now all are healed. Therefore, that pool too was a figure, so that you might believe that divine power descends into this Font. Finally, that paralytic was awaiting a man. Whom, if not that Lord Jesus born of the Virgin, at whose advent it would no longer be a shadow that healed individuals, but the truth that healed all?

**The grace of God.** Namely, after the advent of Christ, the Author of grace.

**They healed.** Our author also uses the same verb in *To Scapula*, ch. 4: “How many honorable men have been healed either of demons or of illnesses.” See also *On Prescription*, ch. 38. Although this verb is not Ciceronian, St. Jerome is nevertheless known to have used it in the *Life of Hilarion*.

**The spirit.** Some read: “They minister healing to the spirit” (*Spiritui medentur*). But what prevents us from retaining the accusative *spiritum*, “the spirit,” as the object? For Terence too has in the *Phormio*: “Which, when circumstances are adverse, you are less [Col. 1158D] able to remedy.”

**They reform.** Simply for “they form,” just as he quite frequently uses *retractare*, “to handle again,” for *tractare*, “to handle,” and similarly uses other compounds in place of simple verbs.

**They delivered.** According to Tertullian’s judgment, therefore, that Angel descended only once each year; accordingly, he takes the words κατὰ καιρὸν, “at the appointed time,” in John 5:3 to mean the annual recurrence of the season.

**Death having been destroyed**, namely, the spiritual death of the soul.

**That is, the liability.** *Reatus*—a word unknown to Cicero and first employed by Messala, as Quintilian, bk. VIII, ch. 3, testifies—denotes an obligation for which someone is *reus*, that is, answerable or liable. Theologians ordinarily distinguish a twofold liability: namely, the liability of guilt, requiring satisfaction, and the liability of punishment, requiring the endurance of punishment. Hence the common saying: “He who has nothing in his purse”—with which, of course, to make satisfaction—“must pay with his hide,” with which he may endure the punishment.

[Col. 1159A] **Punishment.** Our author may be understood here in two ways: either as referring to the liability to punishment, upon whose removal the punishment itself no longer has any place; or, as I judge more likely, to the liability of guilt. In that case, the statement must by no means be understood universally, since it is certain among all theologians that, even after mortal guilt has been destroyed through repentance, a liability to punishment nevertheless remains—though, whereas it had formerly been eternal, it must now be discharged only temporally in purgatory. It is proper to Baptism alone that, when through it the liability for all antecedent guilt has been removed, every liability to punishment is simultaneously taken away.

**To the image of God.** He undoubtedly alludes to what God said at Gen. 1:26: “Let us make man to our image and likeness.” And: “God created man to his own image; to the image of God he created him.” There all the Greek interpreters render “image” by εἰκόνα (*eikona*) and “likeness” by ὁμοίωσιν (*homoiōsin*). Long ago, St. Augustine, *Eighty-Three Questions*, q. 51, noted that, if we wish to speak strictly, a distinction exists between [Col. 1159B] these two:

> There are some who understand that the two expressions, “to the image” and “likeness,” were not used without reason, since, if they signified one thing, one term could have sufficed.

Above all, as St. Thomas explains at I, q. 93, art. 9, in the *corpus*, likeness is considered a preliminary to image insofar as it is more general than image. For, as he says at the cited question, art. 1, in the *corpus*:

> We must say, as St. Augustine says in *Eighty-Three Questions*, q. 74: “Where there is an image, there is immediately a likeness; but where there is a likeness, there is not immediately an image.”

“From this,” St. Thomas continues, “it is clear that likeness belongs to the nature of an image, while an image adds something to the nature of likeness, namely, that it is copied from another. For something is called an image because it is made in imitation of another. Hence an egg, however similar and equal it may be to another egg, is nevertheless not called its image, because it was not copied from that egg.” Yet he also discloses another meaning of likeness in the cited q. 93, art. 9, speaking thus [Col. 1159C]:

> It is also considered as subsequent to an image, insofar as it signifies a certain perfection of the image; for we say that someone’s image is like, or is not like, the person whose image it is.

Thus at Ps. 39:7 “image” is used of a certain fleeting—that is, imperfect—image: “Nevertheless man passes on in an image.” Among the Greeks, however, “likeness” is ordinarily used for an image that expresses its prototype precisely and, as it were, down to the last detail. Hence at Gen. 5:5 those words are employed to express the perfect likeness existing between Father and Son: “Adam begot [a son] to his image and likeness, and called his name Seth.”

Man, therefore, was made to the image of God not as regards his body—for he shares this with other animals, which nevertheless were not created to the image of God—but as regards the soul, which by its nature is spiritual, simple, immortal, and endowed with intellect and will, including freedom of the will, etc. Thus the soul displays within itself, [Col. 1159D] as in a kind of mirror, the attributes of God shining forth. Hence St. Augustine beautifully observes in *On Genesis against the Manichaeans*, bk. II, ch. 7, that when God fashioned man from the clay of the earth, the words “to his image and likeness” were not added, because the formation of the body was then being described. “But the inner man”—that is, the soul, which is within the body—“was signified when it was said: ‘God made man to the image and likeness of God.’”

And this likeness is, as it were, the perfection and completion of that image, since it expresses not only the perfections of God but also, as it were, their finest and final features; and it even constitutes a certain symbol of the Most Holy Trinity. This is elegantly explained by the author of the book *On the Dignity of Man*, ch. 2, which was formerly ascribed to St. Ambrose. He says: “Just as the Son is begotten from the Father and the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, so the will is generated from the intellect, and from these two in turn proceeds memory,” etc. But what is more splendid [Col. 1160A] than the passage in St. Augustine, *Confessions*, bk. XIII, ch. 2? He says:

> Who understands the omnipotent Trinity? And who does not speak of it, if indeed he understands it? Rare is the soul that, when speaking of it, knows what it is saying. . . . I would wish that people considered these three things within themselves: to be, to know, and to will. For I am, and I know, and I will; I am knowing and willing; and I know that I am and that I will to be; and I will to be and to know.

A golden confession!

There is also another image of God in the soul, one far more excellent because it is entirely supernatural, through which we approach God much more perfectly than through the gifts naturally implanted in us: namely, sanctifying grace. As our scholastics demonstrate, this is a formal participation in the divine essence and nature, through which we are called, and are, children of God. Furthermore, through faith our intellect knows him, and through charity embraces him toward whom it tends through hope. It is of this image that the Apostle must be understood when he says at Col. 3:9 ff.: “Stripping off the old man with his deeds and putting on the new, the one who is renewed unto knowledge, [Col. 1160B] according to the image of him who created him.” St. Ambrose, moreover, discusses both images beautifully and fully in *Hexaemeron*, bk. VI, ch. 8.

Finally, man was made in a singular manner to an image—namely, that of Christ. As St. Thomas notes at I, q. 93, art. 1, reply to objection 2:

> The Firstborn of every creature is the perfect image of God, perfectly representing him whose image he is: Man, not similar to God, but himself God. He is therefore called the image, and never “to the image.”

For the Apostle says at Rom. 8:29: “Those whom he foreknew and predestined to be made conformable to the image of his Son.” Likewise, at 1 Cor. 15:49, he speaks as follows: “As we have borne the image of the earthly”—Adam—“let us also bear the image of the Heavenly,” namely, Christ, whom immediately before, in v. 45, he had called “the last Adam, a life-giving Spirit.” Thus through Baptism man is restored to the image of God—not to that natural image, which he could not lose through the sin committed in Adam, but to the supernatural image of Grace, which he lost through Adam’s sin.

[Col. 1160C] **Is reckoned in eternity.** We human beings are the image of God in a representation so long as we see him through a mirror in an enigma; we are made his likeness when we see face to face, 1 Cor. 13:12. For “we know,” says John at 1 Ep. 3:2, “that, when he appears, we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is”—namely then, when “we all, beholding the glory of the Lord with unveiled face, are transformed into the same image, from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord,” 2 Cor. 3:18. And thus, finally, this statement of Tertullian must be understood: “The image in the representation, the likeness in eternity.”

**From his breath.** He undoubtedly alludes to Gen. 2:7: “God therefore formed man from the clay of the earth and breathed into his face”—literally, “into his nostrils,” as the principal organ of respiration—“the breath of life, and man became a living soul.” By “living soul” the life of the body is literally understood; but certain Holy Fathers explain it in the mystical sense as the life of the soul, which is sanctifying grace. Thus St. Ambrose, [Col. 1160D] *On Paradise*, ch. 5:

> Life, moreover, was, as the Apostle said (Col. 3:3), hidden with Christ in God. Man, therefore, was either in the shadow of life, on account of the figure of what was to come, etc., or he possessed a certain pledge of life, because he had the inbreathing of God, etc.

### Notes on Chapter VI

**Under an angel.** He appears to indicate the minister of baptism, alluding to that Angel at the Pool of Bethsaida in the preceding chapter.

**We are prepared.** But had he not said a little earlier that through baptism we receive the Holy Spirit whom we had lost through sin? Here, however, it must be carefully noted that Holy Scripture frequently distinguishes between the Holy Spirit and sanctifying Grace, which, having been lost through sin, we receive in baptism. It is certain, first of all, that the Apostles had already been baptized before the day of Pentecost, or had at least otherwise obtained the grace of the Holy Spirit; [Col. 1161A] and nevertheless it is only on the day of Pentecost that Acts 2:4 says of them: “They were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in various tongues, as the Holy Spirit gave them utterance.”

Next, at Acts 8:14 ff.: “When the Apostles who were at Jerusalem heard that Samaria had received the word of God, they sent Peter and John to them. When these had come, they prayed for them that they might receive the Holy Spirit; for he had not yet come upon any of them, but they had only been baptized in the name of the Lord.” They had therefore been baptized and cleansed of their sins through baptism; accordingly, they possessed the grace of the life-giving Holy Spirit. Yet they are said not yet to have had the Holy Spirit. “Then they laid their hands upon them, and they received the Holy Spirit.” The Apostles, therefore, received the Holy Spirit on the day of Pentecost not merely in some manner, but received his fullness, which not only flooded the soul but overflowed into the body itself through certain visible signs: “They were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in various tongues, as the Holy Spirit gave them utterance.” Likewise those Samaritans, [Col. 1161B] already baptized, received the HOLY SPIRIT through the laying on of hands at Acts 8:17—that is, his fullness.

And this is that Holy Spirit whom the impious Simon wished to purchase for himself with money, ibid., v. 18: “When Simon saw that through the laying on of the Apostles’ hands the HOLY SPIRIT was given, he offered them money,” etc. Another passage, Acts 19:5 ff., must also be explained in this way: “When they heard these things, certain Ephesians were baptized in the name of the Lord JESUS. And when Paul had laid his hands upon them”—after Baptism, of course—“the HOLY SPIRIT came upon them, and they spoke in tongues and prophesied.”

Furthermore, that laying on of hands performed upon the newly baptized was the very Sacrament of Confirmation. For just as through Baptism we are initiated into the Faith by the Holy Spirit through what may be called the first fruits of his grace, so through Confirmation we obtain the very Strength of Faith and the fullness of that Spirit, having now, through a new grace, become more mature [Col. 1161C] in the faith. Thus the Spirit who had been given invisibly through Baptism was also given visibly by the Apostles through Confirmation, together, namely, with the attached graces that we call “gratuitously given”: healing the sick, prophesying, interpreting, speaking in various tongues, etc., and other gifts of this kind, which at that time adhered to sacramental grace.

St. Justin, who suffered martyrdom in A.D. 166, and St. Irenaeus, who suffered martyrdom twelve years [Note: [Col. 1161D] **Twelve years.** This is an error by the commentator and easily corrected. He confuses the martyrdom of St. Irenaeus, by which he was crowned in A.D. 202, with the martyrdom of his predecessor St. Pothinus, which in fact is assigned to the year 178. EDD.] later, testify that these signs long continued in the Church: the former in *Apology* I and the *Dialogue with Trypho*, and the latter in *Against Heresies*, bk. V, ch. 6, and elsewhere. But it is sufficiently established that even toward the end of the second century such gratuitously given graces still flourished in some Churches, and that many people who were excessively credulous or otherwise incautious—because they failed properly to distinguish true prophecies from false—were deceived by Montanus and his wretched women, who rashly and deceitfully boasted of the gift of prophecy and the inspiration of the Holy Spirit. Our own Tertullian in particular fell most disgracefully into the heresy of Montanus, [Col. 1161D] as we demonstrated in Critical Question 4 prefixed to the book *On Prescription*. See also what we said in the *Theological Miscellany*, *On the Church of Christ*, Disputation VI, no. XIII ff.

Thus, through Baptism, once the Holy Spirit has already been received—though invisibly—we are cleansed from sins through the infusion of grace; at the same time, we are prepared to receive that same Spirit more fully in the sacrament of Confirmation—not now, indeed, under visible signs of this kind, but with an invisible fullness and with strength of faith against internal and external temptations.

**The arbiter of Baptism.** I understand this as the minister, in whose arbitration and power, as it were, the administration of Baptism has been placed. In this sense our author says in *On Monogamy*, ch. 6: [Col. 1162A] “You have Moses, the arbiter of God at close hand.” Here, moreover, he again alludes quite clearly to the frequently mentioned history of the Sheep Pool in John 5. For just as the Angel there descended from heaven, stirring the waters, etc., and just as St. John the Baptist was sent as an Angel to preach and administer the baptism of repentance in the river Jordan, so now the priest, as another Angel officially appointed or sent for this purpose, prepares through the ministry of Baptism the way for the Holy Spirit to be infused more fully through Confirmation, as we have said.

**Faith obtains.** Not the faith of the baptizer or of the person baptized—for the effect of Baptism does not depend upon the work of the one performing it or of the subject receiving it; for who could require faith, or an act of faith, from an infant?—but the faith intrinsic to the Sacrament itself, as an *opus operatum* (“work wrought”), insofar as Baptism was instituted by Christ to be administered in the faith and name of the entire HOLY TRINITY. This faith is the intrinsic form of this Sacrament.

[Col. 1162B] **With the Holy Spirit.** Thus, for the substance and validity of this form, a distinct invocation of the three Persons of the Divinity is required, by which that Faith is sealed in Baptism, as our author likewise says in *On Idolatry*, ch. 12. Hence, from its sacramental form, Baptism is called by the ancient Fathers “the seal,” “the sign,” “the sealing of faith,” “the Sacrament of the Trinity,” “of Faith,” etc., as may be seen in Tournély, *Treatise on Baptism*, q. 1, art. 1, p. 146. Moreover, from this passage it is plainly gathered that already in Tertullian’s time the three Divine Persons necessarily had to be expressed in the form of Baptism, and that Baptism was by no means then conferred in the name of Christ alone. Thus he says in *Against Praxeas*, ch. 26: “Not once, but THREE TIMES, at each several name, we are dipped into the several PERSONS.”

**Through the blessing.** By the term “blessing” he understands the sacramental form of Baptism.
