### 1579

**PAMELIUS EDITION—FIRST ANTWERP EDITION, folio.** “All the works of Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian, presbyter of Carthage, that have been found up to the present. Now at last revised as accurately as possible after comparison with manuscript copies, and enlarged by several books; now first divided into chapters and arranged in a fixed order, with attention, so far as possible, to chronology, and distributed into five volumes, with the summaries and annotations of Jacques Pamelius of Bruges, theologian and archdeacon of Saint-Omer, interspersed throughout the whole work. By them the more obscure passages are explained and everything pertaining to ecclesiastical antiquity is illustrated. Newly added by the same [Col. 0040D] Pamelius are a life of Tertullian, a very full index of the Scriptures cited, and other prolegomena.”

The author of this new recension, Jacques Pamelius (van Pamele), was assisted chiefly by three parchment manuscripts from the Vatican, compared with the printed text through the care and by the order of Cardinal Sirleto, then prefect of the Vatican Library. He also had manuscripts from the monasteries of Saint-Amand and Saint-Bavo and an English manuscript owned by John Clement, an Englishman; also the conjectures of the Italian Latino Latini and the Englishman John Harris, communicated to him by the men themselves, and finally various observations of numerous [Col. 0041A] learned men. With these aids he certainly accomplished so much that he believed himself to have corrected innumerable places, filled lacunae, and rightly excised certain superfluous matter that had crept from the margin into the text. He was also the first to divide all the books into chapters and to add new summaries suited to those divisions and composed in Tertullian’s own words.

He then divided the whole work into five volumes: the first contains books against the Gentiles and Jews; the second, hortatory writings; the third, writings against heresies; the fourth, poems together with fragments of lost works; the fifth, works written against the Church and certain spurious works. Within each part the books are ordered according to the time at which they are thought to have been written. He also published an [Col. 0041B] *Admonition on Tertullian’s Dogmas or Errors*, far richer than Rhenanus’s treatment, arranged alphabetically and supplied with what he calls an “Antidote” [Note: [Col. 0041D] The first edition of Pamelius is not at hand, and we could not discover the printers and booksellers through whom it appeared; these particulars are therefore taken from the Paris edition of 1598, whose further additions are recorded in their proper place. SCHOENEMANN.].

Its contents are arranged thus: (1) a dedication to Pope Gregory XIII, dated at Douai on the Feast of the Exaltation of the Cross, 1579; (2) a second dedication to Philip II, king of Spain, written there on the same 14 September; (3) indexes of the books contained in each volume, with an account of the authors and manuscripts used in correcting Tertullian; (4) a life of Tertullian by Pamelius; (5) Tertullian’s paradoxes with Pamelius’s antidote; (6) rescripts of laws by Tertullian the Roman jurist; and finally (7) indexes to Tertullian—of passages, of other authors cited by him, and of subjects.

Pamelius was the first to insert into Tertullian’s works the [Col. 0041C] poetic books and fragments—or rather notices—of lost books that form the fourth volume and are encountered in other authors or in Tertullian himself. As to the poems, in vindicating for Tertullian the books *Against Marcion* and the poem *On the Judgment of the Lord*, he followed the authority of George Fabricius in his *Christian Poets*; for the poem *On Sodom*, that of Sixtus of Siena in *Sacred Library*, book IV. *Genesis* and *To a Senator*, which he had formerly attributed to Cyprian together with the poem *On Sodom*, he now at last assigned to Tertullian because of similarity of style and edited them anew.

These are followed by fragments of Greek works: *To a Philosopher Friend, on the Difficulties of Marriage, or On Virginity*; *On Fate*; *On Clean and Unclean Animals*; *On Circumcision*; [Col. 0041D] *On the Vestments of Aaron*; *On the Trinity*; *On the Origin of the Soul Against Hermogenes*; *Against the Apelleans*; *On Paradise*; *On the Hope of the Faithful*; six books *On Ecstasy*; *Against Apollonius*; several other and falsely attributed works. Volume V contains: *On the Exhortation to Chastity*; *On Monogamy*; *On Flight in Persecution, to Fabius*; *On Fasts Against the Psychics*; *On Modesty*; Novatian’s *On the Trinity*; the same author’s *On Jewish Foods*; and notices of his letters *On True Circumcision* and *On the True Sabbath*.

Laurent de la Barré was the first to gather what various learned men—especially Beroaldo, Alciati, [Col. 0042B] Crinito, Turnèbe, Vertranius, Hotman, Cujas, and Scaliger—had contributed here and there in their writings to correcting or explaining Tertullian, and to write those excerpts, together with his own annotations, alongside Rhenanus’s commentaries. Where Rhenanus’s notes failed—that is, in the books added after Rhenanus—he supplied material of his own. He was nevertheless troublesome to readers because he nowhere distinguished his own material from that of Rhenanus or others. He had Rhenanus’s dedicatory letter and *Admonition on Certain Dogmas of Tertullian* reprinted, added a life of Tertullian of his own, and also prefixed an extensive catalogue of the authors on whose authority the annotations were composed. He dedicated this edition to his teacher, De La Bigne, doctor of theology. As regards outward appearance, the edition is very handsome and is quite conveniently furnished with annotations [Col. 0042C] placed beneath the text and printed in different typefaces.

In correcting this book of Tertullian, Heraldus used two manuscripts, one belonging to the elder Dupuy but communicated to him by Dupuy’s sons, the other belonging to Jacques Bongars. Although Pamelius had used the first long before, he seemed to Heraldus, deprived as he was of the support of other books, to have left not a gleaning but a whole harvest. Besides those manuscripts he also used a book annotated by Cujas, which he obtained through Bongars’s care, and from it drew what seemed useful, naming the author.

“In our commentary,” he says, “we sought to observe this moderation: [Col. 0047A] neither to illuminate matters sufficiently clear to moderately learned men—that is, to light a torch for the sun—nor wholly to omit without explanation matters that might delay men otherwise learned but occupied in other disciplines. This book has such a mixture that its reading belongs not only to students of humane learning but especially to theologians and even jurists; its subject and disputes are such that the book ought to be in the hands of everyone wishing to guard against the leaven of the age. These considerations caused me sometimes merely to touch points that, without this reason, I would have wholly omitted—to touch them only, and only so far as sufficed for clarity.

“But since we see the custom has become established that, at the occasion of some word, rite, or [Col. 0047B] ancient practice, men very often range rather far afield and treat many things that contribute absolutely nothing to explaining the author before them, we wished both to satisfy custom and to escape the disadvantages that follow from it. We therefore decided to cast into other books matters lying outside [non-Latin text omitted]; for that reason we gave them the name *Digressions*. In the first we included matters belonging to humane literature; in the second, matters illustrating ecclesiastical rites.” His dedicatory letter to Nicolas Brûlart, chancellor of France, deserves reading because of the events of those times. It is dated at Paris, 1 December 1613. In the copy of this book owned by the Royal Academic Library, variant readings from a Balliol College, Oxford, manuscript have been entered [Col. 0047C].

### 1624–1630

[Col. 0048C] **THE INCOMPLETE LA CERDA EDITION.** *The Works of Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian, Presbyter of Carthage, Illustrated with Summaries, Explanations, and Notes, by Juan Luis de la Cerda, S.J., with a Very Full Index*. Paris, at the expense of Michel Sonnius, rue Saint-Jacques, under the Basel Shield; vol. I, 1624; vol. II, 1630.

The first volume includes sixteen books, in this order: *On the Pallium*, book I; *Apology*, book II; *On the Testimony of the Soul*, book III; *To Scapula*, book IV; *On Repentance*, book V; *On Prayer*, book VI; *To the Martyrs*, book VII; *On Patience*, book VIII; *On Spectacles*, book IX; *On Idolatry*, book X; *On Female Dress*, book XI; *On the Adornment of Women*, book XII; *To His Wife*, books XIII and XIV; *On the Soldier’s Crown*, book XV; *On the Veiling of Virgins*, book XVI. Nothing preliminary concerning the editor’s work or Tertullian occurs; there is only [Col. 0048D] a dedicatory letter to the most illustrious hero Don Antonio de la Cerda, lord of Atanzón, Pioz, and Olmillos, master of Pioz and its castle, his patron and most loving brother.

The other volume is prefixed by a dedication to the excellent lord Gaspar de Guzmán, Count of Olivares, etc. These letters are so wholly occupied with praising patrons that Tertullian’s name is scarcely mentioned once. The second volume contains the following five books: *On Prescriptions Against Heretics*, book I; *On Baptism Against Quintilla*, book II; *On the Soul*, book III; *On the Flesh of Christ Against Four Heresies*, book IV; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, book V. The remaining books of Tertullian are lacking, and it is regrettable that this edition [Col. 0049A]—generally disdained with unjust hauteur—remained incomplete.

In both published volumes, the text is divided into the chapters received from Pamelius’s edition and also into a great many sections. Each book is prefaced by a brief, excessively meager summary; each chapter has a fuller title in the manner of a synopsis. Each section is followed by an explanation in which the text’s [non-Latin text omitted] is unfolded; after the chapter is completed there follow very extensive notes, growing more abundant as the work progresses. Many concern the illustration of words and style, fewer the investigation of subjects and dogmas; in both respects they are somewhat prolix, yet by no means wholly superfluous.

At the end of volume I are added exercises on *On the Pallium*, in which Salmasius’s book is brought before the strict points of a judge’s scrutiny, approved in some things, condemned in many, and [Col. 0049B] weighed most learnedly in all. It is worth praising the Reverend Author’s concluding words: “It has often happened to me, when considering Salmasius’s readings beforehand, to rejoice that we possess Holy Scripture so whole and uncorrupted that no room for doubt remains. Were it otherwise and were it handed over to Salmasius or another, how many things would they change, delete, and transpose, when in Tertullian alone the readings vary so much in the letter?”

These two volumes of La Cerda rarely occur even in the best-stocked libraries, and more rarely still is the first joined to the second. Hence, at the beginning, compilers of library catalogues admitted many errors, as did even authors most expert in patristic bibliography. Thus both volumes are assigned to 1624 in the catalogues of the Barberini Library, vols. I and II. Thus Oudin, *Commentary on Ecclesiastical Writers*, vol. I, p. 218, falsely asserts that [Col. 0049C] all Tertullian’s works, and Fabricius, *Selections of Sacred Writers on the Truth of the Christian Religion*, p. 213, that most of them, appeared in two volumes in 1624 and were repeated in as many volumes in 1630. Ittig had already warned of Oudin’s error in his *Ecclesiastical History of the Second Century*, p. 69, and Schoenemann of Fabricius’s in *Library of the Latin Fathers*, vol. I, p. 36. So the most learned Schoenemann, a strict corrector of errors of this kind, himself erred in describing volume I, which he had not seen, when he asserted, with insufficient basis, that it contained only thirteen books.

### 1625

**FIRST EDITION OF THE BOOKS TO THE NATIONS.** Geneva, at Pierre and Jacques Chouet, quarto: *Two Books of Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian to the Nations, from the Copy of Agobard, Bishop of Lyons, with Notes, through the Labor and Study of Jacques Godefroy*. This edition itself is [Col. 0049D] not at hand. Concerning the manuscript called Agobard’s—certainly very ancient and undoubtedly the sole witness to these books—the editor later gave the Royal Library in Paris a witness to his fidelity and diligence. Rigaltius writes in the next edition of Tertullian that many pages had been so foully gnawed and corrupted by the injury of age, or rather the neglect of barbarians, that gaps frequently occur even in Godefroy’s edition; he entirely despairs of filling them without the aid of a more complete manuscript.

“Besides the gaps,” he continues, “there are other places where the copyist’s own hand not rarely erred. Some of these at least seem capable of correction by our conjectures. Yet we admit that conjectures unsupported by traces of the old writing are poorly trusted; we did not permit this to ourselves [Col. 0050A] except in very few places, and never without preserving the old manuscript reading.” This edition is sometimes said to have been produced in 1624; we therefore suspect that the dates of both years were printed on different copies. I dare not say whether, and in what way, the edition said to have been printed in the same year (1625) at “Aureliopolis” differs from the Geneva edition. **1628, 1629** [Note: [Col. 0050D] In the Catalogue of the Royal Library, p. 364, the title is given thus: “Nine books or various treatises of Tertullian, corrected in very many places and published from the Royal Library, revised and supplied with observations by Nicolas Rigaltius, 1628.” The year is elsewhere transcribed in varying ways, so it must have been found thus in different copies. In the 1641 Rigaltian edition of Tertullian’s works it is expressly said that Rigaltius’s dedication to the Duke of Richelieu was repeated from the 1629 edition of the nine books. SCHOENEMANN.].

Paris, at the press of Robert Estienne, with the widow of Olivier de Varennes, octavo: *Nine Books of Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian, Corrected from the Agobard Manuscript in the Royal Library and Illustrated with Observations*. Nicolas Rigaltius, whose [Col. 0050B] name is suppressed here, wished through this edition of the minor works to make a prelude to his later outstanding services to Tertullian. The nine works are: *On Prayer*; two books *To His Wife*; two books *On the Adornment of Women* [Note: [Col. 0050D] On the authority of the cited manuscript, he combined the works *On Female Dress* and *On the Adornment of Women* into one treatise of two books. SCHOENEMANN]; *On the Exhortation to Chastity*; *On the Crown*; *On the Prescription of Heretics*; and *Scorpiace*.

The Agobard manuscript had contained twenty-four books: besides those nine, books I and II *To the Nations*, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, *On Spectacles*, *On Idolatry*, *On the Origin of the Soul*, and *On the Flesh of Christ*; and the following writings of Tertullian now lost: *On the Hope of the Faithful*, *On Paradise*, *On the Submission of the Soul*, and *On the Superstition of the Age*. Rigaltius dedicated these writings of Tertullian to Cardinal Armand, Duke of Richelieu.
