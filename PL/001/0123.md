He says that this is the “seal of prayer,” just as above in this book he called prayer “consigned,” that is, completed and finished [Col. 1176B].

He does not inquire into the cause of this corrupt custom, nor do I see any cause commonly advanced except that they might say that the kiss contained an element of cheerfulness foreign to the sadness and mourning of a fast. The profane suggested another cause, namely the bad breath of a fasting mouth; hence Martial, in epigram 4 of book IV, says that Bassa smells of:

> The fasts of Sabbath-keepers,  
> And the breath of sorrowful defendants.

But it hardly seems credible that the softness of so delicate a stomach existed in those stout hearts of the nearly apostolic age, when even mere women did not shrink from embracing and kissing the filth of martyrs in prison and wounds flowing with gore in the contest. What then? The same poet indicates that even among pagans the kisses of legacy-hunters were so unavoidable and importunate, in book IX, epigram [Col. 1176C] 99:

> There is no escaping kissers, Bassus:  
> Neither a painful ulcer nor shining pustules,  
> Neither a repulsive chin nor filthy scabs,  
> Nor lips smeared with greasy salve,  
> Nor the drip of a frozen nose will help.

Leaving aside, therefore, the cause of that superstition so peevish that those fasting believed it unlawful to perform the duty of kissing, Tertullian censures the custom, chiefly on the ground that peace should especially be exchanged with the brethren when prayer rises more commendably. The subject itself indicates that this is certainly how the text must be read, not, as Pamelius edited it, “when it rises more commendably with prayer.” For the question was not whether peace should be exchanged with the brethren after prayer had been offered, but whether it should be exchanged by one who had prayed in church with the brethren on days when he had voluntarily undertaken a private fast. So great was the Fathers’ concern for charity that they preferred to tolerate many rites adopted on an unsound rationale rather than provide, through a severe censure, even the smallest occasion for schism. Such was the Christian *συγκατάβασις*—accommodation—of that age. Accordingly, Tertullian supposes that the rite which he taught above should be abolished can be defended by some rationale; at the same time he prescribes a method of observing the rite while preserving the Lord’s command [Col. 1178C], namely, that those fasting may, after prayer with their household and close associates, abstain from the kiss of peace. There were, moreover, appointed days [Col. 1179B] on which assemblies were proclaimed at the tombs of the martyrs. At the ninth hour, after the Eucharist and the kisses, the assembly and the fasts were concluded. Such at that time were the *stations*; such was the Christians’ military discipline.

Meanwhile, even on the days of the stations, the private and voluntary fasts of which I have spoken were by no means prohibited, even if those private fasts were complete and continued until evening, whereas the station fasts lasted only until the ninth hour and were, by comparison with the former, like half-fasts. Yet he appears to take insufficient precaution against the danger which he most strongly warned must be avoided: namely, that they not become guilty in respect of the command by which we are ordered to conceal our fasts. Certainly, the safer interpretation is that of one who rigorously establishes Tertullian’s proposition: that a devout act of obedience to God, such as fasting, is not dissolved by the Eucharist. For, as the most holy Nicene Fathers profess, we do not receive much bread or wine, but a little, so that we may understand that we receive it for sanctification, not for satiation—“οὐκ εἰς πλησμονὴν, ἀλλ᾽ εἰς ἁγιασμόν,” “not for fullness, but for sanctification.” Furthermore, those final words about Christian stations being performed after the military model are cited with quite inadequate consideration by Isidore, *Origins* VI, and no more suitably or judiciously by Rabanus. For if—as he reasons—the kiss was not employed on Good Friday because the fast was common to everyone and there was no imminent danger of ostentation in disclosing that one was fasting when one fasted with everyone else [Col. 1180A], then Christians always abstained from the kiss whenever the fast was solemn and common. I shall not reproduce his words at length.

But I fear that this most learned man did not grasp Tertullian’s meaning and the ancient custom of the Church. First, Saint Ambrose and the most ancient liturgies attest that the kiss of peace was employed in Christian assemblies on every fast day and during Lent itself, except only on its final days. Second, since it is certain that neither priests nor people customarily approached the celebration of the unbloody sacrifice without the kiss, and since it is also well established that the sacrifice was customarily performed on many fast days, who does not understand that Christians employed the kiss even on days of public fasting [Col. 1180B]? “What sort of sacrifice,” Tertullian had just said, “is it from which one departs without peace?” But since, according to the common rite of the Church, the sacrifice was by no means offered on the final two days of Lent, it is no wonder that Christians then dispensed with the kiss.

From this passage of Tertullian, therefore, Albaspinaeus should, as I have begun to suspect, have reasoned differently: namely, that in Tertullian’s age, at least in the African churches, a common and public religious fast was observed only on the last two days of Lent, while throughout the rest of the year and even during Lent itself Christians were free either to practice fasting or to forgo it. Hence, even if we were to grant—which I certainly do not venture to affirm—that fasting alone was the reason why the kiss of peace was omitted on the Preparation and the vigil of the Lord’s Resurrection, nevertheless, since in Tertullian’s view no public fast was held on any other days of the year, omission of the kiss must have been restricted to those two days of Lent alone [Col. 1180C].

Albaspinaeus could therefore truly have said that Christians abstained from the kiss on those days when a complete and full fast was prescribed. Yet according to Tertullian’s meaning he should have added that there were no more than two such days in the whole year, and that on the remaining days the kiss of peace was employed, although people fasted out of private devotion.

Since, however, almost everyone is persuaded that at least the Lenten fast, as instituted by apostolic tradition, was religiously observed by all Christians even in the earliest centuries of the Church, and that no one abstained from it without fault, I may easily appear to interpret Tertullian with boldness and rashly conjecture that there were once only two public fast days during Lent. But Tertullian himself will stand for me. After he had fallen into the heresy of Montanus, he wrote the following against the *Psychics*—the spiteful name by which he called Catholics—in chapter 2 of his book *On Fasting* [Col. 1180D]:

> Certainly they—the Psychics or Catholics—believe that in the Gospel those days were appointed for fasts on which the Bridegroom was taken away, and that these alone are now the lawful days of Christian fasting, the antiquated legal and prophetic observances having been abolished.

He then continues:

> Accordingly, for the rest, one must fast without distinction, by choice and not by command of the new discipline, according to the circumstances and reasons of each person. The Apostles also observed this practice, imposing no other yoke of appointed fasts to be undertaken in common by everyone.

Here you have the opinion and practice of the Catholics, at least in Africa, at that time. Following the Lord’s command, they fasted only on the two days when the Bridegroom was taken away: namely, when Christ the Bridegroom was placed upon the cross and laid in the tomb, just as the ancient Christians were accustomed to interpret the words of Mark 2 and Luke 5. On the other days Christians did indeed observe fasts, but by choice, not by command.

For although Lent was observed by Christians even in those times [Col. 1181A], and everyone held it certain that it had its origin in apostolic tradition, as could easily be shown by many testimonies of the ancients, nevertheless the observance of this complete fast had not yet been established by law and command—or at least had not yet gained force in all the churches. The fasts of the Four Seasons, other fasts, and several points of discipline, although flowing from apostolic tradition itself, were not immediately received throughout the whole Church of God, nor made binding by command. This was accomplished gradually through councils and through the decrees of the Fathers, and especially of the Roman pontiffs, so that fasts which once depended upon individual choice [Col. 1181B] are now observed with a concordant religious practice and by command.

The same Tertullian again bears witness in chapter 13 of the same book *On Fasting* that, besides Passover and apart from those days on which the Bridegroom was taken away, Catholics fasted not by the law of a command or by compulsion, but by choice. From this we may perhaps infer why Tertullian disapproves the custom of those who, when fasting, shrank from the kiss of peace: by disclosing that they were engaged in fasting, they exposed their modesty to danger, since it was uncertain whether the other brethren were fasting on the same day. Tertullian appears, however, to permit and praise this omission of the kiss only at Passover and on those days when the Bridegroom was taken away—that is, on the Friday of the Preparation and on Holy Saturday.
