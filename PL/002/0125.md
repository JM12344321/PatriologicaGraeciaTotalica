Represents. Not in a mere image and symbol, but in a present effect; for this is properly what “to represent” means, that is, to render the thing present. Thus Jesus Christ himself says that money is “represented” when it is produced and counted out on the spot. Our author himself likewise, in the last chapter of *On the Soldier’s Crown*, contrasts representation with a vision, or mere appearance: “If such are the images in the vision, what are the realities in the representation?”

But how the Baptism of blood supplies the place of, and produces the effect of, Baptism by water, we explained just above in the note “Baptism.” Add that the Baptism of martyrdom participates much more efficaciously in the power of Christ’s Passion [Col. 1188C]. For the Passion, as St. Thomas says (*Summa theologiae*, III, q. 66, a. 12, in the body of the article), operates in Baptism by water through a certain figurative representation, but in the Baptism of blood through imitation of the deed. This is assuredly not a simple representation, but a wonderfully efficacious manifestation of the thing itself in its power and effect. To this pertains Revelation 7:14: “These are they who came out of the great tribulation and washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.”

Restores. Baptism is lost with respect, namely, to its effect, when the baptized person again loses through mortal sin the innocence that he obtained in Baptism. It is recovered through martyrdom, by which the former innocence is fully restored, as we said above in the note “Baptism.”

## Notes on Chapter XVII

Little subject matter. This very brief treatise.

Of receiving. Concerning the minister and the subject, although he discusses the latter in the following chapter.

Bishop. That is, one who possesses the supreme [Col. 1188D] priesthood in his own Church and surpasses all the rest in the dignity of his authority. For since bishops alone are the true and genuine successors of the Apostles, they also succeeded to the right of those to whom it was particularly entrusted by Christ: “Go, teach all nations, baptizing them,” etc. Matthew, last chapter, v. 19. Accordingly, in the earliest centuries of the Church, bishops administered Baptism publicly—that is, in the church itself—by right as something proper to themselves alone, as long as baptisteries had been erected only in cathedral churches. Nevertheless, even then presbyters are recorded as having assisted, and indeed as having baptized themselves whenever so great a multitude of persons to be baptized assembled that they could not have been attended to by the bishop alone. They did so, of course, at the bishop’s express command, while the bishop immediately afterward imparted the sacrament of Confirmation to those baptized. But later, when the number of the faithful had increased still further, especially in the larger dioceses, and access to [Col. 1189A] the cathedral baptistery had become more difficult for some, baptisteries were also erected in each parish church, to provide more securely for the salvation of souls. These were committed to the care and administration of each pastor, while bishops reserved to themselves only solemn Baptism, which was conferred no more than twice a year, leaving parish priests at other times the full right of baptizing.

Accordingly, this discipline always varied at different times, and even at the same time in different places. Thus in the second century St. Ignatius, Bishop of Antioch, says in his *Epistle to the Smyrnaeans*: Οὐκ ἐξόν ἐστι χωρὶς τοῦ Ἐπισκόπου, οὔτε βαπτίζειν, οὔτε προσφέρειν, κτλ., that is: “It is not lawful without the bishop”—meaning without his presence or command—“either to baptize or to make the offering,” etc. Presbyters therefore baptized even then, but only when furnished with the authority of their bishops. In the fourth century St. Ambrose, as Paulinus testifies in his *Life*, adhered so firmly to this custom that the work concerning those to be baptized which he had been accustomed to perform alone could scarcely be performed by five bishops after his death. Likewise in the fifth century, in [Col. 1189B] the *Instruction of the Clergy of Edessa to the Bishops Photius and Eustathius*, found in the Council of Chalcedon, Act X, they ask that Ibas, their bishop, be restored to them as soon as possible, especially since the saving festival of the holy day of Easter was approaching, on which, both because of the catechetical instructions—καὶ διὰ τοὺς ἀξιουμένους τοῦ ἁγίου βαπτίσματος χρεία τῆς αὐτοῦ παρουσίας, “and because of those deemed worthy of holy Baptism”—his presence was needed. Similarly, in the sixth century, in a letter written by the clergy of Italy to the Frankish legates in the year 552, they asked that Dacius, Bishop of Milan, finally be restored after fifteen or sixteen years, because, since almost all the bishops whom he was accustomed to ordain had died, an immense multitude of the people was dying without Baptism. In the same century St. Gregory the Great, in a letter to the Roman patrician, complains gravely against him because Blandus, Bishop of Orta, had now been detained by him for a long time at Ravenna. The chief cause of this complaint was that infants there were dying for their sins without Baptism. So too in the seventh century, at the Second Council of Seville [Col. 1189C], celebrated in 619, canon 7, it was decreed that presbyters were not permitted to enter the baptistery in the bishop’s presence, nor to immerse an infant while the bishop was present, etc. We therefore see how very tenaciously the bishops of certain Churches at those times guarded their right: they judged that it should not be communicated to presbyters even in their absence, perhaps because they considered Baptism without Confirmation, or the imposition of the bishop’s hands, not to be rightly professed.

Meanwhile, however, it gradually came about that public baptisteries were erected in parish churches as well. Bishops now commonly reserved to themselves only the power of conferring solemn Baptism, namely on Holy Saturday and the vigil of Pentecost, while they made a certain general right to confer Baptism outside those times available to presbyters and, as it were, annexed to the sacred ordination that presbyters received from bishops. Nor did even this form of discipline endure for long. When dioceses had expanded and the multitude of catechumens, etc., had increased excessively, solemn [Col. 1189D] Baptism also passed to presbyters. Thereafter it remained with the bishop almost solely in the Church of Milan—perhaps in memory of that singular zeal and example which St. Ambrose, as we recalled above, had so conspicuously displayed there—as our most learned Edmond Martène reports in Book I of *On the Ancient Rites of the Church*, chapter I, article III, no. 3, from Constitution II of the Fourth Council of Milan, celebrated in 1575 under St. Charles Borromeo.

And deacons. Although the duty of baptizing was imposed by Christ upon the Apostles alone, Acts 8:12 nevertheless establishes that Philip, though only a deacon, baptized in Samaria—assuredly because the Apostles themselves had entrusted that duty to him. Moreover, ancient records testify that deacons, as persons immediately subordinate to presbyters, were not permitted to baptize without the presbyters’ command. Thus in the fifth century Pope Gelasius, in Letter V to the bishops of Lucania, chapter 5, says of deacons: “Let them not presume to baptize [Col. 1190A] without a bishop or presbyter, unless perhaps those aforementioned ministers are too far away”—that is, farther away than they could conveniently be approached to obtain permission—“and extreme necessity compels them.” In those earlier centuries there was not so great an abundance of priests that someone was always available to every particular congregation in villages and towns, or even in every city. And what had to be done if a priest was present but ill or otherwise impeded, or in sudden and extraordinary cases? The deacons—at least one of whom, according to Martène, Book I, chapter I, article 3, was required to reside in a church together with a presbyter—had to undertake that duty, readily commissioned by the presbyter.

Honor. Namely, the honor that each Church paid to its bishop as its hierarch, or rather spontaneously acknowledged as having been divinely conferred upon him.

Peace remains intact. But PEACE would not remain intact if the bishops’ authority were not preserved, with presbyters indiscriminately seizing upon every ecclesiastical duty [Col. 1190B] on their own authority; for bishops are those whom the Holy Spirit has appointed to govern the Church of God, etc. Accordingly, in Tertullian’s time this authority, as something quite recently derived from the Apostles, had been preserved for bishops, and in them rather for the Apostles themselves. Thus nothing was permitted in the administration of the sacraments—the treasures of the Church, deposited by Christ through the Apostles themselves and to be guarded by their successors—except at the commission of the lawful, immediate, and, as we say, direct successors of the Apostles, that is, the bishops. This was especially true in Baptism, concerning which the Apostles alone had been told: “Go, teach, baptize.”

Otherwise, that is, “in other circumstances,” or “but if not”; so that the meaning is: “But if the Church’s proper honor and the authority of those called and appointed to divine offices are not preserved, that right must equally be granted to the laity, with the whole order now thrown into confusion,” etc.

Can be given. For laypeople can confer no less perfect a Baptism than bishops and the others who baptized before them conferred.

[Col. 1190C] Hidden from anyone. Latinius, attempting to restore this wretchedly broken passage, reads it thus: “Unless bishops now,” etc., “are called learners or disciples”—that is, unless they have been specially called to this duty as disciples of the Lord, or at least as their true successors—“the Lord’s word ought not to be hidden from anyone”; rather, that command of the Lord (Matthew 28:19) will pertain equally to all: “Go, teach all nations, baptizing them,” etc. This conjecture of Latinius is not inept.

Junius, however, retains the word *dicentes*, “saying,” and changes only the punctuation, beginning as follows: “Unless bishops now,” etc., “are called, saying: ‘The Lord’s word ought not to be hidden from anyone’; consequently Baptism also, equally God’s possession, can be administered by all. But how much more,” etc. He therefore wishes this entire context to be understood thus: “Unless perhaps—which is absurd—it should be decided that all who speak and affirm as follows are indiscriminately to be regarded as bishops, presbyters, and deacons: ‘Those whose business it is to proclaim God’s word and not to hide it also have the right to baptize; [Col. 1190D] but it is everyone’s business to proclaim God’s word; therefore it is also everyone’s business to baptize,’” etc.

This conjecture certainly seems to me happier and more acute than the preceding one of Latinius. The word *dicentes*, “saying,” should therefore refer to laypeople, as though Tertullian introduced them speaking and claiming Baptism for themselves no less than the preaching of the divine word. Perhaps they even considered that Christ’s saying in Matthew 10:27 was also addressed to them: “What you hear in the ear, preach upon the housetops”; as though the Lord’s other saying, “Go, teach all nations, baptizing them,” which was addressed particularly to the Apostles alone, pertained in the form of a command to every other person, and thus the right to preach had also been granted to them by such a commission.

Equally. Namely, the word of God and its preaching.

God’s possession. *Census* does not properly mean annual revenue, but an assessment of each person’s goods entered in public registers, according to which taxes were imposed in a fixed proportion. Later, however [Col. 1191A], the word was also transferred to the taxes themselves that had to be paid. Hence in Matthew 17:23 Christ says: “From whom do the kings of the earth receive τέλη ἢ κῆνσον, tribute or census-tax?” Similarly, in Luke 20:22, when the Pharisees tempt Christ, they ask: “Is it lawful for us to give φόρον, tribute, to Caesar, or not?” In place of Luke’s φόρον, Matthew 22:17 and Mark 12:14 have κῆνσον, “census-tax,” which our Vulgate likewise renders as “tribute.”

In another sense it also signifies the registration or enumeration of each person’s goods, in which each individual declared his name and possessions. Thus Luke 2:1 says: “A decree went out from Caesar Augustus that the whole world should be enrolled”—that is, assessed or entered in the census registers. And shortly afterward, v. 3: “All went to be registered, each to his own city.” And since the proportionate imposition of taxes arose from that census or registration, *census* is also used by our author in *Apology*, chapter 7, for origin, source, or profession: “The *census* of that discipline is from Tiberius.” Likewise in Book I *Against Marcion*, chapter 21: “You will find no [Col. 1191B] Church of apostolic *census* that professes Christianity in the Creator.” See also *On Prescription*, chapter 32. Likewise, in Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 40, speaking of the tribe of Judah, he says: “From whose tribe the carnal *census* of Christ was to proceed,” etc. Again in *Apology*, chapter 10, he says: “From him”—Saturn—“comes the *census* of all divinity, or of the greater or better-known divinity.” It is also established that the word is sometimes taken for the number ordinarily determined by an enumeration; in this sense he says in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 4: “The whole *census* of the elements,” etc.

Finally, it is taken for substance, resources, or goods belonging to someone, as when he says in *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 25: “Thus the *census* of each substance exhibited man and God.” For shortly afterward there follows: “The distinctive character of the conditions, divine and human, was duly assigned according to the equally genuine reality of each nature.” Our author can therefore conveniently be understood in this sense when he calls Baptism “God’s *census*,” as something to be numbered among God’s own possessions.

Consecrated. Proper to the bishop, particularly assigned to him [Col. 1191C], and sanctified by God himself.

The bishop’s office. The force of the argument lies in this: laypeople should claim the right of baptizing all the less, since that right does not belong even to deacons or presbyters themselves without the bishop’s authority.

Rivalry. Some think that this passage should be punctuated thus: “The office of the episcopate is consecrated to bishops. Rivalry is the mother of schisms,” etc. But others rightly object to this tautology, ταυτολογία. The arrangement of this text that I have restored against my own manuscript must therefore be retained.

Is the mother of schisms. There are many and sufficiently lamentable examples of this in the Church, which it is not appropriate to recount here. See also our Tertullian in *On Prescription*, Critical Question IV.

Expedient. As though he wished to say: “Even if laypeople also possess the power to baptize, nevertheless it is not expedient for them to exercise it to the prejudice of the honor due to bishops. It is better that, out of reverence for the bishops’ preeminence, they abstain from exercising it.”

[Col. 1191D] You may use. Junius thinks these words were addressed only to presbyters and deacons. Indeed, St. Basil seems to have judged Baptism administered by laypeople wholly invalid and therefore to be repeated, in his First Canonical Epistle to Amphilochius, canon 1. From this very argument he attempts to prove that the Baptism of heretics is invalid—for after St. Cyprian, he had adhered to this position together with other Easterners—because those who had broken away from the Church, even if they were otherwise bishops or presbyters, had fallen back into the status of laypeople. “Those who have broken away,” he says, “have become laypeople and possess no power either to baptize or to ordain.” Therefore, he says, those baptized by them had been baptized by laypeople, and when they came to the Church they were ordered to be cleansed by the true Baptism of the Church.

But the context itself proves that Tertullian’s preceding words were spoken about laypeople, since immediately afterward he excludes only women from administering Baptism in a case of necessity. And certainly [Col. 1192A] it was always a most ancient custom in the Church that, in a case of necessity, when the bishop, presbyters, deacons, etc., were absent, laypeople also baptized.

Or of place. When in that place no bishop, presbyter, or even deacon is present, or when one cannot be summoned because of some other impediment.

Or of time. Namely, when delay would create an immediate danger that the person to be baptized might die in the meantime.

Condition. Namely, if some other legitimate cause requires that Baptism be imparted at once.

Steadfastness. *Constantia* here seems to be used for courage or firmness of mind, so that the meaning is: “For then it will be lawful to bring assistance courageously and fearlessly,” etc. Thus in Book V *Against Marcion*, chapter 1, he says: “With such *constantia* to proclaim another god.” It can also be taken for liberty or fitness, a meaning that agrees entirely with what is added immediately afterward. See also what we said above at chapter 10 concerning the word *constanter*. Whatever may be said of these interpretations, I would certainly prefer to understand *constantia* here as a stable and canonically established order and rule [Col. 1192B] relating to the person of the minister. That rule, like other ecclesiastical and human laws, has no application in a case of necessity.

To teach. He again alludes to what he recalled above in chapter 1 concerning Quintilla and her poisonous doctrine. He likewise complains bitterly about this insolence of women in *On Prescription*, chapter 41.

Moreover, some infer from this passage, not ineptly, that Tertullian wrote this book *On Baptism* while he was still truly orthodox. For after becoming a Montanist, he treated that talkative sex with much greater indulgence, and indeed with excessively tender reverence, although Paul himself in 1 Corinthians 14:34 condemned it to perpetual silence in the Church and could not adequately restrain it even so. To this also pertain the things we said in Critical Questions IV and V on *On Prescription*.

Will give birth for herself. A not inelegant metaphor, though not without sarcasm, directed against women who everywhere give birth to innovations in sacred matters. Moreover, when he speaks of “right,” [Col. 1192C] he does not thereby exclude every capacity of women to baptize, at least in a case of extreme necessity. Indeed, they are permitted and obliged to baptize if no man is present, or if modesty forbids Baptism to be administered by a man. It is clear from John Moschus—who flourished in the seventh century—*Spiritual Meadow*, chapter 3, that a case of necessity, at least extreme necessity, was not excluded even in those ancient times. There he asserts that it is contrary to the canons for deaconesses to baptize, except, of course, in a case of the strictest necessity. In such a case Tertullian’s own principle must certainly come into operation: “Since he will be guilty of the loss of a human being,” etc.

## Notes on Chapter XVIII

Former. Namely, Quintilla, whom he censured shortly before. He now calls her “former” because he had spoken about her at the very beginning of this book, and because she had already dared some time earlier to attack Baptism.

Was taking away, that is, had attempted to remove it from their midst.

On her own authority. By her own right—something that had not hitherto been tolerated even among male laypeople [Col. 1192D].

Falsely inscribed, that is, ψευδεπίγραφα, “pseudepigraphical,” or falsely entitled in his name: the *Journeys of Paul and Thecla*, whose spurious character no critic now doubts. These acts contain certain things wholly opposed to St. Paul’s teaching and many others that are entirely fabulous: for example, that Thecla was called by Paul to the apostolic duties of preaching the Gospel and baptizing, and that she performed both with more than masculine courage, etc. St. Jerome, however, says of these in *On Ecclesiastical Writers*: “We count the *Journeys of Paul and Thecla*, and the whole fable of the baptized lion”—though it has hitherto remained unknown whose work this was—“among the apocryphal Scriptures.” We know that they were likewise cast into the category of apocryphal writings by Pope Gelasius at the Roman Council held in 494.

But this spurious book must be carefully distinguished from other books commonly attributed to Basil, Bishop of Seleucia, who flourished in the middle of the fifth century. In treating them, Basnage, in his *Political-Ecclesiastical Annals* for A.D. 46, wishes to overturn completely the entire account [Col. 1193A] of St. Thecla’s deeds and miracles, although Baronius had long before established it much too forcefully under A.D. 47. But this is by no means the place to investigate these questions more laboriously.

From his own resources, that is, compiling material invented by his own mind.

Heaping up. I certainly do not think that he did this out of malice, but through a pious fraud and excessive zeal—that is, zeal lacking proper discretion—which also took possession of others around that time, especially the authors of certain Gospels. To secure authority for them, they attached the names of certain Apostles, as though those names would give value to such putrid wares.

Had departed from his position, that is, had been removed from the presbyterate and in some fashion reduced to the order of laypeople. Tertullian was the first to disclose this history, which was afterward affirmed by many others.

Near. He here calls something “near to faith” when it possesses great probability, so that it can prudently be believed with human faith [Col. 1193B].

Steadfastly, or freely and fearlessly. See above the note “Steadfastness,” and at chapter 10 the note “Steadfastly.”

Let them consult. “Let women keep silence in the Church; for they are not permitted to speak, but must be subject, as the Law says. But if they wish to learn anything, let them question their husbands at home. For it is shameful for a woman to speak in the Church.” Thus St. Paul, 1 Corinthians 14:34ff. Therefore, if they have learned anything in the Church, they are directed to their husbands, so that, if they have hesitated anywhere, they may be more fully instructed by them, or may be made more stable in teaching otherwise rightly received, against their natural volubility.

## Notes on Chapter XVIII

To be entrusted rashly, or to be administered indiscriminately to anyone. For it is like a pledge of salvation, which is ordinarily given both as security for a debt and to a creditor who will preserve it safely.

It is a duty, namely, to administer the same to others.

[Col. 1193C] Give to one who asks. “Give to everyone who asks of you,” says Christ, Luke 6:30.

Title. A *titulus* is elsewhere a certain mark, and as it were an inscription upon any action, by which it is rendered honorable and praiseworthy. Therefore, for both the request and the granting to be praiseworthy, each must have a title—that is, a reason and cause both for asking and for giving. The title for giving is the needful person’s request; the title for receiving is the petitioner’s need. Accordingly, Baptism must be conferred only upon those who ask for it, and not upon those who ask in just any manner, but upon those duly prepared to receive it, both through instruction in doctrine and through the fervor of a will that ardently desires it.

For almsgiving. What has just been said makes it sufficiently clear that, like alms, Baptism is to be given to those in need, but only to those who ask for it seriously. With respect to the Baptism of adults, this is necessary for it to be lawfully administered to someone; for validity, however, nothing is required except what is called a habitual intention to receive it [Col. 1193D]. But more on this elsewhere.

Express, that is, open. Thus our author says in *On the Shows*, chapter 3: “Certainly we nowhere find, in the same way as it is openly laid down, ‘You shall not kill,’” etc., “an equally express determination: ‘You shall not go to the circus,’” etc. And in Book II *To His Wife*, chapter 1, he says: “Now he does not advise, but expressly commands.” He likewise says: “It is a thoroughly examined and express judgment.”

Favor. He seems to have used *dignatio* for “grace,” following the example of Livy, Book II, who says of Appius: “Not long afterward he attained the favor of the leading men.”

Had intervened. See the entire sequence of this history carefully woven together in Acts, chapter 8.

To go on ahead. “But an angel of the Lord spoke to Philip, saying: ‘Arise and go southward, to the road that descends from Jerusalem to Gaza.’” Acts 8:26. Here *praetendere* means to direct oneself there first, or to set out beforehand and, as it were, to occupy the road in advance so as to meet him; or it is used simply for *tendere*, “to proceed,” as our author often uses compounds in place of simple verbs [Col. 1194A].

Absorbed. “He had come to worship in Jerusalem and was returning, seated in his chariot and reading the prophet Isaiah.” Ibid., vv. 27ff. He was therefore fixed upon his reading with the greatest attention and, as it were, absorbed into it, so that Scripture seemed to receive the reader most intimately into itself. In almost the same sense our author says in *Apology*, chapter 18: “But so that we might approach both him”—GOD himself—“and his dispositions and wills more fully and deeply,” etc. And in *Exhortation to Chastity*, chapter 3, he says that the will of GOD must be considered deeply and with an abiding impression.

Apostle. From this very passage some have asserted that this Philip was an Apostle; but others, more commonly, place him among the seven deacons who were chosen by the Apostles in Acts 6:5. For if he was an Apostle, why was it necessary for Peter and John to be sent to the inhabitants of Samaria, who had recently been converted by this Philip, so that they might lay hands upon them and they might receive the Holy Spirit? Acts 8:17. For if Philip was an Apostle, could he not equally have given them the sacrament of Confirmation, whose administration then belonged to the Apostles alone—that is, to the bishops—by imparting it through the imposition of hands? He was therefore called an “apostle” as one specially sent by the Apostles properly so called, in the same sense in which Andronicus and Junia are called “notable among the apostles” by Paul in Romans 16:7.

Ordered. “But the Spirit said to Philip: ‘Go near and join yourself to this chariot.’” Acts 8:29.

At the right time. “Now the passage of Scripture that he was reading was this: ‘Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter,’” etc. Ibid., v. 32.

Invited. But who was invited? Was the eunuch taken up by Philip for Baptism after the exhortation, or was Philip taken up by the eunuch into the chariot? If we understand the former, the word *exhortatus* must be taken in a passive sense. This is not altogether unusual, since I also read in Cicero’s *Cato*: “He was urged at a banquet by a prostitute [Col. 1194C] to strike with the axe,” etc. But I prefer the latter interpretation. For after Philip had exhorted the eunuch, the latter asked Philip to come up and sit with him. Acts 8:31.

Is shown. “Then Philip opened his mouth and, beginning from this Scripture, preached Jesus to him.” Ibid., v. 35.

Does not delay. “‘Behold, water: what prevents me from being baptized?’ Philip said: ‘If you believe with your whole heart, you may.’ And he answered and said: ‘I BELIEVE that Jesus Christ is the Son of God.’” Ibid., vv. 36ff.

Is awaited. “And they both went down into the water, Philip and the eunuch, and he baptized him.” Ibid., v. 38.

Is carried away. “But when they had come up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord caught Philip away, and the eunuch saw him no more,” etc. Ibid., v. 39.

Was baptized. Namely, only three days after his conversion, as is clear from Acts 9:9 and 18.

Host. For Ananias had been ordered to seek him in the house of Judas. Ibid., v. 11. Nothing further is known about who this man was [Col. 1194D].

## Notes on Chapter XVIII

Favors, that is, grace. See above.

Prerogatives, that is, singular preliminary dispositions and marvelous circumstances from which one may readily perceive what, and in what manner, God wishes human beings to do something outside the common order and rule.

To be deceived. A person asking for Baptism in a disorderly manner can deceive its minister, namely by approaching without a lawful disposition and not with a true intention, but only a feigned one. He can also deceive himself when he approaches out of immature zeal and believes himself impelled by God, although he has not yet been furnished with the necessary knowledge of the things to be believed so that he may receive it fruitfully.

It is more advantageous. In former times Baptism was postponed by many until adulthood, and sometimes almost until the end of life. Thus St. Martin, who had already become a catechumen at the age of [Col. 1195A] ten, was not baptized until eight years afterward, as Sulpicius Severus testifies in his *Life*, Book I, chapter 2. Some postponed it until the age of thirty, relying upon Christ’s example, as may be seen in St. Gregory Nazianzen’s *Oration on Holy Baptism*. The common opinion concerning the emperor Constantine is that he was baptized at Nicomedia shortly before his death. Likewise the emperor Constantius wished to be baptized by Euzoius at Antioch only at the very end of his life. What, moreover, of the fact that Theodosius the Great, after surviving many dangers in war, asked to be washed by Ascholius, Bishop of Thessalonica, only when he finally fell gravely ill? Perhaps our author himself, by ordering Baptism to be postponed through delay, was responsible for many people’s previously refusing Baptism, and doing so in good faith, out of excessive religious scruple and fear of conscience.

There were also those who delayed receiving Baptism with a worse intention, namely so that they might not afterward be bound by the severity of ecclesiastical laws and could thus sin more freely in the meantime. This policy, as rash, was rightly and most sharply censured by the two St. Gregories, Nazianzen and Nyssen, as well as by St. John Chrysostom and other Fathers [Col. 1195B].

As for the examples of others who formerly postponed Baptism excessively for other reasons, they have by no means been set before us for imitation. It should be enough for us to measure from them the gravity of the undertaking: we should neither rush into it suddenly nor receive those who rush into it in such a way, but should defer it until the desire has grown sufficiently and adequate testing has preceded it. Yet doing the same thing when a person already considers himself sufficiently prepared is not free from danger: drowsiness may meanwhile steal upon him, or the opportunity may afterward be lost. We know for certain that the younger Emperor Valentinian, overtaken by a violent death after delaying Baptism too long, was taken from this life while still a catechumen.

Finally, St. Basil should be heard on this subject in his *Exhortation to Baptism*, where, among many other things, he speaks as follows [Col. 1195C]:

> If the Church were distributing gold, you certainly would not say, “Send it tomorrow; you will give it tomorrow.” Rather, you would diligently and swiftly seek your portion and would bear delay with difficulty. But since the munificent King promises you, not the false coloring of some material thing, but the cleansing of the soul, you weave delays and excuses and offer impediments and reasons, though you ought to run to this gift.

And after other remarks:

> Indeed, if you were subject to public taxes, and a remission of debts were proclaimed everywhere to those liable, and someone then attempted to deprive you of this public benefit, you would grieve and cry out that you were being wrongfully deprived of your share in the common favor. But when not only remission of past things but also gifts for the future are openly offered, you do not listen, but injure yourself more than even enemies perhaps would injure you.

And again, shortly afterward:

> If some physician undertook, by certain devices and contrivances, to make you young again from being old, you would certainly take every care to come to the day on which you saw that you were to be restored to the flower of youth. But now, when it is announced that your soul, which you have rendered defiled by every crime, is to be renewed [Col. 1195D] and regenerated through Baptism, you so despise the benefit that you do not hasten of your own accord or go to meet the one who promises it.

And finally:

> Delay in approaching Baptism seems to cry out nothing other than this: “Let sin reign in me first, until at some point the Lord also reigns; let the members of injustice and the weapons of iniquity be with me, and afterward I shall also take up the weapons of righteousness for God,” etc.

Basil says these things against those who, although already duly instructed, contemplate continually postponing Baptism—and the same also applies to the sacrament of Penance.

Concerning little children. This opinion was certainly peculiar to Tertullian alone, since even while still a Catholic he was always inclined toward excessive scrupulousness and severity. Nor was it adopted by any of the ancient Fathers; still less did the Henricians, Peter Abelard, Peter of Bruys, the Anabaptists, or other heretics find any support in it. For they held that infants were wholly [Col. 1196A] incapable of receiving Baptism validly and that it therefore had to be repeated when they reached the use of reason. Tertullian’s statement, however, was only that of one advising against the practice. For that very reason it indicates unambiguously that a contrary custom existed even in the African Churches and at those same times, with only Tertullian, so far as we know, opposing it. His reasons were approved by none of his contemporaries or by any other of the holy Fathers, not even by St. Cyprian, who was otherwise so scrupulous concerning Baptism and who is nevertheless said to have constantly studied Tertullian as his teacher. For when Fidus, a bishop, raised the question and contended that little children should be baptized only after the eighth day, Cyprian, no longer taking any account of Tertullian’s argument, concluded as follows at the Council of Carthage of sixty-six bishops in the year 253: “No one agreed that infants ought to be baptized only after the eighth day from birth; rather, we all judged that God’s mercy and grace must be denied to no human being who has been born.” Thus he writes in his Letter 69 to Fidus.

[Col. 1196B] Moreover, St. Augustine is an abundant witness that paedobaptism, or the washing of little children, descends from apostolic practice and tradition. Speaking of little children in Sermon X *On the Words of the Apostle*, he says:

> They themselves are carried to the Church; and if they cannot run there on their own feet, they run on the feet of others so that they may be healed. Mother Church lends them the feet of others so that they may come, the heart of others so that they may believe, and the tongue of others so that they may confess. Thus, because those who are sick are burdened by another’s sin—namely Adam’s—when they are made healthy they may likewise be saved through another’s confession—namely that of the sponsor—made on their behalf. Therefore, let no one whisper alien doctrines to you. The Church has always had this practice and has always maintained it; she receives it from the faith of her forebears and preserves it steadfastly to the end.

In Book X *On the Literal Meaning of Genesis*, chapter 23, he confirms the same point, saying:

> The custom of Mother Church in baptizing little children is by no means to be despised, nor in any way to be regarded as superfluous; nor should it be believed at all unless it is an apostolic tradition.

A much earlier witness to this is Origen, who expressly says in his commentary on chapter 13 of the *Epistle to the Romans* [Col. 1196C]: “The Church received from the Apostles the tradition of giving Baptism even to little children.”

For this reason it was most salutarily decreed, and under severe penalties in several councils, that the Baptism of little children should not be delayed any longer, since sudden dangers of death can often overtake them. St. Charles Borromeo in particular decreed at the First Council of Milan that those responsible for the care of a newborn infant should ensure that the child was brought to the church to receive Baptism before the ninth day. If they neglected this, they were to incur the penalty of excommunication.
