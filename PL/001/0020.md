Our discussion therefore turns to the book *On Monogamy*. Since throughout it Tertullian refutes the Psychics, celebrates the Paraclete as his authority (ch. 4, beginning; ch. 14; etc.) together with the new prophecy, and repeatedly proclaims his own adherents to be “spiritual”—so called because they acknowledge the charisms [Col. 0162A]—and, finally, devotes the whole work to attacking second marriages, there is no need of lengthy argument to assign this book to his Montanist writings. It is nevertheless useful to inquire briefly into the date at which this treatise was written. After Tertullian had turned to his own purpose that passage of Paul in which the apostle recommends abstinence from marriage because of the distress of that time, when Christians were continually exposed to danger, he adds, “Now still more the time has been shortened” (ch. 3). By these very words he indicates, with sufficient clarity, that a great storm of calamities had fallen upon the Christians when he was writing *On Monogamy*. Even if someone were to say that Tertullian could have written this while no public calamity had yet overtaken the Christians, the other conclusion is nevertheless far more probable, [Col. 0162B] because it is highly credible that, immediately after embracing Montanism, Tertullian applied himself to defending the doctrine of that discipline. If this is true, the book unquestionably falls within the period when the fire of public calamities had already broken out among the Christians. If, then, we ask what persecution of the Christians is meant here, the answer can be demonstrated without difficulty. For shortly after Tertullian’s separation to the Montanists there arose that upheaval which the Emperor Severus had stirred up by his edict. Since this persecution, which began in A.D. 202, ceased around 204, and since the books *On Fasting* and *On Modesty* were written after our book, there is no difficulty in assigning our book approximately to the year 203. [Col. 0162C]

Nor do I hesitate to place after it the book *On Fasting*. The beginning of that book sufficiently shows that it is later than *On Monogamy* and is, as it were, a second part of the controversy against the Psychics; it also teaches that Tertullian published it not very long after the former work. It too, therefore, was produced during some public persecution, which it seems to indicate here and there, as, for example, in the final part of the book. Nor can it be cleared of suspicion of Montanism, not only because of the period to which it belongs, but also because of its defense of the Paraclete in chapters 10, 11, and 13, and because war is publicly declared upon the Psychics in this book.

After this there remains a single treatise deliberately directed against the Psychics, namely that entitled *On Modesty*. No one has ever doubted that it was written against the opponents of the Montanists, [Col. 0162D] nor can anyone doubt it, because Tertullian himself states this and professes that he has separated himself from the fellowship of the Psychics (chs. 1, 6, 10, 16, etc.). There are also many indications, either stamped upon the book itself (ch. 22) or established from elsewhere, of the kind produced by Orsi (*Dissertation on the Peace and Absolution Not Denied by the Catholic Church to Those Guilty of Capital Sins during the First Three Centuries*, ch. 4, digression 3, p. 120 ff.), which show that this book was written not much later than the calamities inflicted by Severus upon the Christian community, and that its approximate year of birth was therefore 204 or the following year.

## § 5.—The Books *Against Praxeas*, *Against Hermogenes*, and *On the Soul*, Published in the Year 204 or 205

Tertullian had scarcely finished these books against the orthodox Church [Col. 0163A] when he turned to another kind of controversy, against heretics. It is not far from the truth that Tertullian attacked their doctrines in the books immediately following those just discussed, because these books themselves betray approximately the same date, shortly after the great calamity of the Christians under Severus. And if conjecture may be admitted, it would not be unreasonable to say that by defending these doctrines against heretics Tertullian wished to confirm his agreement with the true Church in matters pertaining to faith.

Among these books, no controversy seems earlier than that *Against Praxeas*. It also appears to have been written shortly before the book *Against the Valentinians*. For in this work against Praxeas, although Tertullian frequently mentions the Valentinians, he nowhere refers to the book written against them, [Col. 0163B] as he otherwise customarily does—a practice familiar to everyone who reads him. More particularly suited to confirming what we have said is the fact that Praxeas was a heretic whom the Montanists were bound to regard as especially troublesome to them. For, besides disseminating a contrary error concerning the doctrine of the most holy Trinity, he had also persuaded the Roman Pontiff—whom learned men have with good reason identified as Victor—to revoke the peace that had already been prepared for the Montanists (see ch. 1). It is therefore not difficult to believe that Tertullian began his controversies by skirmishing with Praxeas.

No one will deny that this dispute was undertaken by Tertullian as a Montanist. The very name “Psychics” (ch. 1), the Paraclete himself, by whom Tertullian professes that he was more fully instructed in the truth of the mystery of the supreme and greatest Trinity (ch. 2; [Col. 0163C] ch. 8; etc.), and, finally, his own declaration that recognition and defense of the Paraclete had separated him from the Psychics, place this beyond all doubt. Yet we do not venture to pronounce confidently upon the precise date of this book, although, if the controversy against Praxeas was the first of his contests with heretics, and these contests immediately followed the books directed against the orthodox Church, it may reasonably be assigned to approximately A.D. 204 or 205.

I would not hesitate to say that it was followed by the book *Against Hermogenes*. For that book is earlier than *On the Soul* and *Against the Valentinians*, as will be shown below. Since these latter works were composed approximately after the Severan fury had passed, our little book should apparently be assigned to the same year as the work *Against Praxeas*. [Col. 0163D] That it proceeded from Tertullian as a Montanist is made sufficiently clear by the exceedingly bitter attack upon Hermogenes’ repeated marriages (ch. 1).

To this, as a sort of appendix—as the beginning of the book and many other passages show—he added the book *On the Soul*, a work filled with innumerable fantasies and bearing unmistakable marks of Montanism.

> **Note [Col. 0163D].** For example, in chapters 55 and 58 he invokes the counsels and testimony of the Paraclete; there and in chapter 9 he says that his own party acknowledges spiritual charisms and has been deemed worthy, even after John, to receive prophecy.

It may, however, be debated whether it was written in approximately the same year as *Against Hermogenes*, or only after A.D. 208. For in chapter 2 he says: “That τὸ αὐτεξούσιον (“free will”) exists in us naturally we have already shown even to Marcion.” This controversy appears in the extant second book *Against Marcion*, chapter 6; [Col. 0164A] but Tertullian could also be referring to one of the lost books against Marcion. If the former is assumed, *On the Soul* will have been written after A.D. 208; if the latter, it may be placed even before that year. Of these opinions I prefer the second. For just as it is more customary to append works of the nature of our little book to their parent works immediately rather than after a long interval, so too in the *Scorpiace* he refers in the same manner to the books against Marcion that had already been lost. Moreover, if this opinion is accepted, the order of Tertullian’s books will be much more coherent.

For in the *Scorpiace*, which we have shown to have been written in A.D. 204, he complains that the heretics, Gnostics, and Valentinians attack the sufferings voluntarily endured by Christians for the sake of religion (chs. 1, [Col. 0164B] 4, 3). But in *On the Soul*, chapter 55, he discusses at length the glory of the martyrs; and, as will appear below, he immediately followed this work with *Against the Valentinians*, in which, at the beginning of chapter 30, he says that the Valentinians had attacked the necessity of martyrdom. It therefore seems sufficiently consonant with the truth that *On the Soul* and *Against the Valentinians* were composed not long after the *Scorpiace*, approximately in A.D. 205.

## § 6.—The Books *Against the Valentinians*, *On Prescriptions*, *On the Flesh of Christ*, and *On the Resurrection*, Written after A.D. 203

The order of the books therefore requires that we now discuss this refutation of the Valentinians. It certainly belongs after [Col. 0164C] Tertullian’s defection to the Montanists. Admittedly, the remarks scattered throughout the controversy do not demonstrate this with complete clarity, since there is nothing doctrinal in the little book that could not also have proceeded from an orthodox author. Nevertheless, it is apparent in two ways.

First, near the end of chapter 16 Tertullian says that he had already engaged Hermogenes on the same subject as that disputed with the Valentinians; and we have just assigned that anti-Hermogenian controversy to Tertullian as a Montanist writer. Secondly, at the end of chapter 5, he ardently wishes that it might be granted to him to equal Proculus in every work, whom he calls “the dignity of virginal old age and Christian eloquence.” I do not doubt that this Proculus was the Montanist Proculus mentioned by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VI, chapter 20. This identification rests both upon the praise of his virginity, [Col. 0164D] which Tertullian commends as an outstanding virtue in him, and upon the fact that he calls him “our own,” distinguishing him by this designation from the writers of the orthodox Church whom he had just praised: Justin Martyr, Miltiades (“the sophist of the Churches,” as he calls him, perhaps inflamed by hatred of this man, who, as Eusebius reports, loc. cit., book V, chapter 17, had also opposed the Montanists), and Irenaeus.

But if anyone inquires more closely into the actual date of the book, it seems sufficiently reasonable to say that it was composed not very long after the cruelty against the Christians had in some degree subsided. For there would have been little need to defend the confession of Christian truth before the authorities of this world and at the tribunals of governors—as he urges in chapter 30—unless either that calamitous period itself [Col. 0165A] or a vivid memory of it still existed. Furthermore, as we have just said, this book was written approximately at the same time as the *Scorpiace*, after the fury of Severus had been extinguished, as the history of the period teaches.

We may therefore proceed to the book *On the Prescription of Heretics*, which we do not hesitate to place after it, whatever Tillemont (*loc. cit.*, part III, p. 203, and note 6 on Tertullian, p. 656) and Whiston (*The True Origin of the Sabellian and Athanasian Doctrines of the Trinity*, p. 3 ff.) may have argued to the contrary. For in book I *Against Marcion*, chapter 1, Tertullian promises that he will sometime compose a controversy on prescription against the heretics. From this it follows by necessary inference that our book was not published until after A.D. 208. And that very fact [Col. 0165B] demonstrates most clearly that Tertullian’s mind was not free from Montanist errors at the time when he wrote our book—not to mention that there are distinct traces of these errors in the book itself, as in chapter 40, where, while teaching that even an evil spirit emulates divine institutions, he says: “What of the fact that it appoints its supreme pontiff to a single marriage? It too has virgins; it too has the continent,” and other things of the same kind.

But let us disclose the passages from which those who take the contrary view derive their entire argument on this question. First, they say that the passage we have cited from *Against Marcion* may also signify a book already written. Whiston even adduces another example of this manner of speaking from book V, chapter 19—an example both unnecessary in a matter known to all and in no way appropriate. [Col. 0165C] But that interpretation cannot adequately be defended. Whenever Tertullian cites a book already composed by him, he never employs the future tense, but always the past; and Tertullian would, I think, likewise have used a past-tense form here—as he did elsewhere concerning this book (*On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 2)—if he had intended to say that he had already composed it.

No less strained is Tillemont’s opinion that the passage in the work against Marcion could have remained from those books which Tertullian had written against him before the extant work. For that is a mere conjecture and wholly improbable, because, when Tertullian set about writing our books against Marcion, he states that one preceding controversy against him had been canceled and another lost. Moreover, Tillemont’s argument is inherently inept. [Col. 0165D] As for his statement that *On the Flesh of Christ* is earlier than our books *Against Marcion*, while the latter is later than *On the Prescription of Heretics*, I shall not attempt to discuss it here. The matter has been sufficiently argued above, from which it may be understood that the first book of our anti-Marcionite work, which is in question here, was not published with the others and is in no way later than *On the Flesh of Christ*.

But at the end of *On Prescription* Tertullian says: “For the present, indeed, we have treated the matter generally against all heresies, which must be repelled from comparison of the Scriptures by definite, just, and necessary prescriptions. Hereafter . . . we shall also reply specifically to certain of them.” I hear the objection. What follows, then? Are all Tertullian’s books against heretics later than *On Prescription*? [Col. 0166A] But Tillemont, as it seems to me, has not understood this passage correctly. For Tertullian does not say there that in this book he has refuted the heretics merely in a general way; he adds that he has done so universally by definite, just, and necessary prescriptions. Accordingly, in the other books he is not promising separately to refute the doctrine of each individual heretic, but to show that individual heretics and their tenets must yield in antiquity to the doctrines of those who hold right belief.

But Whiston again, *loc. cit.*, p. 4, says: “The treatise *On Prescription* is earlier than Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*.” Why, I ask? He answers that in *Apologeticum*, chapter 47, Tertullian says: “We readily prescribe against our adulterators that the rule of truth is that which comes from [Col. 0166B] Christ, transmitted through his companions, to whom these various later commentators will be proved subsequent.” What an acute man, who, when he hears of “prescription,” immediately sees a book written on the subject!

That objection was indeed more pertinent to the date of the book. The remaining arguments by which they say they were driven to expel this work from the number of Montanist writings are as follows. First, Whiston adduces the small book appended to ours, which lists the heretics who arose within the Christian community and in which the οἱ κατά φρύγας (“Phrygian”) heretics are very plainly condemned. That argument could not be evaded if this little book could be vindicated for Tertullian, as Whiston undertakes to prove. But let us nevertheless hear what he argues in favor of an opinion now utterly rejected by learned men.

“The style in this piece,” he says, “is the same as in Tertullian’s writings.” [Col. 0166C] How so? The little book frequently mingles Greek words with Latin; it speaks in the manner customary among Africans, in that rough and nearly barbarous style; it pursues the heretics with clever little conclusions; it also takes much from Irenaeus; finally, it recounts heretics mentioned by Tertullian, including even those whom he alone mentions, such as Ebion and Praxeas. But what if the author of this little book was an African by birth, or copied Tertullian? Whiston has no answer to this. Let us proceed, then.

Augustine states most clearly in *On Heresies*, no. 86: “Tertullian passed over to the Phrygians, whom he had previously destroyed.” Where, then? “Nowhere,” says Whiston, “so fully as in this appendix.” I should certainly like to hear how Whiston [Col. 0166D] learned this. Jerome, in *Against the Luciferians*, draws many things from the little book in question as from an ancient writing; Epiphanius does likewise. Let it be so; let them, then, have copied many things from it. Therefore this little book possesses great antiquity. Indeed it does, and may even be contemporary with Tertullian; but whether Tertullian was also its father I do not yet see Whiston’s testimonies to have established.

They next think it inconsistent that Tertullian, after separating from the common Church, should have written so fine and powerful a book against all who had separated themselves from the Church by their errors. Although truth possesses such force that it offers itself to be perceived and defended even by the unwilling, and although Tertullian himself repeatedly insists upon prescription against heretics in books undoubtedly composed during his Montanist period, [Col. 0167A] they nevertheless bid us observe that nowhere did he wield this weapon with such deliberation and fullness. This argument is subtler than is fitting. For the Montanists, including Tertullian himself—as is established (*On Fasting*, ch. 1; *On the Veiling of Virgins*, ch. 2)—never professed themselves estranged from the orthodox in doctrines pertaining to faith. Nor does Tertullian anywhere number the Psychics among the heretics; on the contrary, he distinguishes them very plainly from heretics (for example, at the beginning of *On Monogamy*). They merely said that their discipline had been corrected and brought to perfection; and they maintained that, in matters falling under faith—disagreement concerning which alone constitutes heresy—they did not differ from the doctrine of Christ and the Apostles. There is therefore no reason to marvel that he drew from the more recent age of the doctrines defended by heretics an argument for attacking them. [Col. 0167B]

Tillemont’s second argument is nothing. Even if I grant that Tertullian discusses this rule for judging doctrines deliberately here but only incidentally in his other books, he nevertheless sets it forth not obscurely but with sufficient clarity in his books against heretics. He had designed our book to discuss the matter systematically, while in the remaining works he refutes the absurdities of the heretics in a different manner.

These same considerations may also be opposed to another argument of our adversaries—by no means a slight argument, but one so strong that it could almost appear capable of drawing us over to the opposing side, although Tillemont, surprisingly, did not elucidate it sufficiently. In chapter 36, Tertullian sets forth the complete agreement of his own doctrines with those of the apostolic Churches and their vehement disagreement with those of the heretics. What is far more significant, in chapter 21 he sharply attacks the heretics because they were accustomed to say that the Apostles did not know everything, [Col. 0167C] something they thought Jesus Christ himself had told them in John in these words: “Many things remain for me to say to you, but you cannot yet receive them.” Tertullian then refutes them by the words of Christ that followed the preceding statement: “When that Spirit comes, he will lead you into knowledge of everything that is true.” From this Tertullian thinks it can be inferred that the Apostles had been taught everything, since he says that it is undoubtedly evident from the Acts of the Apostles that they subsequently received that very Spirit from Christ in full.

Yet would someone reflecting upon these words and considering the tenets of the Montanists—who, appealing approximately to that same latter passage of John, defended the Paraclete’s discipline as more perfect even than the discipline of Christ and the Apostles—think that Tertullian here pronounces so clearly against the Montanists themselves that there could be no doubt about the ὀρθοδοξίᾳ (“orthodoxy”) of this book? [Col. 0167D] I certainly do not think so. For although this passage carries great weight, it could nevertheless have been put forward even by one who stood with the Montanist party.

For, as we have said, Tertullian nowhere admits that there is any disagreement between the Montanists and the orthodox followers of the Apostles concerning those things which must be accepted by faith; and any Montanist could grant his contention that the Apostles lacked no true doctrine. What they maintained was that discipline had at last been perfected by the Paraclete, and that Christian doctrines were not new but the old ones more firmly confirmed. Indeed, in many passages Tertullian strives to prove from Holy Scripture, and even from the customs or precepts of the Apostles themselves, those very rites which the orthodox protested had been more recently introduced among Montanus’ followers. [Col. 0168A]

But those heretics against whom Tertullian’s controversy in *On Prescription* is directed continually asserted that the Apostles were either ignorant of many of Christ’s doctrines or that Christ had not transmitted everything to everyone, as our author says in the very passage under consideration. Since, therefore, the passage in dispute could have been employed no less by a Montanist than by an adherent of the orthodox Church, the question is which opinion or interpretation is more inclined toward the truth. Here I have little hesitation in saying that this very book proceeded from Tertullian as a Montanist.

First, there are works of Septimius derived from Montanist discipline in which he insists upon prescription in the case of heretics, yet nowhere—as was his usual practice with all his books to which he could refer readers—does he cite his own work *On the Prescription of Heretics* in advance as his authority; examples are *Against Praxeas*, chapter 2, and *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 1. [Col. 0168B] Secondly, if the meaning of the passage from our book discussed above were such as Tillemont imagines, it is scarcely credible that, after writing this book, Tertullian could have fallen into Montanism, since he had pronounced so clearly against the school of Montanus; or that, in books written by him as a Montanist, he could even have praised this book, in which Montanus’ enterprises had been condemned with such force, as he does near the end of *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 2.

Nor should anyone wonder that Tertullian so closely attached himself to the defense of doctrines accepted in the Christian Churches in a book written at the time of his defection from those Churches. For by that time Tertullian’s fury against the orthodox members who thought otherwise had already been extinguished in some degree; after his spirit had been pacified for a while, he turned against the heretics, whence there arose greater agreement with the orthodox and a milder judgment concerning them.

> **Note [Col. 0168D].** Difficult indeed, if any controversy ever was, and entangled in straits, is this question concerning the date of *On Prescriptions*. In the course of this work there will again be an opportunity to examine it to some extent and to explain our own opinion, such as it is, even if we are not permitted to settle such great disputes. For the present, however, it is useful to say only in passing that the opinion intimated by Tillemont is no longer displeasing to us: namely, that this book was conceived and begun before Tertullian’s defection, while he still flourished in sound faith and perhaps while, residing at Rome, he was examining the apostolic traditions deeply; yet it was not published until after the books against Marcion, when its author was already clinging to the dregs of Montanism. —**Editors**

## ARTICLE VI  
### The Fifth Class of Books, Whose Date and Doctrine Are Both Uncertain

[Col. 0168D]

## § 1.—The Book *On Repentance*

There is no reason to say much about the books *On the Flesh of Christ* and *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, since the latter’s citation of the former sufficiently [Col. 0169A] shows it to be later (ch. 2), while the former was composed only after *On the Prescription of Heretics*, as is likewise evident (see art. 4, § 6). The precise year of these books, however, is not established.

There remain, therefore, those books for which, as it seems to me, no account can be given either of their date or of their doctrine—for this was the third topic of our work. Since others, who believed that they had settled the matter, have thought themselves able to decide with some certainty concerning them, we, who prefer to speak more cautiously in this question, must finally examine briefly whatever they have argued on the subject.

To this class belongs the book *On Repentance*. Erasmus went so far as to deny that it was Tertullian’s, and on this matter he was at one time approved by Dallaeus and also by Hoffmann, chiefly because of the dissimilarity of style between this and Tertullian’s other books. I confess that I cannot adequately detect that dissimilarity, [Col. 0169B] although here the involved and compressed manner of speaking that predominates in the other works is less apparent. For the dissimilarity is not so great as is supposed: the same broken and interrupted expression found here also occurs in other books whose style is somewhat moderated, such as *On Patience* and *On Flight in Persecution*.

Let this book, therefore, be the work of our Septimius; but I do not venture to say to which sect he belonged when he wrote it. Here almost everyone intervenes and says that it is absolutely certain that Tertullian then agreed with the orthodox. For, they argue, he grants to the sacred assembly the power to forgive the sins of those who had committed graver offenses—a power he completely destroyed in the book *On Modesty*—and in that work he expressly professes that he now wishes to refute the book which he had formerly written in favor of that power while devoted to the heresy of the common Church. [Col. 0169C]

In this entire controversy Hoffmann seems to me much wiser than in the others. At *loc. cit.*, § XIII, he aptly observes that the question proposed in *On Repentance* is very different from that posed by Tertullian in the treatise *On Modesty*, and that the Montanists too could have defended everything set forth in *On Repentance*.

The question between the orthodox and the Montanists was not whether those who had relapsed into their former impurity through graver sins could, after performing repentance, obtain pardon for their offenses from God. Neither party—not even the Montanists—denied this. Indeed, it is established that the Montanists required ἐξομολόγησιν (“confession”) from the guilty, though not in such a way that the sinner thereby obtained pardon from the Church and restoration to union with it; rather, the upright were to be moved to supplicate God that he might at some time finally grant pardon to that sinner. [Col. 0169D] The question was instead whether the Church might exercise the power which it itself believed it possessed, of receiving sinners who had been separated from its fellowship back into their former friendship and communion in sacred rites. The Montanists denied that this ought to be done; the orthodox said that it could be done.

Tertullian sharply and wittily attacks the exercise of this liberty by the orthodox in *On Modesty*. In *On Repentance*, however, he neither confirms nor attacks it, but speaks of that pardon before God to which access was not barred even to those immersed in the gravest crimes, provided that they returned to a sound mind. From this it also follows that in *On Modesty* Tertullian does not retract what he had once written, while adhering to the orthodox opinion, in a separate little book. He admits only that he now thinks differently concerning that pardon granted by the sacred assembly from what, while among the orthodox, he had formerly—not indeed written, but nevertheless thought.

What, then? Should we suppose that Tertullian wrote this book during his Montanist period? I do not venture to say that either. For although in chapter 8 he seems to promise sinners who return to their former and perverse life only one final hope of obtaining pardon from God, [Col. 0170A] he nevertheless teaches nothing in that matter which we read to have been peculiar to the Montanists. Rather, it is something that we find was also vigorously defended by others among the orthodox (see the *Shepherd* of Hermas, Mandate 4, in Fabricius’ *Apocrypha of the New Testament*, vol. III, p. 845 ff., and the passages cited there by Fabricius): that there is no hope of salvation for those who, after receiving baptism, again sin gravely and to the loss of true piety. This later introduced into the Church the most pernicious custom of deferring baptism. And what might appear too harshly stated Tertullian himself mitigates by adding these words: “But the spirit must not at once be cut down and overthrown by despair.”

Accordingly, since this book contains no indications capable of deciding the question, I frankly profess that I am entirely ignorant of the period or doctrine to which it should be assigned.

## § 2.—The Book *Against the Jews*

[Col. 0170C]

Nor am I ashamed to confess the same concerning the book which Tertullian wrote *Against the Jews*. It contains nothing savoring of the absurdities of Montanus; but neither is that surprising, since Tertullian had scarcely any occasion in this book to mention such matters. We must therefore determine whether any indication of its date can be found there.

There is indeed a rather obscure indication in two passages. The first is in chapter 7, where he mentions regions of the Britons inaccessible to the Romans, and shortly afterward the Britons “enclosed within the circuit of their own Ocean.” This led Baronius (*Ecclesiastical Annals*, at the year 212), Allix, and others to believe that the book was written after Severus’ victories over the Britons, and thus approximately in A.D. 209. [Col. 0170D] I would not say so. For how could regions of the Britons then have been called inaccessible when the Romans had gained access to them? And how could Tertullian have said that the Romans could not extend the power of their empire beyond those peoples, when they had done precisely that among the Britons?

Herodian confirms all too clearly (*History*, book III, ch. 14, p. 170 in my edition) that Severus not only left Geta in those regions of Britain which were subject to Roman rule, but also that Severus himself, he continues, advanced farther. Nor is the wall constructed by Severus in Britain—which deceived Baronius and the others—the boundary by which Tertullian means that the Britons were enclosed within their own territory. For he states with complete clarity that the Britons were enclosed not by a wall but within the circuit of the Ocean. [Col. 0171A]

The things that other writers record concerning the Britons belong only to Severus’ expedition against them, which is established to have been undertaken in A.D. 208. Tertullian’s book is therefore earlier, and indeed much earlier, if I see correctly.

Tertullian also argued rightly in a second passage, chapter 13. For at the time this book was written no Jew was permitted access to Bethlehem, the surrounding countryside, or Jerusalem. Yet it is established that Severus enacted many laws favorable to the Jews, which Jacob Basnage briefly enumerated (*History of the Jews*, vol. VIII, p. 49 ff.); and Spartianus says (*Life of Severus*, ch. 16) that these rights were established for them when Severus, after capturing Ctesiphon, was proceeding into Egypt, that is, approximately in A.D. 202. [Col. 0171B] It is therefore not credible that, after granting them many rights, he continued to prohibit anyone from entering either Jerusalem or any other place in Palestine. Nor can it be said that the exile of the Jews, or Tertullian’s book *Against the Jews*, arose only after that year; it must rather have been earlier.

I seem to be confirmed in this opinion by a passage from Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, chapter 21, where, among other things, he tells the Jews that they are not permitted even under the right of strangers to greet their ancestral land with so much as the sole of the foot. Although this is false of the whole territory of Palestine—from which the Jews do not seem to have been expelled even by Hadrian, as Joannes Masson demonstrated especially from many passages of ancient writers (*Historical Collection appended to the works of Aristides*, edited by Samuel Jebb, § 6, no. 8)—Tertullian must nevertheless have held that opinion when writing the *Apologeticum*. [Col. 0171C] From this, together with the other considerations we have presented, it may in some degree be inferred that *Against the Jews* belongs approximately to the same period.

But since neither the precise year of this book can be determined nor is it clear whether Tertullian already agreed with the Montanists at that time, I have chosen to append this book too to those whose date and doctrine are not apparent. When all the points thus far proposed have been collected, the order of Tertullian’s books may be established approximately as follows.

### Before His Adoption of Montanism

*On Prayer*. *To the Martyrs*. *On the Shows*. *On Idolatry*. *Apologeticum*. *On the Testimony of the Soul*. [Col. 0171D] Two books *To the Nations*. *On Baptism*. Two books *To His Wife*. (*Against the Jews*.)

### During His Montanist Period

*On the Crown*. *On the Veiling of Virgins*. *On the Dress of Women*. *On Flight in Persecution*. *Exhortation to Chastity*. *On Monogamy*. *On Fasting*. *On Modesty*. *Against Praxeas*. *Against Hermogenes*. *On the Soul*. *Scorpiace*. *Against the Valentinians*. *Against Marcion*, book I. *On the Prescription of Heretics*. *Against Marcion*, books II, III, and IV. *On the Flesh of Christ*. *On the Resurrection*. *Against Marcion*, book V. *On the Pallium*. (*On Patience*.) *To Scapula*. (*On Repentance*.)
