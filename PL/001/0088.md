Tertullian’s second argument is drawn from the common agreement of nearly all the Gentile philosophers. Although, as we shall show a little later, they disagree considerably with one another in defining the nature of God, nevertheless, willingly or unwillingly, they confessed that he truly exists [Col. 0805D] (Idem, cap. 47, pag. 516; below, this chapter, art. 4; Dissertat. in Minut. cap. 4, art. 1, and in Lactant. cap. 12, art. 1). But elsewhere we have discussed their opinion at length, and what we said there need not now be repeated.

Tertullian derives a third argument for the existence of the one God from his works. Just as no one, unless he is utterly foolish, can fail to acknowledge them, so no one can deny that the one God is their author. For the world, as Tertullian rightly says, was made by him “as an adornment of his majesty” (Tertullian. Apologet. cap. 17, pag. 375). What, then, is that “as an adornment of the divine majesty,” unless it means as a proof and argument of it, whereby, from the creation of the world, his divine [Col. 0806A] majesty and omnipotence are made manifest and known to everyone, even to those of duller understanding? Lactantius therefore expressly said afterward: “Who kindled the sun, the brightest and singular light for human affairs, as a proof of his unique majesty” (Lactant., lib. II divin. Instit. cap. 5, pag. 161). And certainly the sun was not created by some accident or by itself, being entirely devoid of reason. By its own light and splendor, therefore, it demonstrates that almighty God is its author. We have treated this argument in our dissertation on the books of Lactantius (Dissertat. in Lactant. cap. 12, art. 1).

Nor are the duller eyes of the human mind compelled to acknowledge God only because they are dazzled by the sun’s most brilliant light, but also by his other works: “So many and so great,” says Tertullian, “by which we are contained, [Col. 0806B] by which we are sustained, by which we are delighted, and by which we are terrified” (Tertullian. Apologet. cap. 17, pag. 376). For what else do so many and such great works proclaim, except that they were produced by the one God, who leads human beings to knowledge of himself either by benefits or by terror? And hence, indeed, he added a little later that the signs of his majesty were displayed through rains—undoubtedly those of the universal flood—and through fires, especially those by which the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah were consumed. By whom, I ask you, could these things have been done except by God, the most equitable judge, who exacted from impious and utterly wicked people the punishments due for their crimes?

There are yet other things by which, as Tertullian says, we are terrified: namely lightning flashes, thunderbolts, thunder, public calamities and storms, and other similar things. He seems [Col. 0806C] deliberately to have added these in order to meet the various subtleties of atheists, who object that thunderbolts which, for example, fall upon mountains, and other things of this kind, are wholly useless, and that no reason can be given why they occur. To these garrulous and impious people, who wish to serve depraved desires after scorning or suppressing the conscience of their minds, he replies that these things occur so that we may be terrified—that is, so that we may fear the true God who is their author, and through that fear, which elsewhere he demonstrates to be innate in human beings, acknowledge that he truly exists and can punish those same impious people and other criminals according to their deserts (Idem, lib. de Testimon. anim. cap. 2).

Moreover, we have shown elsewhere that, even if the infinite greatness of God and the cause of some of his works are not fully known [Col. 0806D] to the weak and purblind human mind, nevertheless his existence, and the end for which he made all things, are most manifestly demonstrated from his effects, which are unknown to no one. We are not now asking why or how all things are done or were made by him, but whether he himself exists and whether this is known from his works. In other dissertations of ours, moreover, we have clearly shown how certainly and evidently God’s existence is demonstrated from them, and how feeble is the argument of the pagans who denied it because God himself cannot be seen (Dissert. in Minut. cap. 5, art. 1, and cap. 6, art. 1).

Tertullian derives a fourth demonstration of God’s real existence from the inmost sense of the human soul, or [Col. 0807A] from its testimony and from the human conscience itself. “Would you have us,” he says, “prove it from the testimony of the soul itself? Although confined in the prison of the body, although surrounded by corrupt training, although deprived of vigor by lusts and desires, although enslaved to false gods, nevertheless, when it recovers its senses—as though from drunkenness, from sleep, or from some illness—and experiences its own soundness, it names God by this name alone, because this one true God is properly one, good, and great. And ‘Whatever God may grant’ is the voice of all; it also calls him to witness as judge: ‘God sees’; and ‘I commend it to God’; and ‘God will repay me’” (Tertull. Apolog., cap. 17, p. 376 et seq.). In this text of our author, however, Rigaltius, in his usual manner, altered and added certain things. First, on the sole, though perhaps not altogether unhappy, conjecture of Latinius, he wished the verb *patitur* to be corrected and written [Col. 0807B] *potitur*: that is, he says, “while it is possessed of a sound mind.” And certainly the noun *sanitas* is sometimes used for a sound condition of mind, while in good authors we read *potiri gaudia*, “to possess joys.” In two more recent manuscripts, however, *pascitur*, “is nourished,” is written. But what if Tertullian, speaking less classically, said *patitur sanitatem*, “it undergoes soundness,” namely a soundness not yet complete?

Whatever the case, Rigaltius seems on his private authority to have substituted for these words, “By this alone, because properly this one God is one, good, and great,” the following: “By this alone, because by the proper name of the true God: ‘God is great, God is good.’” But were not the words of the manuscripts sufficiently perspicuous, and no less clear than these? Yet suppose that they were more obscure: must hands therefore be laid upon the text of an ancient author, so that it may be corrected at pleasure and no longer remain his?

[Col. 0807C] Our Tertullian does not use this argument against the pagans only in this book, but also presses it more fully and forcefully throughout his book *On the Testimony of the Soul against the Nations*. There he teaches that this testimony of the soul itself is “better known than all literature, more discussed than all doctrine, more widely published than any edition, greater than the whole human being”—that is, the whole of what belongs to a human being (Idem, lib. de testim. anim. cap. 1). We have cited other words of his elsewhere and shown openly that the same argument was employed by Minucius Felix, Cyprian, Arnobius, and Lactantius, who, following in our author’s footsteps, overwhelmed all worshipers of false gods and all atheists by the force and weight of this argument (Dissertat. in Minut., cap. 2, art. 5, and cap. 3, art. 3; Dissertat. in Arnob. cap. 5, art. 1, and Dissertat. in Lactant. cap. 12, art. 1).

[Col. 0807D] We therefore judge that these matters should not be repeated here. We must examine only why he said that this natural invocation of God is the testimony of a soul “naturally Christian.” Is the soul, then, naturally Christian? Did he not say a little later: “Christians are made, not born” (Tertullian. Apologet. cap. 17, pag. 377; Ibid. cap. 18, pag. 378)? Elsewhere, moreover, he addresses the soul itself in this manner: “You are not, so far as I know, Christian; for you are accustomed to become, not to be born, Christian” (Idem, lib. de Testim. anim., cap. 1). But if a human being is not born but becomes Christian, then his soul is not naturally Christian. Yet Tertullian, who certainly did not contradict himself within a few lines, means by the earlier words nothing other than that a conception of the true God is naturally implanted in the human soul [Col. 0808A], and this is the foundation of the whole Christian religion. It does not, however, know all the other mysteries and teachings of that religion unless it is first instructed by others. In this way, therefore, Christians are made and are not born, although they naturally acknowledge and invoke the one God.

Would you have this proved to you from Tertullian himself? In writing *To the Nations*, he states that Father Liber, together with his sacred rites, was expelled from the city of Rome and from all Italy by authority of the senate, and that the statues of Serapis, Isis, and Harpocrates were overthrown. From this, however, he draws the following conclusion: “You therefore have among your ancestors, if not the name, at least the Christian sect, which disregards the gods” (Tertull. lib. I ad Nation. cap. 10, pag. 573). Do you see that merely because of this deed he calls certain pagans by the name of the Christian sect [Col. 0808B]? In the same way, therefore, the soul could be called naturally Christian because, by the testimony of its own conscience and by a natural light and impulse, it acknowledges the one true God. But we have already observed that Minucius Felix advances statements wholly similar to these (Dissertat. in Minut., cap. 13, art. 4).

Rightly and deservedly, therefore, Tertullian concludes from this, and from all the things he had proved thus far, as follows: “This is the sum of the offense of those who refuse to recognize him whom they cannot fail to know” (Apol., c. 17, pag. 376). The conclusion of Cyprian, his pupil and follower, is wholly the same: “And this is the sum of the offense: to refuse to acknowledge him whom one cannot fail to know” (Cyprian., lib. de idolor. Vanit., pag. 15). For no one can plead ignorance of God, whom one is compelled to acknowledge by so many arguments and by the inmost sense of heart and soul [Col. 0808C]. It is therefore a very great offense—indeed, the greatest of all offenses—to refuse to recognize God, who cannot be unknown to anyone unless he is utterly witless and insane.

A fifth demonstration of what is called the divine existence is contained in these words, by which our author draws his conclusion from the infinite perfections of God: “For this reason,” he says, “God is true and so great” (Ibid.). Does he not argue in the following manner? By the name “true God” is understood one whose perfections are infinite and than whom nothing greater or more perfect can be conceived; for he would not be the true God if he were not so great. But he who embraces infinite perfections within himself assuredly has from himself both the fact that he exists and the power to grant existence and being to others. For since the sequence of all created things [Col. 0808D] is such that later things received from earlier ones the origin by which they exist, there had to be, before all time, someone who received his existence from no one but rather possessed it from himself, and who bestowed upon every created thing all the perfections with which it is endowed. But he is true precisely because he is so great. In his book *Against Marcion*, moreover, from the supreme perfection of God he concludes that God is unique, so that he would not exist if he were not one. I should like you to hear what his argument is:

“Since God is the supreme greatness, our truth has rightly pronounced: ‘If God is not one, he does not exist.’ We do not say, ‘If he is not one, he does not exist,’ as though we doubted that God exists; rather, because we trust that he exists, we define him as being that which, if he is not, he is not God—namely, the supreme greatness. [Col. 0809A] Moreover, the supreme greatness must necessarily be unique; therefore God also will be unique: he is not otherwise God unless he is the supreme greatness, nor otherwise the supreme greatness unless he has no equal, nor otherwise without an equal unless he is unique” (Idem., lib. I advers. Marcion., cap. 3).

A sixth and final demonstration proving that God exists was furnished to Tertullian by the supreme and marvelous power of Christians to expel demons, against which even the most obstinate pagan could not utter so much as a murmur. For very often demons, adjured by Christians in the name of Christ, were not only expelled from the persons whom they possessed, but were also compelled openly and publicly to confess that neither they nor the others worshiped by the pagans were gods, but that only one God existed, whom the disciples [Col. 0809B] of Christ worshiped. “But this entire confession of theirs,” says Tertullian, “by which they deny that they are gods and by which they answer that there is no other God besides the one to whom we are bound, is sufficiently effective to repel the charge of having violated especially the Roman religion,” which worshiped not the one true God, but false gods indeed (Tertullian. Apologet., cap. 24, pag. 416). For all the pagans were assuredly obliged to believe demons when they confessed the truth against themselves, since they believed them when they lied. For no one, as he rightly argues, “lies to his own disgrace, but rather to his own honor. Credence is more readily accorded to those who confess against themselves than to those who deny in their own favor” (Ibid., cap. 23, pag. 415). So public a testimony could plainly be doubtful or suspect to no one. For that reason, our author [Col. 0809C] continues, it was accustomed “to make Christians out of pagans” (Ibid.). For what person could reject testimony so certain and clear, unless false prejudices or private interests had utterly deprived him of the light of reason? Rightly and deservedly, therefore, our author reproves the extreme injustice of the pagans, who accused Christians of violating the Roman religion and, contrary to conscience and to so many demonstrations of such great weight, strove by every kind of torture to compel them to worship false gods and deny the true God.

## ARTICLE II. On the perfections or attributes of God; in what sense Tertullian says that God is known to human beings although he is invisible, incomprehensible, and inestimable; and on the knowledge, wisdom, and eternity of this true and living God

[Col. 0809D]

Tertullian rightly infers that God is supremely perfect from the fact that the world and everything in it were created by him, as we saw in the preceding article (Tertullian. Apologet., cap. 11). “For that which perfected all things,” he says, “could not have been imperfect”; that is, because God exists from himself and made all other things, he must have absolutely nothing imperfect in him, but must possess every perfection whatsoever.

These perfections, however, are so great, and so far surpass the weakness of human understanding, that he is “invisible” (Ibid., cap. 17, pag. 375), says our author—that is, he cannot [Col. 0810A] be seen by human eyes. For, as he himself speaks in Exodus (Exod., cap. XXXIII, 20): “Man shall not see me and live.” Tertullian nevertheless adds, “although he is seen”; that is, although human beings, by the arguments and demonstrations set forth above (Art. super.), mentally see and know that he truly exists.

“He is also incomprehensible,” Tertullian continues (Tertullian., ibid.). For Clement of Alexandria acknowledges (Clem. Alexandr., lib. V Strom., pag. 548): Δῆλον μηδένα ποτὲ δυνάσθαι παρὰ τὸν τῆς ζωῆς χρόνον τὸν Θεὸν ἐναργῶς καταλαβέσθαι. No one can ever comprehend God clearly during the course of this life. By these words both authors mean that the infinite perfections of God cannot be fully and thoroughly comprehended by human beings living this mortal life through a clear and distinct idea and conception [Col. 0810B]. Yet he adds: “Although he is represented through grace.” But what is this grace if not that of Christ our Savior, who, having become man, taught human beings how they might understand both him and his divine Father to be the one true God? For, as the author of the Epistle to Diognetus writes, “No one has seen or perfectly known God” (Epist. Diognet., pag. 499, edit. Justin. Martyr): Αὐτὸς δὲ ἑαυτὸν ἐπέδειξεν· ἐπέδειξε δὲ διὰ πίστεως ᾗ μονῇ Θεὸν ἰδεῖν συγκεχώρηται. “But he revealed himself; and he revealed himself through faith, by which alone it has been granted to see God.” Did not both authors have in view those words of Matthew (Matth. XI, 17): “No one knows the Son except the Father, nor does anyone know the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him”? Or those words of John (Joan., cap. I, 18): [Col. 0810C] “No one has ever seen God. The only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, has himself made him known.”

Our author continues: “Inestimable, although he is estimated by human senses”; that is, human beings estimate him rightly in this respect, that they know him to be inestimable (Tertullian. Apologet., cap. 17, pag. 375). For he himself immediately added: “This is what enables God to be estimated: that he does not admit of estimation,” just as if he had said: We estimate God rightly when we confess that neither we nor any other human being can estimate him according to his worth.

If you ask him for the reason for all these statements, he will give it in the same place: “That which can commonly be seen, comprehended, and estimated is less than the eyes by which it is occupied, the hands by which it is defiled, and the senses by which it is discovered”; that is, whatever is commonly perceived by [Col. 0810D] bodily eyes, touched by hands, or falls under the other senses is less than all those senses and belongs to an order lower than God (Ibid.). “For that which is immeasurable,” as God is, “is known to itself alone” (Ibid.). By the term “immeasurable,” however, Tertullian does not mean precisely that which exists everywhere and in every place, but that which is infinite. For in this sense good authors speak of “immeasurable toil,” “immeasurable grief,” or “immeasurable desire.” God, therefore, being infinite, is known only by himself in the full measure of what he is (Ibid.). And thus, as our author again says, “the force of his greatness presents him to human beings as both known and unknown” (Dissertat. in Minut. Octav., cap. 5, art. 1; Dissert. in Lactant. cap. 13, [Col. 0811A] art. 1). For so great indeed is his greatness and the excellence of that divine nature that, although human beings know with absolute certainty that he exists, they nevertheless cannot grasp how great and of what character all his perfections are. But we have already discussed all these matters in our preceding dissertations.

Moreover, we observe that Tertullian asserts not only these so-called negative attributes of God, but also several others called affirmative attributes (Tertullian. Apologet., cap. 30, pag. 441). For he calls God true and living, and thus sets him in opposition to the false and dead gods of the pagans.

He then teaches that God is the observer of all things, whose fullness of knowledge is infinite and from whom absolutely nothing can ever be hidden by any difficulty of concealment [Col. 0811B] (Ibid., cap. 45, pag. 500). He proclaims this divine knowledge not only in the *Apologeticum*, but also in another book. For elsewhere we read these words committed to writing by him (Idem. lib. de Poenit. cap. 3): “Nor, if human mediocrity judges the deed alone because it is unequal to penetrating the hiding places of the will, should we therefore suppose that the will’s offenses are disregarded even under God. God is sufficient for all things. Nothing is removed from his sight, however the sin may be committed.” He therefore penetrates all the inmost and more deeply hidden recesses of the human heart, and accordingly knows perfectly all other things whatsoever.

With no less assurance he declares that God has been rational from eternity, since he brought forth the Word—as will be shown more fully below—the Discourse, *Sophia*, or Wisdom; and, having fashioned the world by word, reason, and wisdom, created it [Col. 0811C] from nothing. He therefore preceded all time and is eternal (Idem, Apologet., cap. 17, pag. 375; cap. 11, pag. 332).

Elsewhere, indeed, he confirms this in the following words (Ibid., cap. 26): “He who existed before time and made the age, the body of times.” Here the noun “body” is used for a collection of several things and an assembled multitude. Thus one speaks of the body of a city or commonwealth. Of Christians, moreover, our author himself says: “We are a body.” His meaning, therefore, is (Ibid., cap. 39): “God made the age, the body of times”; that is, he caused the world to consist of the complete collection of times (Ibid., cap. 18, pag. 377). For this reason he had previously said: “God arranged the age according to fixed arrangements and endings of times.” But he could not have done this unless, as he himself says, he had existed before all [Col. 0811D] time and was therefore eternal. Nor should you doubt that this is the meaning of the one who elsewhere wrote in the name of the Christians (Ibid., cap. 30): “We invoke the eternal God.” There he proclaims him not only eternal, but also almighty, in whose power all things are placed.

## ARTICLE III. It is shown that Tertullian truly believed God to be spirit and to possess nothing corporeal, and contrary arguments are answered

What has thus far been explained concerning so many and such great perfections and attributes of God manifestly demonstrates that they are supreme, and that God possesses from himself alone, and [Col. 0812A] from no other, whatever he is. But if nothing accrues to him from another, if his perfections are infinite, and if they can never be circumscribed by anyone, his nature is undoubtedly most simple and therefore spiritual and wholly free from every corporeal composition. He therefore has no body, since a body is necessarily composed of parts and can consequently be dissolved and is imperfect. But what need is there to prove this teaching of our author by argument? Did he not proclaim it most openly in these words (Tertullian. Apologet., cap. 21): “We have declared him to be God; as his proper substance we designate spirit”? And a little later: “For God also is spirit.”

I would not have you reply that by the word “spirit” he there understands some body, albeit one far more subtle than other bodies. [Col. 0812B] For in the same place he says that the Word, Discourse, and Son of God proceeded from the bosom of his eternal Father, “spirit from spirit, and God from God” (Ibid.). Yet that eternal bringing-forth by God, prior to every created body, can plainly admit nothing corporeal. For it is God’s own thought and intelligence, which must be entirely incorporeal and purely spiritual. Tertullian therefore truly believed God the Father and his Son to be spirit, possessing absolutely nothing of body or of matter, however subtle.

Nevertheless, some protest against this and object to us that Tertullian taught that whatever exists is either a body or nothing, and that nothing intermediate between the two can be assigned. For these are his very words: “Everything that exists is a body of its own kind. [Col. 0812C] Nothing is incorporeal except that which does not exist” (Idem, lib. de Carne Christi, cap. 11). This proposition of his is undoubtedly universal, and accordingly he expressly defines whatever exists—whether God or anything else—as being a body by the very fact that it exists. Since, therefore, he proved by so many arguments, as we have seen, that the one true God exists, he assuredly believed him to be truly corporeal.

But what need is there, others will say, for this or any other argument? Does he not speak of God himself in this manner? “Who will deny that God is a body, even though God is spirit? For spirit is a body of its own kind, in its own form. But even all those invisible things, whatever they may be, have before God both their own body and their own form, through which they are visible to God alone” (Idem, liber adv. Prax., [Col. 0812D] cap. 7).

Yet however universal his first proposition may be, he nevertheless distinguishes some substance intermediate between body and nothing: one that is spiritual, and a spirit distinct from body. Indeed, in the *Apologeticum* with which we are dealing, he said that this mass of the world was created by God “with its whole apparatus of elements, bodies, and spirits” (Idem, Apologet. cap. 17). But what need was there to add “spirits” if he had believed body and spirit to be one and the same? Will you perhaps reply that by “bodies” are meant dense and coarser bodies, while by the word “spirits” more subtle and extremely fine bodies are meant? But these are merely different qualities of bodies, which [Col. 0813A] could adequately have been designated by the word “bodies” alone. By the word “spirits,” however, he undoubtedly seems to signify not qualities of bodies but something distinct from every body.

What of the fact that in another book he wholly and completely distinguishes a spiritual thing from a corporeal one? For when discussing the sins and offenses of a human being, who, as he says, consists of body and spirit, he openly declares that “some offenses are carnal, that is, corporeal, while others are spiritual” (Idem, lib. de Poenit. cap. 3). Then a little later: “Hence they are called spiritual and corporeal, because every offense is either enacted or conceived. That which is in the deed is corporeal, because the deed, like a body, is capable of being both seen and touched. That which is in the mind, however, is spiritual, because spirit [Col. 0813B] is neither seen nor touched. From this it is understood that not only offenses of deed but also offenses of will must be avoided.” He therefore acknowledges that sins are of two kinds: namely corporeal sins, which are perpetrated by the body with no resistance from the mind and will, and other sins belonging to the spirit alone, which are committed by thought and will alone, without any service of the body. Indeed, he also teaches that there are other acts proper to our soul which are in no way dependent upon its body, and for which not only punishment but also reward is due (Idem, lib. de Anim., cap. 58). Yet he considered these good or evil acts of the soul alone, deserving reward or punishment, to be neither body nor nothing, but something intermediate. He therefore thought that some things are solely spiritual and not at all corporeal.

[Col. 0813C] As regards that second proposition of his concerning God, does he not, as we have said, expressly affirm that God’s Son is his own wisdom, internal word and discourse, or intelligence, which is entirely incorporeal and wholly spiritual? How, then, can this be so unless God is wholly incorporeal and spirit? Furthermore, it is quite sufficient that he admits some spiritual substance intermediate between body and nothing. For if there is any such substance, who will deny that God is such above all?

You will nevertheless insist that he recognizes nothing third between the corporeal and incorporeal. For he argues against Hermogenes as follows: “Unless I am mistaken, every thing must necessarily be either corporeal or incorporeal—even if for the moment I concede that there is something incorporeal among substances alone [Col. 0813D], though substance itself is the body of each thing. Certainly, after the corporeal and incorporeal there is nothing third” (Idem, ad Hermog., cap. 35). Yet there he undoubtedly seems to admit incorporeal things rather than deny them. For there, as has been said, he refutes Hermogenes, who babbled that matter, although truly existing, was both corporeal and incorporeal. Tertullian, in order utterly to overturn this fiction of a vain opinion, divides every thing into corporeal and incorporeal. He thus acknowledges that something is incorporeal. And if anything is incorporeal, God certainly is so before all else.

You may perhaps urge that this division did not seem wholly complete to Tertullian himself. For he does not pronounce it absolutely, but, [Col. 0814A] without asserting anything, concedes gratuitously and for the moment to his adversary that some substance is incorporeal, although it is nevertheless the body of the thing itself. He therefore thought that nothing existed except the corporeal. But observe, I ask, that he is concerned only with the meaning of this word “body.” In the same place he explains with sufficient clarity what he understood by this word: “Substance itself,” he says, “is the body of each thing.” He therefore thinks that by the word “body” the substance of each thing is signified. Yet there is no one who does not acknowledge that there is absolutely no intermediate or third thing between substance and nothing itself. And what wonder is it if he thought the term “body” should be understood as the substance itself of each thing, since the apostle Paul sets this very word “body” in opposition to shadows and figures and said that it is that which is the thing itself and the truth? For he writes in the following manner concerning the figures of the Old Testament [Col. 0814B] (Paul. ad Coloss., II, 17): “Which are a shadow of things to come, but the body is Christ”; that is: These ceremonies are nothing other than shadows, but their body—the thing itself and the truth—is Christ, in whom they are fulfilled.

For this reason, in those passages of our Tertullian which have been objected against us, where he said that every substance is a body or that whatever exists is a body, he did not without deliberation add “of its own kind”; that is, the substance is of its own kind, whether corporeal or spiritual.

But someone will insist that, even if this were most clearly proved, it might nevertheless be questioned whether Tertullian at one time thought God to be a body and afterward changed his mind and opinion. For Augustine speaks of him in this manner [Col. 0814C] (August., lib de Genes. ad litter., cap. 26, pag. 273):

“Tertullian believed the soul to be a body for no other reason than that he could not conceive it as incorporeal; and therefore he feared that it would be nothing if it were not a body. Nor did he wish to think differently about God. Yet because he was indeed acute, at times he is overcome by the truth when it presents itself, contrary to his own opinion. For what could he have said more truly than what he states in a certain passage: ‘Everything corporeal is passible’ (Tertul. lib. de Anim. cap. 7)? He ought therefore to have changed the opinion by which a little earlier he had said that even God is a body. For I do not think that he was so foolish as to believe that even the nature of God is passible, so that Christ might now be believed passible and mutable not only in the flesh but also in the Word itself, through which all things were made—a thought far from the Christian heart.”

[Col. 0814D] Petavius, however, disagrees with Augustine and does not think that Tertullian contradicts himself or changed his opinion concerning a corporeal God, although he believed every body to be passible but the nature of God to be in no way passible (Petav. tom. I, Theolog. dogmat., cap. 1, § 5 et seqq.). For the passions that he attributes to a corporeal thing, Petavius says, are of one kind, while those attributed to God—that is, other movements and affections—are of another. For when Marcion denied that there was a movement of anger in God and objected that, if God were ever angry, he would be liable to corruption and death, Tertullian himself replied that the passions, movements, feelings, and affections of the mind in God and in man are indeed called by the same names, but are as different in each as substance and body differ from one another. For he addresses Marcion in these words [Col. 0815A]:

“Distinguish the substances and distribute to them their respective feelings, as different as the substances require, although they may appear to share their names” (Idem, lib. II advers. Marcion., cap. 16) . . . . “As great as the difference between the divine body and the human body under the same names for their members, so great will be the difference between the divine and human mind, although under the same names for their members; the corruptibility of human substance makes these as corruptive in man as the incorruptibility of the divine substance makes them incorruptive in God.”

Petavius therefore infers from this that Tertullian thought there was a body both in God and in man, just as there were passions or sensations, movements, and affections, but with this difference: that in man they are corruptive, whereas in God they are incorruptive. Nothing therefore prevents God, [Col. 0815B] he says, from having a body according to Tertullian’s opinion, a body which he thought not passible but incorruptive. But consider, I ask, whether Petavius fully perceived the sense in which Tertullian understood the term “passible,” which he excludes from God. For by it Tertullian seems to signify nothing other than that which carries with it something imperfect and mutable. Did Augustine not therefore rightly say that Tertullian either contradicted himself or changed his opinion when he asserted that God is a body and that every body is passible? For God is supremely perfect and is endowed with the highest simplicity and every kind of perfection. He can therefore have absolutely nothing imperfect. Thus the supreme perfection of God and body conflict with one another and cannot exist together in God.

[Col. 0815C] Moreover, although Petavius had proved that Tertullian did not fall into any contradiction, that most learned man nevertheless acknowledges that what Augustine reported in another book in order to excuse Tertullian is of greater importance (August., lib. de Haeresib., cap. 86, pag. 25):

“Tertullian might in some fashion be thought to have regarded the divine nature and substance itself as a body whose parts could or should be conceived as some greater and others smaller, as is the case with all things that we properly call bodies . . . But he could, as I have said, have been thought to call God a body for this reason: because he is not nothing, is not emptiness, and is not a quality of body or soul, but is whole everywhere, divided by no intervals of space, and yet remains immutably in his own nature and substance.”

It will perhaps seem surprising to some who do not sufficiently know [Col. 0815D] Augustine’s modesty why he does not dare fully to affirm that this was truly Tertullian’s opinion, but says only that it can in some fashion be thought to have been so. For we have already shown that Tertullian declared with sufficient clarity that by the term “body” he understood substance, which exists in some manner and is opposed to nothing. Furthermore, we have observed that he divides substance into corporeal and incorporeal—that is, wholly free from every body and purely spiritual. But if he ever thought that even one such substance existed, he was certainly persuaded that God is spirit and by far more excellent than all others. And surely his supreme perfections and his infinite cognition and knowledge of all things, so clearly vindicated by Tertullian, as we observed a little [Col. 0816A] above, can possess nothing corporeal whatsoever. For no body, however fine; no matter, however agitated by motion; and no atoms, however joined together by collision, can produce knowledge of any thing, much less of all things. Tertullian therefore could not have attributed a body or anything corporeal to God unless, as we have seen, by this word he understood a substance of its own kind—that is, divine and spiritual. But further light will be cast upon these matters by what will be said in the following chapter concerning the divinity of Christ, and afterward concerning angels and demons and the human soul.
