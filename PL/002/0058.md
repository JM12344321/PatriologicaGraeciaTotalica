The spiritual kind, however, she could not touch in this way, since she herself was spiritual. With this in mind, she turned her attention exclusively to the psychic kind [Note: [Col. 0572B] “If one,” Rhen. Seml.; Jun. suggests “her hand toward the psychic kind.”], bringing forth the Savior’s teachings.

And first—a thing that must be spoken, read, and heard with a great shudder at its blasphemy—she fashions [Note: [Col. 0572B] “Our God is still being fashioned,” Rhen. Seml.] this God of ours and of everyone else except the heretics: Father, Demiurge, and King of all things that come after him. [Note: [Col. 0572B] “God the Demiurge,” Rhen. Seml.]

For all things came from him—[Note: [Col. 0572B] “For from him, if . . .” Seml. omitted “all things,” perhaps through a printer’s error.] if indeed they came from him and not rather from Achamoth herself. Unaware of anything originating from her hidden influence, he was moved toward every operation by her from outside, like a puppet [Note: [Col. 0573C] “By an isolated impulse from outside.” The old books do indeed read this, but falsely. One must certainly read “by a puppeteer’s impulse.” Thus in *On the Soul*: “By a puppeteer’s movement agitating the surface from within.” The old glosses explain *puppets* as Νευρόσπαστοι, “things moved by strings.” Marcus Aurelius, at the beginning of Book VII, said σιγιλλάρια νευροσπαστούμενα, “little figures moved by strings.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0573B] “Like a puppet,” Jun.] pulled by strings.

Finally, because of this ambiguity concerning the agents responsible for the works, they compounded for him the name *Metropator* (“Mother-Father”), while distinguishing his other titles according to the conditions and positions of his works. Thus, with respect to the psychic substances, which they assign to the right, they call him Father; with respect to the material substances, [Note: [Col. 0573B] “Maternal,” Rhen. Seml.; perhaps “material,” Jun.] which they assign to the left, they name him Demiurge; but with respect to the totality [Note: [Col. 0573C] “Totality.” In this chapter, as in both the preceding and the following chapters, Tertullian returns to a restrained and serious style of argument, temporarily renouncing his jokes. He not only reproduces with exactness the description of the *gnosis* given by Irenaeus, recasting it in his own diction and distinctive manner of writing, but also develops it clearly and expounds it more fully.

Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, ch. 5, § 1.—“. . . But they teach that she was unable to form the spiritual substance, because it was consubstantial with her. And first, they say, she formed [Col. 0573D] from the psychic substance the Father and King of all things consubstantial with him. . . . They say that the things after him were formed by him, though he was moved secretly and without his knowledge by the Mother; hence they call him *Metropator* . . . and they call him Demiurge and Father: Father of the things on the right, that is, of the psychic things; Demiurge of the things on the left, that is, of the material things; and King of them all.”

## CHAPTER XIX

But not even the proper application of the names corresponds to the proper attribution of the works from which all those names arise. She ought to have been called all these things, since she was the one by whom the works were being performed—except that by now they were not performed even by her.

For although they say that Achamoth devised images in honor [Col. 0573B] of the Aeons, they again divert authorship of this to the Savior, who supposedly worked through her. Thus he caused her herself to serve as the image of the invisible and unknown Father—unknown, of course, and invisible to the Demiurge—while the Demiurge himself represented the Son, Nous, and the archangels, the Demiurge’s work, represented the remaining Aeons.

When I hear of three such images, do you not now wish me to laugh at the images painted by their utterly perverse artist? Achamoth, a female, the image of the Father? And the Demiurge ignorant of his mother, much more of the Father? [Note: [Col. 0573B] Jun. places “much more of the Father” in brackets.] Nous the image of one who is ignorant of the Father? And servant-angels likenesses of their masters?

[Note: [Col. 0574C] “This is to paint a mule from an ass.” He uses “to paint a mule from an ass” as a proverb: that is, to reproduce one set of absurdities after the pattern of similar absurdities, or to fabricate new lies from old lies. For he adds, “and to copy Ptolemy from Valentinus.” Valentinus first devised these monstrous follies; Ptolemy added something of his own, varying somewhat from his author. Thus the name “ass” appropriately belongs to Valentinus, the author of this heresy, and “mule” to his disciple Ptolemy. A mule is born from an ass and a mare in the twelfth month, says Pliny, Book VIII. RHEN.] This is to paint a mule from an ass, and to copy Ptolemy from Valentinus.
