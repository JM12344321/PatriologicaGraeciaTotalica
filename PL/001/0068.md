## The Book *On the Testimony of the Soul*, Against the Nations

[Col. 0607]

# The Book of Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullian, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, Against the Nations

[Note: [Col. 0607D] The treatise *On the Testimony of the Soul* is connected with the *Apologeticum* and the books *Against the Nations*. For in it he develops and pursues at greater length the argument for religion that he had advanced in *Apologeticum*, ch. 17: namely, that the soul naturally recognizes, almost against its will, that there is only one God and that there is another life after this one has passed. He demonstrates this from a perception implanted in the soul by nature and so deeply engraved upon it that nothing can erase it; so much so that it slips out even from the unwary soul, as when one thoughtlessly exclaims, “God willing,” “Whatever shall please God,” “May God be with me,” and so forth. These expressions presuppose that the soul is imbued with knowledge of God, just as the fear of death implanted by nature, the desire to commend one’s name to immortality, and compassion for the dead presuppose that there is a life other than this one. Since these perceptions of the soul are naturally implanted in individual human beings, they must flow from God. See what Tertullian develops with great wit in this short treatise. LUMP.] (C)

## Argument

After the most abundant harvest of writers [Col. 0607D] who had pleaded the cause of the Christian religion, Tertullian had entered the arena, as is evident from the *Apologeticum* and the two books *Against the Nations*. Yet, since he seemed to have done nothing to advance that cause, he began this little book *On the Testimony of the Soul*, so that he might assail the enemies of the Christians through a new kind of combat, tear [Col. 0608C] from their minds the perverse opinion they had conceived, and place the [Col. 0608D] Christian cause in safety. He had laid, as it were, the foundations of this work in chapter 17 of the *Apologeticum*; here, however, he pursues more fully what he had touched on there only in passing, and demonstrates especially that God is one and that the soul has a state of existence after death.

## Chapter III

Indeed, why do we affirm [Note: [Col. 0612C] “When,” Rigault.] that they are demons? Surely it is not as though we do not also prove it, since we alone drive them out of bodies. [Note: [Col. 0612D] “Since we alone drive them out of bodies.” Thus in *On the Soul*, p. 304: “Christian power alone exposes that most destructive force.” Yet Irenaeus, book II, ch. 5, affirms that Jews also performed this, even after we were grafted, like a wild olive shoot, into their olive tree. RIG.]

Some adherent of Chrysippus [Col. 0613A] [Note: [Col. 0613C] “Some adherent of Chrysippus.” The use of *aliqui* for *aliquis* is thoroughly Tertullianic. Chrysippus the philosopher—concerning whom he speaks more fully below in *On the Soul*—appears to have mocked demons in a book, apparently *On Divination*, which Lactantius cites in book I. PAM.] mocks both their existence [Note: [Col. 0613B] “Their existence” absent from Rigault.] and their enduring our abhorrence; your curses answer him. You call a person a “demon” because of uncleanness, malice, insolence, or any stain that we attribute to demons, or because he is suitable [Note: [Col. 0613C] “And,” Wouwer.] to the necessity of hatred. [Note: [Col. 0613C] “Troublesome,” Rigault; “suitable for hatreds,” Junius.]

Finally, in every [Note: [Col. 0613C] “Vexation,” Paris.] act of aversion, rejection, and detestation you pronounce the name Satan [Note: [Col. 0613C] “Finally, in every act of aversion you pronounce the name Satan.” Was the name Satan, then, used by Greeks or Latins who were not yet Christians? Certainly not. Rather, they said *Malum*—“the evil!”—a familiar interjection among comic writers when expressing aversion to, rejection of, or detestation of something. Terence, in the *Eunuch*: “Who, the evil, is the other?” Plautus, in the *Epidicus*: “What, the evil, is this shamelessness?” Among Christians, however, “the Evil One,” used absolutely, stands for Satan; and in this passage Tertullian in turn has placed “Satan” for “the evil.” RIG.], whom we call the angel of malice, the maker of every error, the corrupter of the whole world. Through him the human being was deceived from the beginning, so that he transgressed God’s command and was therefore delivered over to death; thereafter he infected the whole race from his own seed and made it the transmitter of his own condemnation as well.

You therefore perceive your destroyer. And although Christians alone—or whatever sect belongs to the Lord—have known him, you nevertheless know him too, because you hate him.
