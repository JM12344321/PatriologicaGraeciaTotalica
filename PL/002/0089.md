Enough on the prophetic instrument; I now appeal to the Gospels. Here too I shall first meet that same cunning of theirs, whereby they contend that the Lord likewise uttered everything in parables, because it is written: “Jesus spoke all these things in parables, and without a parable he did not speak to them”—that is, [Note: [Col. 0841C] “To” added by Rig.] to the Jews. For the disciples also say, “Why do you speak in parables?” And the Lord replies: “For this reason I speak to them in parables, so that seeing they may not see, and hearing they may not hear,” in accordance with [Col. 0841B] Isaiah. But if he spoke to the Jews in parables, then he did not speak so to everyone; if not to everyone in parables [Note: [Col. 0841C] “Then not to everyone; if not to everyone in parables” is absent from Ven.], then neither did he always speak in parables, nor is everything a parable, but only certain things, when addressed to certain persons—to certain persons, namely the Jews, though sometimes plainly to the disciples as well.

But consider how Scripture reports it: “He also spoke a parable to them.” Therefore he also spoke what was not a parable, since it would not have been noted when he spoke a parable if he always spoke in that manner. And yet you will find no parable [Note: [Col. 0841C] “Parable” is absent from Par.] that is not either explained by him himself, as that of the sower in its application to the ministry of the word; or elucidated beforehand by the Evangelist as commentator, as that of the arrogant judge and the importunate widow, concerning perseverance in prayer; or readily open to inference, as that of the fig tree granted a reprieve in hope, as an image of Jewish unfruitfulness.

But if even the parables do not obscure [Col. 0841C] [Note: [Col. 0841C] “Adumbrate,” Rhen., Seml.] the light of the Gospel, far less can propositions and definitions, whose nature is explicit, mean something other than what they say. Now it is by definitions and propositions that the Lord declares either the judgment, the kingdom of God, or the resurrection: [Note: [Col. 0841D] “It will be more tolerable for Tyre,” etc., Matt. 11. The Greek word ἀνεκτότερον, “more tolerable,” as the Author translates it, also means “more lenient,” as in the Vulgate edition. PAM.] “It will be more tolerable,” he says (Matt. 11), “for Tyre and Sidon on the day of judgment”; and (Matt. 10), [Col. 0842A] “Tell them that the kingdom of God has drawn near”; and (Luke 14), “You will be repaid at the resurrection of the righteous.”

If these are unqualified names [Note: [Col. 0841C] “Absolutely,” Rhen., Seml.] of things—that is, of the judgment, the kingdom of God, and the resurrection—so that none of them can be compressed into a parable, neither should those things be forced into parables [Note: [Col. 0841D] “Which are declared concerning the experience of the resurrection.” Just as in the book *On the Veiling of Virgins* he speaks of the “experience of marriage,” so here he speaks of the “experience of the resurrection,” signifying that manifestly divine force to be brought upon bodies through the return of their souls to them. RIG.] which are declared concerning the ordering [Note: [Col. 0842C] “Dispensation,” Rig.], accomplishment, and experience of the kingdom, judgment, and resurrection. They should therefore be maintained [Note: [Col. 0842C] “Will be maintained,” Pam., Rhen.] as bodily realities intended for bodies—that is, [Note: [Col. 0841D] Not spiritual, because not figurative. Conversely, things are figurative when they are spoken of the body in such a way that they must be understood of the spirit. “The words that I speak,” says Christ, “are spirit and life”; they are therefore figurative: they mean something other than what they sound like. The lineaments of Christ, the image of Christ, holiness, righteousness, truth. Paul, [Col. 0842A] 1 Corinthians 15: “As we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear the image of the heavenly.” RIG.] not spiritual realities, because they are not figurative.

[Note: [Col. 0842D] “For this reason we also established beforehand that both the body of the soul,” etc. In chapter 16 the Author established beforehand that both soul and body are liable either to punishment or to reward; and in chapter 17, because, according to his own position, the soul was corporeal and possessed a body of its own kind, he also proved that it can be tormented without the flesh in hell. Therefore he speaks here of both the body of the soul and that of the flesh, because he also assigned a body to the soul. LAC.] For this reason we also established beforehand [Note: [Col. 0842C] “Touched upon beforehand,” Rhen.] that both the body of the soul and that of the flesh are liable to recompenses apportioned for their joint activity, lest the corporeality of the soul, by providing an occasion for figurative interpretations, exclude the corporeality of the flesh, since both must be believed to share in the kingdom, the judgment, and the resurrection. [Col. 0842B] We now proceed to demonstrate that, in every mention of the resurrection, the Lord signifies the corporeality of the flesh in the proper sense, without prejudice to that of the soul [Note: [Col. 0842D] “Without prejudice to that of the soul.” That is, with the corporeality of the soul left unshaken. For he wishes the things declared concerning the resurrection to be referred to the corporeality of the flesh, yet not in consequence to deny that the soul has its own particular body. But he immediately reveals this position to be paradoxical when he adds, “which itself few have accepted”—namely, the corporeality of the soul. LAC.], which itself few have accepted.

## CHAPTER XXXIV

First of all, when he says that he came for this purpose, to save what had perished, what do you say [Note: [Col. 0842C] “Do you say,” Rhen., Seml.] had perished? The human being, without doubt. The whole human being, or only a part? Certainly the whole. For the transgression that caused humanity’s perdition was committed both at the soul’s instigation, through desire, and by the flesh’s act, through tasting; [Note: [Col. 0842D] “Branded the whole human being with the official record of transgression,” etc. An *elogium* is the public and lawful declaration of a crime and sentence, sometimes made by the voice [Col. 0843D] of a herald, and sometimes written as a title upon a tablet. LAC.] it branded the whole human being with the official record of transgression, and consequently filled the whole with liability to perdition. Therefore the whole one who perished will be saved, since the whole perished [Note: [Col. 0842C] “Perishes,” Rhen., Rig., Ven., Seml.] by sinning [Col. 0842C] [Note: [Col. 0842C] “The whole” added by Pam., Fran.]—unless perhaps that sheep too is lost without its body and brought back without its body. For if its flesh also, together with its soul—which constitutes the whole animal—is carried back upon the shoulders of the good shepherd, it is surely an illustration of the restoration of the human being in both substances.

Or how unworthy of God it would be to restore half a human being to salvation! It would be almost [Col. 0843A] to do less, since even [Note: [Col. 0843D] “The full pardon of secular rulers is always insisted upon,” etc. That age began commonly to use this word in this sense. Thus below: “If divine indulgence did not dry every shower of tears.” RHEN.] secular rulers always lay claim to the prerogative of full pardon. The devil would be understood to be stronger in injuring a human being, since he crushes the whole; God would be declared weaker, since he does not raise up the whole. Yet the Apostle also teaches (Rom. 5) that where sin abounded, there grace superabounded.

Moreover, how will one be regarded as saved who can also be called lost—lost, namely, in the flesh, though saved in the soul—unless the soul too must now be included in what was lost, so that it can be made safe? For that which was lost will need to be saved. We accept, however, the immortality of the soul, so that when it is lost it is believed to pass not into annihilation but into punishment—that is, into Gehenna. And if this is so, salvation will no longer concern the soul, since by its own nature it is safe through immortality, but rather the flesh, which all agree is perishable.

Or if [Col. 0843B] the soul also is perishable—that is, not immortal—just as the flesh is, then that pattern of salvation must equally benefit the flesh also, since it is likewise mortal and perishable, because [Note: [Col. 0843C] “By which,” Fran.] the Lord will save that which perishes. [Note: [Col. 0843D] “I do not now wish to draw the matter out with a contentious rope.” A proverbial expression for arguments that are both lengthy and highly intricate. RHEN.] I do not now wish to draw the matter out with a contentious rope, debating whether perdition lays claim to the human being on this side or on that [Note: [Col. 0843C] “Claims,” Rig., Ven.], since salvation, being destined for both sides, is equally distributed between the two substances.

For see: from whichever substance you assume the human being to have perished, he did not perish in the other. He will therefore already be safe in that in which he did not perish, and will nevertheless be made safe in that in which he did perish. You have the restoration of the whole human being, since whatever part of him perishes the Lord will save, and whatever part does not perish he certainly will not cause to perish. Who will any longer doubt [Note: [Col. 0843C] “Doubted,” Pam., Fran.] the security of both substances, when one [Note: [Col. 0843C] “Either one,” Rhen.] will obtain salvation [Col. 0843C] and the other will not lose it?

And yet the Lord expresses the meaning of the matter still further, saying: “I came not to do my own will, but the will of the Father who sent me.” What will, I ask you? “That I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but should raise it up on the last day.” What had Christ received from the Father except that which he had also put on? The human being, without doubt—the interwoven fabric of flesh and soul. He will therefore allow neither of the things he received to perish; indeed, not anything belonging to either of them; indeed, not even a small part. [Col. 0844A]

[Note: [Col. 0843D] “But if the flesh is a small thing,” etc. Here he alludes to these heretics’ contempt for our flesh, which they despised as cheap, trifling, and devoid of all dignity, as was sufficiently indicated above. For even if it were so that the flesh was as cheap and insignificant as these heretics reproachfully claim, it would still have to be raised. The works performed through the service of the flesh are not so insignificant that God should neglect them; and for that reason the flesh must be raised for God’s judgment, as Athenagoras argues [Col. 0844D] in *On the Resurrection*. LAC.] But if the flesh is a small thing, then he will not lose even the flesh, because he will lose not even a small thing; nor anything at all, because not anything at all [Note: [Col. 0843C] “Because not anything at all” is absent from Rhen., Seml.]. Yet if he will not also raise the flesh on the last day, he will allow to perish not a small portion of the human being, but, I would say, in view of so great a part, almost the whole.

He presses the point further: [Note: [Col. 0844D] “This is the Father’s will.” This is “the will”; John 6:40: τοῦτο δὲ ἐστι τὸ θέλημα, “and this is the will.” RIG.] “This [Note: [Col. 0843D] “This thing,” Rhen., Seml.] is the Father’s will: that everyone who sees [Note: [Col. 0844C] “Looks upon,” Rhen., Seml.] the Son and believes in him should have eternal life, and I will raise him on the last day.” He establishes the fullness of the resurrection, for he apportions to each substance, according to its respective function, its own [Note: [Col. 0844C] “Former,” Par.] reward of salvation: to the flesh, through which the Son [Note: [Col. 0844C] “Of Man” added by Rhen.] was seen, and to the soul, through which he was believed.

“Therefore,” you will say, “the reality promised will belong to those by whom Christ was seen.” Let it plainly be so, provided that the same [Col. 0844B] hope has flowed from them to us as well. For if the works of flesh and soul were fruitful at that time for those who saw and therefore believed, much more are they so for us. “For more blessed,” he says, “are those who have not seen [Note: [Col. 0844C] “Do not see,” Rhen., Seml.] and yet believe.” Even if the resurrection of the flesh were denied to the former, it would certainly have belonged to those who are more blessed. For how are they blessed if they are to perish in part?

## CHAPTER XXXV

He also commands that we should rather fear [Note: [Col. 0844D] “Who can kill body and soul,” etc. Here and near the end of the chapter he rendered another meaning of the Greek word ἀπολέσαι, translating it as “to kill,” whereas in the preceding chapter he gave it another meaning, namely “to destroy” or “lose,” together with the Vulgate translator, when he says of the soul that, when lost, it is believed to pass into Gehenna. PAM.] him who can kill body and soul in Gehenna—that is, the Lord alone—and not those who kill the body but can do no harm to the soul—that is, human authorities. Thus the soul’s natural immortality is acknowledged here, since it cannot be killed by human beings, as is also the mortality of the flesh, since it can be killed; and thus the resurrection [Col. 0844C] of the dead is likewise shown to belong to the flesh, which could not be killed in Gehenna unless it had been raised.

[Note: [Col. 0844D] “But since even here a captious question is raised about the interpretation of ‘body,’” etc. He points to the heretics’ evasion by which, in order to elude that Gospel authority, they dragged the word “body” into a foreign and absurd meaning through novel quibbles and sophistical inventions. St. Jerome, *Against John of Jerusalem*, addressed to Pammachius, severely censures in him the ridiculous use of the word “body” to deny that our flesh will rise. LAC.] But since even here a captious question is raised about the interpretation of “body,” I shall understand the human body to be nothing other than this entire structure of flesh, of whatever kinds of materials it is fashioned and diversified [Note: [Col. 0844C] “May be diversified,” Ven., erroneously.]: that which is seen [Note: [Col. 0844D] “That which is seen” is absent from Ven.], that which is touched, and finally that which is killed by human beings. So too I shall recognize the body of a wall as nothing other than mortar, stones, and bricks. [Col. 0845A] If anyone introduces some hidden body, let him show, reveal, and prove that it too is what is killed by a human being, and then the statement will concern that body.

Likewise, if “body” is set in opposition [Note: [Col. 0845D] “He sets in opposition,” Pam., Fran.] to “soul,” their cunning will be idle. For when both are presented—body and soul—as being killed in Gehenna, the body is distinguished from the soul, and we are left to understand “body” as that which is readily apparent: namely, the flesh. Just as it will be killed [Note: [Col. 0845D] “Is killed,” Rig., Ven.] in Gehenna if it has not chosen rather to fear being killed by God, so it will also be brought to life for eternal life if it has chosen rather to be slain by human beings.

Accordingly, if anyone takes the killing of flesh and soul in Gehenna to mean the destruction and termination of both substances—not their punishment, as though they were to be consumed rather than chastised—let him remember that the fire of Gehenna is proclaimed eternal, for eternal punishment. From this let him recognize the eternity of that killing, on account of which it must be feared more than human killing, [Col. 0845B] since the latter is temporal. He will then also believe the substances to be eternal whose killing in punishment is eternal.

Certainly, since after the resurrection the body together with the soul is to be killed by God [Note: [Col. 0845D] “By God” omitted by Rhen., Seml.] in Gehenna, both points will be sufficiently established: the resurrection of the flesh and the eternal killing. Otherwise it would be utterly absurd if the flesh were raised only to be killed in Gehenna so that it might cease to exist, when it would have suffered the same thing by not being raised. It would, apparently, be restored for this purpose: that it might not exist, although nonexistence had already befallen it.

Supporting us in the same hope, he also adds [Note: [Col. 0845D] “Added,” Rig., Ven.] the example of the sparrows: not one [Note: [Col. 0845D] “The other,” all editions except Jun.] of two falls to the ground without God’s will. Thus you may believe that the flesh too, which has fallen into the earth, can likewise rise again through the will of the same God. For even if this is not permitted to sparrows, we rank above many sparrows [Col. 0845C] precisely because, though we fall, we rise again.

Moreover, by affirming [Note: [Col. 0845D] “He affirms,” Rig., Ven.] that all the hairs of their heads have been numbered [Note: [Col. 0845D] “Counted out,” Rig., Ven.], he surely promises that they will be preserved. For what reason would he have brought into a reckoning things that were to perish, unless because this is the meaning of: “Of all that the Father has given me, I will lose nothing” (John 6)—that is, not even a hair, just as not an eye or a tooth?

Besides, whence will come weeping and gnashing of teeth in Gehenna unless from eyes and [Note: [Col. 0845D] “From” added by Rhen., Seml.] teeth? The body too, of course, has been killed in Gehenna and thrust into the outer darkness—torments particularly suited to the eyes—if anyone has come to the wedding clothed in works of insufficient worth. He is immediately to be bound by hands [Col. 0846A] and feet, as one who has risen with the body. Thus reclining in the kingdom of God, sitting upon the thrones [Note: [Col. 0845D] “Twelve” added by Fran.] of Christ, standing then at his right [Note: [Col. 0846D] “Then at his right,” Rig., Ven.] or left hand, and eating from the tree of life are all most trustworthy indications of a bodily ordering.

## CHAPTER XXXVI

Let us now see whether, in crushing the Sadducees’ subtlety, he has not established our position all the more. The occasion of the question, I think, was the destruction of belief in the resurrection. For the Sadducees [Note: [Col. 0845D] “For the Sadducees admit the salvation neither of the soul,” etc. More than once in this work and elsewhere I have noted concerning those heretics that they denied the immortality of the soul and the resurrection of bodies. LE PR.] admit salvation neither of the soul nor of the flesh. They therefore adapted to their problem an argument drawn from precisely that aspect under which faith in the resurrection is most severely shaken [Note: [Col. 0846D] “From precisely that source from which hope in the resurrection is most severely shaken,” Paris.]: [Note: [Col. 0845D] “Namely, under the pretext of the flesh.” Because legitimate arguments for proving their inventions were lacking, the Sadducees put forward fictitious ones. They asked Christ concerning the wife of seven husbands, whose wife she would be at the resurrection—a question not merely frivolous but ridiculous. LE PR.] namely, under the pretext of the flesh, asking whether it would marry after the resurrection. They used the case of the woman who had married seven [Col. 0846B] brothers, where it would be uncertain to which of them she would be restored.

Now preserve the meaning both of the question and of the answer, and the controversy has been met. For if the Sadducees rejected the resurrection, while the Lord [Note: [Col. 0845D] “Confirmed it.” It might perhaps be no bad reading to have “confirms”; this would please me more, for it speaks more pointedly and forcefully. LE PR.] confirmed it [Note: [Col. 0846D] “Confirms,” Rhen., Seml.], rebuking them as ignorant of the Scriptures—namely, those that proclaimed the resurrection—and as unbelieving in the power of God, which was surely adequate to raise the dead; and if he finally added, “But concerning the fact that [Note: [Col. 0846D] “How,” Rhen., Seml.] the dead rise” [Note: [Col. 0846D] I would read “rise” or “will rise.” LE PR.], then, without doubt, by confirming the very thing that was denied—namely, the resurrection of the dead, who are alive before God [Note: [Col. 0846D] “The Lord,” Rhen., Seml.]—he also confirmed that it is of the same kind as that which was being denied: namely, a resurrection of both human substances.

Nor, because he denied that they would marry then, did he thereby show that they would not rise [Note: [Col. 0846D] “Will deny … will show,” Rhen., Seml.]. On the contrary, he called them “children of the resurrection” [Col. 0846C] [Note: [Col. 0846D] “Calls,” Rhen., Seml.], since through it they will in a sense be born, after which they will not marry. Rather, once raised, they will be like the angels: [Note: [Col. 0846D] “Insofar as they will not marry, because they will not die.” After the resurrection there will be no use for marriage. Since marriages were instituted solely for the propagation of the human race, when that necessity of dying ceases, that form of propagation also ceases. LE PR.] insofar as they will not marry, because they will not die, but insofar as they will pass into an angelic condition through that clothing of incorruptibility, through the transformation of a substance that nevertheless has been raised.

Besides, the question whether we would marry or die again would never have arisen unless the restoration of precisely that substance which properly performs the functions both of dying and of marrying—that is, the flesh—were being called into doubt. You therefore have the Lord confirming against the Jewish heretics what is now denied among [Note: [Col. 0846D] “The Sadducees of the Christians.” Just as the Sadducees were heretics among the Jews, so he calls the Christian heretics of his own time “the Sadducees of the Christians.” LE PR.] the Sadducees of the Christians: a solid resurrection.

## CHAPTER XXXVII

[Col. 0847A]

Thus, even if [Note: [Col. 0847C] “Although,” Rhen.] he says that the flesh profits nothing, the meaning must be determined from the subject matter of the saying. [Note: [Col. 0847C] “For because they considered his discourse harsh and intolerable, as though he had actually ordained that they should eat his flesh.” Theodorus of Heraclea excellently softens all the harshness of this saying: Σκληρὸς ἦν ἀληθῶς ὁ λόγος ἐνομιζόμενος σαρκοφάγους τινὰς καὶ αἱμοβόρους τοὺς ἀκούοντας ἀποτελεῖν· τοῖς δὲ πνευματικῶς τὰ πνευματικὰ ἐκλαμβάνουσιν, οὐδὲν ἐφαίνετο σκληρόν, ἀλλὰ ῥήματα εὐσεβείας ὑπάρχοντα, τὴν αἰώνιον αὐτοῖς πρυτανεύοντα ζωήν: “The saying was truly harsh when it was thought to turn its hearers into people who ate flesh and drank blood; but to those who received spiritual things spiritually, nothing in it appeared harsh. Rather, its words were words of piety, procuring eternal life for them.” See Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, book IV, chapter 15. RIG.] For because they considered his discourse harsh and intolerable, as though he had actually ordained that they should eat his flesh, he first said, in order to assign the basis of salvation to the spirit [Note: [Col. 0847C] “In the spirit,” Pam., Fran.]: “It is the Spirit who gives life”; and thus he added: [Note: [Col. 0847D] “The flesh profits nothing.” So too her maternal kinship would have profited Mary nothing unless she had borne Christ more blessedly in her heart than in her flesh. Augustine, *On Holy Virginity*. RIG.] “The flesh profits nothing” [Note: [Col. 0847C] “But” inserted by Rhen., Seml.]—for giving life, that is.

He also explains what he wishes “spirit” to mean: “The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and are life.” So too above: “He who hears my words and believes in him who sent me has eternal life, and will not come into judgment, but will pass [Note: [Col. 0847C] “Passes,” some editions; “has passed,” others; “will pass,” others.] from death to life” (John 5–6).

Thus, by establishing that the word gives life, because the word is spirit and life, he also called that same word his flesh, because [Col. 0847B] the Word had also been made flesh. Accordingly, it must be sought as the cause of life, [Note: [Col. 0847D] “And devoured by hearing.” This unusual metaphor is derived from the fact that, since Christ is the Word of God and his flesh is bread, he is said to be devoured by hearing just as bread is devoured by the mouth. LE PR.] devoured by hearing, ruminated upon by the understanding, and digested by faith [Note: [Col. 0847C] “Directed,” Rhen.]. For shortly before this he had also declared his flesh to be heavenly bread, everywhere pressing upon them, through the allegory of necessary nourishment [Note: [Col. 0847C] “Peoples,” Rig., Ven.], the memory of the fathers who had preferred the bread and meat of the Egyptians to the divine calling.

Therefore, turning to their thoughts, because he perceived that they were about to disperse [Note: [Col. 0848C] Variant spelling in Rhen., Seml.], he said: “The flesh profits nothing.” What has this to do with destroying the resurrection of the flesh? As though something might not exist which, although it profits nothing, may nevertheless itself be profited by something else! The Spirit profits, for it gives life. The flesh profits nothing, for it is put to death. Thus he has arranged [Note: [Col. 0848C] “Illuminated,” Lat.] the proposition concerning both substances in greater accordance with our position. For by showing what profits and what does not profit, he equally illuminated [Col. 0847C] what profits what: namely, that the Spirit gives life to the flesh that has been put to death.

“For the hour will come,” he says, “when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live.” [Col. 0848A] What is dead but the flesh? What is God’s voice but the word? And what is the word but spirit? He is deservedly going to raise the flesh, because that is what he himself became; to raise it from the death that he himself suffered, and from the tomb into which he himself was carried.

Finally, when he says, “Do not marvel that the hour will come in which all who are in the tombs will hear the voice of the Son of God and will come forth: those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment,” no one will any longer be able to interpret the dead who are in the tombs as anything other than bodies and flesh, because the tombs themselves are nothing other than lodging places for corpses.

For even those “old human beings”—that is, those dead through ignorance of God—whom the heretics argue should be understood as the tombs, are expressly proclaimed as persons who will come forth from the tombs to judgment. Otherwise, how will tombs come forth from [Col. 0848B] tombs?

## CHAPTER XXXVIII

After the Lord’s sayings, what are we to believe his deeds mean—his raising the dead from their biers and tombs? To what purpose was this? If it was for a mere display of power or for the present favor of restoring life, it was no very great thing for him to raise persons who would die again [Note: [Col. 0848C] “To be raised,” Rhen., Seml.].

But indeed, [Note: [Col. 0847D] “If rather to place the faith of the future resurrection in safekeeping.” *Sequestrare* means to deposit something with a custodian so that it may be secure. Hence the jurists’ expression “property placed in sequestration.” Explain the phrase, therefore, as meaning “to place faith in the future [Col. 0848C] resurrection in safekeeping”—that is, so to speak, to certify it and secure it in safety. This word also occurs above, unless I am mistaken. Moreover, when he says shortly afterward, “lest he seem to have presented the proofs in one form but deposited the reality in another,” he explains what he previously meant by “placing the faith in safekeeping.” RHEN.—We must not depart from Rhenanus’s interpretation. I add only that a *sequester* is a person with whom several parties deposit the same disputed object; Digest 50.16.209. Yet the observation of the grammarians who derive the word from *sequi*, “to follow,” appears pointless in this legal text. In Basil it is called μεσογενήτης, “an intermediary,” and the objects are the disputed property. LE PR.] if it was rather to place faith in the future resurrection in safekeeping, then by the form of its own evidence that resurrection is prescribed as bodily. Nor shall I tolerate those who say that the resurrection, though destined at that time for the soul alone, was anticipated [Note: [Col. 0848C] “Had preceded,” Rig., Ven.] in the flesh as well because the resurrection of an invisible soul could not otherwise have been shown except through the raising of a visible [Note: [Col. 0848C] “Invisible,” Rig., Ven.] substance. [Col. 0848C]

They know God badly who do not think that he can do what they themselves do not think possible; and yet they know that he could have done so, if [Note: [Col. 0848D] “They know John’s instrument.” Among the masters of law, *instruments* are public documents by which proof is furnished in the absence of the persons concerned. Likewise John’s Gospel is called an “instrument,” because it hands down, proves, and teaches the mysteries of religion; for *instrumentum* is derived from *instruere*, “to instruct”—Digest, *On Production of Documents*, “The Banker,” § “He Shall Produce.” It is customary for Tertullian to call Sacred Scripture an “instrument,” as in chapter 2 of *On the Flesh of Christ*: “So many original instruments”; and several times in this book, in the following book, and elsewhere. LE PR.] they know John’s instrument. For he who submitted to sight the souls of the martyrs still resting alone beneath the altar could certainly also have presented those souls rising without flesh to the eyes. [Col. 0849A]

But I prefer to believe that God cannot deceive—that deception alone is a weakness in him—lest he seem to have presented the proofs in one form but arranged the reality in another; indeed, lest, if he was unable to provide an example of resurrection without flesh, he should be even less able to display the fullness of the example in the same substance. No example, however, is greater than that of which it is the example. Yet it would be greater [Note: [Col. 0849C] “Would have been,” Wouwer manuscript.] if souls were to be raised together with bodies [Note: [Col. 0849C] “Were raised,” Wouwer manuscript.] as evidence of a resurrection without bodies, so that the salvation of the whole human being supported the case for the salvation of half—whereas the nature of examples would instead require that which would be regarded as the lesser thing—I mean the resurrection of the soul alone—as a foretaste of the flesh that would also [Note: [Col. 0849C] “Also” is absent from Fran.] rise in its own time.

And so [Note: [Col. 0849C] “For this reason,” Wouwer manuscript.], according to our assessment of the truth [Note: [Col. 0849C] “True,” Ven.; [Col. 0849D] “Opinion,” Fran., Pam.], those examples [Col. 0849B] of dead persons raised by the Lord did indeed commend the resurrection both of flesh and of soul, lest this gift be denied to either substance. Yet those examples displayed something less—for they were raised not to glory and incorruptibility, but to another death—than Christ displayed.

## CHAPTER XXXIX

The apostolic instruments also testify to the resurrection. For the Apostles had no other task, at least among Israel, than [Note: [Col. 0849D] “Unsealing the Old Testament and sealing the New.” An excellent metaphor indicating that the Apostles explained the Old Testament and confirmed the New. For a testament is opened by breaking its seal and is established by sealing it. The labor of apostolic preaching was therefore the exposition of the Old Testament and the confirmation of the New. LAC.] to unseal the Old Testament and to seal the New—or rather, by then, to proclaim God in Christ. Thus they introduced [Note: [Col. 0850C] “Had introduced,” Rhen.] nothing new about the resurrection either, except that they also proclaimed it [Col. 0849C] to the glory of Christ. In all other respects it had already been received in a simple and familiar faith, [Note: [Col. 0849D] “Without any question about its character.” That is, without any controversy over what kind it would be—namely, whether of the body or of the soul. For the dispute with the Sadducees did not concern which resurrection was to occur, that of the soul or that of the flesh, as the Author argues against the heretics, but the reality of resurrection, which the Sadducees denied absolutely and entirely. LAC.] without any question about its character, with the Sadducees alone opposing it. So much easier was it for the resurrection of the dead to be denied altogether than to be understood in a different sense.

You have Paul, before the chief priests and under the tribune, professing his faith among Sadducees and Pharisees: “Men and brothers,” he says, “I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees; it is concerning the hope and the resurrection that I am now judged before you.” He certainly means the resurrection common to them all, lest, because he now appeared to be a transgressor of the law, he should be thought to agree with the Sadducees concerning the chief article of the entire faith—that is, the resurrection. Thus he certainly confirmed the faith of the resurrection according to the Pharisees, which he did not wish [Col. 0850A] to appear to abolish, rejecting the Sadducees who denied it.

Similarly, before Agrippa he says that he puts forward nothing beyond what the prophets had proclaimed. Therefore he preserved the resurrection too in the form in which the prophets had proclaimed it. For when he recalled that the resurrection of the dead was written of in Moses, he knew it to be bodily—the resurrection in which, namely, the blood of a human being will have to be required. Thus he preached it in the form in which the Pharisees had accepted it, the Lord himself had defended it, and the Sadducees, lest they believe it even in that form, had wished it not to exist at all.

Nor had the Athenians understood Paul to be presenting any other kind of resurrection. In fact, they ridiculed it; they certainly would not have ridiculed him if they had heard from him of the restoration of the soul alone, for they would have accepted an assumption commonly held in their native philosophy. [Col. 0850B]

But when the proclamation of the resurrection—previously unheard of—shook the nations by its very novelty, and an unbelief commensurate with so great a reality began to put faith to the torture with questions [Note: [Col. 0850C] “To extort faith,” Rhen., Seml.], then the Apostle too [Note: [Col. 0849D] “Throughout almost the entire instrument.” He means the Epistles of blessed Paul, as Rhenanus testifies, in which powerful proofs of the resurrection are offered. You see that in this chapter he calls Scripture an “instrument” more than once. LE PR.] took care throughout almost the entire instrument to strengthen faith in this hope, showing that it exists, that it has not yet been accomplished, that it is bodily—which was the principal question—and, what was additionally doubted, that it is bodily in no other manner.
