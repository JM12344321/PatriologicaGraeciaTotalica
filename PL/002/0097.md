Deeply [Note: [Col. 0916D] “By you” (*A te*) in the printed editions, MSS., Wouwer, Paris.] and firmly, I say, one must reflect upon God’s pleasure—[Col. 0917A] what he wills even in secret. For what he wills openly [Note: [Col. 0917C] “For openly” (*Enim manifesto*), Rhenanus, Semler.] we all know; and one must examine precisely how those very things are openly willed. For although [Note: [Col. 0917C] “For if” (*Nam si*), ibid.] certain things seem to savor of God’s will because they are permitted by God, not everything that is permitted proceeds immediately from the pure and entire will of him who permits it. [Note: [Col. 0917D] It is by indulgence that anything is permitted. He is evidently distorting the Apostle’s passage, 1 Cor. 7: “But I say this by way of indulgence, not by way of command.” He does the same in *On Monogamy*, ch. 3. LE PR.] Whatever is permitted is so by indulgence. Although indulgence is not without an act of will, nevertheless, because it has some cause in the person [Note: [Col. 0917C] “In the place” (*In loco*), ibid.] to whom it is granted, it proceeds as though from an unwilling will: the will has admitted a cause for itself, and that cause constrains it. Consider the nature of a will whose cause lies in someone else. A second kind [Note: [Col. 0917C] “A second kind,” Rhenanus, Semler.] of will must likewise be considered—one that is not [Note: [Col. 0917C] “Not” is absent from the same editions.] pure. God wills us to do certain things pleasing to him, [Note: [Col. 0917C] “God wills us to do certain pleasing things,” Rhenanus, Semler.] in which indulgence does not provide support [Note: [Col. 0917C] “Indulgence operates,” the same editions.], but discipline rules. Yet if he has placed other things before these [Note: [Col. 0917C] “Sets these before,” the same editions.]—things which he assuredly wills more—[Col. 0917B] can there be any doubt that we must pursue [Note: [Col. 0917C] “Must be assessed,” the same editions.] the things he prefers? For the things he wills less, because [Note: [Col. 0917C] “Who wills other things more,” the same editions.] he wills other things more, are likewise [Note: [Col. 0917C] “Consequently must be regarded,” the same editions.] to be regarded as though he did not will them. [Note: [Col. 0917D] Semler omits “as though he alone.”] For by showing [Note: [Col. 0917D] “I show,” Semler; “by showing,” Pamelius, Wouwer.] what he wills more, he has effaced the lesser will by the greater. And insofar as he has set both before your knowledge, he has thereby determined that you must pursue what he has declared [Note: [Col. 0917D] “Prepared you,” Semler.] himself to will more. Therefore, if he declared it so that [Note: [Col. 0917D] Wouwer corrects the whole passage from a manuscript thus: “It has some cause in the person to whom—Consider how great that will is—it must be considered as not belonging to a pure will. God wills us to do things pleasing to himself, in which—Yet if also other things—he wills more. Nor is it doubtful that we must assess the things he wills, since the things he wills less, because he wills others more, must be regarded just as though he did not will them. For by showing.”] you might pursue what he wills more, then unquestionably, unless you do so, your inclination is contrary to his will, because it is contrary to his stronger will; and you offend him more than you earn his favor [Note: [Col. 0917D] Rhenanus and Semler place punctuation after *promereris* (“earn favor”). They then read what follows continuously with [Col. 0918C] “And by rejecting in part.”]: you do what he wills, certainly, but reject what he prefers. In part you sin; and in the part in which you do not sin, nevertheless you earn no favor. Moreover, to refuse to earn favor is itself to sin. Therefore, if a second marriage derives from that will of God which is called [Col. 0917C] indulgence [Note: [Col. 0918C] “Is compelled,” Rhenanus, Semler.], we shall deny [Note: [Col. 0918C] “We denied,” the same editions.] that this is a pure will [Note: [Col. 0918C] “Will” is absent from Rhenanus.], since indulgence is its cause. If it derives from that will before which another, stronger will is set—the will that continence should be more eagerly desired—we shall have learned that the weaker is annulled [Note: [Col. 0918C] “Divided,” Rhenanus.] by the stronger.

I have briefly outlined these points so that I may now run through the Apostle’s statements. First of all, I shall not appear irreligious [Col. 0918A] if I observe what he himself professes: that he introduced the whole indulgence concerning marriage from his own judgment—that is, from human understanding—not from a divine ordinance. For when he laid down concerning widows and [Note: [Col. 0918C] “And” is absent from Rhenanus.] the unmarried that they should marry if they cannot exercise self-control, because it is better to marry than to burn, he turned to [Note: [Col. 0918C] “Another,” Rhenanus, Semler, Pamelius.] the other category and said: “But to the married I give notice—not I, indeed, but the Lord” (1 Cor. 7:10). Thus, by transferring [Note: [Col. 0918C] “Outside reason,” Rhenanus, Semler.] the speaking person from himself to the Lord, he showed that what he had said above—“It is better to marry [Note: [Col. 0918C] The impersonal *nubi*, Rhenanus, Paris.] than to burn”—had been pronounced not in the Lord’s person but in his own. Although this statement concerns those whom the faith finds unmarried [Note: [Col. 0918C] “Married,” Rhenanus.] or widowed, [Note: [Col. 0918D] “Widows,” Rhenanus, Semler; the words from “by the faith” are absent from the same editions.] nevertheless, because [Note: [Col. 0918D] “Who,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.] everyone embraces it as a license to [Note: [Col. 0918D] “To” is absent from the same editions.] marry, I should like to examine [Note: [Col. 0918D] “Reconsider,” Rigault.] what sort of good it shows to be better than [Col. 0918B] punishment. [Note: [Col. 0917D] What kind of good does he show to be better than punishment? It is entirely certain that in those early times virginity was valued more highly, and that marriages, if compared with it, appeared to be a kind of evil, though in reality they were not. Apart from the praiseworthy continence of that age, the basis of this opinion was the Apostle’s passage, “It is better to marry than to burn.” Those ancients explained it as though Paul had said that it was better to lack one eye than two. Blessed Jerome himself, who had sharpened his pen against Jovinian because, among his other delusions, he assigned equal merit to virginity [Col. 0919D] and marriage, sometimes appears to follow this author’s opinion. Yet in his letter on monogamy to Ageruchia he admits that second and even third marriages, if necessary, were conceded by the Apostle to the incontinent. LE PR.]

That cannot appear good except in comparison with what is worst, so that marriage is good only because burning is worse. A thing is good as such [Note: [Col. 0918D] “If it possesses this name in itself,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler.] if it continues to possess the name without comparison—not merely with an evil, but even with another good—so that, even if it is compared with another good and overshadowed [Note: [Col. 0918D] “Is compared and overshadowed by it,” Wouwer’s codex.] by that other, it nevertheless retains the name of good. But if it is compelled to be called good by comparison with evil [Note: [Col. 0918D] “By comparison with evil,” Semler, Rhenanus.], it is not so much a good as a lower species of evil, which, because it is obscured by a greater evil, is forced to assume the name of good. [Note: [Col. 0918D] The same editions read: “is forced, and remove the expression of comparison.”] Remove, then, the condition of comparison, so that you do not say, “It is better to marry than to burn,” and I ask whether you would dare to say simply, “It is better to marry,” without adding what it is better than. Therefore, what is not “better” is surely not “good” either, because you have taken away and removed the condition of comparison which, [Col. 0918C] by making it “better,” compels it to be regarded [Note: [Col. 0918D] “Thus good, thus compels one to regard it,” the same editions.] as good.

“It is better to marry than to burn” must be understood in the same way [Note: [Col. 0918D] “As if,” the same editions.] as “It is better to lack one eye than two.” Yet if you leave the comparison aside, possessing one eye will not be better [Note: [Col. 0918D] “Nor good,” the same editions.], because it is not good. Let no one, therefore, seize upon this passage as a defense, [Col. 0919A] for it properly concerns the unmarried and widows [Note: [Col. 0919D] “The married and widows,” Rhenanus, Semler.], for whom no union has yet been counted. Even so, I have shown that they too must understand [Note: [Col. 0919D] “Must count.”] the condition attached to the permission.

## CHAPTER IV

As for a second marriage, however, we know perfectly well that the Apostle pronounced: “Are you released from a wife? Do not seek a wife; but if you marry, you do not sin.” Yet he likewise introduces the tenor of this statement as his own counsel, not as a divine command. There is a great difference between God’s command and a man’s counsel. “I have,” he says, “no command from the Lord; but I give counsel, as one who has obtained mercy from the Lord to be trustworthy.” Moreover, neither in the Gospel nor in Paul’s own epistles will you find remarriage permitted by God’s command. From this [Col. 0919B] it is confirmed that one marriage must be maintained, because what is not found to have been permitted by the Lord is merely pardoned.

Add that this very intervention of human counsel, as though it had already reflected upon its own excess, immediately checks and recalls itself when he adds: “Nevertheless, such people will have affliction of the flesh”; when he says that he is sparing them; when he adds that the time has been drawn short [Note: [Col. 0919D] “Is in a shortened state,” Rhenanus, Semler, Pamelius.], in which even those who have marriages ought to behave as though they had none; and when he compares the anxieties of the married and the unmarried. [Note: [Col. 0919D] The words “and of the unmarried” are absent there.] For by teaching through these statements why it is not expedient to marry, he discourages what he had permitted above. And this concerns a first marriage; how much more a second? [Note: [Col. 0919D] “But when he exhorts us to follow his own example.” This passage also supports the continence of the blessed Apostle Paul from marriage. PAMELIUS.]

But when he exhorts us to his own example, he plainly shows what he wills us to be—namely, continent—and equally declares what he does not will us to be, [Col. 0919C] namely, incontinent. [Note: [Col. 0919D] These final words are absent from Rhenanus and Semler.] Thus, when he wills one thing, even he does not permit what he does not will either voluntarily or in truth. For if he willed it, he would not have permitted it; indeed, he would have commanded it.

[Note: [Col. 0920D] “But behold, once again he says that a woman may marry after her husband’s death.” Here the author still proceeds with sufficient moderation: he does not absolutely prohibit second marriage, but proves monogamy happier. Hence blessed Jerome too admits that, in these words, the Apostle concedes second and third marriages in the Lord. PAMELIUS.]

But behold, once again he says (1 Cor. 7) that, after her husband’s death, a woman may marry whomever she wishes, provided that it is in the Lord. “But she will be happier,” he says, “if she remains as she is, according to my counsel. And I think that I too have the Spirit of God.” We see two counsels: the one above, by which he grants permission to marry, and the later one, by which he enjoins the continence that abstains from marriage. [Note: [Col. 0919D] “Introduces,” according to others.] Which, then, you ask, shall we follow? Look and read. When he grants permission, he alleges the counsel of a prudent man. When he enjoins continence, he affirms the counsel of the Holy Spirit. Follow the admonition that has divinity as its advocate.

[Col. 0920A] The faithful, certainly, also possess the Spirit of God, but not all the faithful are Apostles. Therefore, after the man who had called himself trustworthy [Note: [Col. 0919D] “Because he had called him trustworthy,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler.] subsequently added that he possessed the Spirit of God—a fact no one would doubt even concerning an ordinary believer—he said this so as to restore to himself the dignity of an Apostle. For the Apostles possess the Holy Spirit in a distinctive manner: in works of prophecy, in the efficacy of miracles, and in demonstrations of tongues, [Note: [Col. 0919D] “And demonstrations of a tongue.”] not [Note: [Col. 0919D] Rhenanus adds “as though.”] partially, as others do. Thus he caused the authority of the Holy Spirit to attach to [Note: [Col. 0920D] “By authority to it,” Rhenanus, Semler.] the position which he wished us more especially to obey; and it became no longer the counsel of the divine Spirit but, in keeping with his majesty, a command.

## CHAPTER V

The very origin of the human race supports the establishment [Note: [Col. 0920D] “I shall establish,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.] of the law of marrying once, bearing witness to what God [Col. 0920B] established in the beginning [Note: [Col. 0920D] “From the beginning,” the same editions.] as a pattern to be followed [Note: [Col. 0920D] “To be reviewed,” others.] by posterity.

For when he had fashioned [Note: [Col. 0920D] Others write “molded as a potter” (*figulasset*).] the man and had foreseen that he needed a corresponding companion, he borrowed one of his ribs and fashioned one woman for him. Certainly neither the craftsman nor the material had failed: there were more ribs in Adam and tireless hands in God, but there were not to be more wives in God’s sight. Accordingly, Adam, the man of God, and Eve, the woman of God, fulfilled one marriage with each other. [Note: [Col. 0920D] “Fulfilled one marriage as a pattern for the people of God, etc.,” Rigault.] From the authority of their origin and God’s first will, they established a pattern for the people of God. Finally, “The two,” he says, “will become one flesh”—not three or four. Otherwise there would no longer be one flesh, nor two in one flesh. They will then [Note: [Col. 0920D] “There will no longer be one flesh; they will then be so, if the union, etc.,” Junius.] be two in one flesh if union and fusion into unity occur once. But if this occurs again [Col. 0920C] or repeatedly, it has ceased to be a unity: there will not be two in one flesh, but plainly one rib [Note: [Col. 0920D] “Flesh,” Semler, Rhenanus.] distributed among several.

Since the Apostle interprets the words “the two will become one flesh” as referring to the Church and Christ, according to the spiritual marriage of the Church and Christ—for there is one Christ and one Church belonging to him—we must recognize that the law [Note: [Col. 0920D] “Law” is absent from Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.] of one marriage has been doubled and intensified for us, both according to the foundation of the race and according to the mystery of Christ. In both respects we are registered under one marriage: carnally in Adam and spiritually in Christ. The rule of monogamy is one for our two births. Whoever departs from monogamy degenerates in both. A plurality of marriages began with a man under a curse. Lamech, the first to be married to two women, made three into one flesh.

## CHAPTER VI

[Col. 0921A]

“But,” you say, “even the blessed [Note: [Col. 0921C] “But even the blessed Patriarchs,” he says. In *To the Martyrs*: “Blessed martyrs designated among the nourishment of the flesh.” You also have this usage in Book IV of the *Life of Blessed Martin*: [Col. 0921D] “And the urn of the blessed martyr Fortunatus.” LE PR.] Patriarchs mingled marriage not merely with several wives but even with concubines.” Therefore shall it also be permissible for us [Note: [Col. 0921D] “To marry without numerical limit.” Elsewhere he said, “To make manifold use of marriages.” RIGAULT.] to marry without limit? Certainly it will be permissible [Note: [Col. 0921D] “If the types—the mysteries of some future reality—still remain.” Thus the exemplar reads, and Jerome reads no differently in his letter to Gerontia on monogamy. He calls types or figures “mysteries of future things,” sacred signs sent beforehand by God as signs of some future reality. RIGAULT.] if types still remain as mysteries of some future reality for your marriages to represent; or if there is still room for that declaration, “Increase and multiply”—that is, if another declaration has not yet supervened: “The time has now been drawn short; it remains that even those who have wives should behave as though they had none.” For by enjoining continence and restraining sexual intercourse, the seedbed of the race, he abolished [Note: [Col. 0921C] “Allows to become obsolete,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.] that command to increase and multiply.

In my judgment, both declarations and both arrangements belong to one and the same God. At the beginning he released the seed of the race, loosening the reins of marriage until the world should be filled, [Col. 0921B] until the material for the new discipline should mature. But now, at the end of the ages, he has restrained what he had released and recalled what he had granted—not without reason for the extension at the beginning and the pruning [Note: [Col. 0921C] “Renewed digging,” the same editions.] at the end. Beginnings are always given latitude; ends are drawn tight. [Note: [Col. 0921C] “Ends are drawn tight” is absent from the Venetian edition.] A person plants a forest and allows it to grow for the very purpose of cutting it down at the proper time. The forest will be the old dispensation, which is assigned to the new Gospel, in which also (Matt. 21) the axe has been laid at the root of the tree. Thus too, “an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth” has grown old since “let no one repay evil for evil” (Rom. 12:17) grew young. I think that even among human enactments and decrees, later provisions [Note: [Col. 0921C] “Subsequent,” Rhenanus, Semler, Leopolde.] prevail over earlier ones.

## CHAPTER VII

[Col. 0921C]

Why, then, should we not instead recognize among the earlier examples [Col. 0922A] those that agree with the later ones [Note: [Col. 0921C] “Subsequent ones,” Rhenanus.] and transmit from antiquity to the new order both discipline and pattern? For behold, in the old law I observe that permission to marry repeatedly was curtailed. It was provided in Leviticus: “My priests shall not marry more than once” (Lev. 21:14). [Note: [Col. 0921D] I can say that anything which is not once is “more.” For “once,” as Asconius notes in his commentary on the second *Against Verres*, has the same sense when, he says, Lucilius writes that Crassus laughed once in his life and Cicero that Verres was terrified once in his life. RIGAULT.] I can say that anything which is not once is “more.” What is not one is a number. Indeed, number begins after one. Everything that occurs once, however, is one.

But the fullness of the law, in this matter as in the rest, was being reserved for Christ. [Note: [Col. 0921C] “For Christ alone,” Frankfurt, Paris, Gelenius; “in Christ,” Semler.] Accordingly, among us it is prescribed more fully and completely [Note: [Col. 0921C] “More structurally,” Rhenanus, Semler; “more strictly,” Frankfurt, Latin edition.] that those selected [Note: [Col. 0921C] Thus Rigault; Rhenanus’s edition: “whom he may select”; the Frankfurt edition: “whom it is lawful to select”; Wouwer’s codex: “whom it is proper to select into holy orders.” Leopolde conjectures that one should perhaps read: “whom they select into the priestly order.”] for the priestly order must be men of one marriage (Titus 1:6). I even remember some twice-married men being removed from office. But you will say, “Then it is permissible for the others, since he singles out those [Note: [Col. 0921C] Rhenanus: “It is permitted, since he does not single out those for whom it is not permitted—vain”; Pamelius: “It is permitted, since he singled out those for whom it is not permitted”; Paris has an abbreviated variant.] for whom it is not.” We shall be foolish [Col. 0922B] if we suppose that what is not lawful for priests is lawful for laypeople. Are not we laypeople also priests? It is written: “He has also made us a kingdom and priests to his God and Father” (Rev. 1:6).

The Church’s authority established the distinction between the order and the people, and the honor was sanctified through the order’s assembly. Accordingly, [Note: [Col. 0922C] “Accordingly,” Rhenanus; “sanctified; therefore, where,” Wouwer’s codex; “accordingly, where,” Frankfurt, Paris, Venetian edition; “by God,” Pamelius, Semler; “sanctified. Accordingly,” Leopolde.] [Note: [Col. 0921D] “Where there is no assembly of the ecclesiastical order.” In *On Baptism* he said that this order consists of bishops, presbyters, and deacons: accordingly, deacons are placed in the third degree of priesthood, presbyters in the second, [Col. 0922C] and bishops at the summit as chiefs of all. Hence bishops are also called high priests, and simply priests. Presbyters, however, are second priests, or, as in Sidonius, [Col. 0922D] prelates in the second order. Finally, laypeople—whether called commoners or the people—are all the others in the Church who are not yet supported by any dignity, as Optatus of Milevis says in Book I; in Book II he also subjects ministers to deacons. “The Church,” he says, “has its definite members: bishops, presbyters, deacons, ministers, and the multitude of the faithful.” The one who presided over both the multitude and the assembly was called by the general term “president,” “presiding officer,” or “one set over them”—Greek *ho proestōs* (ὁ προεστώς). RIGAULT.] where there is no assembly of the ecclesiastical order, you both offer and baptize, and you are a priest for yourself alone. [Note: [Col. 0922D] “You both offer and baptize, and you are a priest for yourself alone.” The Agobard exemplar reads exactly thus. These words appear obscure to those who do not observe what Tertullian usually signifies by the verb “to offer” and by the terms “offerings,” “prayers,” “sacrifices,” and, finally, “sacraments.” All these matters may be gathered from the *Index of Glosses*. RIGAULT.] Yet where there are three, there is a church, even though they are laypeople. For each person lives [Note: [Col. 0922C] “By his faith,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler.] by his own faith, and there is no partiality with God, because it is not the hearers of the law who are justified [Note: [Col. 0922D] “Will be justified,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.] by God but its doers, as the Apostle also says (Rom. 2:11, 13).

Therefore, if you possess a priest’s right within yourself when necessity requires it, you must also possess a priest’s discipline when it is necessary to possess a priest’s right. [Note: [Col. 0922D] “You, a twice-married man, baptize!” Those who had married twice were not admitted by the Church to the priesthood; nor does the Church allow those who have “taken a widow” (*chēran labōn*, χήραν λαβών; the printed Greek, χήραν λαβίον, is corrupt) to preside, this same author says in Book I of *To His Wife*, ch. 7. LE PR.] [Col. 0922C] Do you, a twice-married man, baptize? Do you, a twice-married man, offer? [Note: [Col. 0922C] “Will you baptize? Will you offer?” Wouwer’s codex.] How much more capital an offense is it for a twice-married layman to act as a priest, [Note: [Col. 0922C] The first edition of Rhenanus is corrupt here; Frankfurt and Pamelius offer other readings.] [Col. 0923A] when even a priest, upon becoming twice married, is deprived of performing the priestly office! [Note: [Col. 0923C] The first edition of Rhenanus read: “to the priest it is said that by this act he is admitted to communion” (for which the Latin edition substituted “is excommunicated”) “from priestly action”; Pamelius read: “who by that act is admitted to communion with priestly action.”]

“But,” you say, “allowance is made for necessity.” No necessity is excused when it is capable of not existing. Take care, then, not to be found twice married, and you will not expose yourself to the necessity of administering [Note: [Col. 0923C] “Those administering under necessity,” Frankfurt.] what is not lawful for a twice-married man. God wills [Note: [Col. 0923C] “Willed,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.] all of us to be so ordered that we may everywhere be fit to perform his sacraments. [Note: [Col. 0923C] “To obey his ordinance,” others; “to obey his ordinances,” Pamelius.] One God, one faith, and one discipline. [Note: [Col. 0923C] Thus Rigault edited the passage; the others either omitted it or preserved nothing sound.] Indeed, unless laypeople too observe the qualifications by which presbyters are selected, how will there be presbyters, since presbyters are selected from the laity? Therefore, we must contend [Note: [Col. 0923C] “Endure,” Rhenanus, Frankfurt, Paris; “prefer,” Pamelius, Frankfurt; “bring forward,” Wouwer’s codex.] that the layman is commanded beforehand to abstain from a second marriage [Note: [Col. 0923C] “Retain,” Pamelius, Semler; “restrain,” Wouwer’s codex.], since no one can become a presbyter except a layman who [Note: [Col. 0923C] “Who” is absent from Semler.] has been the husband of one wife. [Note: [Col. 0923C] “Has married once,” Rhenanus, Semler, Pamelius.]

## CHAPTER VIII

[Col. 0923B] Let it now be lawful to marry again, if everything lawful is good. The same Apostle cries out: “All things are lawful, but not all things are beneficial” [Note: [Col. 0923D] “For salvation,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler.] (1 Cor. 6:12). I ask you: can what is not beneficial be called good? If even things that do not conduce to salvation are lawful, then things that are not good are also lawful. What, however, should you desire more: that which is good because [Note: [Col. 0924C] “Therefore,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.] it is lawful, or that which is good because [Note: [Col. 0924C] “Therefore,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.] it is beneficial? I consider the distance between license and salvation [Note: [Col. 0924C] “Utility,” Venetian edition; the others read “salvation.”] to be great.

One does not say of a good thing, [Note: [Col. 0924C] “You do not prevail, namely,” Rhenanus.] “It is lawful,” because a good thing does not wait to be permitted but to be embraced. What is permitted is something whose goodness is in doubt—something that could also be forbidden, were there not some prior cause for permitting it. A second marriage is permitted because of the danger of incontinence; for unless permission to do some thing that is not good lay before us, there would be no circumstance in which it could be proved [Col. 0923C] who obeyed the divine will and who obeyed his own power, [Note: [Col. 0923D] “Which of us pursues the presence of utility.” Perhaps *praestantiam* (“superior advantage”), a word he uses elsewhere when he means what is more advantageous. RIGAULT.] which of us pursued the immediate advantage [Note: [Col. 0924C] Perhaps “superior advantage,” Rigault.], and which embraced the opportunity furnished by license.

License is frequently a trial of discipline, because discipline is proved through trial, and trial operates through license. Thus it comes about that all things are lawful but not all are expedient: the person to whom permission is given is tested, and he is judged [Note: [Col. 0924C] Wouwer’s manuscript: “the one to whom it is permitted is also conquered.”] while being tested through the permission.

[Note: [Col. 0923D] It was lawful even for the Apostles to marry and take wives about with them. They had them as sisters, as Clement of Alexandria teaches in *Stromata* III. Hence the effort of the Lutherans, who attempt to establish clerical marriage from Tertullian, vanishes into smoke. After the Magdeburg Centuriators, Callistus, a distinguished Lutheran, undertook to defend this position and published a very large book on clerical marriage. Yet Socrates and Sozomen fall into error when they say that the Council of Nicaea left marriage free to clerics. How false this is appears from its third canon: *Apēgoreusen katholou hē megalē Synodos mēte episkopō, mēte presbyterō, mēte diakonō, mēte holōs tini tōn en klērō exeinai syneisakton echein; plēn ei mē ara mētera ē adelphēn, ē theian, ē ha mona prosōpa pasan hypopsian diapephygen* (ἀπηγόρευσεν καθόλου ἡ μεγάλη Σύνοδος μήτε ἐπισκόπῳ, μήτε πρεσβυτέρῳ, μήτε διακόνῳ, μήτε ὅλως τινὶ τῶν ἐν κλήρῳ ἐξεῖναι συνείσακτον ἔχειν· πλὴν εἰ μὴ ἄρα μητέρα ἢ ἀδελφήν, ἢ θείαν, ἢ ἃ μόνα πρόσωπα πᾶσαν ὑποψίαν διαπέφευγεν): “The great Synod absolutely forbade any bishop, presbyter, deacon, or anyone at all belonging to the clergy to keep a woman brought into his household, except perhaps a mother, sister, aunt, or only such persons as are beyond every suspicion.” You have the same in canon 3 of the Council of Carthage and, from civil law, in the law *Sacris*, canon 45, *Concerning Bishops and Clerics*. LE PR.]

It was lawful even for the Apostles to marry and take wives about with them (1 Cor. 6:12); it was also lawful to live from the Gospel. But the one who did not use this right as an opportunity calls us to follow his example, teaching that the test lies precisely where permission has laid the groundwork for an experiment in abstinence. [Note: [Col. 0924C] “Laid the groundwork for abstinence,” Junius; “will have laid the groundwork,” Wouwer’s codex.]

## CHAPTER IX

If we interpret his meaning thoroughly, a second marriage will have to be called nothing other than a species of fornication. For when he says that husbands have this concern—how they may please themselves—he assuredly does not mean in respect of character, for he would not censure a good concern. Rather, he wishes them to be understood as concerned with dress, adornment, and every effort of beauty calculated to arouse [Note: [Col. 0924C] “Contrive,” Semler, Venetian edition.] enticements. But since pleasing by beauty and adornment belongs to the character of carnal desire, which is also the cause of fornication, does a second marriage not seem to you akin to fornication, since I find in it the features that belong to fornication?

The Lord himself says: “Whoever looks upon a woman in order to desire her has already committed fornication with her in his heart” (Matt. 5:28). But he who looks upon her in order to marry her—has he done less [Note: [Col. 0924D] “Has he done less or more?” The exemplar reads, “has done less more.” Perhaps the particle *an* (“or”) coalesced with the following word through similarity of sound: *minus an plus fecit*.] or more? [Note: [Col. 0924C] “Has done less completely,” Frankfurt, Paris; “has done something less equal,” Rhenanus.] What if he has actually married her? He would not have done so unless he had desired her with a view to marrying her and had looked upon her with a view to desiring her—unless, perhaps, a wife can be married whom you have neither seen nor desired. [Note: [Col. 0924C] “Even if you have not seen her, at least you will have desired her when you begin to marry her,” Rhenanus, Paris, and others.]

There is certainly a great difference whether a married or unmarried man desires another woman. [Note: [Col. 0924C] “A husband who is not desiring out of jealousy,” Frankfurt, Paris; “certainly a husband is far removed from jealousy,” Pamelius.] [Col. 0924C] Every woman is “another woman” even to an unmarried man [Note: [Col. 0924C] “A woman has a jealous husband,” Pamelius, Paris, Frankfurt.] so long as she belongs to another; and yet she becomes a wife through no other act than that by which she also becomes an adulteress. The laws appear to distinguish marriage from fornication through a difference in lawfulness, not through the nature [Col. 0925A] of the act itself. Otherwise, what act [Note: [Col. 0925C] “What” is absent from Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler.] is equally present for all men and women [Note: [Col. 0925C] “The same thing imposes; for it is also marriage,” Frankfurt, Paris, Pamelius.] in marriage and fornication? The union of the flesh, of course, whose desire the Lord [Note: [Col. 0925C] “Certainly we must contend that its desire,” Pamelius.] equated with fornication.

“Then,” you say, “you are now destroying even first—that is, single—marriages.” Not without reason, since they too consist of the same act as fornication. Therefore it is best for a man not to touch a woman; and therefore the preeminent sanctity of virginity, because it lacks affinity with fornication. And if these arguments can be advanced in favor of continence even against first and single marriages, how much more decisively will they prejudge the case for rejecting a second marriage! [Note: [Col. 0925C] “Will be foreseen,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.]

Be grateful if God has granted you permission to marry once; you will be grateful, however, if you do not suppose that he has granted you permission a second time. Otherwise you abuse indulgence when you use it without moderation. “Moderation” (*modestia*) is understood from “measure” (*modus*). [Col. 0925B] Is it not enough for you to have fallen, by marrying, from that highest degree of immaculate [Note: [Col. 0925C] Rhenanus joined the words; he conjectured “pure”; Pamelius and Paris, “unmarried.”] virginity to the second degree? [Note: [Col. 0925C] “To have sinned,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.] Must you still tumble down to the third, the fourth, and perhaps to still more, after failing to remain continent at the second station? For if he hesitated to provoke people toward second marriages, he certainly did not intend to prohibit a greater number! Let us marry every day, then, and let us be found marrying at the last day, like Sodom and Gomorrah—the day on which that “Woe to those who are pregnant” will be fulfilled, [Note: [Col. 0925C] Rigault supplied this word.] that is, against the married and incontinent; for wombs, breasts, and infants arise from marriage. And when will there be an end of marrying? [Note: [Col. 0925D] “I believe, after the end of living.” Jerome read thus in his letter to Gerontia, not as in the commonly printed editions, “after the end”—with “of living” understood. “Let us neigh,” he says, “after every woman, and, following the example of Sodom and Gomorrah, let us be found on the last day selling and buying, marrying and giving in marriage, so that the end of marriage comes only when life reaches its limit.” RIGAULT.] I believe, after the end of living.

## CHAPTER X

Let us renounce carnal things, so that at last we may turn again to spiritual things. [Note: [Col. 0925C] “May bear fruit,” Rigault.] Seize the opportunity—even if it is not the most desirable one, nevertheless a suitable one—of having no one to whom you must pay the marital debt and no one by whom it must be paid to you. You have ceased to be a debtor. O happy man! You have released your debtor; endure the loss. [Note: [Col. 0925D] “A similar loss,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.] What if you experience as profit what we have called a loss? For through continence you will trade for a great wealth of sanctity; through economy in the flesh you will acquire the spirit. [Note: [Col. 0925D] “Of the subservient flesh,” Frankfurt, Paris; “for the raging flesh,” Junius.]

Let us consider our own consciousness—how different a person a man feels himself to be when, perhaps, he abstains from his own woman: his inclination becomes spiritual. If [Col. 0926A] he offers prayer to the Lord, he is close to heaven. If he applies himself to the Scriptures, he is wholly present there. If he sings a psalm, he is pleasing to himself. If he adjures a demon, he has confidence in himself.

[Note: [Col. 0925D] For this reason the Apostle added a temporary purification for the sake of making prayers acceptable. He means a religious truce from marital relations. Juvenal’s statement in the sixth satire testifies that something of this kind also existed [Col. 0926D] among the Gentiles:

> He begs forgiveness whenever his wife does not abstain  
> From intercourse on sacred days that must be observed.

RIGAULT.]

For this reason the Apostle added a temporary purification for the sake of making prayers acceptable, so that we might know that what is beneficial for a time [Note: [Col. 0925D] “He recommended prayer for purification, alleging as the reason what he had permitted for a time,” Pamelius, Paris, Frankfurt.] must always be practiced by us, so that it may always be beneficial. Daily [Note: [Col. 0926C] Wouwer’s codex: “Daily—indeed, if at every moment.” Rigault: “Daily—at every moment.” Others: “If daily, at every moment.”]—indeed, at every moment—prayer is necessary for human beings; therefore continence too is necessary, once prayer is necessary.

Prayer proceeds from the conscience. If the conscience blushes, [Note: [Col. 0926C] Wouwer’s codex: “If the conscience blushes.” Rigault: “Of a blushing conscience.”] prayer blushes. The spirit conducts prayer to God. If the spirit is guilty in its own judgment because of a blushing conscience, how will it dare to conduct prayer from that conscience, [Note: [Col. 0926C] Agobard’s codex: “to conduct prayer from another matter.” Rigault alone reports the words from “from which” to “minister.”] when its holy minister is himself put to shame by the conscience’s shame? For there is a prophetic declaration of the Old Testament: [Col. 0926B] “You shall be holy, because God is holy” (Lev. 11:44–45; 19:2; 1 Pet. 1:16); and again: “With the holy you will be made holy, and with the innocent man you will be innocent, and with the chosen you will be chosen” (Ps. 18:26).

For we must enter into the Lord’s discipline in a worthy manner, not according to the flesh’s burning desires. [Note: [Col. 0926C] Rhenanus and others preserve a corrupt reading: “so that a fruit worthy of God, not according to the flesh’s…” Rigault: “burning desires”; Paris perhaps: “filthy desires”; Junius likewise offers a conjecture.] For thus the Apostle too says that to be inclined according to the flesh is death, but to be inclined according to the spirit [Note: [Col. 0926D] “Is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.” After these words, a somewhat longer passage is found in a very ancient exemplar. It appears to have been rejected because of its excessive praises of that Montanist Prisca, whose words are also cited. Nevertheless, we reproduce it here, so that we may marvel all the more that so great a man could have been deceived even by such things. RIGAULT. [Col. 0927D] Rigault found the following inserted in his manuscript, and August Neander does not doubt that Tertullian himself wrote it: “Likewise, through the holy prophetess Prisca it is proclaimed as gospel that the holy minister should know how to minister holiness. ‘For purifying things are in harmony,’ she says, ‘and they see visions; and those who turn their faces downward also hear voices—manifest voices, as salutary as they are mysterious.’”] is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord. If this defilement [Note: [Col. 0926D] “Dulling,” Frankfurt, Paris, Pamelius.] turns away the Holy Spirit even when the activity of the flesh is exercised within a single marriage, how much more when it is exercised in a second marriage?

## CHAPTER XI

For the shame is twofold, because in a second marriage two wives stand around the same husband: one in spirit, the other in the flesh. For you will not be able to hate the former wife, toward whom you preserve an even more religious [Note: [Col. 0926D] “Dearer,” Junius; “more illustrious,” Frankfurt, Paris.] affection now that she has been received by the Lord—for whose spirit you pray, and for whom you render annual offerings.

Will you therefore stand before the Lord with as many wives as you commemorate in prayer? Will you offer for two? Will you commend [Note: [Col. 0926D] “Commemorate,” Frankfurt, Paris.] those two through a priest ordained on the basis of monogamy, or even consecrated on the basis of virginity, surrounded by virgins and persons married only once? And will your sacrifice [Col. 0927A] ascend with an unashamed countenance? And among the other desires of a well-disposed mind, will you ask for chastity for yourself and your wife?
