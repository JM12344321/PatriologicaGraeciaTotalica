I would also note the following concerning motion. For, according to the analogy of a pot, such, you say, was the motion of matter [Note: [Col. 0235D] “Will be,” Rhenanus, Semler], [Col. 0236A] before it was put into order: coagulated, restless, incomprehensible, because of the excessive violence of its struggle. Then you add: “But it came to rest for God’s ordering, and its ordered motion was incomprehensible because of the sluggishness of unordered motion” [Note: [Col. 0235D] “Unordered,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür]. Above, you ascribe struggle to motion [Note: [Col. 0235D] “To the motion,” Rhenanus and his two followers]; here, sluggishness. Take note [Note: [Col. 0236D] “You take”; above, “you judge,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] how often you fall away from your account of the nature of matter.

Above you say: “But if matter were evil by nature, it would not have admitted transformation into something better, nor would God have applied any ordering to it; for he would have labored in vain.” You have therefore settled two propositions: that matter was not evil by nature, and that its nature could not have been altered by God. Forgetful of these, you afterward infer [Note: [Col. 0236D] “Inserting,” Rhenanus, against all the manuscripts]: “But when it received ordering from God and was adorned, it ceased from its nature.” If it was reformed into something good, it was certainly reformed from something evil. And if [Col. 0236B] through God’s ordering it ceased from the nature of evil, then its nature ceased; its nature was therefore evil before that ordering, and after its reformation it was able to cease from its nature.

### Chapter XLIV

It remains for me to show how you represent God as having worked. You plainly depart from the philosophers, but also from the prophets. The Stoics hold that God permeated matter as honey runs through the comb. But you say: “No; he does not make the world by passing through matter, but merely by appearing and approaching it, just as beauty acts merely by appearing, and a magnetic stone merely by approaching.”

[Note: [Col. 0235D] “Just as beauty acts.” That is, some instance of beauty. But this reading comes from Rhenanus. Previously the text read: “Beauty, merely appearing, does something.” This is correct. Beauty, merely by appearing, does something—that is, it wounds the mind, as he will presently say. Lucretius, IV:

> But grant that she now possesses however great a beauty of countenance,  
> One in whose every limb the power of Venus is aroused, etc.

(Rigault).]

What resemblance is there between God’s fashioning the world and beauty’s wounding the mind, or a magnet’s attracting iron? For even if God appeared [Col. 0236C] to matter, he did not wound it as beauty wounds the soul; and if he approached it, he did not adhere to it as a magnet does to iron.

Suppose for the moment that your examples are applicable. Certainly, if by appearing and approaching matter God made the world from it, then he made it from the moment when he appeared and from the moment when he approached. Therefore, when previously he had not made it, he had neither appeared to it nor approached it. And to whom is it credible that God had not appeared to matter, which was co-substantial with him in eternity? Or that he had been far from it, whom we believe to be everywhere and to appear everywhere [Note: [Col. 0236D] “On every side,” Latinus]? Even inanimate and incorporeal things sing his praises in Daniel (Dan. III).

[Note: [Col. 0235D] **Inanimate and incorporeal things.** He alludes to the hymn of the three young men in Daniel 3, according to Theodotion’s edition; for St. Jerome correctly noted that it is not found in the Hebrew. St. Cyprian also cites the same hymn under the title “confession” [*exomologesis*] [Col. 0236D] in his books *On the Lapsed* and *On the Lord’s Prayer*. These passages should be noted in support of the canonical authority of that hymn, which the Church also accords it. In the Church it is sung in the sacred rites of the faithful not only now, on the Saturdays of the four Ember seasons, but, from the very beginning of the nascent Church, it was always sung on every solemn occasion. Augustine bears witness to this in Sermon 47, *On Time*—and he also expressly cites it in Letter 186, *On the Saturday Fast*—as does Rufinus, book 2, *Against Jerome*. Jerome replies to him that whatever he had written about it in his preface to Daniel was stated according to the opinion of the Hebrews. What then? The same Jerome, in his letter to Demetrias *On Preserving Virginity*, cites from the prefatory material to that hymn—which likewise is not found in the Hebrew—the statement that the fire blazed up forty-nine cubits. St. Augustine similarly cites from the same prologue, “For you are just in all that you have done to us,” in Letter 122. Pamelius.]

How vast, then, was that space [Col. 0237A] in which God stood so far apart from matter that before the construction of the world he neither appeared nor drew near to it? I suppose he journeyed to it from afar when he first wished to appear and approach it.

### Chapter XLV

But the prophets and apostles do not teach that the world was made by God in this fashion, through his merely appearing and approaching matter—for they never even named any matter. Rather, they teach that Wisdom [*Sophia*] was first created as the beginning [Note: [Col. 0237C] “Beginnings,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] of his ways for his works (Prov. 8:22); and thereafter that the Word was brought forth, through whom all things were made, and without whom nothing was made (John 1:3). Finally, “By his word the heavens were established, and by his spirit all their powers” (Ps. 33:6). This Word is God’s right hand, and Wisdom and the Word are the two hands through which he accomplished the things [Col. 0237B] that he fashioned [Note: [Col. 0237C] “And fashioned,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] (Isa. 48:13). For, he says, “The heavens are the works of your hands” (Ps. 102:26); by these hands he also measured the earth and the heaven with his palm [Note: [Col. 0237C] Rhenanus, and after him, as usual, Semler and Oberthür, read with inverted order: “the heaven, and the earth with his palm”] (Isa. 40:12).

Do not flatter God in such a way [Note: [Col. 0237C] “Not in such a way,” Rhenanus, Semler] that you would have him produced so many and such great substances by sight and approach alone, without establishing them by his own powers. For Jeremiah also commends him in this manner: “God, who makes the earth by his might, prepares the world by his understanding, and by his perception stretches out the heaven” [Note: [Col. 0237C] “The heavens,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] (Jer. 51:15; Ps. 64:7). These are his powers, by exerting which he created this entire universe. His glory is greater if he labored. Accordingly, on the seventh day he rested from his works (Gen. 2:2)—each act in his own manner.

Or, if he made this world merely by appearing and approaching, did he, when he ceased making it, again [Col. 0238A] cease to appear and approach? On the contrary, from the moment the world was made, God began to appear all the more and to be encountered everywhere. You see, therefore, how all things subsist through God’s operation [Note: [Col. 0237C] “He established all things,” Rhenanus, Semler]: through the might of him who makes the earth, the understanding of him who prepares the world, and the perception of him who stretches out the heaven—not of one who merely appears and approaches, but of one who employs such mighty exertions of his mind: wisdom [*sophia*], might, perception, word, spirit, and power. These would not have been necessary to him [Note: [Col. 0237C] “Will not be,” Rhenanus, Semler] if he had accomplished his purpose [Note: [Col. 0238C] Oberthür, with the same editors, reads “so that by appearing”; Latinus wishes “had been” to be absent] merely by appearing and approaching.

[Note: [Col. 0237C] In the phrase “the might of him who makes the earth,” etc., in Jeremiah 51, the Greek words are **τῇ ἰσχύϊ, τῇ σοφίᾳ, τῇ συνέσει**—“by might, by wisdom, by understanding.” Here and shortly afterward the author renders these as *valentia* (“might,” that is, strength), *intelligentia* (“understanding”), and *sensus* (“perception”). Pamelius.]

These, moreover, are his invisible attributes, which according to the apostle are perceived from the establishment of the world [Note: [Col. 0238C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür do not insert “from”] through the things he has made (Rom. 1:20) [Note: [Col. 0237C] “His invisible attributes,” etc., in place of: “The invisible attributes of God are seen, being understood through created things from the creation of the world.” Rom. 1. Le Prieur]—not the attributes of some matter or other, but his own capacities of perception. For who has known the mind of the Lord? [Col. 0238B] Concerning this he exclaims [Note: [Col. 0238C] The same editors read: “And he exclaims: O depth”]: “O depth of the riches and wisdom! How undiscoverable are his judgments and untraceable his ways!” (Rom. 11:33).

[Note: [Col. 0237D] **His undiscoverable judgments, and untraceable**, etc. Since all the copies we have seen agree persistently, I think *ininventibilia* (“undiscoverable”) was used just as one might say *incompetibilia*—namely, things that cannot be discovered and known, however much we strive; just as *investigabile* here signifies something that cannot be traced. We must remember that Tertullian is somewhat harsh in rendering Greek expressions, as we have noted in the preceding books. Perhaps, however, this word was once more commonly used. Attending to the force of the Greek term **ἀνεξερεύνητα**, we once thought that *inervibilia* should be read. Rhenanus.—“His undiscoverable judgments,” etc. The Greek has **ἀνεξερεύνητα**, that is, inaccessible and incomprehensible, as the Vulgate reads. Moreover, it makes little difference whether one reads “traceable” or “untraceable” [Col. 0238D], for either can represent the Greek **ἀνεξιχνίαστοι**. Le Prieur.]

What do these words more strongly suggest than that all things were made from nothing—things that could neither be discovered nor traced except by God alone? Otherwise they would be traceable, if they had been sought out and discovered from matter.

Therefore, insofar as it has been established that there was no matter—also because no such matter as is alleged could appropriately have existed—to that same extent it is proved [Note: [Col. 0238C] “Would be proved,” the same editors, against all the manuscripts] that all things were made by God from nothing. The only exception is that Hermogenes, in describing matter in the very condition in which he himself exists—unformed, confused, turbulent, with motion uncertain, precipitous, and seething—while displaying a specimen [Note: [Col. 0238C] “Specimens,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] of his art, has painted his own portrait.

# The Five Books of Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullian Against Marcion

## Preface

## Historical Synopsis

[Col. 0239A]

Marcion was born at Sinope in Pontus around the year 148, during the reign of Antoninus. At the beginning of his life he made a show of chastity and professed the way of life of monks. His father was a bishop of the catholic communion. Later, having fallen in love with a virgin, he violated her. Because of this incest, therefore, he was expelled from the Church by his own father, a man exceptionally religious, most ardent in his love of truth, and outstanding in the administration of the episcopal office. Although Marcion pleaded with him long and earnestly and sought admission to penance, he obtained nothing from his father by any entreaties.

He therefore went to Rome and approached the elders who had been instructed by disciples of the apostles and were still living, but he sought admission to communion [Col. 0239B] in vain. Consequently, provoked by envy and driven by anger, he said to them: “I shall tear your Church apart and introduce into it an everlasting schism.” Departing from them, he resolved to take refuge in the heresy of the impostor Cerdo (St. Epiphanius, *Heresy* 42).

Cerdo’s doctrines were these: that the God who established the Law was different from the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ; that the latter was good, but the former evil; and that Jesus Christ did not assume true flesh consubstantial with ours, nor was he born of the Virgin Mary. Dupin.

Tertullian composed his work *Against Marcion* after he had fallen into the error of the Montanists, as [Col. 0239C] appears from these words (book 1, chap. 29): “A limit is now placed upon marrying; on the authority of the Paraclete, one marriage in the faith is prescribed.” This is also evident when he mentions the new prophecies and, speaking of certain revelations, writes (book 4, chap. 22): “Concerning this there is a dispute between us and the ‘psychics’”—a distinction that plainly indicates a difference of communion.

Yet these things by no means prevent this work of Tertullian from being called excellent and, as it were, a treasury of more ancient theology. He had first written a short work on this same subject, but in a hurried style, as they say. He afterward recast it with a fuller treatment (book 4, chap. 1) [Col. 0239D], but says that this work too was fraudulently taken from him by an apostate brother. To repair that loss, he labored upon this third version in the fifteenth year of the emperor Severus, A.D. 207. Lumper.

## Overview of the Entire Work

In the first place, therefore, he demonstrates God’s unity and uniqueness, since he proves that it is impossible for there to be two supreme beings. For whoever could admit two could likewise admit more; [Col. 0240A] after two comes plurality, once unity has been overthrown, whereas the number of divinity must, by the highest reasoning, consist in unity.

Moreover, to establish his two principles, Marcion said that the Creator deserved blame, since he would have to be called the author of evils, as Scripture says (Isa. 45:7): “I form peace and create evil.” But Tertullian shows that it is impious, blasphemous, and rash to say of God (chaps. 3–4): “God ought not to have acted thus, and ought rather to have acted thus.”

He then reveals the Creator’s goodness shining forth in all his works, especially in the formation and creation of the first man. Next, he shows the necessity of divine justice in the punishment of evils, in order to restrain the wicked and avert sins committed [Col. 0240B] against God’s command and will (book 2, chap. 13): “For it was not enough that good should commend itself by itself unless there presided some power inspiring fear, which would make even the unwilling seek good and compel them to preserve it.” An unbridled freedom of sinning is therefore laid open to sinners when God is presented to them as one who cannot punish with fire and Tartarus, who cannot be feared—and does not wish to be feared (chap. 16).

“As injustice is evil, so justice is good. Severity, therefore, is good because it is just, if the judge is good—that is, just. So too are all the other good attributes through which the good work of good severity proceeds, whether anger, jealousy, or fierceness—that is, the volitions of God, which we signify by these words, while nevertheless removing [Col. 0240C] far from God all human passions.”

Yet the philosophers and the heretics who followed them, lest they should represent God either as subject to passions or as mutable and consequently corruptible and mortal, imagined him to be altogether without feeling (chap. 16). “It is well,” Tertullian replied, “that it belongs to Christians to believe even that God died, and yet lives for ever and ever. Most foolish are those who predicate divine things from human ones, so that, because passions of this kind are found in man’s corruptible condition, they therefore suppose them to be of the same character in God.”

From these words it is evident that in Tertullian’s age the doctrine of Christ’s divinity and of the unity of the three Persons was established [Col. 0240D] and confirmed. Then, continuing his argument, he adds: “Distinguish the substance, and assign to it its own affections, as diverse as the substances require, even though they seem to share the same names. For we also read of God’s right hand, eyes, and feet; yet they will not therefore be compared with human members merely because they share their designations. . . . God’s goodness is prior according to nature; his severity is subsequent according to cause” (chaps. 13–14).

The first evil, therefore, was sin; punishment followed it, and [Col. 0241A] punishment is called an evil. But these latter evils are of another kind: they are the things by which sins are repaid, though in their own proper character they are good, because they are just, defend good things, oppose transgressions, and in this order are worthy of God.

To disclose the origin of evil, Tertullian establishes the freedom of the human will, saying: “In this very respect man would be reckoned the image and likeness of God—namely, in freedom and power of choice.” But an image is always inferior to its original, and a work inferior to its craftsman; it is therefore by its nature capable of failing (chap. 10). Just as God introduced man into the state of life, so man drew upon himself the state of death. The same must be said of the angel: God made an angel, who transformed himself into a demon.

In this way he solves the objection drawn from the fall of man and the angels to accuse the Creator—namely, [Col. 0241B] that he was guilty either of lack of knowledge and ignorance, if he did not foresee the sin of each, or of malice, if he foresaw it and did not prevent it. For God had to preserve both his faithfulness and his steadfastness in every work. He therefore preserved his work as he had created it—that is, endowed with free choice (chap. 7). For if he had once granted man freedom and power of choice, he had certainly granted him the enjoyment of them by that very institution. The ability to sin, however, is a consequence of this freedom, which God permitted together with its adjuncts—namely, sins and punishments—all of which he afterward turned to the manifestation of his glory.

Proceeding afterward to the Messiah’s mission and incarnation, he says (book 3, chap. 3) that Christ was not to be believed merely on the basis of miracles, but on the basis of the prophets’ prior predictions concerning him, since it had been foretold [Col. 0241C] that many would come, work signs, and display great powers, yet were not therefore to be accepted.

Here he also observes that future events are sometimes announced by the prophets as though they had already occurred (chap. 5), because with God there is no difference of time: eternity directs all times into one uniform state before him. It is characteristic of prophetic foreknowledge that what it foresees, while foreseeing it, it regards as already seen and thus already accomplished—that is, it demonstrates it as something that will certainly come to pass.

To show that God was truly man, not shadowy and phantasmal, he says that if there had not been true humanity in Christ, neither should true divinity be believed in him (chap. 8): “For if that which appeared in Christ was not truly there, and what was there falsely represented itself, then his flesh was not flesh and the man was not man; consequently, Christ as God was not God either. For why should he not also have borne the phantom [Col. 0241D] of God? Shall I believe him concerning his inward substance when I have been deceived concerning the outward?”

That he was a true man is shown by everything we read concerning him in the Gospels: namely, that he both touched others and was touched by them. For it is written that by touching the sick he delivered them; that his feet were touched by the sinful woman and anointed with poured-out ointment; and also that he suffered, died, and yielded up his spirit. After his resurrection he demonstrated that he possessed flesh and bones, commanding them to touch and handle him. Otherwise, if he had not possessed true flesh, he would neither have suffered nor died and therefore would not have risen. Consequently, our faith is vain, and vain also is the apostles’ preaching, for they would be found false witnesses of God.

[Col. 0242A] The Marcionites objected that it was unworthy of Christ to assume flesh. They described that earthly substance as thrust amid filth and called the virgin’s womb a sewer; and they dwelt at length upon the unclean and shameful torments of childbirth and the foul, harmful, and ridiculous issues of delivery (chap. 11).

But Tertullian says that all these are most holy and reverend works of nature. Although Marcion might represent them as unworthy of God, he would nevertheless gain nothing by doing so, for birth will not be more unworthy than death, infancy than the cross, nature than punishment, or flesh than condemnation. “If Christ truly suffered those things, it would have been a lesser thing for him to be born” (chap. 10).

Moreover, if we look to the matter strictly, no substance is worthy for God to put on. Whatever he puts on, he himself makes worthy—provided that there is no falsehood. How, therefore, can it be that he regarded the truth of flesh as a disgrace rather than its falsehood? [Col. 0242B] Indeed, since it had been foretold of Christ that he would be filled with reproaches and covered with confusion, so that he appeared a worm and not a man, it was necessary for him to undergo shame (book 4, chap. 21), so that our salvation might rest upon his disgrace. Since man had felt no shame in worshiping stone and wood, God received satisfaction for the shamelessness of idolatry through the corresponding shamelessness of faith—through a steadfastness that was not ashamed of Christ.

He adds that it was known and established by everyone that Christ was born from the family of David, while the distinction of tribes and families was still preserved among the Jews (book 4, chap. 7), and that this could be found in the census of Augustus, which the Roman archives preserve as a most trustworthy witness to the Lord’s birth.

He called himself the Son of Man while claiming for himself the authority to forgive sins, so that it might be evident that he was at once true God [Col. 0242C] and true man. For Christ could not lie by declaring himself the Son of Man if he were not truly man; nor could he forgive sins if he were not true God. Thus he was the man whom Daniel’s prophecy foretold would receive authority to judge.

But in speaking of Christ’s kingdom, Tertullian shows with perfect clarity that he adhered to the opinion of the chiliasts. This ought not to seem surprising to anyone who considers that he also placed faith in the visions of the Montanists.

To accuse the Creator of all things, the Marcionites chiefly employed the device and deceit of opposing the New Testament to the Old. If they encountered anything either harsh or rather humble in the Law or the Prophets, they treated it as worthless and exaggerated it as something contemptible. But Tertullian showed that those two Testaments [Col. 0242D] had not proceeded from different authors, but from one and the same author, who nevertheless conducted himself differently according to the state and condition of the human race.

At first God promised only lesser rewards, as pledges of the sublime riches he reserved for them. Tertullian says that it is not incongruous for God to bestow riches, by which many works of justice and love are accomplished, just as he gave and promised other earthly goods in the Old Testament. It belongs to the Creator of heaven and earth to distribute and bestow these no less than heavenly goods (1).

He also disposes of objections drawn from the theft of the Israelites, which the Hebrews appear to have committed against the Egyptians at God’s command, and others drawn from an alleged contradiction among the commandments—for example, when he forbade them (2) [Col. 0243A] to make a likeness of anything in heaven, on earth, or in the waters, and at the same time commanded them to fashion the image of the bronze serpent and golden representations of the cherubim and seraphim upon the ark (3). Here he says that images are forbidden solely when made for the sake of idolatry.

Likewise, he says that the law of retaliation was given so that the commission of injury might be rooted out by fear that an equivalent requital would immediately follow, and so that permission to retaliate might serve as a prohibition against provocation.

The heretics mocked and despised those things in the Old Testament that seemed rather base, such as bloody sacrifices (4), the burdensome scruples of washings, and the selection of foods. But all these things were prescribed by God so that, with these legal disciplines confronting them everywhere, they might not for a single moment be free from regard for God . . . “to say nothing of their hidden significations—namely, those [Col. 0243B] of the spiritual and prophetic law, which was figurative in almost all its subjects” (5).

“If the Law,” he writes, “withholds something in respect of foods and pronounces unclean animals that were once blessed, understand this as a plan for exercising self-restraint, and recognize the bridles imposed upon that appetite which, though it had eaten the bread of angels, desired the cucumbers and melons of Egypt. Recognize at the same time the provision made against appetite’s companions—namely, lust and luxury. . . . Likewise, so that the passion for money, which is defended on the ground of the necessity of sustenance, might in part be extinguished, the ambition for costly foods was taken away. Finally, [Col. 0244A] this was done so that man might more readily be trained to fast for God. . . . Let no one criticize the burdens of sacrifices, observances, and the burdensome scruples of washings . . . but let him perceive that providential skill of God by which he wished to bind to his own religion, through duties of this kind, a people prone to idolatry and transgression. These were the very rites through which worldly superstition was practiced, and so he used them to call the people away from it (6).

“But even in the ordinary dealings of life and human intercourse, at home and abroad, down to the care of vessels, he made distinctions of every kind, so that, with these legal disciplines confronting them everywhere, they might not for a single moment be free from regard for God. . . . To support this benefit, not burden, of the Law, he appointed the prophets, who taught things worthy of God: to remove wickedness from the soul, to seek judgment, and the other things that prove the essence and [Col. 0244B] perfection of religion to consist in virtue and good works.”

He proceeds further in explaining this doctrine and proves that the Law prescribed love and the forgiveness of injuries, with vengeance reserved to God, which is necessary so that wicked men may be held in order (7). He adds that Christ made the prohibition of divorce conditional: it applies if someone dismisses his wife in order to marry another.

Finally, he lays down this rule concerning faith (8): “Nothing is to be believed rashly; and whatever is believed without recognition of its origin is believed rashly.” Lumper.

# Book One

## Subject

[Col. 0243]

[Col. 0243C] Since Tertullian had afterward recast his earlier short work *Against Marcion* with a fuller composition, and a certain brother who subsequently apostatized had then published it in an extremely corrupt form, he was compelled to correct it; and the occasion for revision persuaded him to add something further. Hence this work grew into five books, of which we here provide the subject matter of the first.

I. First, after an invective against Marcion of Pontus because he abolished marriage, mutilated the Gospels, and lost the God whom he had discovered by extinguishing the light of faith, Tertullian teaches that Marcion must now be classified as a heretic: because, abandoning what had existed before, he afterward chose for himself that which previously had not existed. He had maintained this more fully in his book *On Prescription Against* [Col. 0243D] *Heretics*.

II. Next, that Marcion introduced two gods: the Creator, that is, our God; and his own god, as well as that of Cerdo, his instructor—the former evil, the latter good.

III. But Christian truth has pronounced categorically that, unless God is one, he is not God, because he is the supremely great being, whom nothing can equal.

IV. Two supremely great beings cannot exist, even if each is bounded within distinct limits.

V. For if two supremely great beings are admitted, even more must also be admitted, as with Valentinus, who poured forth a whole swarm of divinity extending to the offspring of thirty aeons.

[Col. 0244C] VI. Accordingly, not only can two equal gods not be established, but neither can two unequal ones.

VII. Nor is Marcion supported by the name “God,” which is also permitted to others in the Scriptures, because for that very reason possession of supreme greatness does not belong to them.

VIII. Moreover, the heresy of the Marcionites is proved even from the fact that they make their God new, whereas the true God is eternal.

IX. This remains so even if they call him new only with respect to knowledge, because God could not have remained unknown or uncertain.

X. Rather, he was always known from the beginning, since even the pagan soul and conscience knew him.

XI. Consequently, since all things belong to God, if Marcion’s deity created nothing, the one whom Marcion sets up is not God.

[Col. 0244D] XII. Even if we could confess him to be a god, we would nevertheless have to argue that he existed without a cause, since he possessed no thing of his own, whereas a thing is the cause for something’s existence.

XIII. Nor is the world a work too unworthy of God; Tertullian proves this from the testimonies of the philosophers themselves.

XIV. Indeed, even the smallest animals can either help or harm. Nor does Marcion’s god reject the water of God the Creator, with which he washes his followers; or the oil with which he anoints them; or the bread with which, in the sacraments, he represents—that is, shows as present—his own body.

XV. Next he adds that Marcion’s god does not possess a [Col. 0245A] heaven and world of his own—a point discussed more fully below in book 5—both because no such substance had been discovered as late as the fifteenth year of the emperor Severus, in which the author indicates that he wrote this work, and because otherwise Marcion will be judged to assign nine gods.

XVI. Nor did Marcion’s god create only invisible things, as is likewise discussed more fully below in the aforesaid book 5; rather, visible and invisible things have the same author.

XVII. Furthermore, the single work of liberating man is insufficient for that god, since even a stern God made himself known through other works.

XVIII. Likewise, he cannot correctly be said to have been revealed when he was revealed neither by nature through his works nor by doctrine through proclamation.

XIX. Nor was he revealed even in Christ Jesus: both because Christ was revealed [Col. 0245B] more than 115 years before Marcion’s god, who appeared under Antoninus; and because Christ preceded that separation of the Law and the Gospel which Marcion was the first to devise in his *Antitheses*.

XX. It is useless for the opposing side to object that Marcion did not innovate the rule of faith but restored one that had previously been corrupted, appealing to the example of Paul, who reproved Peter. For in Peter he reproved only his conduct, not his faith in the Creator.

XXI. Faith in the Creator and in his Christ therefore always remained firm, not only under the apostles but also in the apostolic churches, among which Christ is shown to belong to no god other than the Creator.

XXII. Moreover, Marcion’s god cannot be called good by nature, since he did not come to the aid of fallen man from the beginning.

[Col. 0245C] XXIII. Nor can he be called reasonably good, because he chose to come to the aid of man who, according to Marcion, was alien to him.

XXIV. Nor is he perfectly good, because, according to Marcion, he saves the soul alone and not the flesh, although among Marcion’s followers the flesh is baptized, withdrawn from marriage, and suffers violence in confessing the divine name.

[Col. 0246A] XXV. Consequently, God must not be judged by goodness alone.

XXVI. Rather, judgment must also be attributed to him, so that he avenges and condemns sins.

XXVII. Otherwise sinners would have no fear.

XXVIII. Thus even baptism, the sacrament of faith, cannot stand. Baptism is the remission of sins, release from death, regeneration, and attainment of the Holy Spirit; finally, it seals and washes. These things can be conferred only by the one who created man from the beginning, bestowed a soul upon him, and condemned him to death because of sin.

XXIX. Lastly, he attacks Marcion’s heresy whereby Marcion wished only virgins and unmarried persons to be baptized: both because God blessed marriage and because, if there is no marriage, there will be no holiness either. Indeed, this would amount to suppressing the entire seed-stock of the human race.

[Col. 0246B] XXX. Finally, in a brief epilogue he prepares the reader for the following books. Pamelius.

### Chapter I

If anything has previously been done by us against Marcion, from this point onward let it be regarded as past.

[Note: [Col. 0245C] **If anything has previously been done by us against Marcion, from this point onward let it be regarded as past.** He most expressly disowns whatever he had composed against Marcion before these books. In the preface to the poems commonly inscribed under Tertullian’s name *Against Marcion* and usually passed off as works of Septimius, we have shown that it must by no means be supposed that this earlier work consisted of verse. Rigault.]

We undertake a new treatment of an old subject.

[Note: [Col. 0245D] **The first short work, as though hastily composed.** It was an initial and unfinished work, of the kind that Jerome, in his commentary on Obadiah, reports had also escaped from Origen, Quintilian, and even Cicero in certain passages of the books *To Herennius*: “If he could say this both of the books *To Herennius* and of the rhetorical works—which I consider nearly perfect—when comparing them with the skill of his old age, how much more freely may I profess that the one work belonged to youthful talent and the other to mature old age? In his books against Marcion, Septimius Tertullian also underwent the same experience; so did Origen in his *Commentary on the Song of Songs*, and Quintilian in the twelve books of his *Institutes of Oratory*.” Rigault.]

I had afterward recast my first short work, which had been produced as though in haste, into a fuller composition. This version too, before a sufficient number of copies had been made, I lost through the fraud of one who was then a brother but afterward an apostate.

[Note: [Col. 0245D] **Then a brother, afterward an apostate.** Marcion, who was a brother for as long as he was truly a Christian. It is clear from the preceding words that Tertullian had written against Marcion. Le Prieur.]

He had perhaps copied certain passages with extreme inaccuracy and released the work to the public.

[Note: [Col. 0246C] **Released it to the public.** That is, he sent it out among the people. So too in the *Apologeticum*, Tertullian used “the populations of the mountains” to mean the wild beasts inhabiting the mountains. Rigault.]

The necessity of correction arose. The occasion of revising it persuaded me to add something. Thus this present composition, now third instead of second, will henceforth be first instead of third. It necessarily provides this account of the history of its own publication, lest anyone [Note: [Col. 0245C] “Anyone at all,” Rhenanus] be confused by encountering its divergent forms in circulation.

[Note: [Col. 0246C] **Lest anyone be confused by encountering its divergent forms in circulation.** He would have had no reason to fear this in connection with any verses he might have written against Marcion, for they distinguish themselves very clearly from this kind of style. Rigault.]
