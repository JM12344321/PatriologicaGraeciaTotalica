**FIRST RIGALTIAN EDITION.** Paris, at the expense of Mathurin Du Puis, rue Saint-Jacques, under the sign of the Crown, folio. “The works of Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian, corrected in very many places according to the oldest copies, and illustrated with observations and notes by the jurist Nicolas Rigault. With a glossary-index of the African style.” It is worth hearing in Rigault’s own, somewhat lengthy words what he achieved—or at least intended—by this new recension of all Tertullian’s works, which even now [Col. 0050D] holds first place.

“Readers of Tertullian’s [Col. 0051A] writings, even those by no means poorly educated, encounter many difficulties of language and text. The lofty African diction, surpassing almost every teaching by its endowment, demanded readers keenly attentive to its every movement. When it afterward fell into very different hands, it began to be altered by men unable to understand it, and the shamelessness of an adulterating hand scattered spurious expressions for genuine ones. Once the author’s words were intercepted, the ruin of the native text, like a battered body, destroyed its very sense and mind with it. Thus the worst correctors destroyed what was most correct. What limit could there be to complaints? What could remain beyond despair after so old and great a calamity?

“This plague had long ago so persuaded Homer that, when Aratus asked where an original copy [Col. 0051B] of the *Iliad* might be found, Timon son of Timarchus replied: ‘It must be sought in ancient copies,’ and added, ‘but copies not yet corrected.’ That dreadful tribe has long treated our Septimius’s books so foully that the antiquity of falsehood now bars truth by prescription of long time. If you appeal to the authority of old books, utterly false readings are produced on the authority of old books; for some old books have been most corruptly corrected. There is no hope of restoring truth unless books are found so old that they precede every antiquity of correctors.

“Our grandfathers’ age saw Rhenanus’s praiseworthy labor, worked out in three editions. Had partisan zeal not begrudged a man of such fidelity, judgment, learning, and diligence access to better copies, he would undoubtedly have achieved what would greatly lighten our [Col. 0051C] labor today. The next age produced Fulvio Orsini, who had examined manuscripts far better than Rhenanus’s. Content to note extracts from their readings in the margins of Pamelius’s edition, he did not even name their owners. Orsini’s readings, copied from the autograph into the book of the most distinguished and learned archbishop of Toulouse, Cardinal Montchal, also enabled us to establish the credit of Wouwer’s edition, perfected by his *Demonstrative Notes, and even of the extracts from Fulda and the Vatican on the *Apology* and *Against the Jews*, made public by Junius from Schoppe. Their credit should everywhere be strongly attested, especially because we derived many excellent readings from them that give this edition much [Col. 0051D] light and ornament.

“This will be our age’s glory among posterity. Vast distinction was added when at last we discovered the codex said to have been offered by vow of Agobard, bishop of Lyons, at St Stephen’s altar. The many passages restored by its aid to their original splendor will prove that this book was copied from manuscripts not yet corrected. To indicate each would have been extremely wearisome and worthless: Tertullian eagerly received his own words, and what had been badly torn apart now coheres so well that one would not believe it ever could have been separated. It will suffice to collect the chief restorations, thus showing what we are prepared to accomplish, but so that notable matters are finally included [Col. 0052A] and smaller yet not contemptible ones are left to scrupulous reckoning, someday to form an immense total.

“We added observations to illuminate certain obscure passages and trace remnants of ancient custom long forgotten or hidden learning. If some points seem stated or investigated somewhat freely—perhaps those on punishment of secret crimes, the form of the Lord’s face, and lamps lit by day—they must be understood as remarks for examining Septimius’s opinions, not for attacking Catholic teaching or rejecting decrees of Fathers later than Septimius [Note: [Col. 0052D] In these words Rigault excuses freer opinions sometimes inserted in his notes on both Cyprian and Tertullian. I do not know whether he satisfied everyone. Some passages plainly show that he speaks from his own mind rather than the author’s and even strays from the matter, sometimes writing notes insufficiently consonant with present Church usage. So Dupin, who adds that Rigault is reproached for excessive boldness in conjecture, insufficient care in revising his author from ancient manuscripts, and inserting his or others’ conjectures into the text without manuscript authority. EDITORS.]. Indeed, we always opposed to Tertullian the judgments and decrees of Fathers most accepted in the Church, so that those who stumble over those African [Col. 0052B] rough places immediately have somewhere safe to set their foot.

“We also inserted notes weighing ambiguous variants, investigating truth, and sometimes explaining or confirming conjectures. Finally, by comparing many passages, we compiled glosses of African style, the surest method of interpretation. We called them ‘glosses’ with the Greek word by which Fabius says unusual words of a more secret language are designated. We know the shorter observations and notes will not satisfy many, but we do not therefore excuse ourselves. This African admits no reader unless already learned.”

So Rigault in the dissertation entitled *On Tertullian and His Writings, Their Various Editions, and What Is Accomplished by This Edition*, which, together [Col. 0052C] with the dedicatory letter to Cardinal Richelieu, precedes the text in the edition of nine books of 1529 (so printed). It is followed by ancient testimonies on Tertullian, the series of works in the volume, and an account of the manuscripts from which each was notably corrected and how often—from at least four to as many as 250 times in the *Apology*, commonly thirty, twenty, or seventy times, but also eight hundred or 130 times, and sometimes in nearly every clause. He says *On the Pallium* is corrected in almost every line according to Salmasius’s most exact edition from old copies, except in six or seven places.

After the text comes an index of Holy Scripture; then, under the title “uncertain author,” poems once customarily ascribed to Tertullian [Col. 0052D]: five books *Against Marcion*, *On the Judgment of the Lord, Genesis,* and *Sodom*; finally an index of memorable subjects. The observations have their own title [Col. 0053A], with an excellently planned index of Tertullian’s glosses. Its elegant, brilliant type greatly commends this edition, assuming the first was no worse than the second before us. The bookseller added substantial utility by publishing in a separate volume many scholars’ notes on Tertullian: *James Pamelius of Bruges, theologian, etc., Arguments and Annotations on the Works of Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian, with Annotations of Various Writers Added; All Corresponding to the Text of the Edition of the Distinguished Jurist Nicolas Rigault.* Paris, Mathurin Du Puis, rue Saint-Jacques, at the Crown, 1635.

Besides Pamelius’s annotations, arguments, life, etc., occupying most of the volume through p. 781, it contains [Col. 0053B] Latino Latini’s conjectures and corrections and notes by Jean Mercier, Edmond Richer, Théodore Marcile, Jan van der Wouwer, and Gabriel de L’Aubespine, bishop of Orléans, with an excellent index. The title’s claim that the annotations correspond to Rigault’s text was effected by marginal page numbers, except that *On the Pallium* and the *Apology*, which appear wholly transformed in Rigault, were reprinted according to Pamelius’s recension. This notes volume is separately listed in many catalogues, including those of Thott, the Bodleian, Firmian, and d’Estrées, vol. I.

Havercamp possessed a rich apparatus: nearly all editions; two Leiden parchment manuscripts, nos. 324 [Col. 0061A] and 376; Modius’s Fulda variants, first published with Junius in 1597, later known chiefly to Godefroy and selectively used by Rigault, leaving Havercamp a rich gleaning; and a collation of an Agobard manuscript distinct from the one that restored many other writings.
