## CHAPTER III

The principal dispute, therefore—and [Note: [Col. 0249C] “Is,” Jun.] consequently the whole dispute—concerns number: whether it is permissible to introduce two gods, perhaps by poetic and pictorial license [Note: [Col. 0249D] “Perhaps by poetic and pictorial license.” Since everything seems to be permitted to painters and poets, he indicates that Marcion wishes to use the same license in order to construct impiety against God. Nor does he mention painting here without reason; for, as we have noted elsewhere, Apelles and Hermogenes were painters by profession before they turned their minds to disturbing the Church. LE PR.], and now by a third, heretical license. But Christian truth has pronounced strictly: if God is not one, he does not exist; for we more fittingly believe that anything which is not as it ought to be does not exist.

To know that God must be one, inquire what God is, and you will find no other answer. Insofar as the human condition can define God, I give this definition, which the consciousness of all will also acknowledge: [Note: [Col. 0249D] “God is the supreme greatness.” A beautiful definition of God, insofar—to use the author’s preceding words—as the human condition can define God. Similarly, below in chapter 7 he says: “You will find this substance of God alone unborn, unmade, alone eternal, and the maker of the universe.” And above, in *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 7: “God is eternal, that is, unborn, greater and more exalted than all things, neither born nor made, without beginning, without end.” PAM.] God is the supreme greatness, established in eternity, unborn, unmade, without beginning, without end. For this condition must be assigned to eternity, which makes God the supreme greatness, since eternity itself is this in God, and so too are the other attributes; with the result that God is the supreme greatness in form, reason, strength, and power.

Since all agree on this—for no one will deny that God is something supremely great, unless he can, on the contrary, pronounce God to be something lowest and slight, thereby denying God through removing what belongs to God—what, then, will be the condition of the supreme greatness itself? Surely that nothing be made equal to it; that is, that there be no other supreme greatness. For if there were, it would be made equal to it; and if it were made equal to it, it would no longer be the supreme [Col. 0250A] greatness, since the condition—and, so to speak, the law—which permits nothing to be made equal to the supreme greatness would have been overthrown. Therefore, whatever is the supreme greatness must necessarily be unique, having no equal, lest it cease to be the supreme greatness. Therefore, it will exist in no other way than by that through which it possesses existence—that is [Note: [Col. 0249C] “And that,” Jun., Venet., Rig.], by being altogether unique.

Accordingly, since God is the supreme greatness, our truth rightly pronounced: if God is not one, he does not exist. We do not say, “If he is not one, he does not exist,” as though we doubted that God exists; rather, because we trust that he exists, we define him as being that which, if he is not that, he is not God [Note: [Col. 0249C] “The same,” Semler; “the same,” Fran.] [Note: [Col. 0249C] Semler inserts “and.”]—namely, the supreme greatness. Moreover, the supreme greatness must necessarily be unique. Therefore God also will be unique. He is not God except as the supreme greatness; nor is he the supreme greatness except as having no equal; nor does he have no equal [Col. 0250B] except by being unique.

Certainly, whatever other God you introduce, you will not be able to uphold him as God under any other form than by ascribing to him also the property of divinity: just as he is eternal, so too he is the supreme greatness. How, then, can two supreme greatnesses coexist, when it is the nature of the supreme greatness to have no equal? And to have no equal [Note: [Col. 0250C] “Since it does not,” Wouw.], since it belongs to one alone [Note: [Col. 0250C] Semler disregards “since.”], can in no way exist in two.

## CHAPTER IV

But someone will argue that two supreme greatnesses can coexist, distinct and separate within their respective boundaries, and he will certainly invoke this example: the kingdoms of the earth are so numerous and yet are supreme greatnesses in each of their respective regions. He will, of course, suppose [Note: [Col. 0250C] “Everywhere,” Seml.] that human things are to be compared with divine things. If, then, a place is granted to this argument, what prevents us from introducing—not merely a third and fourth god—but as many gods in number as there are kings of nations?

The subject is God, whose foremost property is that he admits comparison with no example. Nature itself will proclaim this, even if no Isaiah does, or God himself through Isaiah: [Note: [Col. 0250D] “To whom will you liken me?” Isaiah 40 reads somewhat differently: “To whom have you likened the Lord?” according to the Septuagint, or “God?” according to blessed Jerome’s version. Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, book III, and after him Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book XIII, in Greek, report that the Sibyl learned from this passage to call God immeasurable and infinite; there he teaches that the things beautifully said by the philosophers were borrowed from the books of the prophets. PAM.] “To whom will you liken me?” (Isa. XL, 25). Will human things perhaps be compared with divine ones? They will not be so compared with God [Note: [Col. 0250C] “Our things,” Rhen.; “with our God,” Seml.]; for God is one thing, and the things of God another.

Finally, you who use the example of a king as though he were the supreme greatness, take care lest you already be unable to use it. For although a king is a supreme [Col. 0251A] greatness on his throne, as far as God, he is nevertheless beneath God; and when compared with God, he will immediately fall from the rank of supreme greatness, since that rank has been transferred [Note: [Col. 0251C] “The transfer,” all the others.] to God. If this is so, how can you employ as an example for comparison with God something which vanishes when brought into the comparison?

What, further, if even among kings the supreme greatness cannot appear in many forms, but is unique and singular—namely, in the man who, because of the supremacy of his greatness and the subjection of all lesser ranks, is acknowledged as king of kings, as though he were the summit of dominion? But even kings of another kind, who rule singly within a unified dominion, if they are brought from all sides together with their petty kingdoms, so to speak, for an examination intended to establish which of them excels in the resources and strength of his kingdom, the summit of greatness must necessarily be refined down to one [Col. 0251B], all the others being gradually squeezed out and excluded from the supreme rank of greatness by the outcome of the comparison. Thus, even if the supreme greatness appears manifold when dispersed, in its own powers, nature, and condition it is unique.

Accordingly, when two gods are compared as two kings and two supreme greatnesses, the unity of supreme greatness must necessarily yield to one of them, according to the verdict of the comparison. The one who stands supreme by his victory, his rival—another greatness, though not the supreme one—having been overcome, and who through his rival’s failure possesses a kind of solitude arising from the singularity of his own excellence, is unique. This inescapable chain of reasoning confines the conclusion to this: either it must be denied that God is the supreme greatness—which no wise person will tolerate—or that status must not be shared with any other.

Whatever principle does not allow several supreme greatnesses to be admitted, the same does not allow two, since two themselves are already several [Col. 0252A] after one; for after one comes number. The principle which could admit two could also admit several; for after [Note: [Col. 0252C] “Can,” Rhen.] two comes plurality, unity having already been exceeded. Finally, among us the force of this principle, by that very limit, does not permit belief in several gods, because it does not permit even two: it establishes one God by the rule that God must be that to whom nothing is equal, as the supreme greatness; and that to which nothing is equal must be unique.

Now, to what purpose or advantage would two supreme greatnesses, two equals, be assigned? What difference would their number make, since two equals do not differ from one? For that which is the same in two is one thing. Even if there were several equals, all of them would nevertheless be one and the same thing, since they differed in no respect [Note: [Col. 0252C] Seml. conjectures “no respect.”] from one another [Note: [Col. 0252C] “From themselves,” Paris.] insofar as they were equal. Yet the number of divinities ought to rest upon the highest rational ground, if only because even their worship would be rendered doubtful.

For behold: contemplating two gods, as equal as two supreme greatnesses, what should I do? If I worshiped both, I would fear that, through an excess of service, it might be judged superstition rather than religion; for, since the two are so equal and both are present in either one, I could conciliate both in one, thereby offering this very act as testimony [Note: [Col. 0252D] Perhaps “a testimony,” editions.] to their equality and unity, since I would venerate the one in the other, and in one would possess two. If I worshiped one of them, I would likewise reconsider, lest I appear to expose the vanity of a number that is superfluous because it makes no difference; that is, I would judge it safer to worship neither than to worship one with misgiving [Col. 0252C], or both in vain.

## CHAPTER VI

Thus far we still seem to be arguing as though Marcion established two equal gods. For while maintaining that the supreme greatness must be believed unique, and excluding equality from it, we have treated these gods as though they were two equals. Nevertheless, by teaching that they cannot be equal under the form of supreme greatness, we have sufficiently confirmed that there cannot be two.

Yet, since we know that Marcion establishes unequal gods—the one a judge, savage, mighty in war; the other gentle, placid, and only good and supremely good—let us examine this side equally: whether diversity, at least [Note: [Col. 0253C] Read “at least.”], can admit two when equality could not. Here too the same rule of supreme greatness will support us, since it vindicates the entire condition of divinity. For, meeting the adversary’s meaning directly and, as it were, seizing and detaining it by force of law, since he does not deny that the Creator is God, I most justly bar him from allowing any place for diversity between those whom, having confessed them equally as gods, he cannot make diverse. This is not because even extremely different human beings may not be included under the same designation, but because nothing may be called or believed to be God unless it is the supreme greatness.

Since, therefore, he is compelled to acknowledge as the supreme greatness the one whom he does not deny to be God, it cannot be admitted that he should ascribe to the supreme greatness any diminution by which it is subordinated to another supreme greatness; for if it is subordinated, it ceases to be supreme. But it is not possible for God to depart from his condition—that is, from supreme greatness. For even in that more powerful god the supreme greatness could be endangered if it can be depreciated in the Creator [Note: [Col. 0253D] “If it begins to be depreciated in the Creator.” He frequently uses the verb *capit* in this sense, like the Greek ἐνδέχεται (*endechetai*, “it is possible/admissible”). Here it has the same force as if you were to say: “If it is conceded, if it is granted, that the Creator may be depreciated.” Thus in book II: “because, if this were admissible by right.” RHEN.].

Thus, when two gods are pronounced to be two supreme greatnesses, necessarily neither is greater or lesser than the other, neither more exalted nor more abased than the other. Deny that he is God whom you call inferior; deny that he is the supreme greatness whom you believe lesser. But once you have confessed him to be God, you have confessed both to be two supreme greatnesses. You will take nothing away from the one or ascribe anything to the other. By acknowledging divinity, you have denied diversity.

## CHAPTER VII

In reply, you will attempt to unsettle [Note: [Col. 0253D] “Redirect,” Franeq.] the discussion by appealing to the name “God” [Col. 0253C], [Note: [Col. 0253D] “As common and permitted to others as well.” Here he plainly interprets himself, showing what he means by *passivum* when he says “and”—that is, “that is”—“permitted to others as well.” For *passivum* signifies for him something common and indiscriminate, from the adverb *passim* (“everywhere, indiscriminately”). Above, at the end of *On the Flesh of Christ*, he said: “another, common among the rest, ignoble”—that is, ordinary and obscure. RHEN.—In African councils, the bodies of martyrs were called *passiva*, that is, indiscriminate, τυχόντα (*tychonta*, “ordinary/chance specimens”). SEML.] claiming that it is common and permitted to others as well, because it is written (Ps. LXXXII, 1): “The God of gods stood in the assembly of gods; in their midst he will judge the gods” [Note: [Col. 0254C] “He judged,” Rhen.]. And again (ibid., v. 6): “I said: You are gods.” Yet, you will argue, possession of supreme greatness does not therefore belong to them merely because they are called gods, and neither, accordingly, does it belong to the Creator.

I shall answer even the fool who has failed to consider that the same objection may be turned equally against Marcion’s god: he too may be called a god without therefore being proved the supreme greatness, just as the Creator’s angels or human beings are not. If community of names prejudges conditions, how many worthless slaves swagger under the names of kings—Alexander, Darius, and Holofernes? [Note: [Col. 0254D] “Alexander, Darius, and Holofernes.” In Pithoeus’s codex the text reads *et Olofernae*, so that these are genitive-case names of slaves belonging to Alexander, Darius, and Holofernes. This reading appears to be Tertullian’s. He said *Olofernae*, just as *Cynopae* and *Syrenarum* in the *Apologeticum*. RIG.] Yet that does not detract from kings what they are. Indeed, even the idols of the nations are commonly called gods; but no one is a god merely because he is called a god.

Thus I defend supreme greatness in the Creator, not on the basis of the name “God,” nor its sound, nor the mark made by that name, but on the basis of the substance itself to which the name happens to belong. Finding this substance alone unborn, unmade, alone eternal and the maker of the universe, I both ascribe and vindicate supreme greatness not to its name but to its condition, not to its designation but to its essential state. And because the substance to which I ascribe it has already come to be called God, you suppose that I ascribe it to the name; whereas it is necessary for me to indicate by a name the substance to which I ascribe it—namely, the substance by which the one called God subsists and is accounted the supreme greatness from substance, not from name.

Finally, when Marcion vindicates this for his own god, he vindicates it according to condition, not according to the word. We therefore maintain that the supreme greatness which we ascribe to God by the law of substance, not by the chance of a name, must belong equally to the two who consist of that substance by virtue of which one is called God. For insofar as they are called gods—that is, supreme greatnesses—by the merit of a substance that is unborn and eternal, and for that reason great [Note: [Col. 0254D] Rhen. omits “and.”] and supreme, to that same extent one supreme greatness cannot be regarded as lesser and inferior to another supreme greatness.

If the felicity, sublimity, and integrity of the supreme greatness stand firm in Marcion’s god, they will stand equally firm in ours; if they do not stand firm in ours, neither will they stand firm in Marcion’s. Therefore, the two supreme greatnesses will be neither equal, because the rule of supreme greatness already established—which permits no comparison—prohibits it [Note: [Col. 0254D] “Once the rule has been established,” etc. Once that rule has been laid down, or that foundation cast, concerning which he has spoken in the three preceding chapters, there cannot be two gods, as Marcion of Pontus maintained. LE PR.]; nor unequal, because another rule of supreme greatness intervenes, admitting no diminution. You are trapped, Marcion, amid the tide of your own Pontus: the waves of truth engulf you from either side. You can establish gods neither equal nor unequal. For there are not two—so far as concerns the discussion of number properly speaking. Although the whole subject is a contest over two gods, we have for the present enclosed it within these lines; inside them we shall now engage with their individual properties.

## CHAPTER VIII

First, the Marcionites build their amazement upon haughtiness because they bring forward a new god, as though we were ashamed of the old God. Even boys are puffed up by new shoes; but once shod by the old schoolmaster [Note: [Col. 0254D] “Shod, their vainglory.” So in book III *Against Marcion*: “having put on as shoes the preparation of the Gospel of peace”; and in *On the Crown*: “shod with the equipment of the Gospel.” RIG.], their vainglory will soon be beaten out of them.

When, therefore, I hear of a new god in an old world and an old age, unknown and unheard of under the old God—a god whom, after so many ages in the past, no one knew, and who, ancient merely by virtue of that very ignorance, was revealed by a certain Jesus Christ [Note: [Col. 0255C] “Jesus Christ” in the accusative, others.], himself new despite his ancient names, and by no one before him—I give thanks for this boast of theirs, since with its powerful assistance I shall now prove that their profession of a new divinity is heresy. This will be the sort of novelty which also gave birth to gods among the pagans, each always under some new title of consecration. What god is new unless he is false? [Note: [Col. 0255C] “Not even Saturn,” etc. He demonstrates the falsity of gods from their novelty; he treated this matter in chapter 10 of the *Apologeticum*. [Col. 0255D] Accordingly, he says, Saturn by his sudden arrival naturally came to be called a celestial being. For the common people also call those whose ancestry is uncertain “sons of the earth.” I omit the fact that human beings were then still so uncultivated that they were stirred by the sight of any new man as though by something divine. Among the pagans, no commodity was more readily available than gods. Yet scarcely anyone was regarded as older than Saturn. Virgil, *Aeneid* VIII:

> First from ethereal Olympus Saturn came,  
> Fleeing Jove’s arms, an exile from his stolen realms.

LE PR.] Not even so great an antiquity as Saturn possesses today will prove him a god, because novelty once brought even him forward when it first consecrated him.

But living and genuine divinity is judged neither by novelty nor by antiquity, but by its own truth. Eternity has no time, for it is all time. What it produces, it cannot itself undergo. That which cannot be born has no age. If God is old, he will not exist; if he is new, he did not exist. Novelty testifies to a beginning; antiquity threatens an end. But God is as alien to beginning and end as he is to time, the arbiter and surveyor of beginning and end.

## CHAPTER IX

I know, of course, in what sense they boast of a new god: namely, new in being known. But I wished also to repel the very novelty of this knowledge [Note: [Col. 0255C] Perhaps “the novelty of knowledge.”], which strikes inexperienced minds, as well as novelty’s own natural attractiveness, and from this point onward to challenge them concerning an unknown god. For in opposing him as new in being known, they certainly demonstrate that he was unknown before being known.

Come, then, back to the lines and the starting position once more: persuade me that God could have been unknown. [Col. 0255C] [Note: [Col. 0255D] “I do indeed find,” etc. He alludes to the Apostle’s words in Acts XVII: “I found also an altar on which was written: ‘To an unknown God.’” Since this occurred at Athens, he rightly calls it Attic idolatry. Oecumenius, commenting on that passage, writes that the inscription was Greek, ἀγνώστῳ θεῷ (*agnōstō theō*, “to an unknown god”), and gives two explanations for it. The first is that, when a specter of Pan appeared near Parthenion to Athenians sent to the Lacedaemonians for aid against the Persians, and accused them of worshiping other gods while neglecting him, after winning the victory they erected not only a temple to Pan but also an altar to an unknown god, lest anything similar should happen afterward. The second is that, when they were suffering from a plague and the other gods, though invoked, brought no help, they understood that there was some God who was sending the plague and inscribed an altar “To the unknown god”; once this was done, the plague ceased. PAM. Jerome, in chapter II of his commentary on the *Epistle to Titus*, noted that the altar’s inscription was formulated thus: “To the gods of Asia, Europe, and Africa, to unknown and foreign gods.” LE PR.] I do indeed find altars prostituted to unknown gods; but that is Attic idolatry. [Note: [Col. 0256D] “Likewise, to uncertain gods; but that is Roman superstition.” Whenever they had felt an earth tremor or received a report of one, they ordered holidays for that event by an edict; but they refrained from proclaiming the god’s name, lest by naming one instead of another they bind the people to false religion. If anyone profaned those holidays and an expiatory sacrifice were required for the matter, he sacrificed a victim “whether to a god or to a goddess.” Aulus Gellius, book II, chapter 28. Tertullian himself cites Varro on this matter in book II *To the Nations*. RIG.] Likewise, I find altars to uncertain gods; but that is Roman superstition.

Moreover, uncertain gods are less known because less certain, and therefore unknown insofar as they are less certain. Which of these two titles shall we inscribe upon Marcion’s god? Both, I think: uncertain now and unknown in the past. For just as the known God, the Creator, made him unknown, so the certain God made him uncertain.

But I shall not wander farther in order to make my point. If God [Note: [Col. 0255C] “If” absent from Fran., Paris.] was unknown and lay concealed, some region of concealment overshadowed him—a region itself certainly new, unknown [Note: [Col. 0256C] “And” absent from Fran., Paris.], and likewise still uncertain; certainly some immeasurable region, and undoubtedly greater than the god whom it concealed. But I shall state my case briefly and pursue it most fully, prescribing [Note: [Col. 0256D] “Prescribing that [he will] make [it] fully equal.” “Prescribing to someone that there is one God” [the reading *cuinam* is uncertain]; that is, in forensic usage, replying to the opponents’ objections and refuting them. LE PR.] that God neither could have remained unknown, on account of his greatness, nor ought to have remained unknown, on account of his kindness—especially if, in both respects, he was more eminent than our Creator.

Yet because I observe that, in certain matters, the proof of every new and formerly unknown god must be challenged according to the pattern of the Creator, I must first establish that we do this rationally, so that I may employ the support of the principle already stated with greater firmness. Before all else, what sense does it make that you, who acknowledge God as Creator and confess that he was earlier in being known, should not understand that another god must be examined for you by the same methods through which you have already learned [Note: [Col. 0256C] “You have learned,” plural, Rhen.] to know God in the former? Every prior thing has furnished a standard in advance for what comes later.

Two gods are now proposed: one unknown, the other known. No question need be raised about the known one: it is established that he exists, because he would not have been known unless he existed. Concerning the unknown one the dispute presses: for he may also not exist, because, if he existed, he would have been known. Whatever is sought, therefore, remains uncertain so long as it is unknown; and so long as it is sought it is uncertain [Note: [Col. 0256C] “In uncertainty,” Rhen.]; and it may not exist so long as it is uncertain [Note: [Col. 0256C] “That,” i.e., the same reading.]. You have one God certain insofar as he is known, and another uncertain insofar as he is unknown.

If this is so, does it not seem to you to be defended by just reasoning that uncertain things should be tested according to the standard, pattern, and rule of certain things? Otherwise, if arguments drawn from uncertain things are applied to this case, which itself is still uncertain, the sequence of questions will become entangled through the examination of those equally uncertain arguments themselves, whose credibility is endangered by uncertainty; and the discussion will proceed into those interminable questions which the Apostle does not approve (1 Tim. I, 4).

If, however, the principles drawn from things certain, undoubted, and settled prejudge matters uncertain, doubtful, and unresolved, then certainly, where a diversity of condition is found, uncertain matters perhaps should not be challenged according to the pattern of certain ones, since the diversity of their principal condition frees them from the remaining demand for comparison. But when two gods are proposed, their principal condition is shared. For what God is [Note: [Col. 0257D] “For what is God?” Seml.], both are: unborn, unmade, eternal. This will be their principal condition. Let Marcion see to the other attributes, if he has arranged them in diversity; for they come later in the examination. Indeed, they will not even be admitted if the principal condition is established.

Moreover, it is established that both are gods, and thus that the condition of those under discussion is shared. When they are summoned under that condition for proof, if they are uncertain they must be challenged according to the pattern of those certain things with which they are judged to share the principal condition, so that they may likewise share in the manner of proof. Hence [Note: [Col. 0257D] “Accordingly I shall proceed most firmly.” I shall turn back upon them the arguments which they have directed against us. *Dirigere* means to proceed by law, or to bring suit and institute an action; thus Digest, book I, § *Si ego*, *If Part of an Inheritance Is Claimed*: “And let him also direct an action against the external possessor.” LE PR.] I shall accordingly maintain most firmly [Col. 0257B] that he is not God who is made uncertain today because he was unknown in the past; since, in the case of him whose existence is established, that existence is established by this very fact: that he was never unknown and therefore never uncertain.
