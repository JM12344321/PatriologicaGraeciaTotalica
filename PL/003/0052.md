## ARTICLE III. Another argument of Octavius concerning the one God, drawn from examples of earthly kingdoms and natural phenomena, is examined

From earthly empires, where no one can tolerate a partner in kingship, [Col. 0437D] Minucius derives another argument, by which he confirms and demonstrates not only that there is but one God, but also that all things are governed by the sovereignty of that one alone: “For when,” he says, “has a partnership in kingship ever begun in good faith, or endured without bloodshed?” Ennius had previously written, as quoted by Cicero:

> No partnership in kingship is sacred, nor does it admit good faith.  
> (Cicero, *On Duties*, I, p. 354, line 9.)

Our Minucius proves this with four examples, the last of which is presented by Cicero in the same place. But the more briefly our author reported them, the more obscurely he did so. For he never even calls by their own names the persons under discussion. It will therefore be appropriate to explain these matters somewhat more clearly.

[Col. 0438A] He thus began with the Persians in these words: “I pass over the Persians, who divined the sovereignty from the neighing of horses.” Yet who will understand this unless he knows, or recalls, the history of Darius, king of the Persians? For after the Magi had been killed and their base domination, as Valerius Maximus calls it, had been overthrown, Darius was appointed sole king by compact and agreement, after his horse, through the trickery of Oebares, had neighed just at sunrise (Valerius Maximus, book VII, ch. 2, Foreign Examples, § 2). See Herodotus, Justin the historian, Valerius Maximus, and others (Herodotus, book III, §§ 82, 85 ff.; Justin, toward the end of book I; Valerius Maximus, loc. cit.).

Nor are the following words of Minucius any clearer: “And I pass over, as a fable, the Theban pair who died”; or, still more obscurely and corruptly in the royal manuscript, [Col. 0438B] “through the dead fable.” In these few words Minucius briefly touches upon the celebrated tale, sung by Statius, of the brothers Polynices and Eteocles, sons of Oedipus, king of Thebes (Statius, books XI and XII of the *Thebaid*). The story is told as follows. When Oedipus voluntarily departed into exile, he surrendered his kingdom to those same sons on the condition that they should rule in alternate years. But after the elder, Eteocles, had reigned for the first year, he refused Polynices his turn to rule in the following year. Having been rejected, Polynices went to Adrastus, king of the Argives, and became betrothed to his daughter Argia. Then, after raising an army, together with Tydeus, Adrastus’ other son-in-law, he made war upon the Thebans. In that war the two brothers, Eteocles and Polynices, engaged in single combat and killed one another by mutually inflicted wounds. Finally, [Col. 0438C] when their bodies were placed upon the funeral pyre, the flame is said to have divided itself into two parts as a sign of their implacable hatred. Hear, if you please, Ovid sing of it in these verses:

> When, by design, a common sacrifice is made upon the altar  
> For the brothers who perished each by the other’s hand,  
> The dark ash, itself at variance, as though commanded by them,  
> Splits apart into two divisions.  
> (Ovid, *Tristia* V, elegy 5, lines 33 ff.)

There are Greek verses on this subject by Branor, which Ausonius rendered into Latin (Ausonius, *Epigram* 129). And, to omit Pausanias, Diodorus Siculus, Seneca, and others, Lucan alluded to it in these verses:

> A flame, showing that Latin affairs were at an end [“affairs” supplied; the source text appears defective],  
> Splits apart and rises with a twin summit,  
> Imitating the Theban pyres.

[Col. 0438D] Minucius then says: “The best-known memory of the twins concerns a kingdom of shepherds and a hut.” Correctly indeed, but much more clearly had he added the names of Romulus and Remus. For no one is unaware that Remus, because he had leapt over the new walls of Rome in mockery of his brother, was killed by his enraged brother Romulus—or at least on Romulus’ orders (Livy, book I; Florus, book I; and others).

He also withheld the names of those whom our author presents as his final example: “The wars,” he says, “of son-in-law and father-in-law spread throughout the whole world, and fortune could not contain two men within so great an empire.” But Cyprian, who compiled these latter three examples, as well as many others, from Minucius, as we have said, calls both men by name: “Pompey,” he says, “and Caesar were related by marriage, yet in their rivalry for power they did not preserve the bond of kinship” [Col. 0439A] (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 14). How they were related by marriage, and how one was father-in-law and the other son-in-law, you may learn from these words of Suetonius concerning Caesar: “In order to retain Pompey’s attachment and goodwill, he offered him in marriage Octavia, his sister’s granddaughter, who was married to Gaius Marcellus, and asked Pompey’s daughter in marriage for himself” (Suetonius, *Life of Caesar*, § 27), although she had been promised to Faustus Sulla. Earlier, however, he had said: “At about the same time he married Calpurnia, daughter of Lucius Piso, who was to succeed him in the consulship, and gave his own daughter Julia in marriage to Gnaeus Pompey, after breaking her prior engagement to Servilius Caepio” (§ 21). See also, if you please, Plutarch’s *Life of Pompey*, and other writers of Roman history (Plutarch, [Col. 0439B] vol. I, p. 644).

Furthermore, as Minucius proceeds, not only have human beings been unable to tolerate a kingdom divided between two persons, even when joined by the closest natural bonds; nature itself has been unable to tolerate it as well. “Look,” he says, “at the other creatures: among bees there is one king; among flocks, one leader; among herds, one ruler.” Cyprian repeated these words verbatim (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 14). From this both conclude that supreme power in heaven cannot be divided either, and therefore that there is but one God. After Lactantius—whom we shall discuss in his proper place—Jerome supplies several further examples of this sort, by which he undertook to prove that there ought to be one superior in a monastery: “Even dumb,” he says, “animals and herds of wild beasts follow their leaders. Among bees there are rulers. Cranes follow one bird in a letter-shaped formation. There is one emperor; one judge of a province. When Rome was founded, it could not have two brothers as kings at once, and it was consecrated by parricide. In Rebecca’s womb Esau and Jacob waged war. . . . On a ship there is one helmsman; in a household, one master. In even the greatest army, the signal of one commander is awaited” [Col. 0439C] (Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* I, ch. 3 ff.; *On the Anger of God*, ch. 11, p. 793; Jerome, Epistle 95, *To Rusticus the Monk*, p. 775).

From these examples Octavius, in the name of Minucius, argues thus against the pagan Caecilius: “Do you believe that supreme power is divided in heaven, and that the entire power of that true and divine sovereignty is split apart?” Cyprian puts it more briefly: “Much more,” he says, “is there [Col. 0439D] one ruler of the world” (Cyprian, ibid.). And Athanasius presses this argument in the following manner, so that everyone may perceive its force and weight even more readily: “The very order of its arrangement, and the harmonious cooperation of all its parts, clearly shows that there are not many leaders and governors of the world, but one. For if several ruled the world, that order could not be preserved; rather, everything would be full of confusion and disorder, as each drew all things according to his own will and fought against the other. For just as we held that many gods are no gods—*tēn polytheotēta atheotēta einai*, ‘polytheism is atheism’—so we must say that many rulers are no rulers. For when one overthrew the sovereignty of another, there would assuredly be no one [Col. 0440A] who exercised command and dominion, no one who ruled and governed” (Athanasius, *Oration Against the Gentiles*, § 38, p. 37).

Who, moreover, does not see that precisely the same point is proved by our Minucius through this reasoning? “Since it is evident that God, the parent of all, has neither beginning nor end; that he bestows birth upon all things, but perpetuity upon himself; that before the world existed he was himself a world unto himself; that he commands by his word all things whatsoever that exist, orders them by reason, and brings them to completion by his power.” We have seen, moreover, that the same things were said earlier by Tertullian and later by Cyprian. Do you see how powerful is this argument, by which those most learned men clearly demonstrate that, if there are several gods, there can in fact be no gods at all, and that it is therefore absolutely necessary that there be only one God and not many?

# CHAPTER V. ON THE NAME, NATURE, AND ATTRIBUTES OF GOD

[Col. 0440B]

## ARTICLE I. Who and what God is, what his name is, and how he is known by humankind, although he cannot be seen with bodily eyes

Although Octavius proved by arguments both certain and evident that God exists and that he is one, Caecilius nevertheless, still wavering with the customary obstinacy of the pagans, attempts to weaken their force and weight—if indeed he could ever have done so—by this question: “Whence, or who, or where is that one solitary and forsaken God, whom no free nation, no kingdoms, [Col. 0440C] not even Roman superstition knew? The Jews alone, a wretched people, themselves also worshipped one God.” To these questions of Caecilius and the other pagans, Minucius steadfastly replies that the God of the Jews and of the Christians is wholly the same; and Caecilius himself had already declared that the Christians were then dispersed throughout the whole world, while Tertullian, as we observed, had declared that they filled every place.

When asked who that God is, Octavius replies: “Do not seek a name for God: ‘God’ is his name. There is need of designations when a multitude must be distinguished individual by individual through the distinctive marks of proper names; for God, who alone exists, the word ‘God’ is the whole of his name. If I call him father, you may think him earthly; if king, you may suspect him to be fleshly; if lord, you will certainly understand him as mortal. Remove the additions made to the name, and you will perceive his radiance.” In Cyprian, who transcribed the opening phrase verbatim, the two words “individual by individual” are omitted, and the passage there reads: “God is his name; there is need of designations where a multitude must be distinguished through the distinctive marks of proper names” [Col. 0440D] (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 15). He adds no more there.

Lactantius, however, says of Trismegistus: “He wrote books, and indeed many of them, in which he affirms the majesty of the supreme and sole God, and calls him by the same names that we use, God and Father; and lest anyone should seek his name, he said that he is *anōnymos* (ἀνώνυμος), ‘nameless,’ because, by reason of his very unity, he has no need of the identifying property of a name” (Lactantius, book I, ch. 6, p. 30). Trismegistus’ words are cited by Lactantius as follows: *Ho de Theos heis, ho de heis, onomatos ou prosdeetai; esti gar ho ōn anōnymos* (Ὁ δὲ Θεὸς εἷς, ὁ δὲ εἷς, ὀνόματος οὐ πρὸσδέεται· ἔστι γὰρ ὁ ὢν ἀνώνυμος): “God is one, and the one has no need of a name; for he who is, is nameless.” [Col. 0441A] Lactantius himself then argues as follows: “God therefore has no name, because he is alone. Nor is there need of a proper designation except when a multitude requires differentiation, so that you may designate each person by his own distinctive mark and appellation. But for God, because he is always one, the proper name is ‘God.’” What he adds there after Trismegistus’ Greek words, he certainly seems to have drawn from our author, as he did many other things, and he rendered the author’s meaning clearer.

Minucius therefore teaches, first, that God’s nature is supremely simple and cannot be divided in any way whatsoever; that God himself is neither earthly, corporeal, nor mortal, and is therefore of a spiritual and immortal substance.

[Col. 0441B]

Second, God as he is in himself—in his nature and greatness—cannot indeed be known by us; nevertheless, any person may arrive at a manifest knowledge of him by removing from one’s conception of him everything earthly and imperfect.

Third, names are imposed only upon things that are distinct and different from one another, so that each individual thing may be designated. Therefore, he says, no name can be given to God, who is supremely simple and, alone before all creatures, has always existed—just as Trismegistus, cited a moment ago by Lactantius, said. Justin Martyr speaks in a wholly similar manner: *Theō de oute ho titheis onoma proypērchen, oute autos heauton onomazein ōēthē dein, heis kai monos hyparchōn* (Θεῷ δὲ οὔτε ὁ τιθεὶς ὄνομα προϋπῆρχεν, οὔτε αὐτὸς ἑαυτὸν ὀνομάζειν ᾠήθη δεῖν, εἷς καὶ μόνος ὑπάρχων): “It is altogether impossible [Col. 0441C] that any proper name should be attributed to God, because names are imposed to designate and distinguish the things that underlie them, which are various and many. But no one existed before God to give him a name; nor did he himself consider it necessary to designate himself by any name, since he exists as one and alone” (Justin, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, p. 19). Elsewhere too, more than once, he, like the other Fathers of the Church, denies that any proper name of God can be devised.

Nor is this contradicted by Origen’s response when Celsus objects that God is unnameable:

*Diastolēs deitai. Ei men gar, hoti ouden tōn en lexesi kai sēmainomenois dynatai parastēsai tas idiotētas tou Theou, alēthes esti to legomenon, ei ge kai pollai poiotētes ouk eisin onomastai. Tis gar dynatai onomasi diaphoran dounai poiotētos phoinikos, kai glykytētos ischados . . . ouden oun thaumaston ei ouk onomastos houtōs ho Theos. Ei de to onomaston lambaneis, kath’ ho hoion te estin onomasi parastēsai ti tōn peri autou, eis to cheiragōgēsai ton akroatēn, kai poiēsai noēsai peri Theou, kata to ephikton tē anthrōpinē physei, tina tōn peri autou, ouden atopon legein auton onomaston* (Διαστολῆς δεῖται . εἰ μὲν γὰρ, ὅτι οὐδὲν τῶν ἐν λέξεσι καὶ σημαινομένοις δύναται παραστῆσαι τὰς ἰδιότητας τοῦ Θεοῦ, ἀληθές ἔστι τὸ λεγόμενον, εἴ γε καὶ πολλαὶ ποιότητες οὐκ εἰσὶν ὀνομασταί . [Col. 0441D] τίς γὰρ δύναται ὀνόμασι διαφορὰν δοῦναι ποιότητος φοίνικος, καὶ γλυκύτητος ἰσχάδος . . . . οὐδὲν οὖν θαυμαστὸν εἰ οὐκ ὀνομαστὸς οὕτως ὁ Θεός . εἰ δὲ τὸ ὀνομαστὸν λαμβάνεις, καθὸ οἷον τέ ἐστιν ὀνόμασι παραστῆσαι τι τῶν περὶ αὐτοῦ, εἰς τὸ χειραγωγῆσαι τὸν ἀκροατὴν, καὶ ποιῆσαι νοῆσαι περὶ Θεοῦ, κατὰ τὸ ἐφικτὸν τῇ ἀνθρωπίνῃ φύσει, τινὰ τῶν περὶ αὐτοῦ, οὐδὲν ἄτοπον λέγειν αὐτὸν ὀνομαστόν):

“A distinction is needed. If he means that nothing contained in verbal expressions and significations can represent the distinctive properties of God, the statement is true, since many qualities are themselves unnameable. For who can give names distinguishing the quality of a date’s sweetness from that of a dried fig? . . . It is therefore no wonder if God is unnameable in this sense. But if ‘nameable’ is understood insofar as it is possible through names to represent something about him, so as to guide the hearer and enable him to understand certain things about God, as far as human nature is capable of attaining them, then it is not absurd to call him nameable” [Col. 0442A] (Origen, book LXI [source reading, apparently corrupt], *Against Celsus*, p. 320, quarto).

For Origen admits that no one can impose a proper name upon God, nor can God be named by anyone except insofar as this has been granted to human frailty.

Yet with these points established as entirely true and correct, our Minucius—and with him Cyprian and Lactantius—asserts that the word “God” is truly, insofar as it is permissible for a human being to name him, as Origen says, the complete and proper name of the true God. We earlier read this point confirmed by Tertullian in these words: The soul, “though weighed down by the body, though . . . it names God for this reason alone: because this God is properly the true, good, and [Col. 0442B] great God” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 17, p. 24). Therefore he asserts that this name “God” must be given to him alone and to no other.

But if anyone should inquire of our Minucius, as Caecilius did, who that God is, he will immediately receive this answer from him: “He cannot be seen, for he is brighter than sight; nor comprehended, for he is purer than touch”—although these last three words, transcribed by Cyprian, are absent from the royal manuscript—“nor appraised, for he is greater than the senses (infinite, immeasurable, and known in his greatness to himself alone. Our heart, however, is too narrow for understanding him), and therefore we appraise him worthily when we call him beyond appraisal” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 14). Cyprian reproduced all these words verbatim, except only those enclosed in parentheses. [Col. 0442C] But before both of them Tertullian had said the same thing in other words, in the following manner: “He is invisible, though he is seen; incomprehensible, though represented through grace; beyond appraisal, though appraised by human senses; therefore he is true and so great. For what can ordinarily be seen, comprehended, and appraised is less than the eyes by which it is occupied, the hands by which it is contaminated, and the senses by which it is discovered. But what is immeasurable is known to itself alone; this is what causes God to be appraised, while he does not admit of appraisal. Thus the force of his greatness presents him to human beings as both known and unknown.” Who is unaware that the same teaching was handed down again and again by Theophilus of Antioch, Gregory Nazianzen, and other Fathers of the Church? (Theophilus of Antioch, book I *To Autolycus*, shortly after the beginning, p. 71.)

[Col. 0442D] Yet against these so certain and true teachings some pagan will still rise up and, with Caecilius, insist that these are monstrosities and prodigies of Christian doctrines, by which Christians vainly affirm that they possess God. For they are compelled to confess that he can neither be shown nor seen. But what wonder is it, says Minucius, if Christians profess that they do not see with bodily eyes their God, whom they clearly recognize from his works? Do you yourself, whoever you are, pagan man, see with your eyes the wind and the blasts by which everything is driven? Is not the gaze of your eyes weakened and dulled when looking upon the sun, which enables all things to be seen? Do you see and behold your own soul, by which you live, are animated, and speak?

[Col. 0443A] And let no one, I ask, persuade himself that the vain subtleties and cavils of the pagans were refuted through these comparisons by our author alone. For the same comparisons were employed not only by the most ancient Fathers of the Church and others, but also by certain of the most learned pagan writers. To select a few from the many, Theophilus of Antioch says: “God created all things when they did not exist, so that through his works it might become known to humankind how excellent he is. The human soul exists in the human body; it is not seen, being invisible, but is known through the body’s movements. In the same way God cannot be seen by human and bodily eyes. . . . Just as, if someone sees a ship equipped with its rigging, ploughing the sea and putting into harbor, he assuredly believes that someone who governs it—a helmsman—is aboard it. . . . If a human being cannot fix his eyes upon the sun, which is indeed the least element”—in comparison with God, that is—“because of the excellence of its heat and power, much less can a mortal human being behold the glory of God, which is ineffable to humankind” [Col. 0443B] (Theophilus of Antioch, book I *To Autolycus*, p. 72). He says still more there; if you wish to see it written in Greek, you will readily be able to read it in his book.

Later Lactantius argues in precisely the same manner and also employs several comparisons of this kind: “Above all, since God cannot be seen by a human being, yet lest anyone for that very reason suppose that God does not exist because he is not seen by mortal eyes, among the other wonders of his ordinances he also made many things whose force is indeed apparent, although their substance is not seen, such as voice, smell, and wind; so that from the argument and example of these things we might also perceive God, even though he did not come before our eyes, from his power, effect, and works. What is clearer than a voice, stronger than wind, or more penetrating than an odor? Yet although these things are carried through the air, reach our senses, and strike them with their power, they are not perceived by the gaze of the eyes, but are sensed by other parts of the body. Similarly, God is not to be comprehended by our sight or by any other frail sense, but must be beheld with the eyes of the mind, when we see his glorious and marvelous works” [Col. 0443C] (Lactantius, book VII, *On the Blessed Life*, ch. 9). You may consult Irenaeus and others who set forth the same things; their discussions are too lengthy for us to transcribe them all.

[Col. 0443D] Moreover, Cicero himself—to oppose a pagan writer even to pagan people—spoke in a wholly similar manner: “Thus, although you do not see the human mind, just as you do not see God, nevertheless, just as you know God from his works, so from its memory of things, its inventiveness, the speed of its movement, and all the beauty of virtue, recognize the divine power of the mind” (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* I, p. 126, line 38).

By these arguments, therefore—arguments confirmed with equal authority by pagan and Christian writers—Minucius answers Caecilius most completely and utterly demolishes his reasoning. Accordingly, he rightly concludes: “From this we believe in God: because we can perceive him, though we cannot see him. For in his works and [Col. 0444A] in all the movements of the world we behold his power as always present: when it thunders, flashes, hurls lightning, and when it clears the sky,” as we showed above.

## ARTICLE II. By what arguments Minucius proved that God is infinite, eternal, immeasurable, and knows all things

Many of God’s attributes, or his supreme and divine perfections, are clearly explained by our Minucius. For he declares God infinite because he lacks beginning and end. Nor could Caecilius deny this, since he ought to have known the saying of Thales recorded by Laertius: *Ti to Theion?* (Τί τὸ Θεῖον;) Thales asked: “What is the divine?” *To mēte archēn echon mēte teleutēn* (τὸ μήτε ἀρχὴν ἔχον μήτε τελευτὴν): “That which has neither beginning [Col. 0444B] nor end” (Laertius, book I, *Life of Thales*, § 36).

Minucius then asserts that God is eternal, since he “bestows birth upon all things, but perpetuity upon himself,” and himself existed before the world. Tertullian appears to derive this eternity of God from Minucius’ foregoing argument, by which God is proved infinite. For he argues against Hermogenes as follows: “What other origin can be assigned to God than eternity? What other condition belongs to eternity than always to have existed and always to exist in the future, by virtue of having neither beginning nor end?” (Tertullian, *Against Hermogenes*, ch. 4, p. 267). But those two attributes, infinity and eternity, are so connected with one another that either can equally be proved from the other. Yet hear, if you please, Gregory Nazianzen arguing further concerning God’s eternity: [Col. 0444C] “God both always was,” he says, “and is, and will be—or, to speak more correctly, he always is; for ‘was’ and ‘will be’ are portions of our time and of our transitory nature. But he always is, and in this manner he names himself when he gives his oracle to Moses on the mountain. For he embraces within himself the totality of that which is, having no beginning and destined to have no end” (Gregory Nazianzen, Oration 38, p. 811). These same words occur in his second Oration above the fortieth [i.e. Oration 42] (ibid., Oration 42, p. 898). Therefore, since God receives neither his being nor whatever he is from another, but from himself, he must consequently be both infinite and eternal.

Caecilius also contended that another novel monstrosity of Christian doctrine was that its adherents proclaimed God to be immeasurable and present everywhere. For how, he asked, can one who is stationed in heaven be present in all places and at the same time “stretched throughout all things”? [Col. 0444D] What then? Minucius replies. Is not the sun, though fixed in heaven, present everywhere? Does it not mingle with all things without corruption of its light, which always remains whole and unimpaired? Yet far more is God not merely present to each individual thing, but also very near to us and infused within us. Indeed Minucius adds: “We do not merely act beneath him, but, as I might almost say, live with him.” From these words some may perhaps suspect that he had in mind the saying of the Apostle: “In him we live, move, and have our being” (Acts ch. XVII, v. 28). But since throughout the whole disputation against the pagans he drew nothing, as we observed, from our sacred Scripture, he surely advanced this reasoning because the pagans could neither weaken nor reject it. Seneca, certainly, though a pagan, had written in one of his Epistles: “He himself—God—renders service to the human race: he is everywhere and present to all.” Elsewhere he says: “Wherever you turn, there you will see him meeting you. Nothing is empty of him” [Col. 0445A] (Seneca, Epistle 95, p. 397 ff.; *On Benefits* IV, ch. 8). Finally, to omit further passages, he says again in another Epistle: “God comes to human beings; indeed, what is still nearer, he comes into human beings” (Epistle 73, p. 305). Since, therefore, all things are full of God, as Minucius rightly teaches after Thales and Aratus, all things must also be known to him [Col. 0445B] (Thales, in Aristotle, *On the Soul* I, ch. 5). Accordingly, he, the observer of all, knows all human actions and thoughts.

But Caecilius stubbornly insisted against all this that God was vainly and gratuitously imagined to be present in every place, deed, and thought. For what else, he says, did the proponents of this opinion thrust upon us by these words, except a God who is “troublesome, restless, and even shamelessly inquisitive”? Yet this was the very impious complaint of the Epicurean philosopher quoted by Cicero: “You have imposed upon our necks,” he says, “an everlasting master whom we must fear by day and night. For who would not fear a God who foresees, considers, and observes everything, thinks that everything concerns himself, and is inquisitive and full of business?” (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I, p. 203, line 29). Hence Lucretius both gives Epicurus the greatest thanks for driving away this fear and congratulates him most warmly. What of the fact that Cicero, when recounting Strato’s teaching that “all things were produced by nature,” afterward adds: “Thus he frees God from a great labor, and me from fear. For who, when he believes that he is watched over by God, can fail to dread the divine power by day and night, and, if any adversity occurs—as happens to everyone—to fear that it has justly befallen him?” [Col. 0445C] (Cicero, *Academica*, p. 33, line 47).

But those philosophers, and the Epicureans above all—men wholly given to pleasure—made these objections. For they were persuaded that, if God knew the actions and thoughts of human beings, he must be feared, [Col. 0445D] and that by this fear their pleasure and happiness would be disturbed. Nevertheless, God is in no way like other kings, who, as Minucius says, “know everything in their kingdom through the services of ministers”; rather, he knows all things through himself. Nor does he “need informers” in order to know them. The most ancient philosophers freely acknowledged this. For Thales of Miletus, as we learn from Laertius, “when asked whether a human being doing wrong escaped the gods’ notice, replied, *All’ oude dianooumenos* (ἀλλ᾽ οὐδὲ διανούμενος), ‘Not even when thinking of it’” (Laertius, book I, *Life of Thales*, § 36). Valerius Maximus likewise handed this down to posterity and gave the following reason for the pronouncement: “Thales spoke marvelously; for when asked whether human deeds escaped the gods, [Col. 0446A] he replied: ‘Not even their thoughts.’ Thus we should wish to possess not only pure hands, but pure minds as well, since we believed that the divine power was present to our secret thoughts” (Valerius Maximus, book VII, ch. 2, § 8, Foreign Examples). The fear that arises in human beings from this conviction, therefore, though it may be inconvenient to Epicureans and wicked persons, is rightly and deservedly judged by everyone else to be supremely good and just. See the authors cited on this passage of Laertius—Lucan, Euripides, and Theon the Sophist, who attributes the saying not to Thales but to Pittacus.

# CHAPTER VI. ON DIVINE PROVIDENCE

## ARTICLE I. The arguments by which Minucius shows that the world and all things, both collectively and individually, are governed by God’s providence are examined; with a discussion of Britain, the Nile, the Euphrates, and the Indus

[Col. 0446B]

From the same arguments by which Minucius proved that God exists, he infers that all things, both collectively and individually, which were first created by God’s command and will, and the whole world, are ruled and governed by his divine Providence. We have already set forth and examined those arguments above. No one can deny their supreme force and very great weight. Cicero, as we have said, in the principal portion of the second book of his *On the Nature of the Gods*, demonstrates against the Epicureans by the same lines of reasoning, though much more copiously than our author, that the world is administered by divine providence.

[Col. 0446C] Cicero, and after him Minucius, affirms and illustrates this teaching concerning divine Providence by the familiar comparison of a house. Our Minucius says: “If, upon entering some house, you saw everything cultivated, arranged, and adorned, you would surely believe that a master presided over it and was better than those good things. So too, in this house of the world, when you behold providence, order, and law in heaven and earth, believe that there is a lord and parent of the universe, more beautiful than the stars themselves and all the parts of the world.” Earlier, however, the Orator had said: “Just as, if someone entered a house, a gymnasium, or a forum, and saw the rational arrangement, measure, and discipline of everything, he could not judge these things to occur without a cause, but would understand that there was someone who presided and was obeyed; much more, amid such great movements, such great alternations, and the orderly arrangements of so many and such great things, in which immeasurable and infinite antiquity has never proved false, must he conclude that such great movements of nature are governed by some mind” [Col. 0446D] (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, p. 216, line 3 ff.).

God’s Providence provides not only for the totality of things, within which all things are contained, but also for the individual parts of the world. But let us hear, if you please, Minucius demonstrating this by several examples as well: “Britain,” he says, “is deprived of the sun, but is refreshed by the warmth of the sea that flows around it. The Nile moderates Egypt’s drought. The Euphrates cultivates Mesopotamia. The Indus is said both to sow and irrigate the East.” [Col. 0447A] In the royal manuscript, however, after the word “Nile” these words are read: “The river Euphrates serves Mesopotamia in place of rains.” Why these were altered in the printed editions is certainly unknown to us.

We have no doubt that these statements were excerpted from the second book of Cicero’s *On the Nature of the Gods*, where he likewise uses the same examples of the Nile, Euphrates, and Indus to prove God’s Providence. We have considered it unnecessary to repeat here Cicero’s words, which we transcribed above (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, p. 234, line 20; see above, ch. 2, art. 6).

But did not Minucius, you will say, wrongly and perversely present Britain first as an example? From where did he derive the claim that it is deprived of the sun? But tell me, I ask: did not Strabo similarly record the following concerning the Britons? *Epombroi d’ eisin hoi aeres mallon ē niphitōdeis; en de tais aithriais homichlē katechei polyn chronon; hōste di’ hēmeras holēs, epi treis monon ē tettaras hōras tas peri tēn mesēmbrian, horasthai ton hēlion; touto de kan tois Morinois symbainei, kai tois Menapiois, kai hosoi toutōn plēsiochōroi* (Ἔπομβροι δ` εἰσὶν οἱ ἀέρες μᾶλλον ἢ νιφιτώδεις . ἐν δὲ ταῖς αἰθρίαις ὁμίχλη κατέχει πολὺν χρόνον· ὥστε δι` ἡμέρας ὅλης, ἐπὶ τρεῖς μόνον ἢ τέτταρας ὥρας τὰς περὶ τὴν μεσημβρίαν, ὁρᾶθαι τὸν ἥλιον· τοῦτο δὲ κἀν τοῖς Μορινοῖς συμβαίνει, καὶ τοῖς Μεναπίοις, καὶ ὅσοι τούτων πλησίοχωροι): “Their air is more subject to rain than to snow; and even under clear skies a kind of mist persists for a long time, so that throughout the whole day the sun can be seen for no more than three or four hours around midday. The same thing also happens among the Morini, the Menapii, and those who live near them” [Col. 0447B] (Strabo, *Geography* IV, p. 200).

Would you like another witness to be produced? Tacitus is at hand, and these are his words: “The sky is disfigured by frequent rains and mists; severe cold is absent. The spans of daylight exceed the measure found in our part of the world, and the night is bright and, in the furthest part of Britain, short, so that you can distinguish the end and beginning of daylight by only a slight interval. They affirm that, if clouds do not obstruct it, the sun’s radiance is seen during the night, and that it does not set and rise, but passes across; evidently the level extremities of the lands, casting only a low shadow, do not raise up darkness, and night falls beneath the sky and the stars” [Col. 0447C] (Tacitus, *Life of Julius Agricola*, well after the beginning).

But if you should desire a third witness besides these, Caesar too is still ready to hand, for he crossed over to this island. He writes of it in the following manner: “The regions are more temperate than in Gaul, with less severe cold. . . . Halfway across this passage lies an island called Mona; several smaller islands besides are believed to lie opposite, concerning which some have written that at the winter solstice night lasts continuously for thirty days” [Col. 0447D] (Caesar, *Gallic War* IV, p. 743). But these writers, like Herodian, Servius, and Jornandes, were either deceived by a false report or are speaking of the furthest northern limits of Scotland. For Tacitus, already cited, says in the *Life of Julius Agricola*: “The night is bright and, in the furthest part of Britain, short, so that you can distinguish the end and beginning of daylight by only a slight interval” (Herodian, *History* III, p. 84; Servius, on Virgil’s *Georgics* I, p. 81; Jornandes, *On the Affairs of the Getae*, ch. 2).

Granted, another will say, let the matter be so; I concede it. But on what grounds, he will ask, does Minucius confidently affirm [Col. 0448A] that Britain is refreshed by the warmth of the sea? We shall therefore answer him in Cicero’s words: “The seas,” he says, “when stirred by the winds, grow so warm that it can readily be understood that heat is contained within those vast masses of moisture. For that warmth must not be regarded as external and adventitious, but as aroused by agitation in the innermost parts of the sea. This also happens to our bodies when they grow warm through movement and exercise” (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, p. 217, line 42 ff.). Yet he is speaking generally about every sea. What wonder, then, if Britain grows warm from the heat of the sea that surrounds it on every side? But perhaps it would have been better to say that this warmth is produced not from heat enclosed within the sea, but merely from the agitation of its saline particles and from its vapors.
