[Col. 0831C] I shall make one further reply to the earlier contention concerning allegorical Scriptures: we too are entitled to vindicate the resurrection of the body by appealing to the figurative language of the prophets. For behold, at the beginning the divine sentence [Note: [Col. 0832C] “In” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.], in declaring man to be earth—“Earth you are, and into earth you shall go”—plainly did so with reference to the substance [Col. 0832A] of the flesh, which had been taken from the earth and, as we have shown, had previously been called “man.” It thus gives me a rule for interpreting with reference to the flesh whatever God decrees against the earth in wrath or for it in grace. For the earth itself is not properly liable to his judgment, since it has committed neither good nor evil. It was indeed cursed because it [Note: [Col. 0832C] “Which,” Rhenanus, Semler.] drank in blood (Gen. 4); but even this was a figure of murderous flesh. For although the earth is capable of being benefited or harmed, that [Note: [Col. 0832C] “That” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.] too is for man’s sake, so that he may be benefited or harmed through what happens to the place in which he has his standing. [Note: [Col. 0832D] **What happens to the place in which he has his standing.** He calls the earth a *consistorium*, from *consistere*, “to stand,” because man stands upon it. Otherwise, we call *consistorium* the place in which cases are heard. RHEN.] All the more, therefore, will he himself bear the consequences of what even the earth suffers on his account. Accordingly, when God threatens the earth, I shall say that he is threatening the flesh instead; and when he promises something to the earth, I shall understand him to be promising it to the flesh instead. Thus in David (Ps. 96): “The Lord has reigned; let the earth [Col. 0832B] rejoice” [Note: [Col. 0832C] “Rejoiced,” Rhenanus, Semler.]—that is, the flesh of the saints, to which the fruit of God’s reign pertains. He then adds: “It saw, and the earth was shaken; the mountains melted like wax before the face of the Lord”—plainly, the flesh of the profane; and: “For they shall see him whom [Note: [Col. 0832C] “Him who,” Franeker; “him whom,” Junius.] they pierced” (Zech. 12:10). Indeed, if both statements are thought to have been uttered simply about the element of earth [Note: [Col. 0832C] “Earth,” Franeker, Rigault, Venice.], how will it be consistent for it both to be shaken and melted before the face of the Lord whose reign above made it rejoice?

Likewise in Isaiah: “You shall eat the good things of the earth.” These will be understood as the good things of the flesh, which await it when it has been refashioned and made angel-like in the kingdom of God, and when it is to obtain those things “which eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor have they entered into the heart of man” (1 Cor. 2). Otherwise, it is quite pointless for God to invite obedience by offering the fruits of the field and the foods of this life, [Note: [Col. 0832D] **Which, once creation had been assigned to man, he shares even with the religious and the blasphemous.** Seneca treated this passage exceptionally well in *On Benefits*, book IV, chapter 28: “God gave certain things universally to the human race, from which no one is excluded,” etc. RIG.] which [Col. 0832C] he shares even with the religious and the blasphemous, once creation had been assigned to man, “raining upon the good and the evil, and sending [Note: [Col. 0832C] “Sending,” Rhenanus, Semler.] his sun upon the just and the unjust” (Matt. 5). Faith will certainly be fortunate if it is to obtain [Note: [Col. 0832C] “They are,” Rhenanus, Rigault, Venice.] things which the enemies of God and Christ not only use but even abuse, worshipping [Note: [Col. 0832D] **Worshipping creation itself.** The world created by God. RIG.] creation itself [Col. 0833A] against its Creator. Will you reckon bulbs and truffles among the good things of the earth [Note: [Col. 0833C] “Earth,” Rhenanus, Semler.], when the Lord declares that man will not live even by bread?

Thus the Jews, by hoping only for earthly things, lose the heavenly, since they are ignorant both of the bread promised from heaven and of [Note: [Col. 0833C] **The oil of the divine anointing, etc.** He enumerates the delights of the Church, namely three sacraments: the Eucharist, Extreme Unction, and Baptism. He enumerates the same in book I *Against Marcion*, chapter 14: “But up to the present he has rejected neither the water with which he washes his people, nor the oil with which he anoints them, nor the bread with which he represents his own body.” He has used “the soul-strengthening” for “receiving strength,” a passive meaning for an active one, as he sometimes does. [Col. 0833D] LAC.] the oil of the divine anointing, the water of the Spirit, and the wine [Note: [Col. 0833C] “The wine of the Spirit and the water,” Venice, Rigault, the Pithou manuscript. “The water of the Spirit and” is absent from Franeker.] that strengthens the soul, from the vine of Christ.

Likewise, they regard the holy land itself, properly speaking Jewish soil, as instead to be interpreted of the Lord’s flesh, which for that reason is also holy land in all who have put on Christ: truly holy [Note: [Col. 0833D] **Through the indwelling of the Holy Spirit.** An *incolatus* is a city of which one is not a citizen but a resident. Modestinus, law 34, Digest, *De muneribus et honoribus*: “Residence indeed establishes one as an inhabitant”; law 3, Code, *De incolis*. But, as Alciatus says in *On the Meaning of Words*, “An *incola* is properly one who, after having been a newcomer, comes to dwell in some city with the intention of remaining there permanently and of maintaining the sum of his affairs there.” Christ was therefore rightly the dwelling place of the Holy Spirit; for the Spirit established in him a perpetual domicile, never interrupted, in a full stream. LAC.] through the indwelling of the Holy Spirit; truly flowing with milk and honey, through the sweetness of that hope; truly Judaea, through intimacy with God: “for he is not a Jew who is one outwardly, but he who is one inwardly.” Thus the same flesh is also the temple of God [Note: [Col. 0833C] “Of God” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.] and Jerusalem, hearing from [Col. 0833B] Isaiah (Isa. 51): “Rise, rise, Jerusalem; put on [Note: [Col. 0833C] “Put on,” Rhenanus, Semler.] the strength of your arm; rise as at the beginning of the day” [Note: [Col. 0833C] “Of the day” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.]—plainly, in that integrity [Note: [Col. 0833C] “In which,” Rhenanus, Semler.] which existed before the transgression’s offense. For how could exhortations and appeals of this kind apply [Note: [Col. 0834C] “Will apply,” Pamelius, Rhenanus.] to that Jerusalem which killed the prophets, stoned those sent to it, and finally crucified its own Lord? Nor indeed is salvation promised to any land whatever [Note: [Col. 0834C] “Land” is absent from Rigault and Venice.], since it must pass away together with the condition of the whole world.

Further, if anyone should venture to argue that the holy land is Paradise instead, let him grasp that it is also [Note: [Col. 0834C] “Even,” Pamelius, Franeker.] called the land of the fathers, namely Adam and Eve. Accordingly, restoration to Paradise will likewise be seen to have been promised to the flesh, which received the lot of dwelling in it and guarding it, so that man may be recalled there [Col. 0833C] in the same condition in which he was expelled [Note: [Col. 0834C] “Driven out,” others.] from it.

## CHAPTER XXVII

[Col. 0834A]

We can also allegorize the mention of garments in the Scriptures with reference to the hope of the flesh, because the Apocalypse of John also says (Rev. 3 and 14): “These are,” it says, [Note: [Col. 0833D] **Who have not defiled their garments.** Scripture combines different passages, as it sometimes does; or rather, it alludes to different passages, from whose words it composes a third statement, explaining the words of one passage through the words of another. LAC.] “those who have not defiled their garments with women”—plainly signifying virgins and those who have castrated themselves for the sake of the kingdoms of heaven. Accordingly, they shall be in white garments, that is, in the brightness of [Note: [Col. 0833D] **Unmarried flesh.** “Virginal,” the Pithou manuscript; *innube* [“unmarried”]. RIG.] unmarried flesh. In the Gospel too, the wedding garment can be recognized as the holiness of the flesh.

Accordingly, when Isaiah teaches what kind of fast the Lord has chosen, he adds concerning the reward of goodness: [Note: [Col. 0833D] **Then, he says, your early light shall break forth.** [Col. 0834C] The Author renders Isaiah 58 as “early,” where others have “morning.” The Greek word πρώϊμον means “early” or “premature.” He then reads ἱμάτια, that is, “garments,” where present-day Greek manuscripts and some ancient ones have ἰάματα, that is, “healings.” There are two readings of this passage even among the Greeks, for agreement with Tertullian is found not only in Blessed Cyprian, *On Almsgiving and Works*, near the beginning, and *On the Lord’s Prayer*, near the end, and Blessed Augustine, sermon 172, but also in Chrysostom, homily 55 on Matthew 16. For more on this, see Leo Castrius on Isaiah, where he also shows that even those who read otherwise interpret this passage of the resurrection. PAM.] “Then,” he says (Isa. 58), “your early light shall break forth, and your garments shall rise swiftly.” He certainly did not mean an undergarment of silk [Note: [Col. 0834C] “I shall write beneath,” Rhenanus; “silken undergarment,” Junius.] or a cloak, but intended the flesh to be understood; he foretold the rising of the flesh destined to rise again from the setting of death. [Col. 0834B] Thus an allegorical defense of bodily resurrection is also available to us.

For when we read (Isa. 26), “My people, enter your storerooms for a little while, until my wrath has passed,” the storerooms will be the tombs in which those who, at the end of the age, under the final wrath, have departed through the violence of Antichrist will have to rest briefly. Or why did he use the term “storerooms” rather than that of some place of reception, unless because [Note: [Col. 0834D] **In storerooms salted flesh is kept.** These rooms, which he here calls *cellae promae*, he will shortly call *cellaria*. Sulpicius Severus uses this word in book II of the *Sacred History*, where he speaks of the bishops summoned to Ariminum: “The Emperor had ordered that provisions and storerooms be supplied to all of them.” RIG.] in storerooms salted flesh is preserved and laid aside for use, to be brought out from there at its proper time? In like manner, bodies treated with the spices of burial are set apart in mausoleums and monuments, to come forth from there when the Lord commands.

Since it is fitting to understand it in this way—for what shelters of [Col. 0834C] storerooms will preserve us from the wrath of God?—the very words “until the wrath has passed,” the wrath that will destroy Antichrist, show that after the wrath the flesh will come forth [Col. 0835A] from the tomb [Note: [Col. 0835C] “From the tombs,” Franeker.] into which it had been placed before the wrath. For nothing is brought out of storerooms except what has been put into them; and after Antichrist has been eradicated, the resurrection will take place.

[Note: [Col. 0834D] **Of storerooms.** A *cellarium* is properly a place in which table provisions, or surplus necessities for sustenance, are gathered. But a distinction relevant to Tertullian’s point has been observed between a *promptuarium* and a *cellarium*: things intended for only a few days are stored in the latter, but things intended for a long time in the former. Tertullian therefore indicated by the term *cellarium* that death would not last long. St Gregory the Great mentions the word *cellarium* in book II of the *Dialogues*, chapter 28: “The monk who was in charge of the storeroom [Col. 0835C] did indeed hear the words of Benedict’s command, [Col. 0835D] but delayed carrying them out.” PAM.]

## CHAPTER XXVIII

We know, moreover, that the resurrection is proclaimed by the prophets not only through words but also through objects—both represented and enacted. When Moses puts his hand into his bosom and brings it out as though dead, [Note: [Col. 0835D] **And brings it out as though dead.** This account is related in Exodus 4, where there appears only ἐγένηθη ἡ χεὶρ αὐτοῦ λεπρῶσα, “and his hand became leprous.” But below, in chapter 55, he explains the matter more clearly: “Moses’ hand,” he says, “is changed, and indeed becomes bloodless, after the likeness of something dead,” etc. Here, then, he calls it dead by a certain resemblance, not in reality. St Jerome uses the same testimony against John of Jerusalem to prove the resurrection: “Moses’ hand was changed to the whiteness of snow and afterward restored to its former color; amid both changes it remained the same hand.” LE PR.] and then puts it in again [Note: [Col. 0835D] **And then puts it in again.** What he has just called “putting it into his bosom.” LE PR.] and displays it alive, does this not portend what is to happen to the whole man? For the threefold power of God was indicated [Note: [Col. 0835C] “Was denoted,” Paris.] by those three signs in their proper order: first, it would subdue for man the serpent, the devil, formidable though he is; next, it would draw [Note: [Col. 0835C] “Would be going to draw,” Rigault, Venice.] the flesh back from the bosom of death; and thus [Col. 0835B] it would exact [Note: [Col. 0835C] “Would seek out,” Junius.] an account of all blood in judgment.

[Note: [Col. 0835D] **Concerning which, in the same prophet.** Namely Moses, tacitly indicating that he is the author both of Exodus and Genesis, from which he cites these testimonies, and therefore of the whole Pentateuch as well. Certainly this is evident regarding Exodus and Deuteronomy from the words of Scripture in Exodus 24 and Deuteronomy 31; and Philo, in *On the World*, and Josephus somewhere, assert it of the entire work. PAM.]

Concerning this, God says in the same prophet [Note: [Col. 0835C] *Prophetem*, Pamelius; *propheta*, Venice, incorrectly.] (Gen. 9): “For I shall also require your blood from all beasts, and from the hand of man, and from the hand of his brother I shall require it.” Now nothing is required unless it is demanded back; nothing is demanded back unless it will also be restored. And certainly that which is demanded back and required in the name of vengeance will be restored. For that which will have no existence at all cannot be avenged. But it will exist when it is restored, so that it may be avenged. Accordingly, whatever is foretold concerning blood is directed toward the flesh, without which there will be no blood. The flesh will be raised so that the blood may be avenged.

There are also certain statements uttered in such a way that, although they lack the cloud of allegory, they nevertheless thirst for an interpretation of their own plain meaning. [Note: [Col. 0836C] **Such as that passage in Isaiah, etc.** The words Tertullian reports from Isaiah are not found verbatim—αὐτολέξει—anywhere in the entire prophecy; yet they can be gathered from those passages in which God displays his power by showing that he can cast the people into the straits of death and recall them from death. They are found more clearly in 1 Kings 2: “The Lord puts to death and gives life”; and in the Psalm. Isaiah 38, however, reads: “You will chastise me and give me life.” LE PR.] Such is the passage in Isaiah: “I shall kill and [Col. 0835C] give life.” Certainly, after he has killed, he will give life. Therefore, killing through death, he will give life through resurrection. It is the flesh that is killed [Note: [Col. 0835C] “He has killed,” Rhenanus.] through death; therefore, it is likewise the flesh that will be given life through resurrection. Certainly, if to kill is to take the soul away from the flesh, then to give life, [Col. 0836A] its opposite, is to restore the soul to the flesh. The flesh must rise again, since the soul taken from it through killing must be restored to it through vivification.

## CHAPTER XXX

I know how they also shake this proclamation into an allegorical interpretation. Because, by saying, “These bones are the whole house of Israel,” [Note: [Col. 0837C] “Israel is,” in Rhenanus and Semler; “is Israel,” in Pamelius.] God made these bones an image of Israel [Note: [Col. 0837C] “For Israel,” Semler; “of Israel,” the Pithou manuscript.] [Note: [Col. 0837D] **Made them an image of Israel.** The passage here in question is, in my judgment, most excellently explained by Tertullian as referring to the resurrection of the dead; nor do I find attractive the interpretation that the Author refutes, which is that of almost all more recent writers. By like reasoning, Blessed Jerome, on Ezekiel 37, proves the resurrection of the dead from the fact that the restoration of the Jewish people is promised and is supported by the resurrection; for if there were no resurrection, the promise would be empty, since no one can prove anything certain by things that do not exist. See the passage. LE PR.] and [Note: [Col. 0837C] “But,” Rhenanus, Semler.] transferred them from their own proper condition, they claim that this proclamation of resurrection is figurative, not literal. For they say that the condition of the Jews was represented as in some fashion dead, dried up, and scattered over the plain of the world. Accordingly, the image of resurrection is allegorized as applying to that condition, because it is to be gathered together again and fitted bone to bone—that is, tribe to tribe and people to people—and clothed again as a body with the flesh of its resources [Note: [Col. 0837C] “The capacity of the flesh,” Rhenanus, Semler.] and the sinews of its kingdom. In this way it is to be led out of the tomb—that is, from the most sorrowful and hideous dwellings of captivity—and, under the name of refreshment, breathe again, and thereafter live in its own land, Judaea.

And what after this? They will undoubtedly die. And what after death? No resurrection, I suppose, if this is not itself the resurrection revealed to Ezekiel. But the resurrection is proclaimed elsewhere too; therefore this will also be a resurrection, and they rashly convert it into a description of Jewish affairs. Or, if that resurrection which we defend is a different one, it makes no difference to me, provided that there is both a resurrection of bodies and a resurrection of Jewish affairs. Finally, by the very fact that the restoration of the Jewish condition is represented by the re-embodiment and reanimation [Note: [Col. 0837D] **Re-embodiment and reanimation.** It is ἀναψύχωσις, “revivification”; those who write *readunatione*, “reunion,” wrongly correct it. LE PR.] of the bones, it is also proved that this will happen to the bones themselves. For a figure could not be constructed from bones if precisely this were not also going to happen to the bones.

[Note: [Col. 0837D] **For even if it is a fabrication of truth.** He means to indicate an example—or rather a metaphor or likeness—which is employed to fashion or represent the truth. LE PR.]

For even if it is a representation of truth in an image, the image itself exists in the truth of its own reality. It must first exist for itself [Col. 0838A] before it can be configured to something else. A likeness cannot properly be made from emptiness; a parable cannot be drawn from nothing. Thus one will also have to believe in the stated restoration of flesh and breath to the bones, from which may be expressed the alleged restoration of Jewish affairs.

But it is more reverent to defend the truth by the authority [Note: [Col. 0838C] “By the authority and simplicity of its truth,” Paris.] of its own plain meaning, as the sense of the divine declaration demands. For if this vision concerned Jewish affairs, immediately after the condition of the bones had been revealed it would have added, “These bones are the whole house of Israel,” and then the rest. But when, after the bones have been displayed, God mentions what they say against their own hope [Note: [Col. 0838C] “Who,” Franeker.]—Israel not yet having been named—and tests the prophet’s faith: “Son of man, shall these bones live?” so that he too answers, “Lord, you know,” God certainly would not have tested the prophet’s faith concerning something that would not happen, of which Israel had never heard, and which ought not to be believed.

But because the resurrection of the dead was indeed being proclaimed, while Israel, dissenting in its unbelief, was scandalized by it, and, looking upon [Note: [Col. 0838D] **The condition of a burial grown old.** Elegantly said of a late and prolonged delay in burial, just as things with a prolonged and long-lasting life grow old; similarly in chapter 32: “how like a coffin, how like a tomb, how like the old age of some resting and hidden burial.” LAC.] the condition of burial grown old, despaired of resurrection—or rather, did not direct its mind toward it but toward its surrounding circumstances—therefore God first prepared both the prophet, as though [Note: [Col. 0838C] “As though,” Rigault, Venice.] he too were doubtful, for constancy in proclamation by revealing the order of resurrection, and commanded the people to believe what he had revealed to the prophet. He said that they themselves, who did not believe that they would rise, were the bones destined to rise. Finally, in the conclusion he says: “And you shall know that I, the Lord, have spoken and accomplished it”—that is, he will certainly accomplish what he had spoken. Otherwise, he would not accomplish [Col. 0838C] what he had spoken if he were to accomplish something different.

## CHAPTER XXXI

Certainly, if the people too had been murmuring allegorically about their bones having dried up and their hope having been lost, complaining of what resulted [Note: [Col. 0838C] “Arousal,” Rhenanus, Semler.] from their dispersion, then God might deservedly be thought [Note: [Col. 0838C] “Is thought,” others.] to have consoled [Note: [Col. 0838D] **A figurative despair.** For by a figurative proverb they explained their extreme calamity, saying, “Our bones have dried up”; and God consoles them in the same manner, through that prophecy and the vision of the dry bones. LAC.] a figurative despair with a figurative promise.

[Note: [Col. 0838D] **But since the injury of dispersion, etc.** The Author seems to have thought that Ezekiel delivered this oracle before that calamity of the Israelite people. This appears false, since Ezekiel’s prophecy came after the captivity. But Tertullian can be understood as meaning, not that he wished to place it entirely before the captivity, but before the complete dispersion and most bitter oppression [Col. 0839D] of Israel. Yet the Author does not cling too tightly to this opinion, since he immediately confirms his interpretation by another route when he says, “Although, even if Israel was then grieving under some conflict involving present circumstances,” etc. For when Ezekiel prophesied, this dispersion had already occurred. Thus Jerome says that, through the parable of resurrection, the restoration of Israel is prophesied—Israel being captive in Babylon at that time. Epiphanius, in *Lives of the Prophets*, records that Ezekiel prophesied in captivity. LAC.]

But since the injury of dispersion had not yet befallen the people, while the hope of resurrection had very often collapsed among them, and the manifest destruction of bodies was undermining confidence in the resurrection [reading contextually restored from uncertain text], so too God was rebuilding the faith which the people were destroying. Although, even if Israel was then grieving [Note: [Col. 0839C] “Was clinging,” Rhenanus, Semler.] under some conflict involving present circumstances, [Note: [Col. 0839D] **It would not for that reason have to be understood as a parable, etc.** He proves that this restoration of the Israelites was not so much a revelation by which God wished to restore them as it was a testimony to the resurrection. LE PR.] the intention of the revelation would not for that reason have to be understood as a parable, but as testimony to the resurrection, so as to raise them toward that hope—namely, of eternal salvation and the more necessary restoration—and turn them away from concern for present circumstances.

For this purpose the other prophets [Note: [Col. 0839C] “Prophets,” Venice.] also say: “You shall come forth from your tombs like calves released from their bonds, and you shall trample your enemies” (Mal. 4:3). And again: “Your heart shall rejoice, and your bones shall spring up like grass” [Note: [Col. 0839C] “Swiftly,” Rhenanus, the Pithou manuscript.] (Isa. 66), because grass too is restored from the dissolution and decay of its seed.

In sum, if it is contended that the image of the rising bones properly concerns the condition of Israel, why is the same hope announced not only to Israel but also to all nations—the hope both of remains being re-embodied and reanimated, and of the dead being raised [Note: [Col. 0839C] “Raised,” Rhenanus, Semler.] from their tombs? For it was said concerning all (Isa. 26): [Note: [Col. 0839D] **The dead shall live.** In Greek it is “shall rise,” ἀναστήσονται. Isaiah 26, according to the Septuagint. LE PR.] “The dead shall live, and those in the tombs shall rise; for the dew that comes from you is healing for their bones.”

Likewise elsewhere (Isa. 66): “All flesh shall come to worship in my sight, says the Lord.” When? When the condition of this world has begun to pass away. For above it says: “As the new heaven and the new earth which I make stand in my sight, says the Lord, so shall your seed stand.” Then, therefore, what he adds will be fulfilled: “And they shall go forth”—certainly from their tombs—“and shall see the limbs of those who have acted impiously, because their worm shall not die and their fire shall not be extinguished, and they shall be a spectacle to all flesh”—namely, to all flesh which, having been raised and having come forth from the tombs, will worship the Lord for this grace.

## CHAPTER XXXII

But lest it appear that the resurrection is proclaimed only of those bodies which are committed to tombs, you have it written (Rev. 3): [Note: [Col. 0839D] **And I shall command the fish, etc.** Revelation 20, although it seems to be a different passage; but the ancients sometimes cite certain things from Scripture which nevertheless are not extant. LE PR.] “And I shall command the fish [Col. 0840A] of the sea, and they shall belch forth the bones that have been consumed; and I shall join [Note: [Col. 0839C] “They shall join,” Rhenanus.] joint to joint and bone to bone.”

“Therefore,” you say, “the fish will also be raised, as will the other beasts and [Note: [Col. 0840D] **And flesh-eating birds.** Σαρκοβόροι, “flesh-eating,” birds. It was the heretics’ objection that those who had been torn apart by beasts and birds could not rise. Tertullian refutes them here. LE PR.] flesh-eating birds, so that they may vomit back [Note: [Col. 0839C] “May vomit,” Rhenanus, Semler.] those whom they ate—because in Moses too you read that blood will be required from all beasts?” Certainly not. But beasts and fish are named for the restoration [Note: [Col. 0839D] “In the restoration,” Pamelius, Franeker.] of flesh and blood, so that the resurrection even of devoured bodies may be expressed all the more strongly, since an accounting is proclaimed even from the devourers themselves.

I also consider Jonah an adequate proof of this divine power, since he emerges [Note: [Col. 0840C] “Is vomited forth,” Latinus.] from the belly of the fish incorrupt in both substances, flesh and soul. And surely, over three days, the entrails of the sea-monster would have been just as capable [Note: [Col. 0840C] “As,” or “as though,” Rhenanus.] of digesting the flesh as any coffin, tomb, or long duration [Col. 0840B] of a resting and concealed burial.

[Note: [Col. 0840D] **Without prejudice to the fact that beasts, savage toward the Christian name, etc.** These words, spoken by Tertullian with some irony, indicate this interpretation of the heretics concerning the passage in Genesis 9 which the Author recently mentioned and had adduced in chapter 28 to prove the resurrection as well. But he immediately proceeded to turn the heretics’ escape back against them. LAC.]

This is without prejudice to the fact that “beasts” [Note: [Col. 0840C] “Savage beasts,” Rhenanus, Semler.]—men exceptionally savage toward the Christian name—or even the angels of wickedness themselves were represented figuratively, and from them blood will be exacted by the payment of vengeance.

Who, then, is more disposed to learn than to presume; more diligent in believing than in disputing; more reverent toward divine wisdom than wanton with his own opinions? Hearing that God has ordained something for flesh, skins, sinews, and bones, will he devise some other meaning for them, as though what is proclaimed concerning these substances were not ordained for man? Either nothing is ordained for man—not the generosity of the kingdom, not the severity of judgment, not resurrection, whatever it may be—or, if it is ordained for man, it must necessarily be ordained for those substances [Col. 0840C] of which the man for whom it is ordained is constructed.

I also ask these exceedingly subtle transformers of bones, flesh, sinews, and tombs [Note: [Col. 0840D] **Why, whenever something is declared concerning the soul, etc.** He asks the heretics to explain why they interpret statements in Scripture about bodily resurrection as referring to the soul, yet are entirely unwilling to let statements about the soul extend to the body. LAC.] why, whenever something is declared concerning the soul, they interpret the soul as nothing other than the soul and do not transform [Note: [Col. 0840C] “Pierce,” Rhenanus, Rigault, Semler, Venice.] it into an argument for some other thing; yet whenever something is proclaimed concerning some bodily substance, they assert that it is anything whatever rather than what is named.

[Col. 0841A] [Note: [Col. 0841D] **If bodily things are parables.** If, among the heretics, the things foretold concerning the body are parables, and they interpret them figuratively and parabolically, why do they not also extend to the body the things said concerning the resurrection of the soul? LAC.] If bodily things are parables, then so are things pertaining to the soul; if things pertaining to the soul are not parables, neither are bodily things. For man is body just as much as he is soul, so that one kind cannot admit enigmas while the other excludes them.
