To come, then, to each of their punishments: “At one time,” says our author, “we are banished to islands” (*ibid.*, ch. 12, p. 341); but this punishment of exile was certainly lighter than all the others. “At another time we are condemned to the mines” (*ibid.*, ch. 12, p. 341), that is, to excavate them with the greatest toil. Very many, moreover, were thrust into dreadful prisons to waste away from want and hunger, had not other Christians, as we said above, come to their aid. Tertullian, however, treats these matters more fully in his brief but excellent book *To the Martyrs* (the same, *To the Martyrs*).

Furthermore, he says, “the sides of Christians are scraped with claws” (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. [Col. 0964C] 12, p. 339). These claws were iron instruments fashioned like the claws of wild beasts, with which the pagans furrowed and tore apart the Christians’ sides. Hence he later said: “As we are stretched out toward God upon the rack, or doubtless upon some other similar machine, let the claws dig into us” (*ibid.*, ch. 30, p. 441). Prudentius likewise sang:

> Inflamed by these words, Asclepiades ordered  
> His body to be disembowelled, suspended aloft  
> Upon the rack, and stretched by hooks and claws.  
>   
> (Prudentius, *On Saint Romanus*, v. 107 ff.)

Tertullian continues: “We are driven to the beasts,” or are cast to them in the theatre so that they may devour us: “the beasts,” as he afterward described it, “leap upon us” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 12, p. 341; *ibid.*, ch. 30, p. 445). [Col. 0964D] How frequent this punishment of Christians was may be gathered from the fact that, whenever any public calamity occurred, the pagans immediately cried out, as he likewise testified, “The Christians to the lion!” (*ibid.*, ch. 40, p. 479). In another book, while censuring and condemning the assemblies of the pagans, he says: “There daily demands are made that lions be sent against us” (the same, *On the Spectacles*, ch. 27). Elsewhere again he declares that Christ’s enemies have already been placed beneath his feet, “though every assembly of the populace still cries, ‘The Christians to the lion!’” (the same, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 22). What more need be said? He warns the enemies of chastity that they need not fear that there will be no one to shout, “The Christians to the lion!” (the same, *Exhortation to Chastity*, ch. 12).

And this was precisely the cry raised against [Col. 0965A] Polycarp, as we learn from Eusebius (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, bk. IV, ch. 15, p. 32). Cyprian likewise wrote of himself: “Immediately upon the first outbreak of the disturbance, when the people had demanded me with violent shouting” (Cyprian, epistle 20). Pontius teaches us in the following words from his *Life* what they had demanded: “Especially since, by repeatedly renewed acclamations, he was demanded for the lion” (Pontius, *Life of Cyprian*, p. 4). I would therefore have you consider whether our Tertullian was also aiming at this when he said of the Roman populace: “None are more insistent in demanding the Christians than the mob.” For common people demanded and cried “The Christians to the lion!” far more frequently than others. If, however, you think that he is speaking there of any form of death demanded by them, and can establish this, we shall not offer much resistance. Meanwhile, however, let us pass to other [Col. 0965B] torments of the Christians.

“Some,” says our author, “were placed upon crosses” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 12, p. 339) and stakes; or, as he afterward said, “crosses suspend them.” “Others lay down their necks”; or, as he preferred to say elsewhere, “swords sever their throats” (*ibid.*, ch. 30, p. 445). Others, moreover, “are burned by fires,” or “fires lick them,” as we afterward read in his words (*ibid.*, loc. cit.).

In his book *On Modesty*, however, he explains these various kinds of punishment thus: “Imagine the martyr now beneath the sword, with his head already poised for the blow; imagine him upon the cross, with his body already outstretched; imagine him upon the stake, with the lion already let loose; imagine him upon the axle, with the fire already built up” (the same, *On Modesty*, ch. 22). Yet he declares that Christians were tormented not only by these tortures. For in another book [Col. 0965C] he mentions not merely “the heavy sword, the lofty cross, the fury of beasts, and the supreme punishment of fire,” but also “every device of the executioner in yet more monstrous torments,” which human ingenuity, more cruel than wild beasts, could contrive (the same, *To the Martyrs*). And so, in those few latter words, he indicated clearly enough that there was no kind of torment with which Christians were not then racked.

Since, however, the punishment of fire was more grievous than the others—or, as we have just seen him write, was the supreme punishment—the pagans demanded it against Christians more frequently. Hence, as we read there in our manuscripts, they called them, with sarcasm and mockery, *sarmenticii* and *semaxii* “now,” that is, in his own time (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 53). The old editions, however, read *semissii*; [Col. 0965D] while some would read *semassii*, and afterward *assis* instead of *axis*, as though Tertullian had meant to say that Christians were bound to a stake or pole of half an *as*. But in what sense? Surely not in the meaning you assert? Yet that is hardly probable. Beyond doubt, the reading of our manuscripts and of all the others, with only one exception—and that perhaps corrupt—appears preferable and genuine. For Tertullian himself, the best interpreter of his own meaning, immediately added: “because, bound to a stake of half an axle, we are burned amid a surrounding mass of brushwood” (*ibid.*). Thus he said that Christians were tied to a stake or pole and burned alive, brushwood having been piled all around them and set alight.

You will say that this is correct; but why is the stake called “half an axle”? Observe, I ask, that by the term *stipes* is meant, as Festus says, a piece of wood fixed in the earth (Festus, at the word *Stipes*). The term *axis*, as the same Festus notes, [Col. 0966A] has various meanings (the same, at the word *Axis*). Sometimes it is used for the northern region of the sky; sometimes for the rounded shaft about which a wheel revolves, whence poets occasionally use it for the whole chariot; and finally, for a sawn plank. Tertullian appears to use the term *axis* in the second sense and to signify that martyrs were sometimes bound and burned upon a whole axle, but more frequently upon a stake—that is, a piece of wood fixed in the earth—which was shorter by half than an axle. The pagans therefore insulted all Christians with the derisive name *semaxii*.

Yet the monstrous and utterly horrible cruelty and fury of the pagans [Col. 0966B] was not sated by such great and dreadful punishments. For, in the very frenzy of Bacchanals, “they do not spare even dead Christians,” says Tertullian; “but tear them from the repose of burial, from what is a kind of asylum of death—some when already altered, others when no longer whole—and cut them up and pull them apart” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 37). In another dissertation (Dissertation on Lactantius, ch. 10, art. 2), we disclosed with what savagery the pagans raged against the bodies of dead and buried martyrs.

## ARTICLE II. How the blood of the martyrs was the seed of Christians, and possessed greater power to persuade than Cicero, Seneca, Diogenes, Pyrrho, Callinicus, and other pagan philosophers and orators

[Col. 0966C] So far were the martyrs’ punishments that we have recounted—punishments that can scarcely even be heard without horror—from deterring other Christians from professing their religion that, made stronger by them, they drew more pagans to embrace it, and thus their number was continually increased. For when the pagans themselves had witnessed the martyrs’ many and great tortures and their altogether astonishing constancy, they were thereby induced to investigate more carefully the cause of such great courage; and when they had discovered and grasped it, they became Christians.

If anyone is unwilling to believe us, let him hear our Tertullian: “Who,” he says, “on beholding the supreme constancy of some martyr amid his torments, is not shaken by contemplating it and moved to inquire what truly lies within the matter? Who, once he has inquired, does not join us? Once he has joined us, who does not desire to die?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 535). [Col. 0966D] That is, once he has recognized and confessed the truth of that religion, he then desires to end his life through any tortures and punishments whatsoever. In his address *To Scapula* he likewise spoke these words: “Everyone who beholds such great endurance is struck by some misgiving and is kindled to inquire what is at issue; and when he has learned the truth, he himself immediately follows it” (the same, *To Scapula*, ch. 4).

Thus every Christian, after professing his religion, is inflamed with supreme zeal to preserve and defend it and is, says our author, “ready for every punishment” (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 30, p. 445). “For nothing,” he used to say, “matters to us in this age except to depart from it” (*ibid.*, ch. 41, p. 489). Yet he believed that he could never depart from it [Col. 0967A] with greater glory than when crowned through martyrdom.

Therefore, as he perceptively observes, “no one is ashamed” of having embraced the Christian religion, “no one regrets it, except plainly that he was not one earlier. If he is marked out, he glories; if accused, he offers no defense; when questioned, he confesses even of his own accord; when condemned, he gives thanks” (*Apologeticus*, ch. 1, p. 269). Nor should you suppose that he asserted this too boldly or rashly. For in another book he repeats it thus: “No one is ashamed, no one regrets it, except with respect to his former life. If he is marked out, he glories; if dragged away, he does not resist; if accused, he offers no defense; when questioned, he confesses; when condemned, he gives thanks” (the same, *To the Nations*, bk. I, ch. 1, p. 559).

No one, therefore, regretted being a Christian, but only that he had not been one earlier or sooner; and he was afflicted with the deepest grief for his former sins, [Col. 0967B] committed in the foul darkness of idolatry. Furthermore, if someone marks him out as professing the Christian religion and for that reason he is dragged before the judges’ tribunals, he offers no defense; if accused or questioned, he does not resist, conceal, or deny, but voluntarily and with glory “confesses” (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 511); and, far stronger than Lycurgus, of whom we shall later speak together with Tertullian (below, ch. 14, art. 3), “when condemned, he gives thanks.” Hence he concludes his *Apologeticus* with these words: “We thank you for your sentences, pagans.” Why, then? “Because when we are condemned by you, we are acquitted by God” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 536).

In precisely the same manner, Lucius, according to Saint Justin, thanked Urbicus, who had condemned him: [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0967C] [non-Latin text omitted]. He professed that he owed him thanks because he was being freed from evil masters and passing to God, the Father and King of heaven (Justin, *Second Apology to the Senate*). But this, if it were necessary, could easily be established by almost countless examples.

It is therefore no wonder that the more cruelly the pagans mowed down Christians with the sword, the more numerous were those who sprang from their blood as from the most fertile seed. But hear, I pray, our author, who addresses the pagans themselves in precisely these words: “Nor does any refinement of your cruelty accomplish anything; it is rather an attraction to the sect. We become more numerous whenever we are mown down by you. The blood of Christians is seed” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 535). Elsewhere he says: [Col. 0967D] “Nor will this sect fail; you may know that it is being built up precisely when it appears to be cut down” (the same, *To Scapula*, at the end).

Indeed, this very blood of the martyrs, shed with such courage and constancy, far surpassed in eloquence absolutely all pagans—whether philosophers or others—who had exhorted anyone to endure pain and despise death. For it was “more eloquent than Cicero in the *Tusculan Disputations*,” as Tertullian says (the same, *Apologeticus*, loc. cit.). The first book of those disputations, to use Cicero’s own words, is “on despising death; the second, on enduring pain; the third, on alleviating distress; the fourth, on the remaining disturbances of the mind; the fifth . . . . . . teaches that [Col. 0968A] virtue is self-sufficient for living happily” (Cicero, *On Divination*, bk. II, p. 248, line 14 ff.).

The martyrs’ blood was also “more eloquent than Seneca in the *Fortuitous Events*,” a work which we have elsewhere noted to be lost (Dissertation on Lactantius, ch. 10, art. 3) and to have been cited by Lactantius (Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, bk. V, ch. 13). In this book Seneca had discussed contempt for death.

That blood was also “more eloquent than Diogenes,” doubtless the Cynic, whom Tertullian himself describes in another book with these words: “A human affectation fashioned into the insensibility of canine equanimity” (Tertullian, *On Patience*, ch. 2). According to Laertius, he trained himself in every respect to endure hardships and wrote a book *On Death*, in which he doubtless exhorted others to bear it patiently (Laertius, bk. VI, *Life of* [Col. 0968B] *Diogenes*, §§ 23 and 80).

Moreover, the same blood, by its supreme power of expression, surpassed Pyrrho, who, as Stobaeus notes, taught that there was no difference between life and death (Stobaeus, discourse 120, p. 606). Laertius has more about him; consult that author (Laertius, bk. IX, *Life of Pyrrho*, § 91 ff.).

Finally, this blood possessed greater eloquence than Callinicus—that is, Suctorius—who is cited by Jerome (Jerome, prolegomenon to Daniel, p. 1073) and was not only a historian but also a sophist. He published various treatises listed by Suidas and Vossius (Suidas, at the word [non-Latin text omitted]; Vossius, *On the Greek Historians*, bk. II, ch. 13, p. 225), as well as several others. Tertullian had doubtless read one of these in which Callinicus discussed patience and contempt for death.

[Col. 0968C] The blood of the martyrs therefore possessed greater persuasive power than the most eloquent discourse of any of these or of any other pagan writers. Nor was this difficult for Tertullian to prove, but evident to everyone from the following irrefutable argument: “Their words,” he says, “do not find as many disciples as Christians do by teaching through their deeds”—that is, by shedding their own blood (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 535). We have already shown how greatly the number of Christians had multiplied by Tertullian’s own time. “Indeed,” he says in addressing the pagans, “that very obstinacy with which you reproach us is a teacher” (*ibid.*).

For Pliny the Younger, whom he had cited earlier (the same, *ibid.*, ch. 2, p. 271), uses this term “obstinacy” in his letter to the emperor Trajan, in which he wrote that he had found nothing among the Christians “except obstinacy in refusing to sacrifice” [Col. 0968D] to their false gods. He then added: “Nor did I doubt that, whatever the nature of what they confessed, their perversity and inflexible obstinacy certainly deserved punishment” (Pliny, bk. X, epistle 101, p. 375).

Thus the pagans called “perversity and inflexible obstinacy” that invincible constancy of the Christians by which they preferred to be tortured and slaughtered with every kind of rack and torment rather than offer even the smallest grain of incense to fictitious gods or deny or conceal their religion. That altogether astonishing courage was therefore a teacher which fully persuaded the pagans that our religion alone was true.

## ARTICLE III. How Tertullian refutes the pagans who objected that the complaint of the Christians whom they persecuted was unjust, since those who voluntarily hastened toward torments and death—which they could have avoided by concealing their religion—ought rather to have been called mad and desperate; and by what right he overturns this disparagement through the examples of Mucius, Empedocles, Dido, Regulus, Anaxarchus, the Spartan boys, the Attic woman, Cleopatra, and the wife of Hasdrubal

[Col. 0969A]

To excuse in some fashion their monstrous and dreadful cruelty toward the Christians, the pagans replied that no injury was done to those who wished to perish; indeed, Christians ought to love those through whom they obtained the death they desired [Col. 0969B] and sought. But Tertullian shows that this reply is wholly empty and futile. For the pagans demanded that the Christians worship false gods and deny that their own God alone was the true God. This they most steadfastly refused, since the matter lay within their own power and will; and “they preferred to be condemned rather than fall away from God” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 49, p. 528)—that is, rather than fall from his divine grace by denying God against the testimony of conscience and, having avoided punishments that would shortly end and committed a supreme crime, deservedly incur eternal punishments.

Thus they plainly wished to suffer with an unconquered and cheerful spirit, but “in the same manner,” says Tertullian, “as a soldier wishes for war” (*ibid.*). This is the reading in all unpublished and published manuscripts before Rigault, who, with his customary license, entirely removed the final word, *soldier*. Yet our author’s own argument plainly appears to require it. For he compares our most holy martyrs, contending for their religion before the tribunals of tyrants, with other soldiers who fight the enemies of the commonwealth in open warfare and hand-to-hand combat (*ibid.*).

“For the martyr’s contest,” as Tertullian and later Lactantius say, is one in which, “before the tribunals of judgment and at the risk of his life” (Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, bk. V, ch. 11, p. 491; Tertullian, loc. cit.), he fights not with an iron weapon in his hand but by unconquered patience, and wins a victory as certain as it is glorious. Yet no soldier willingly commits himself to battle, in which, because he is exposed to danger, he trembles with natural fear. But once he has been compelled to enter battle, he fights with all his strength and then rejoices when victory has been won.

In this manner, therefore, the martyr, fighting against tyrants, dreads the danger with the fear innate in everyone. Yet he is filled with such joy over the victory won that he not only wills but even loves the contest that he can no longer avoid, and fights the tyrants with all his strength. Tertullian therefore did not think that the time of martyrdom should be anticipated through heedless rashness; but whenever it was offered, with God so permitting, it was to be undergone with unconquered courage.

Some pagans, however, who were gentler than others and more favorably disposed toward the Christians, insisted that they were mad, since they could easily, “while preserving their intention within the mind” (Tertullian, [Col. 0970A] *Apologeticus*, ch. 27, p. 433)—or by a “mental reservation,” as they call it—sacrifice to the gods and thus depart unharmed. Yet under the deceptive appearance of humanity, these people were giving the Christians advice intended to exploit their credulity. This counsel, Tertullian continues, could have emanated from no source other than the devil, the father of lies, who was attempting to entrap those same Christians either by violence and cruelty or at least by cunning and deceit.

Thus, as we have shown above (above, ch. 9, art. 5; vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 357), they considered it a supreme sacrilege ever to “lie about their religion” in any fashion whatsoever, as Tertullian says. Hence they preferred to experience any tortures, however dreadful, and to be slaughtered by them rather than by even the slightest [Col. 0970B] lie, concealment, or mental reservation pretend to worship any god whom they knew to be false.

“But if it was not the act of a madman,” the pagans said, “it was certainly that of a desperate man to expose himself to every kind of punishment and to an extremely cruel death, which could most easily have been avoided by casting two or three grains of incense into the fire.” We have already observed elsewhere (Dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius, ch. 12, art. 1; and Dissertation on Lactantius, ch. 10, art. 3) that the pagans called Christians “men belonging to a ruined and desperate faction,” because they despised and did not fear every torment and every most cruel form of death. We likewise saw there how others refuted this absurd accusation. But what does Tertullian say? “There is no despair [Col. 0970C] or ruin,” he says, “where victory is by no means doubtful, where there is a certain pledge of pleasing God, and where the reward of eternal happiness is secure” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530).

But come now: with what effrontery, I ask, could the pagans accuse our martyrs of wicked desperation because they met death for their true religion with a cheerful and willing spirit (*ibid.*)? Did not those very pagans adorn many of their own people, who had voluntarily allowed themselves to be killed by the most dreadful torments and a horrible death, not only with the highest praises but also with titles of eternal glory and with statues (*ibid.*)?

For, to pass over the great number of gladiators who, as we shall say below, repeatedly lost their lives after voluntarily exposing them to beasts, while those same pagans praised them, “Mucius,” says [Col. 0970D] our author, “willingly burned his right hand upon the altar so that this deed might become his title to fame” (the same, *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4). We have discussed this very deed of Mucius elsewhere (Dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius, ch. 6, art. 3).

“Did not Empedocles,” he also says, “give his whole self to the flames of the Catanian Etna?” The manuscripts incorrectly read “the Athenian flames of Athena.” For in another book already cited by us, we read his words: “He who leapt down into the fires of Mount Etna” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 49, p. 528; and *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4). Elsewhere he says: “Because Empedocles had raved that he was a god . . . . . lest he decay in a burial-place of some finer sort, he preferred to be roasted by hurling himself into Etna” (the same, *On the Soul*, ch. 32). [Col. 0971A] Earlier, Horace had sung:

> . . . . I shall tell, and recount, the death  
> Of the Sicilian poet: while Empedocles desires  
> To be regarded as an immortal god, he coolly  
> Leaps into blazing Etna.  
>   
> (Horace, *Art of Poetry*, v. 463 ff.)

If you desire more, consult Laertius, Lucian, Gregory of Nazianzus, and others (Laertius, bk. VIII, *Life of Empedocles*, § 69; Lucian, *Dialogue of Menippus*, p. 142; *Icaromenippus*, p. 731; and *Histories*, bk. II, p. 398).

Tertullian then says: “A certain foundress of Carthage gave her second marriage to the funeral pyre” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530). So read all our manuscripts, both unpublished and published. Thus some people vainly think that the reading should not be “a certain woman,” but “Elissa.” For Dido is so clearly designated by these words that there is no one who would not [Col. 0971B] recognize her immediately. Hence Jerome wrote somewhere in precisely the same manner: “I shall briefly touch upon the queen of Carthage, who preferred to burn rather than marry King Iarbas” (Jerome, epistle 91, to Ageruchia; and *Against Jovinian*, bk. I). Yet, like Tertullian, when recounting the same story elsewhere, he calls her by her own name (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, bk. I, ch. 18, p. 584). But not even schoolboys studying grammar, who read Virgil every day, can be ignorant of these matters.

Our author continues: “Regulus, lest he alone should live in exchange for many enemies, suffers crosses over his whole body” (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530); or, as he preferred to phrase it in another book: “Your Regulus willingly inaugurated a new form of the cross, both manifold and hidden” (the same, *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4; and [Col. 0971C] *Exhortation to Chastity*, ch. 31). Elsewhere, however, he explained the matter somewhat more fully in these words: “Regulus, commander of the Romans, having been captured by the Carthaginians, when he had wished that he alone be exchanged for many Carthaginian prisoners, preferred to be returned to the enemy; and, confined in a kind of chest studded on every side externally with nails, he felt so many crosses” (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530).

Thus he calls this most cruel form of punishment a “new form” of the hitherto unused, “manifold cross”—that is, one fitted with sharp nails projecting on every side—and a “hidden” cross (the same, *To the Nations*, bk. I, ch. 18, p. 584; and *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4), because, as Tubero testifies in Aulus Gellius (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, bk. V, ch. 4), they shut him up in that chest every day amid black and profound darkness, until they exposed him to be scorched when the sun’s rays had grown intensely hot. [Col. 0971D] We have treated this punishment of Regulus elsewhere (Dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius, ch. 6, art. 3; and Dissertation on Lactantius, ch. 10, art. 3).

We have also explained what Tertullian added concerning Anaxarchus: “When, as an example to others, he was being pounded with a pestle as though he were barley-groats, he said, ‘Pound, pound the skin-bag of Anaxarchus; for Anaxarchus himself you do not pound.’” He used “skin-bag” there in the same sense in which Lucilius, cited by Nonius, wrote “little skin-bag” for the body (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530; vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 1147; Nonius, *On the Meaning of Words*, ch. 2). Arnobius likewise says: “Nothing has been promised to us for this life, nor in the little skin-bag of this morsel of flesh” (Arnobius, at the end of bk. II *Against the Pagans*). Gregory of Nazianzus, however, wrote in Greek, [non-Latin text omitted] (Gregory [Col. 0972A] of Nazianzus, *Iambic Poem* 18), “his vessel and wineskin.”

Tertullian then returns to examples of women and says: “An Attic courtesan, when she had already exhausted her executioner, at last bit off her own tongue and spat it into the face of the raging tyrant, so that she might spit out her power of speech as well and thus be unable to betray the conspirators” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530). He reported the same thing, though in different words, in two other books (*To the Nations*, ch. 18, p. 584; *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4, § 17). Ambrose, however, calls her “one of the Pythagorean virgins” (Ambrose, *On Virgins*, bk. I, ch. 4, § 17). See, if you please, what we noted upon this passage of Ambrose and what we said about her in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus*, where we observed that the name Leaena was given to her (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 1250).

Far more difficult will appear what our author immediately [Col. 0972B] added: “Zeno of Elea, when Dionysius asked him what philosophy conferred, replied, ‘Contempt for death’; subjected to the tyrant’s scourges, he sealed his statement even unto death” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530). In our manuscripts, the word *impassibilis* (“incapable of suffering”) is read before “scourges,” while the old editions have *impassibilibus*. Rigault, however, excised this word as having been improperly added by an ignorant copyist. But what if Zeno is called *impassibilis* in the sense of “unconquerable,” as one who could never be overcome by any tortures?

Perhaps Rigault would have had better grounds for saying that in this text of our author the name Dionysius had been corrupted by copyists. For Laertius expressly testified that Zeno of Elea died around the seventy-ninth Olympiad (Laertius, bk. IX, *Life of Zeno*, [Col. 0972C] § 29). But Dionysius the Elder is said to have exercised his tyranny around the ninety-fourth. Zeno therefore could neither have been tortured nor killed by him. Accordingly, it is not entirely without reason either that Tertullian may be accused of a lapse of memory, or that careless copyists may be supposed, through a far from unusual error, later to have written “Dionysius” in place of “Nearchus,” “Demysus,” or “Diomedon.” For various writers, as we have shown elsewhere (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 1246), relate that Zeno of Elea was tortured and killed by one of these men.

Nor should anyone be troubled by the fact that Zeno was questioned by him about an attempted tyranny, and not, as Tertullian says, about the benefit of philosophy. For what prevents the tyrant, while questioning him about those who were privy to the treason, from having asked what philosophy provided and taught, in order by this question to reproach him more bitterly [Col. 0972D] for that crime of treason?

Finally, Tertullian closes the ranks of pagans glorying in their endurance with these words in the *Apologeticus*: “Certainly the scourging of the Spartans, made more severe even under the eyes of relatives who encourage them, confers as much honor as the blood it sheds” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530). In his book *To the Nations*, however, he declares that he wishes to pass over this matter in silence: “Not to speak of Spartan glory” (bk. I, *To the Nations*, ch. 18, p. 584); yet he stings the pagans with no less sharp a barb.

But in *To the Martyrs* he wrote more clearly that this utterly inhuman custom had continued down to his own age: “Today,” he says, “among the Lacedaemonians, the greatest solemnity is [non-Latin text omitted], that is, a scourging. It is no secret at what rite, before the altar, [Col. 0973A] all the young men of noble birth are beaten with scourges, while their parents and relatives stand by and urge them to persevere. For it will be accounted an ornament and a glory, with a still greater title, if the soul rather than the body yields to the blows” (the same, *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4). But we have already related more about these matters in the first volume of our *Apparatus* (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 1246).

He also produced the examples of two women in his book *To the Nations* (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, bk. I, ch. 18, p. 584). The first is called by him simply “the Queen of Egypt,” namely Cleopatra, who made use of “her beasts”—that is, of the bite of an asp secretly brought to her—and thereby brought about her own death. In *To the Martyrs* he wrote of this more explicitly: “A woman willingly sought beasts—and indeed asps, serpents more dreadful than a bull or bear—which Cleopatra [Col. 0973B] let loose upon herself lest she fall into the hands of her enemy” (*To the Martyrs*, ch. 4).

But this deed of the notorious woman has frequently been celebrated by the historians Suetonius (Suetonius, bk. II, *Life of Augustus*, § 30), Plutarch (Plutarch, *Life of Antony*, p. 954), and others, as well as by poets. It will therefore suffice to cite these verses of Horace, which are especially suited to Tertullian’s purpose:

> She dared to look upon her fallen palace,  
> Serene of countenance and brave, and to handle  
> The rough serpents, that she might drink  
> Their black venom into her body;  
>   
> Fiercer because her death was resolved,  
> Scorning, no doubt, to be carried away  
> By savage Liburnian ships and, no humble woman,  
> Be led as a private captive in a proud triumph.  
>   
> (Horace, *Odes*, bk. I, ode 37, v. 25 ff.)

[Col. 0973C] The second woman was a Carthaginian, “more steadfast than her husband Hasdrubal at the final crisis of her country,” says Tertullian (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, bk. I, ch. 18, p. 584); or, as he said elsewhere, “when Carthage was already burning, upon seeing her husband as a suppliant before Scipio, she plunged with her children into the conflagration of her country” (the same, *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4). For when she learned, after Carthage had been captured, that Hasdrubal had obtained his life from Scipio and had abandoned her, she is said to have cast herself with her children into the fires of the burning city. See Valerius Maximus (Valerius Maximus, bk. III, ch. 2, Foreign Examples, § 8) and other historical writers.

By so many examples of both men and women, Tertullian rightly demonstrates that the Christians, who met with the most willing [Col. 0973D] spirit the death inflicted upon them by the pagans solely because of their religion, were wrongly accused by those pagans of madness and despair. For the pagans heaped the highest praises and titles of honor upon their own people whom we have thus far mentioned. Christian martyrs, however, were worthy of far greater honors, since they astonishingly allowed themselves to be racked by truly dreadful tortures not for the sake of acquiring the fleeting and empty glory of men, but to obtain eternal happiness. For they held it as wholly certain that through martyrdom they would attain everlasting blessedness. But this must be explained by us somewhat more distinctly.
