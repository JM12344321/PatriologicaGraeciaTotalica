Our author calls breasts *abundant* (*uberes*) when they are full of much milk (something that can perhaps be inferred from the size of the breasts, although otherwise abundance of milk is inferred from breast size with less accuracy). LINDNER.—Thus *uber ager*, “fertile field,” in Livy XXIX, 25 and XXXIV, 62; *uber solum*, “fertile soil,” in Tacitus, *Histories* V, 6, and Curtius VI, 4. In the manuscript codex of Minucius the reading is *veribus*. Rigaltius acutely conjectures that this arose from *uveribus*—for scribes often wrote it so, interchanging the letters *b* and *u*—and therefore does not hesitate to restore *uberibus*. Heraldus also approved this. Lipsius emended this passage in the same way in his notes on Tacitus, *Annals* III, 61. Above too, in chapter 19, the manuscript codex has *verius* for *uberius*; and below, in chapter 36, the Roman edition has *verius* for [Col. 0305D] *uberius*. It is now apparent that the correction of Lucas Holstenius is entirely false: in his *Letter on the Spits of Diana of Ephesus*, first published at Rome in 1688, reviewed in the *Acta Eruditorum*, supplement I, p. 350, and afterward reprinted in volume VII of Gronovius’s *Greek Antiquities*, he judged that *verubus*, “with spits,” should be restored. I find that reading also in the Roman edition. Davisius, however, likewise rejects this correction, although it had found favor with Jacob Gronovius. HEUM.—Heumannus was not the first author of this explanation, but J. Tristanus, according to Lucas Holstenius, whose *Letter on the Spits of Diana* I have appended to the end of this book. **The bearded one is placed.** Heumannus judges that the verb *locatur*, “is placed,” should be removed, but it is more suitably retained. It stands for the compound *collocare*, “to set up,” which Phaedrus uses in the epilogue to book II:

> The Athenians set up an enormous statue to Aesop,  
> And placed a slave upon an everlasting pedestal.

LINDNER.

**Then he bears thunderbolts.** Gronovius was troubled by the particle [Col. 0306B] *tunc*, “then”; yet it should trouble no one here any more than *tum*, “then,” in Cicero, *For the Manilian Law*, ch. 6: “When disaster comes, then loss is suffered”; and in Plautus: “I shall then be present when I have an opportunity to see you.” A similar redundancy occurred in chapter 17: “which from the earth,” etc. LINDNER.

**He is not approached.** That is, by those intending to make supplication. For those intending to supplicate or give thanks, says Broukhusius on Tibullus I, 593, p. 104, were in the proper idiom said “to approach the gods” and “to approach unto the gods.” Jupiter Feretrius did not exist in order to be approached by suppliants—an honor paid rather to Capitoline Jupiter—but so that the *spolia opima*, the choice spoils won from defeated enemies, might be brought to him. Thus Wowerus and Rigaltius read *non aditur*, “is not approached,” in place of the manuscript’s *non auditur*, “is not heard,” but explain it differently. Wowerus says that in slightly fewer than 530 years, according to Festus, only three sets of *spolia opima* were hung up for Jupiter Feretrius. Rigaltius writes that Jupiter Feretrius was not approached because the *spolia opima* were brought not to Feretrius but to Mars and Quirinus. Jacob Gronovius: “The name is heard, but no statue or form is seen.” J. F. [Col. 0306C] Gronovius: “He is clothed in the choice spoils.” Schefferus: “He is not shut up,” supposing that his temple, like that of Peace, always stood open because Feretrius also was believed to bring peace. Withofius, in his *Critical Dedication-Festival*, published in 1741: “He is approached with vows”; but this is something he shares with Hammon, Capitolinus, and Latiaris. Perizonius: “He is approached in procession”; Heumannus: “He is approached with a procession.” But they also approached Capitoline Jupiter with a procession. LINDNER.—“When he is Feretrius, he is not heard.” The passage has been assailed by various scholars. Davisius reviews and rejects their conjectures, and finally concludes as follows: “It is indeed much easier to refute false readings than to discover the true one; yet perhaps I might not unsuitably correct it to: ‘When he is Feretrius, he does not hear.’ Minucius covertly mocks the Romans because for so long a time they had not carried off any *spolia opima*.” GALLAND.

**And, lest at greater length.** I greatly approve Heumannus’s opinion that the words “many Jupiters” crept in from the margin, and his advice to read *et ne longius abeam*, “and, lest I go farther,” as in Cicero, *On Ends* II, ch. 30: “Lest I go farther, what does Epicurus say?” But [Col. 0306D] I was unwilling to alter anything in a matter still uncertain, especially since Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* III, ch. 16, mentions several Jupiters, and since *obire terram* in the sense “to traverse the earth” is well known. Thus Pallas in Virgil, *Aeneid* X, 446:

> Across his massive frame she rolls her eyes,  
> And surveys everything afar with a savage gaze.

**Kindled.** All editors except Jacob Gronovius have followed Rigaltius and Heraldus in writing *ignita*, “kindled,” for the manuscript’s *ignata*. Gronovius thought that *signata*, “marked out,” should be restored—surely against the will of the critical Minerva. Yet he correctly perceives that the word *ignata* must be joined not with “stars” but with “maiden.” Minucius could certainly have spoken more clearly: “So that, as a kindled maiden, she might be among the stars.” HEUM.

**In the fires of Oeta.** The manuscript has *Henneis*. It had been dictated as *Aetnaeis*, “of Etna,” incorrectly for *Oetaeis*, “of Oeta.” RIGALT.]

[Col. 0299B] **SUMMARY.—**Octavius confirms that mortals were taken up among the gods by the testimony of Euhemerus, Prodicus, Perses, and Alexander the Great, who enumerate the gods’ native countries, births, and tombs. He further sets forth the gods’ sorrowful ends, deaths, and funerals. In addition, he exposes the foolish and noisome nonsense that the pagans peddle concerning the form and appearance of their gods.

Read the writings of the Stoics, or the writings of the wise, and you will acknowledge the same things together with me. Euhemerus [printed: *Erueret*] pursues the theme that men were regarded as gods because of the merit of their virtue or service; he enumerates their births, native countries, and tombs, and points them out province by province: Dictaean [printed: *dicta*] Jupiter, Delphic [printed: *Delphiciae*] Apollo, Pharian [printed: *Pariae*] Isis, and Eleusinian Ceres. Prodicus [printed: *Prodigiis*] says that those who, in their wanderings, benefited human welfare by discovering new things were taken up among [Col. 0300B] the gods. [Col. 0301A] Persaeus philosophizes in the same sense, and connects the crops that were discovered and the discoverers of those very crops under the same names—as the comic saying has it, “Without Liber and Ceres, Venus grows cold.”^29 Alexander the Great, the Macedonian, wrote in a celebrated volume to his mother that a priest, out of fear of Alexander’s power, had revealed to him the secret that the gods were men. The priest makes Vulcan the first of them all, and afterward the race of Jupiter. And you may look down upon Isis’s swallow, rattle, and the empty tomb of your Serapis or Osiris, with its scattered limbs. (XVI) Finally, consider the sacred rites themselves and the mysteries themselves: you will find the sorrowful ends, deaths, and funerals, [Col. 0303A] and the mourning and lamentation of wretched gods. Isis, together with her dog-headed attendant and bald priests, mourns, laments, and searches for her lost son; the wretched devotees of Isis beat their breasts and imitate the grief of the most unhappy mother. Soon, when the child has been found, Isis rejoices, the priests exult, and the dog-headed discoverer boasts. Every year they never cease either losing what they find or finding what they lose. Is it not ridiculous either to mourn what you worship or to worship what you mourn? Yet these rites, once Egyptian, are now Roman rites as well. Ceres, with torches lit and encircled by a serpent, wanders anxiously and solicitously in search of Libera, who was stolen away and violated. These are the Eleusinian mysteries.

And what are the rites of Jupiter? His nurse is a she-goat, and the infant is snatched from his ravenous father lest he be devoured; the clang of the Corybantes’ cymbals drowns out his crying so that his father will not hear it. Cybele [Col. 0304A] of Dindymus—it is shameful to say it—castrated the lover who had unhappily pleased her, because she herself, deformed and aged though she was, as the mother of many^30 gods, could not entice him to debauchery; thus, of course, she made the god a eunuch. Because of this fable, her Galli and half-men worship her by inflicting punishment upon their own bodies. These are no longer sacred rites; they are tortures.

What of their very forms and appearances? Do they not reveal the mockeries and ornaments [printed: “disgraces”] of your gods? Vulcan is a lame and disabled god; (XVII) Apollo remains smooth-cheeked through so many ages; Aesculapius is fully bearded, although he is forever the son of the youthful Apollo; Neptune has sea-green eyes, Minerva gray-blue eyes, Juno ox-like eyes; Mercury has winged feet, Pan hoofed feet, Saturn fettered feet; (XVIII) Janus indeed bears two faces, as though to walk backward. [Col. 0304B] Diana, meanwhile, is the huntress with her tunic girded high; the Ephesian Diana [Col. 0305A] is built with many full breasts; Trivia is terrifying with three heads and many hands. What then? Your Jupiter himself is at one time erected without a beard, at another installed bearded; when he is called Hammon, he has horns; when Capitolinus, then he carries thunderbolts; when Latiaris, he is drenched with blood; and when Feretrius, he is not approached. (XIX) And, lest I traverse the many Jupiters at greater length, there are as many monstrosities of Jupiter as there are names. Erigone hanged herself with a noose so that, as the Maiden, she might be kindled among the stars. The Castors die by turns so that they may live; Aesculapius is struck by lightning so that he may rise into godhood; Hercules is consumed in the fires of Oeta [manuscript: *Henneis*] so that he may put off the man.

**CHAPTER XXII.—We learn.** Thus the royal manuscript, better than the common reading *we have learned*. HERALD. Ludovicus Carrio also defends the manuscript reading, *Emendations* II, ch. 18. HEUM.

**We elaborate.** *Elaborare* means “to adorn, to polish.” Cicero [Col. 0307B], *Orator* 84: “Elaborate symmetry.” Heumannus reads *corroboramus*, “we strengthen.” LINDNER.

**Very greatly.** Cellarius and several others printed *wonderfully greatly* (*permirum quantum*), which is also found in the Roman edition. But the manuscript has *very greatly* (*plurimum quantum*), which Carrio correctly defends in the chapter just cited. The phrase *plurimum quantum* occurs again below, in chapter 40, and that Roman edition has it there as well. HEUM.

**By their own authority.** Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I, 16: “For the things poured forth in the voices of the poets are not much more absurd, and they have done harm by their very charm.” And our author below: “By these and similar fictions and sweeter falsehoods the minds of boys are corrupted.” Consider whether the reading ought to be: “by their very own charm.” LINDNER.

**Praised and crowned.** In a brief summary Minucius has reported what Plato said at greater length in this discourse in book III of the *Republic*: “If anyone in our city should wish to display his poems, we shall indeed venerate him as sacred, marvelous, and delightful; but we shall say that it is not lawful for such a man to be in our commonwealth, and we shall send him away to another city after pouring perfume upon his head and placing a woolen crown upon him.” Behold the poet first praised, then crowned, and finally expelled from the city! HEUM.

**In his discourse.** R. Bentley, on Horace, *Odes* III, 8, verse 5, p. 110, proves that *sermo*, “discourse,” is elegantly used for a book, treatise, dialogue, or history. Likewise Cicero, *Letters* IX, 8: “I therefore composed the conversation held between us at Cumae, when Pomponius was with us.” LINDNER.

**By Venus’s strap.** This is the reading in the manuscript codex of Minucius, not “by Venus’s girdle”; in place of it the Roman edition incorrectly prints *toro*, “couch,” which Gronovius also printed. HEUM.—In the royal codex it is clearly written *thoro*. EDD.

[Col. 0307D] **Constructed.** Heusingerus, according to Heumannus, reads “equipped.” I alter nothing and adopt the position of Burmannus, who, on seeing variation between these words in Quintilian, *Institutes* I, 1, p. 17, says: “I think it matters little, since either reading can readily be defended.” I would not suppose that the other should perhaps be preferred because “unhappy builder” follows. LINDNER.

**There [Vulcan].** Delete the latter word, since it is not found in the manuscript but only in the Roman edition. Rigaltius, Davisius, and Gronovius therefore rightly omitted it from their editions. HEUM.

**The sky and thunderbolts.** We have added the conjunction from the royal manuscript. HERALD.

**Against Ganymede.** More idiomatically, “in Ganymede”; or *in* should be deleted, so that the sense is: “Why should I speak of Ganymede, Jupiter’s sexual outrage,” etc. LINDNER.

**So that for the vices of men.** Heraldus printed this without mentioning any variant. Rigaltius, however, declares that “so that by men’s judgments” is plainly the reading of the ancient copy. I have retained Heraldus’s reading, which Sabaeus also has; that is:

> Why should I think shameful for myself  
> What was becoming for the gods?

as someone says in the words of Ovid, *Amores* II, 8.—Rigaltius approves the reading of the ancient copy. For, he says, through corrupted judgment—when men judge badly—the vices they read were perpetrated by the gods are regarded as virtues. But Gelenius and Cellarius printed “so that for the vices of men.” Davisius, coming somewhat closer to the traces of the manuscript, thinks that *uti vitiis hominum* should be read. That is good enough. For I approve less of another conjecture by the learned man, who in his second edition reads: “so that a certain authority might be secured for the crimes of men.” GALLAND.

**Are corrupted.** Bayle, in his *Dictionary*, under the entry JUPITER, letter D, teaches that the system of paganism is exceedingly well suited to corrupting morals. LINDNER.

**By these same.** The manuscript has *hisdem*; nevertheless, that form means *iisdem*, “by these same.” See Bünemannus on Lactantius, *Deaths of the Persecutors* XXIII. LINDNER.

**Clinging.** That is, clinging in the mind. Nor would “while they cling” be a bad reading. Above, chapter 1: “whereby Caecilius was still clinging to superstitious vanities.” Lactantius I, 1: “For many, stubbornly clinging to empty superstitions.” LINDNER.

**Swarm.** Pacatus, Pliny, and others used *examen*, “swarm,” for a multitude or flock. Tertullian likewise does so in a passage corresponding to this one, *Apology* ch. 10: “Enough now about Saturn, although only a little has been said. We shall also show that Jupiter was as much a man as he was born from a man, and thereafter that the whole swarm of his line was as mortal as the father of its stock.” Minucius appears to have had this passage before his eyes. [Col. 0308D] LINDNER.

**And by Janus.** This is the entirely correct reading from the royal manuscript. Previously: “and he too was received as a guest.” HERALD.

**To imprint letters.** Compare Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, ch. 2. From this Pomponius Laetus divined that the art of typography was born in Italy in Saturn’s age, an opinion sharply attacked by Peutinger in his *Conversational Discourse on the Marvels of Germany*. Meanwhile, see Cicero discussing the following, with Balbus as speaker, in *On the Nature of the Gods* II, 37: “Why should he not likewise suppose that, if innumerable shapes of the twenty-one letters, whether of gold or any material one pleases, were thrown together somewhere, the *Annals* of Ennius could be produced when they were shaken out onto the ground, so that they could then be read in sequence? Yet I do not know whether chance could accomplish so much even in a single verse.” Indeed, even before Saturn’s age, if the most learned Grotefend is to be believed, a genuine art of printing characters from bronze types already existed in the East, as Babylonian tablets attest. Compare Lambinet, *Origins of Printing*, vol. I, p. 359; Prosper Marchand, *History of Printing*, pp. 3–5; Porthmann, *Essay on Printing*; Cicero, ed. Leclerc, vol. XXV, p. 328. EDD.

**And he gave the city Saturnia from his own name.** Thus the manuscript. Fulvius Ursinus was the first to print: “the city Saturnia from his own name,” omitting the word “gave.” Some editions have: “he gave the city Saturnia his own name,” omitting “from.” Arntzenius, on Aurelius Victor, thought that *founded* should be read for *gave*. But Barthius, on Statius, *Thebaid* III, 16, showed that *give* is commonly used for “found,” so that no change is necessary. Two verbs are present here, *gave* and *left*, one of which must be absent. If you delete the latter, the words run thus: “He gave the city Saturnia from his own name, as Janus did the Janiculum, each as a memorial for posterity.” The latter method, however, in which “gave” is absent, has found more supporters. LINDNER.

**Because among the Italians.** Not likewise among the Cretans. “To be of unknown parents” means “to have unknown parents.” Heumannus does not doubt that the true reading is: “because among the Italians his parents were unknown.” LINDNER.

**Those seen unexpectedly.** Barthius, on Statius, *Thebaid* X, 752, inferred that there was a lacuna containing certain words here, because persons appearing unexpectedly were also called “sons of the earth” or “earth-born”; once these words were expressed, there would be a more suitable opposition between “those seen unexpectedly” and “the lowborn and unknown.” But the author appears to have made his distinction such that by the former he means specifically men who are distinguished or outstanding in some respect, and by the latter those who, besides being of obscure birth, do not greatly distinguish themselves by deeds or virtues. Lactantius also appears to have understood it thus at I, 11. The designation “sons of the earth” is generally applied to persons of lower station. WOPK.

**A cave still.** Read: “There a cave still.” HEUM.]

**SUMMARY.—**These fables, first handed down by uneducated men, were afterward celebrated by others, and especially by the poets, who did no small harm to truth through their authority. By such fictions and sweeter falsehoods the minds of boys are corrupted, and consequently the wretches grow old in them, although truth is readily accessible—but only to those who seek it.

(XX) We learn these fables and errors from ignorant parents and—what is more serious—we elaborate them through our very studies and disciplines, especially through the poems of the poets, who [Col. 0307A] very greatly harmed truth itself by their own authority.^31 And for this reason Plato acted splendidly when he expelled that illustrious Homer, after praising and crowning him, from the city he was establishing in his discourse. For Homer, chiefly in the Trojan War, although he made sport of your gods, nevertheless involved them in human affairs and actions. He matched them against one another, wounded Venus, bound, wounded, and routed Mars. He relates that Jupiter was freed by Briareus lest he be bound by the other gods; that he wept bloody showers for his son Sarpedon because he could not rescue him from death; and that, enticed by Venus’s couch [printed: “cord”], he lay with his wife Juno more ardently than he was accustomed to lie with adulteresses. Elsewhere Hercules carries off dung, and Apollo tends Admetus’s flock. Neptune, meanwhile, constructs walls for Laomedon, yet the unhappy builder receives no payment for his work. There [Col. 0307B] Jupiter’s thunderbolt is fashioned upon the anvil together with Aeneas’s arms, although the sky, thunderbolts, and lightning had existed long before Jupiter was born in Crete, and neither could a Cyclops imitate the flames [Col. 0308A] of a real thunderbolt, nor could Jupiter himself avoid fearing them.

Why should I speak of the discovered adultery of Mars and Venus, or of Jupiter’s sexual outrage against Ganymede, consecrated in heaven? All these things were handed down so that a certain authority might be secured for the vices of men. By these and similar fictions and sweeter falsehoods the minds of boys are corrupted; with these same fables clinging to them, they grow up even to the full strength of mature age, and in these same opinions the wretches grow old, although truth is readily accessible—but only to those who seek it.

For all the writers of antiquity, Greek and Roman, have represented Saturn,^32 the founder of this race and swarm, as a man. Nepos and Cassius know this in their historical writings, and Thallus and Diodorus say it. Accordingly, Saturn, a refugee from Crete, had come to Italy in fear of his raging son and, having been received as a guest by Janus, taught those rude and rustic men many things, since he was a polished little Greek: how to imprint letters, stamp coins, [Col. 0309A] and fashion implements. He therefore preferred that his hiding place, because he had lain there safely concealed, be called Latium; and from their own names Saturn and Janus left behind the city Saturnia and the Janiculum, each as a memorial for posterity. He was therefore surely a man who fled, surely a man who lay hidden, both the father of a man and born from a man. For he was represented as the son of earth or [printed: “and”] heaven because among the Italians his parents were unknown, just as to this very day we call those who appear unexpectedly “sent from heaven,” and those of low or unknown birth “sons of the earth.” His son Jupiter reigned in Crete after driving out his father; there he died, there he had children. A cave of Jupiter is still visited, his tomb is pointed out, and by his own sacred rites he is convicted of humanity.

## CHAPTER XXIII

[Note: [Col. 0309D]

**CHAPTER XXIII.—And when Proculus swore falsely.** Meursius wished, not unsuitably, to restore “as Proculus swore falsely.” But since this correction is unnecessary, I do not endorse it. The Roman edition has “and when he swore falsely,” perhaps written for “and when he committed perjury.” See what I wrote on Lactantius, *On Anger*, ch. 16. HEUM.—Cyprian 2, 5 has *pejerante*, “swearing falsely.” LINDNER.

**And Juba.** Scholars dispute whether he was king of Numidia or Mauretania. Cellarius proves from Dio that he was king of Numidia; Dio at the same time attests that during Pompey’s civil war the kingdom of Mauretania was divided, Bocchus reigning in the nearer part and Bogud in the farther. Lucan, IV, 672, meanwhile extends Juba’s kingdom to the sea of Gades. But grant that this was poetic license; he is nevertheless expressly called king of Mauretania by Florus IV, 2, and Eutropius VI, 18. Tacitus likewise says in *Annals* IV, 5: “Juba received the Moors as a gift from the Roman people.” The same author, *Histories* II, 58, says there was a report that Albinus, scorning the title of procurator, had assumed the insignia of royalty and the name of Juba. Albinus, however, was procurator [Col. 0310C] of Mauretania. As for Cellarius’s further claim that it is improbable that Juba—defeated by Caesar at Thapsus and, when his cause was hopeless, laying violent hands upon himself—was readily deified by his own people: this, so far as I see, presents no difficulty, because the Moors regarded all their kings indiscriminately as gods, whatever kind of death they suffered. Perhaps Minucius selected this man in preference to the others so that the absurdity of his deification might be more evident. LINDNER.

**They desire to persist as men.** Heumannus restores: “men desire to be preserved.” I alter nothing. The expressions “to put on the man” and “to put off the man,” that is, human nature, are well known; on these see Bünemannus on Lactantius IV, 10. Paul himself, 1 Corinthians 15:53: “For this corruptible thing must put on incorruption, and this mortal thing must put on immortality” (*Δεῖ γὰρ τὸ φθαρτὸν τοῦτο ἐνδύσασθαι ἀφθαρσίαν, καὶ τὸ θνητὸν τοῦτο ἐνδύσασθαι ἀθανασίαν*). Lactantius IV, 25: “So that it might appear that even as a man he was heavenly.” Therefore, “they desire to persist as men” means “they desire to remain men longer.” LINDN.

**And if already old.** J. Meursius corrected this to: “Although already old, they therefore are not …” But there is no need to alter anything except to combine two words into one and read: “although already old, they are unwilling”; in this way the passage will be complete. DAVIS.—I read: “and, although already old.” HEUM.

**And childbearing has failed in Juno.** This could appear all the more remarkable because she possessed a mystery for annually renewing her virginity by washing herself in the Canathus, a river of the Peloponnese. Pausanias, *Peloponnese*, book II, at the end. She also conceived partly by the breath of a breeze, partly by touching a flower, and partly by eating lettuce. LINDNER.

**If the gods could beget.** Thus the manuscript correctly, although Jacob Ouzelius thought otherwise and preferred “could be begotten.” But the good man did not recall that this verb contains the meaning “to beget”; being, I suppose, more skilled in theology than in textual criticism, he immediately dreamed of creation from nothing. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* VII, 666:

> At once those begotten with Pallas  
> Assemble.

And book XIII, 22:

> I should be powerful in nobility, begotten by Telamon.

[Col. 0311B] Lucretius I, 734:

> So that he scarcely seems begotten from human stock.

And again V, 1012:

> And to see offspring begotten from himself.

Lactantius, *Epitome*, ch. 5: “Let desire … serve for the begetting of children.” More examples could readily be assembled, but I shall not linger over a familiar matter. DAVIS.—Compare also Burmannus on Phaedrus I, [uncertain chapter number] 6: “If he has begotten children”; and Torrenius, with others, on Valerius Maximus VII, 7: “because the marriage had not been contracted for the sake of begetting children.” LINDNER.

**Is dazzled.** Dionysius Lambinus, on Lucretius III, p. 411, writes: “is blinded by the gleam of gold”; and on Horace, *Epistles* II, 1, p. 234, says that wherever one reads *praestringere*, whether “to dazzle the eyes” or “the eyesight,” *praestinguere*, “to extinguish,” should be read. But this opinion is excessively dogmatic. Lactantius II, ch. 6: “Their beauty and splendor dazzle the eyes.” Nonius Marcellus, p. 695: “*Praestringere*: to darken.” Plautus, *The Braggart Soldier*:

> Let him dazzle the eyesight of enemies in the battle line.

The passage is found at act I, scene 1, line 4. There the learned man not only repeated the same error in his notes, but also wrongly printed “let him extinguish the eyesight”—an exceedingly rash act, since it is established that Cicero, Livy, and others spoke in precisely the former way, quite apart from the fact that Apuleius also quotes that verse in this form in *On the God of Socrates*, p. 77, Lyon edition. DAVIS.—Compare also Bünemannus on Lactantius II, 6. LINDNER.

**By what instruments of torment.** Read “by what lathes”; for the word *tormentum* denotes: first, a military engine; second, punishment and torture; third, a stretched rope. The first meaning is wholly unsuitable here. The second could indeed be applied in some fashion if you understood it to mean that statues are imagined to suffer pain when cut, hewn, planed, and rasped. So too could the third, since the statue is suspended by ropes, though I believe this was done rather by inserting hooks. But a *tornus* is a craftsman’s lathe, invented, according to Pliny VII, 56, by Theodorus of Samos. LINDNER.—Lindner wishes to read “by what lathes,” but the word *tormentum*, “instrument of torment,” appears better and more suitably adapted to the sense and context of the passage. RUSWUM.

**To fear material.** We have printed this on the authority of the royal manuscript. The former reading was: “He will blush at having rashly been mocked by material so as to make a god,” which has no meaning. Everything Minucius discusses in this passage has been explained in the notes on Tertullian’s *Apology*. HERALD.

**Golden.** The Roman edition wrongly reads: “A god of wax or silver.” HERALD.—Gatakerus, in his *Adversaria Miscellanea* II, ch. 19, p. 371, restores “golden,” though hesitantly. He would have made this emendation with greater confidence had he read in Minucius, ch. 24, that avarice was consecrated by the pagans “in gold and silver, from which the gods are fashioned.” The same scholar correctly observes that “more often” must be emended here, since that Egyptian king did it not more often, but only once. He therefore thinks that Minucius wrote either “we read” or “we know” in place of “more often.” I have at hand an easier and therefore more probable correction. By transposing the words I write: “from an unclean vessel more often, as was done by the Egyptian king.” I now see that Jacob Gronovius also thinks it should be restored in this way. HEUM.

**From an unclean vessel, as was done by the Egyptian king.** Minucius has in mind the deed of Amasis, related by Herodotus II, ch. 172, and from him by Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, ch. 23. Cotelerius, on the *Clementine Homilies* X, ch. 8, punctuates this passage differently; Gatakerus, *Adversaria Miscellanea* II, ch. 19, p. 371, reads it differently, but without compelling reason. DAVIS.

[Col. 0312C] **And with anvils.** I write “and upon anvils.” MEURS.

**By an impure man.** The manuscript correctly has this, not “by an unskilled man,” as the Roman edition and therefore several others have it. Pareus presents in his *Critical Lexicon* the passages of Plautus in which the same word occurs. Elmenhorstius adduced at this passage a text from Justin Martyr very similar to this one in Minucius, and Heraldus another from Origen. HEUM.

**The stone is not yet a god.** Davisius says that these words contain not merely no wit but not even any meaning unless the word is divided and one reads: “Unless perhaps it is not stone while it is a god.” There is no need. The sense is: “Unless perhaps stone, wood, or silver is not yet a god”—that is, the raw and unworked material of stone, wood, or silver is not yet a god, something our author assumes hypothetically. He then continues: “When, therefore, is it born?” That is, “When, therefore, does the stone, wood, or silver become a god?” LINDNER.

**When a man willed it.** The most learned Rigaltius and Heraldus rightly vindicated this manuscript reading, which has been attacked in vain with conjectures ever since the first edition. For *voluit*, “willed,” is Minucius’s expression, just as conversely he said, “those whom we deny by day, whom we reject”; and, “on my birthday I reject this trickery—I do not believe it.” Does he not derive it from Cicero’s most pleasing *On the Orator* II, ch. 6, where it is supplied from Lucilius: “I want Decimus Laelius,” which Lucilius in his own context understands both before and afterward as meaning “according to his judgment”? GRONOV.—Previously the reading was “that man willed,” as Cellarius printed it. Meursius corrects it to: “when the man vowed it”; Heumannus: “when the man willed and dedicated it as the one whom he wished to be God.” LINDNER.—“When a man willed it”—namely, willed it to be God, says Rigaltius, maintaining that the manuscript reading is sound. Cellarius: “when that man willed.” Meursius: “vowed.” Davisius, however: “worshiped,” a word that, in his judgment, fits this passage well, whether one adopts the common meaning “to adore” or the less ordinary meaning “to adorn.” GALLAND.]

**SUMMARY.—**Although the pagans acknowledge that their kings are mortal, they nevertheless fashion them into gods, even against their will—not from belief in their divinity, but to confer the honor of a meritorious posterity. Yet the true God has neither rising nor setting. Octavius next attacks the gods’ images and statues.

It is idle to go through them individually and unfold the whole succession of that race, since mortality, once proved in the case of the earliest ancestors, has passed into all the others by the very order of succession—unless perhaps you fashion gods after their death: Romulus became a god when Proculus swore falsely, and Juba is a god because the Moors willed it, as are the other deified kings who are consecrated, not from belief in their divinity, but in honor of their power after it has run its course. In the end, this name is assigned to them against their will:^33 they desire to persist as men; they fear being made gods, and although already old, they are unwilling. Therefore the dead are not gods, since God could not [printed: “cannot”] die; nor are those who have been born gods, since everything born dies, whereas the divine is that which has neither rising nor setting.

For if they were born, why are they not still being born today? Unless perhaps Jupiter has now grown old, childbearing has failed in Juno, and Minerva grew gray before she gave birth. Or has this generation ceased because no assent is offered to fables of this kind? Besides, if the gods could beget, they could not perish; we would have more gods than [printed: “born”] all mankind, so that by now neither heaven could contain them, nor air hold them, nor earth sustain them. Hence it is manifest that they were men, whom we both read were born and know to have died.

Who, then, doubts that the common people pray to the consecrated images of these men and publicly worship them, while the opinion and mind of the ignorant are deceived by artistic symmetry, dazzled by the gleam of gold, and blunted by the luster of silver and the whiteness of ivory? But if anyone were to consider with what instruments of torment and by what machinery every statue is formed, he would blush to fear material that the craftsman mocked in order to make a god. For a wooden god—perhaps a portion of a funeral pyre or an ill-omened log—is hung up, cut, hewn, planed, and rasped; and a god of gold or silver, often made from an unclean vessel, as was done by the Egyptian king, is melted, beaten with hammers, and shaped upon anvils; and a stone god is cut, carved, and polished by an impure^34 man. It does not feel the injury of its own birth, just as [Col. 0312A] it afterward feels no worship arising from your veneration—unless perhaps stone, wood, or silver is not yet a god.

When, then, is it born? Look: it is cast, fashioned, and carved; it is not yet a god. Look: it is soldered with lead, assembled, and erected; it is still not a god. Look: it is adorned, consecrated, and prayed to. Only then, finally, is it a god, when a man has willed and dedicated it.

## CHAPTER XXIV

[Note: [Col. 0312D]

**CHAPTER XXIV.—How much more truly.** The manuscript has “how great, however,” for which Fulvius Ursinus was the first to restore “how much more truly.” LINDNER.

**By nature.** This is not a gloss. By this adverb our author meant that animals, under nature’s guidance, judge much more truly concerning the gods than do pagans endowed with the use of reason. LINDNER.

**They know, they recognize.** Thus the manuscript correctly, and it must be punctuated as we have done. There is no need to follow Wowerus, Ouzelius, Cellarius, and others, who prefer “they know, they gnaw.” The learned men should have remembered that African writers frequently join synonyms (*συνώνυμα*). Arnobius III, p. 106: “To judge, to believe far more honorably than you concerning the dignity of his own name.” And p. 109: “How great, I say, were the flames of anger that they would kindle, arouse.” We have assembled further examples in the notes on chapter 11 of this book. DAVIS.—Wowerus correctly restored “they recognize,” and Ouzelius and Cellarius followed him. For “gnaw” belongs to the mice; “leap repeatedly upon” to the swallows; and “perch upon” to the kites. Moreover, this whole passage appears to me to require the following punctuation and reading: “How much more truly do dumb animals judge concerning your gods! Mice, swallows, and kites—for they know that the gods do not feel—gnaw, leap upon, and perch upon them.” HEUM.—I do not think this parenthesis is needed in a concise style, which must be supplied as follows: “Because they know that the gods do not feel, they therefore gnaw,” etc. In a connection of words such as this, those particles are understood rather than expressed. Gronovius, defending the word “recognize,” says that “gnaw” is inept, although all lexicographers unanimously affirm that this action properly belongs to mice, which wear something away—and false in regard to objects that are cast and soldered with lead. But is it also false in regard to objects made from wood, which are cut, fashioned, and carved? LINDNER.

**They trample upon.** They tread on, trample underfoot. Tertullian, *On Repentance*, ch. 7: “They fear to appear to trample upon what they have attained.” The same author, *To the Nations* I, ch. 10: “Those Lares and Penates whom you claim to consecrate by domestic devotion, you trample upon with domestic license.” Wowerus’s and Cellarius’s conjecture “leap upon” is therefore unnecessary. DAVIS.

**You protect and fear them.** That is, “and yet you fear them.” Below, chapter 26: “Regulus observed the auspices and was captured”; chapter 28: “You both sacrifice and worship the heads of wethers”; chapter 35: “You prohibit and commit adulteries.” Lactantius IV, 2: “Nowhere have they grasped wisdom, and yet it must exist somewhere.” There was therefore no reason for Wopkenius to read: “and you fear those whom you make and protect.” That reading indeed yields a sufficiently convenient sense, but it is not merely inferior to the former sense produced by the received reading; it cannot even be regarded as equal to it. It is indeed absurd to fear what one has made—to speak with Lactantius, *Epitome* XXV, 5—but this could still be tolerated in some fashion, provided that, after being made, the gods ceased to remain under human protection. Yet when the gods continually require human protection, who could endure the fact that these same gods are feared? Meanwhile, the nations are so empty-headed that they protect the gods they have made and nevertheless fear them. LINDNER.
