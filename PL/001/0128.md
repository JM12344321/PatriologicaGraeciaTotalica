When, however, it is laid down that salvation belongs to no one without Baptism—above all on the basis of that declaration of the Lord who says, “Unless someone is born of water, he does not have life” (John 3)—there arise scrupulous, or rather rash, reconsiderations on the part of certain people: how, under this rule, can salvation belong to the Apostles, whom we do not find baptized in the Lord, apart from Paul? Indeed, since Paul alone among them put on the Baptism of Christ, either judgment has already been given concerning the peril of the others, who lack the water of Christ, so that the rule may stand; or the rule must be rescinded, if salvation has also been appointed for the unbaptized. As the Lord is my witness, I have heard arguments of this kind—lest anyone think me so perverse that, from the mere wantonness [Note: [Col. 1213C] “Wantonness,” Rigalt.; Fran.] of my pen, I gratuitously stir up matters that cause others scruple [Note: [Col. 1213C] “That causes,” Fran.]. And now, [Col. 1213B] as best I can, I shall answer those who deny that the Apostles were baptized.

For if they had undergone John’s human baptism, but desired the Lord’s [Note: [Col. 1213C] “But desired the Lord’s.” So that, reborn of water and the Holy Spirit, they might have eternal life. For that necessity proclaimed in John 3—“Unless someone has been reborn of water and the Holy Spirit, he cannot enter into [Col. 1213D] the kingdom of God”—must be understood of Christ’s Baptism. The author therefore answers those who, because they said that the Apostles had not been baptized, called their salvation into question or were altogether doubtful about it. He begins by arguing against those who expressly denied that the Apostles had received Christ’s Baptism; and Tertullian does not deny this in the present chapter. But he abstains from that question and seeks only to establish that their salvation must not be doubted, even though they had not been initiated by Christ’s Baptism—a Baptism which, in the preceding chapter, he denied that the Apostles had received before Christ’s resurrection. For, according to Tertullian, Christ did not baptize them; nor had the sacrament of Baptism which they themselves could administer yet been instituted, as the same Tertullian proves. He does not discuss whether they were baptized after Christ’s resurrection, nor does he incline to either side. His sole purpose in this chapter is that salvation belongs altogether to the Apostles, whether they were baptized or remained unwashed; and, taking the opportunity, he proves that they received John’s Baptism, [Col. 1214C] but prudently remains silent about the Lord’s. Consequently, the title which Pamelius prefixed to this chapter—“That the Apostles appear to have been baptized”—plainly shows that he entirely missed the author’s meaning. Hence the most illustrious Baronius, at the year 31, [Col. 1214D] no. 4, although he does not explain himself fully, and Bellarmine, *On Baptism*, book I, chapter 25, received too little illumination from the testimony of our African in this chapter when they sought to prove that the Apostles had been baptized with Christ’s Baptism. LAC.], inasmuch as the Lord himself had defined that there was one Baptism [Note: [Col. 1213C] “Was defining,” variant reading, Wouw.], when he said to Peter, who was unwilling to be washed [Note: [Col. 1213C] “Willing,” Junius, Corbinian.], “He who has once washed has no need to do so again” [Note: [Col. 1214D] “He who has once washed.” These words appear to speak of Baptism as not to be repeated. LE PR.] (John 13:6)—something he certainly would not have said at all to one who had not been baptized—this is the express proof against those who deprive the Apostles even of John’s Baptism, in order to destroy the sacrament of water.

Can it appear credible that the way of the Lord had not then been prepared in those persons—that is, through John’s Baptism—who were being appointed to open the way of the Lord throughout the entire world? The Lord himself, who owed no repentance, was baptized; was it not necessary for sinners? What then? Were others not baptized [Note: [Col. 1213C] “What therefore,” Junius; “that others were not baptized,” variant reading, Wouw.]? Not, however, Christ’s companions, but the rivals [Col. 1213C] of the faith—the teachers of the Law and the Pharisees. Hence it is also suggested that, since those who opposed the Lord refused to be baptized, those who followed the Lord had been baptized and did not share the judgment of his rivals—especially when the Lord to whom they adhered had exalted John by his testimony, saying [Col. 1214A] that no one among those born of women was greater than John the Baptist (Matt. 11:11).

Others, plainly enough under considerable strain, introduce the claim that the Apostles then fulfilled the equivalent of Baptism when, in the little boat, they were sprinkled and covered by the waves (Matt. 8); and that Peter himself, when walking upon the sea, was sufficiently immersed (Matt. 14). In my opinion, however, it is one thing to be sprinkled or overwhelmed by the violence of the sea, and another to be baptized according to the discipline of religion. Moreover, that little boat displayed a figure of the Church, because upon the sea—that is, in the world—it is disturbed by waves—that is, by persecutions and temptations—while the Lord, through his patience, is as though asleep (Matt. 8), until, awakened in the last times by the prayers of the saints [Note: [Col. 1214D] “Awakened in the last times by the prayers of the saints, he stills the world.” That is, by this petition: “May your kingdom come.” RIG.], he stills the world and restores tranquility to his own.

Now, whether they were baptized in some manner or remained unwashed—so that the Lord’s statement (John 13) about one washing, spoken in the person of Peter [Note: [Col. 1214D] “About one washing, spoken in the person of Peter.” Historically it was said to Peter: “He who has been washed has no need,” etc. LE PR.], may pertain only to us—it is nevertheless rash enough to pass judgment concerning the salvation of the Apostles, because [Note: [Col. 1213C] “Because not to them,” variant reading, Wouw.] the privilege of their first calling, and thereafter of their inseparable intimacy [Note: [Col. 1214D] “Inseparable intimacy.” The Apostles are called companions and members of Christ the Lord’s household, and *homōrophioi hetairoi* (ὁμωρόφιοι ἑταῖροι), “companions under the same roof,” by Nonnus of Panopolis in his paraphrase of John. LE PR.], could have conferred upon them a shortened equivalent of Baptism [Note: [Col. 1214D] “The privilege of intimacy could have conferred a shortened equivalent of Baptism.” In that manner, namely, in which the legal authorities themselves teach that sometimes, through the speed with which actions are joined [Col. 1215C] to one another, one action is concealed within another. RIG.].

I think they followed him [Note: [Col. 1213C] Junius emends: “Thereafter, the privilege of inseparable intimacy with [Col. 1214C] him could have conferred a shortened equivalent of Baptism. I think,” etc. Rigalt.: “Could have, when they, I think, followed,” etc. Leopold: “Could have conferred a shortened equivalent of Baptism. I think they followed.”] who promised salvation to everyone who believed. “Your faith,” he said, “has saved you”; and, “Your sins are forgiven you” (Mark 10)—to one who believed, certainly, yet had not been baptized. If such faith was lacking in the Apostles, I do not know whose faith is genuine. At a single word of the Lord, one of them arose [Note: [Col. 1214C] “Arose,” feminine form, Wouw.] and abandoned the tax booth (Matt. 9); another left his father, his boat, and the craft by which he supported his life [Note: [Col. 1214C] Fran. adds “who”]; another disregarded his father’s funeral rites; and he fulfilled that supreme [Note: [Col. 1214C] “That supreme command of his,” Fran., Corbinian.] command of the Lord—“Whoever prefers father or mother to me is not worthy of me” [Col. 1214C] (Matt. 10:37)—before he had heard it.

## CHAPTER XIII

Here, therefore, those most criminal men raise questions. “Baptism,” they say, “is therefore unnecessary for those for whom faith is sufficient, for even Abraham pleased God by no sacrament of water, but only by that of faith” (Gen. 15). But in every matter later things determine the conclusion, and what follows prevails over what came before. Granted that in former times, before the Lord’s passion and resurrection, salvation came through bare faith. But when faith was enlarged by the requirement of believing in his nativity, passion, and resurrection, an enlargement was added to the sacrament: the seal of Baptism [Note: [Col. 1215C] “The seal of Baptism, in some manner the garment of faith.” Even the Stoics knew that the faithful are perfected by Baptism. Thus Arrian speaks most explicitly, *Epictetus* XI, 9: Ὅταν δὲ ἀναλαβῇ τὸ πάθος τὸ τοῦ βεβαμμένου, καὶ ἡρημένου, τότε καὶ ἔστιν τῷ ὄντι, καὶ καλεῖται Ἰουδαῖος: “But when he assumes the disposition of one who has been baptized and has made his choice, then he both truly is and is called a Jew.” RIG.], which is, in a certain sense, the garment of the faith that formerly was bare and possessed no power apart from its own law.

For the law of baptizing was imposed and its form prescribed: “Go,” he says, “teach the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (Matt. 28:19). The definition joined to this law—“Unless someone has been reborn of water and the Spirit, he shall not enter the kingdom of heaven” (John 3:5)—bound faith to the [Col. 1215B] necessity of Baptism. Accordingly, from that time onward all who believed were baptized. Then Paul also, when he believed, was baptized (Acts 9:6). And this is what the Lord had commanded during that affliction of blindness: “Rise,” he said, “and enter Damascus; there it will be shown to you what you must do” (*ibid.*)—namely, be baptized, which alone was lacking to him. Otherwise, he had sufficiently learned and believed that the Nazarene was the Lord, the Son of God.

## CHAPTER XIV

But concerning the Apostle himself they object that he said, “For Christ did not send me to baptize” (1 Cor. 1:17), as though Baptism were abolished by this argument. Why, then, did he baptize Gaius and Crispus and the household of Stephanas (*ibid.*)? And although Christ had not sent him to [Col. 1215C] baptize, he had nevertheless commanded the other Apostles to baptize. [Col. 1216A] But these words were written to the Corinthians in view of the circumstances of that time (*ibid.*), because schisms and dissensions were arising among them, while one attached himself to Paul and another to Apollos. For that reason the peaceable Apostle, lest he seem to claim everything for himself, said that he had not been sent to baptize, but to preach. For preaching comes first; baptizing comes afterward. If preaching came first, I think [Note: [Col. 1215C] “To baptize. But let preaching have come first. I think,” etc., codex Wouw.] that one who was permitted to preach was also permitted to baptize [Note: [Col. 1215C] “I think, however, that one who was permitted to preach was also permitted to baptize.” The rule of faith is preached or taught; then faith is sealed by Baptism. RIG.].

## CHAPTER XV

I do not know whether anything further is debated in the controversy over Baptism. I shall, however, take up again what I omitted above, lest I appear to have broken off the train of thought then pressing forward.

For us there is altogether one Baptism, both according to the Lord’s Gospel (John 3) and according to the Apostle’s letters (Eph. 4), since [Col. 1216B] there is one Lord, one Baptism, and one Church in heaven. But as to what rule is properly to be observed with regard to heretics, one may rightly reconsider the matter; for the declaration was issued to us [Note: [Col. 1215C] “For the declaration was issued to us.” This seems exceedingly obscure to Pamelius, yet it is not so very obscure. The sense is: In the Gospel and in Paul one Baptism is prescribed; but [Col. 1215D] who will decide whether this is to be observed in relation to heretics, since what has been issued and promulgated in Holy Scripture is directed to us Catholics? Nor is there any reason for Pamelius to remain uncertain whether Tertullian means that Baptism cannot be conferred by heretics or cannot be received by them, since he must mean both when he says that they do not possess Baptism.—“For the declaration was issued to us”: namely, what he said in the preceding clause, “For us there is altogether one Baptism.” This rule was issued to us, both from the Lord’s Gospel and from the Apostle’s letters, not to heretics. Among us—that is, among Catholics—it has been issued, made manifest and explicit, what must be observed by us in the discipline of Baptism; heretics, however, have no share at all in our discipline. We must not recognize among them what was issued to us and commanded to us. See Baronius, year of Christ 217, no. 8. LAC.].

Heretics, however, have no share at all in our discipline; the very withdrawal of communion certainly bears witness that they are outsiders. I am not bound to recognize among them [Note: [Col. 1216C] “To know,” Rigalt.] what has been commanded to me, because we and they do not have the same God, nor one Christ—that is, the same Christ. Therefore neither is there one Baptism [Note: [Col. 1215D] “Therefore neither is there one Baptism,” etc. Some think that Cyprian, Tertullian’s disciple, derived from this the error [Col. 1216C] of baptizing again those who had been baptized by heretics. Yet if the present author is rightly explained, perhaps no suspicion of error will remain. For many considerations suggest that a defect in what they call the matter may be understood here, or even a defect in the form, since St. Cyprian taught nothing of the kind. Hence canon 19 of the Council of Nicaea decreed that those who took refuge in the Church from the Paulianists should be baptized afresh. LE PR.], because it is not the same. Since they do not possess it rightly, they undoubtedly do not possess it at all; and what is not possessed cannot be counted [Note: [Col. 1216C] “What is not possessed cannot be counted.” In the Church there is one Baptism. Unity, however, is not a number, but the principle of number. Since, therefore, heretics are outside the Church, there is no Baptism among them; [Col. 1216D] consequently there is not even one Baptism, because what does not exist cannot be counted or supply a beginning to number. This was Tertullian’s opinion, which his disciple Cyprian maintained rather obstinately against Stephen in several letters. Yet perhaps he ought especially to be excused because the crimes of heretics in those times were such that they were in some respect guilty of violating the most holy Trinity; since the Trinity was not intact among them, and therefore was no Trinity at all, they could neither possess nor give anything that seemed to savor of Christ’s Baptism. RIG.]. Thus they cannot receive it either, because they do not possess it [Note: [Col. 1216D] “Thus they cannot receive it either, because they do not possess it.” The matter is expressed more clearly in Cyprian: “But we say that those who come from there are not rebaptized among us, but baptized; for they receive nothing there, where there is nothing.” RIG.].

But this subject has already been more fully set out by us in Greek [Note: [Col. 1216D] “Set out in Greek.” You see that Tertullian made a preliminary treatment of this subject in Greek, as he did in others—a point many have observed. LE PR.]. We therefore enter the washing once; sins are washed away once, because they ought not to be repeated. The Jewish Israel, however (Mark 7), [Col. 1217A] washes daily [Note: [Col. 1217B] “The Jewish Israel washes daily because it is daily defiled.” Thus in the book *On Prayer*: “Although Israel may wash all its members [Col. 1217C] every day, it is nevertheless never clean. Certainly its hands are always unclean with the blood of the prophets,” etc. RIG.—“The Jewish Israel.” For there is now also a Catholic Israel, just as there is a Catholic Jerusalem. LE PR.], because it is daily defiled (Heb. 6). It was precisely to prevent this practice among us as well that the rule concerning one washing was established. Happy is the water that washes once, that is no object of sport for fishermen, and that, being unclean through the continual recurrence of filth, does not again defile those whom it washes.

## CHAPTER XVI

We do indeed also have a second washing, itself one: namely, that of blood. Of this the Lord says, “I have a Baptism with which to be baptized” (Luke 12:50), although he had already been baptized. For he had come through water and blood (1 John 5:6), as John wrote, so that he might be baptized by water and glorified by blood, and accordingly might make us called by water (John 19) and chosen by blood.

He sent forth these two baptisms [Note: [Col. 1217C] “These two Baptisms.” The author’s meaning is that we first need the Baptism of water so that afterward we may drink the blood which flowed from the pierced side—that is, become participants in the Eucharist. LE PR.] from the wound in his pierced side [Col. 1217B] [Note: [Col. 1217B] The manuscripts read: “Accordingly, to make us by water.” Semler and the Franeker edition: “Accordingly, so that he might make us—chosen, he sent forth these—. Because those,” etc. With Leopold, we have adopted Wouwer’s emendation approved by Rigalt. The same applies in what follows: “This is the Baptism,” namely that of blood, or martyrdom, “which supplies the washing—and restores it.”], because those who believed in his blood were to be washed with water, while those who had washed with water were also to drink blood [Note: [Col. 1217C] “Those who had washed with water were also to drink blood.” This passage concerns the twofold Baptism of water and blood. Septimius says that both flowed from the wound in the pierced side, so that we who believe in Christ’s blood may be washed with his water; and, when cleansed by the benefit of such saving water, may also drink the cup of martyrdom for Christ’s name fearlessly and steadfastly whenever occasion arises. For he says that this Baptism is so efficacious that it both supplies the washing when it has not been received and restores it when it has been lost. These words must undoubtedly be understood of martyrdom. RIG.]. This is the Baptism that both supplies the place of the washing when it has not been received and restores it when it has been lost.

## CHAPTER XVII

To conclude this little subject, it remains to offer an admonition also concerning the rule for giving and receiving Baptism. The right of giving it belongs to the chief priest, who is the bishop [Note: [Col. 1217C] “The chief priest, who is the bishop.—For the sake of [Col. 1217D] the honor of the Church, which, when preserved, preserves peace. Otherwise, even laypeople possess the right.” There is a similar passage in the book *Exhortation to Chastity*. From both passages Jerome gathered what he says against the Luciferians: that Baptism is a priesthood. For it is written, “He made us a kingdom and priests to his God and Father”; and again, “A holy nation, a royal priesthood.” To the same point pertains what Septimius noted at the beginning of this little work: that those who have come out of the washing are anointed with blessed anointing, according to the ancient discipline by which they customarily were anointed into the priesthood with oil from a horn. The same Jerome, against those same heretics, borrowing the words of our Septimius as well, discusses the bishop’s honor and power and calls him the chief priest. Before Jerome, the author of the *Questions on the Old and New Testaments* had done the same, question 101: “That by ‘presbyter’ a bishop is understood,” he says, “Paul the Apostle proves when he instructs Timothy, whom he ordained a presbyter, regarding what sort of [Col. 1218B] person he ought to appoint as bishop.” He then adds: “For what is a bishop except the first presbyter—that is, the chief priest?” [Col. 1218C] And it certainly follows that the chief priest is the person set over not merely the people of priests—that is, laypeople endowed with the priesthood or sacrament of Baptism—but also presbyters and deacons. Indeed, he is called chief priest because, in addition to the priesthood of Baptism, he possesses the priesthood of order or honor, which order or honor is the highest in the Church. Augustine, *City of God*, book XX, chapter 10, says that bishops and presbyters are now properly called priests in the Church: “But just as we call all Christians such because of the mystical chrism, so we call all of them priests, because they are members of the one Priest.” Finally, since every Christian, even a layperson, is everywhere called by Tertullian and others an altar of God and a temple of God, and is said to offer and sacrifice to God praises, thanksgivings, fasts, and alms; and since he also plays the part of one expressing assent to the bishop or presbyter who formulates the prayers of the sacrifices by answering “Amen,” which means “Let it be done,” in this sense even Christian laypeople are called priests. RIG.].

Next come the presbyters and deacons, though not without the bishop’s authorization [Note: [Col. 1218D] “Not, however, without the bishop’s authorization.” The same is taught by Clement, *Constitutions* VIII, chapter 46, and St. Ignatius, *To the Smyrnaeans*: Οὐκ ἐξόν ἐστι χωρὶς τοῦ ἐπισκόπου οὔτε βαπτίζειν, οὔτε προσφέρειν: “It is not permitted apart from the bishop either to baptize or to offer the sacred rites.” LE PR.], for the sake of the Church’s honor. When that is preserved, peace is preserved. Otherwise, even laypeople possess the right. For what is received equally may be given equally—unless bishops or presbyters or deacons are now called “learners” [Note: [Col. 1217B] “Were called speakers,” Latinus. “Are called speakers,” Franeker, [Col. 1218B] Paris.]—and the Lord’s word ought not to be hidden from anyone. Accordingly, Baptism too, being equally a possession of God, may be administered by all [Note: [Col. 1218D] “Being equally a possession of God, may be administered by all.” A similar passage concerning the honor of bishops and presidents occurs in the book *On the Crown*. RIG.].

But how much more does the discipline of reverence and modesty rest upon laypeople, since this responsibility belongs to their superiors [Note: [Col. 1218D] “Since this responsibility belongs to their superiors”: namely, bishops, presbyters, and deacons. RIG.], so that they may not assume for themselves the office dedicated to the bishop [Note: [Col. 1218B] “Bishops,” variant reading, Wouw.]—the episcopate [Note: [Col. 1218B] Leopold thinks that this word was written in the margin by some copyist and inserted into the text. Junius: “Office. Rivalry for the episcopate,” etc.]. Rivalry is the mother of schisms. “All things are permissible,” said the most holy Apostle, [Col. 1218B] “but not all things are expedient” (1 Cor. 6 and 10). Let it suffice, then, that you exercise the right in cases of necessity [Note: [Col. 1218B] Rigalt. omits “that.”], wherever a condition of place, time, or person compels it. For then the resolution of the person bringing assistance [Note: [Col. 1218D] “The resolution of the person bringing assistance.” This signifies the confidence of the helper, who, conscious of his right, assists a person in peril without doubt or hesitation; that is, such conduct is admitted, accepted, and praised. By “resolution” he means boldness. He might have said “to assist resolutely,” in the same form in which he will shortly say “to learn resolutely,” and in the book *On Patience*, “he did not resolutely denounce.” RIG.] is accepted when the circumstances of the person in peril are urgent. For he will be guilty of the loss of a human being if he has neglected to provide what he freely could [Col. 1219A] [Note: [Col. 1219B] “For he will be guilty of the loss of a human being if he has neglected to provide what he freely could.” How can he be guilty of the loss of a human being when, in what follows, he says that those who understand the weight of Baptism should fear receiving it more than delaying it, and that unimpaired faith is secure of salvation? He does not contradict himself. For anyone who has neglected to give Baptism to a person desiring it amid a sudden and violent danger is guilty of that person’s loss. For, so far as it lay with him, he destroyed the person for whom, through either inhuman negligence or ill-timed diffidence, he did not hasten to procure the saving washing. But one who with his whole heart and mind desired to become a Christian was, through faith, made for Christ what Christ granted to faith; and he who was lost through a wickedly delaying human being is saved by the most generous saving God. RIG.].

The presumption of a woman [Note: [Col. 1219A] “Of women,” Franeker.] who has usurped the right to teach will certainly not also produce for her a right to baptize—unless some new beast should arise similar to the former one, so that, just as the one abolished Baptism, another might confer it by herself [Note: [Col. 1219C] “So that another might confer it by herself”: that is, so that she herself might baptize. RIG.].

But if writings wrongly attributed to Paul [Note: [Col. 1219C] “But if writings wrongly attributed to Paul.” He means the writing entitled *Acts of Paul*, long since classified among the apocrypha. Eusebius mentions it, *History* III, chapter 19. We have emended this passage from Ursinus’s book, for the name of Thecla had undoubtedly crept into the text from some marginal note. Her story, of the same stamp as those Pauline writings, still circulates under the name of Basil of Seleucia. In the age of our Septimius, writers of such fables were expelled from whatever position they held in the Church. RIG.—“But if writings attributed to Paul,” etc. Here Rigalt.’s manuscript struck out “the example of Thecla,” which occurs in almost all the others and has exercised the commentators. I think these words crept into the text from the margin. Yet lest Thecla’s story be altogether unknown, it should be noted that most regard it as apocryphal, although Baronius defends it in his notes on the martyrology. It runs as follows: When the virgin Thecla heard Paul preaching [Col. 1219D] at Iconium, she rejected the young man to whom she had been betrothed in marriage. Her mother denounced her daughter to the judges as a Christian. By the sign of the cross and Paul’s prayers she overcame innumerable tortures and finally gained the martyr’s palm. All these things up to this point are true; but other things are doubtful—namely, that she became a nun and received a veil from blessed Paul. The spurious “Damascenus VI” drew these things from Palladius’s *Lausiac History*, as he did certain other matters of this kind concerning the souls of Falconilla and Trajan, supposedly delivered by the prayers of Thecla and Gregory. LE PR.—Thus far the Catholic authors, whose arguments contain more indignation than persuasion. Having considered them, the upright, sober-minded, and more judicious Grabe, a most learned man from the Protestant ranks, offers the following observations. Until we produce the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* themselves, it will be sufficient to oppose them to the harsher disdain of our own people: “From this,” he says, “it is indeed established that this little book was falsely inscribed with Paul’s name. Yet the question still remains [Col. 1220B] whether the story of Thecla narrated in that book was a mere fable invented by that presbyter—as though no such most holy virgin and martyr ever existed, or did and suffered those things for the Christian faith which she is said to have learned from Paul. Certainly Tertullian’s words do not establish this; they establish only that the book of that Asiatic presbyter was wrongly attributed to Paul. Those who claim that it is wrongly related therein that Thecla herself exercised the office of teaching and baptizing are mistaken. Indeed, if you examine this very feature more deeply, does it not attest the antiquity of these Acts? For a writer of a later age would scarcely have included it; nor would any later person have attributed such a thing to the female sex, or introduced either the Apostle Paul assigning that sacred office or Thecla exercising it. These things agree better with the earliest times and are sufficiently consistent with the history of the churches of Asia. For it is gathered from Paul’s words to Timothy, whom he left at Ephesus in Asia, that certain women in those churches arrogated to themselves the power of preaching in the Church. He writes: ‘Let a woman learn in silence with all subjection. But I do not permit a woman to teach’ (1 Tim. 2:11–12). Why, I ask, did Timothy need this instruction unless women in the Ephesian Church were aspiring to this sacred office? Likewise, in another Asiatic church—namely, Thyatira—a woman, Jezebel, was teaching and calling herself προφῆτιν, ‘a prophetess,’ which is condemned (Rev. 2:20). This Asiatic presbyter who wrote the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* likewise appears to have been a patron of women of this kind who preached in the churches; and for that reason he was rightly deposed from his rank by St. John, as Tertullian and Jerome have recorded” (*Spicilegium of the Holy Fathers*, vol. I, pp. 88, 94, and 334). Thus far the learned Grabe. He also observes that Jerome called not Thecla’s story, but the story of the baptized lion, a fable. That fable is not found in our text of the Acts; consequently, it may rightly be judged that the holy Father either was entirely unacquainted with these Acts or encountered a corrupted copy of them. EDD.] defend the example of Thecla [Note: [Col. 1219A] “But if writings wrongly attributed to Paul—the writing [Col. 1219B] of Thecla,” etc., manuscript Wouw. “The example of Thecla” was deleted after Rigalt. by the Paris, Venetian, Semler, and Leopold editions.] as authorization for women to teach and baptize [Note: [Col. 1220D] “As authorization for women,” etc. That is, authorization both to teach and to baptize. He said “as authorization,” just as he says “for confidence,” “for communion,” etc. RIG.], let them know that in Asia the presbyter who composed that writing, as though adding something of his own to Paul’s title [Note: [Col. 1220D] “As though adding something of his own to Paul’s title”: that is, as though from his own resources he were adding something to Paul’s titles and glory, something resembling an accumulation. RIG.], when convicted and having confessed [Note: [Col. 1220D] “When convicted and having confessed.” Jerome added: “when convicted before John.” RIG.] that he had done it from love of Paul, departed from his office.

For how close to the faith would it appear that the man who did not even permit a woman to learn [Note: [Col. 1220A] Others read “to teach.”] with self-assertion should give a woman the power of teaching and baptizing [Col. 1220A] [Note: [Col. 1220D] “Did not even permit a woman to learn with self-assertion.” That is, women should seek to learn with modesty. “Not to learn with self-assertion” means not to inquire imperiously or question obstinately. Thus in the book *On Patience*: “He did not even resolutely denounce his betrayer.” RIG.]? “Let them be silent and consult their husbands at home” (1 Cor. 14).

## CHAPTER XVIII

Moreover, those whose office it is know that Baptism is not to be entrusted indiscriminately. “Give to everyone who asks of you” has its own subject matter, since it pertains specifically [Note: [Col. 1220A] “Accordingly,” variant reading, Wouw.] to almsgiving. Rather, this command must be considered: “Do not give what is holy to dogs” (Luke 6), nor “cast your pearls before swine” (Matt. 7:6) [Note: [Col. 1220B] “Your pearl,” Paris; “and cast your pearl before swine,” variant reading, Wouw.]; and, “Do not readily lay hands upon anyone, lest you share in another’s sins” (1 Tim. [Col. 1221A] 5:22; Acts 8).

If Philip baptized the eunuch so readily, let us reflect that the Lord’s manifest and explicit favor had intervened. The Spirit had commanded Philip to proceed [Note: [Col. 1221C] “To continue,” variant reading, Wouw.] upon that road. The eunuch himself was found neither idle nor suddenly seized by a desire for Baptism; rather, he had gone to the Temple for prayer and was intent upon the divine Scripture. This is how the one to whom God had voluntarily sent an Apostle ought to have been found. Again, the Spirit commanded Philip to join himself to the eunuch’s carriage; a Scripture belonging to the faith met him at the proper moment; when invited, the Apostle was taken up into the carriage; the Lord was revealed; faith did not delay; water did not have to be awaited; and, once the matter was completed, the Apostle was taken away.

Paul too was indeed baptized quickly (Acts 9). For Simon [Note: [Col. 1221C] “Judas,” Franeker; “Ananias,” Latinus.], his host, had quickly learned that Paul had been appointed a vessel of election. God’s favor sends its own privileges ahead of it. Every petition can both deceive and be deceived. Accordingly, in keeping with each person’s condition and disposition, and also with age, delay in Baptism is more beneficial—especially, however, in the case of little children.

For what necessity is there [Note: [Col. 1221C] Franeker adds “if the necessity is not so great”; “if it is not so necessary,” variant reading, Wouw.] that sponsors should also be thrust into danger [Note: [Col. 1221C] “Sponsors should also be thrust into danger.” The sponsors of children, whom the Greeks call ἀνάδοχοι (*anadochoi*), are sponsors in the sense of guarantors. Their office is to instruct the infant and exhort it to live well; from this you can discern the author’s meaning. LE PR.]? For they themselves may fail to fulfill their promises through death, and may be deceived by the development of a bad disposition. The Lord indeed says, “Do not prevent them from coming to me” (Matt. 19:14). Let them come, then, while they are growing up; let them come while they are learning, while they are being taught to whom they are coming. Let them become Christians when they have been able to know Christ.

Why does innocent childhood hasten [Note: [Col. 1221C] “Why does innocent childhood hasten?” Tertullian thus thought that one who did not yet possess any understanding of the Christian faith ought not to be admitted to the Christian name, and that one who was not yet capable of any deceit or sin had no need of the remission of sins. It nevertheless seemed right to the Catholic Church, in accordance with Cyprian’s opinion, to prevent no one from receiving Baptism and grace—not even infants, who, being newly born, have committed no sin except that, having been born carnally according to Adam, they contracted the contagion of ancient death in their first birth. Therefore, they come all the more readily to receive remission of sins precisely because what is remitted to them is not their own sins but another’s. RIG.] to the remission of sins? Greater caution is exercised in worldly affairs: should divine property [Col. 1221C] be entrusted to one to whom earthly property is not entrusted [Note: [Col. 1222C] “Be entrusted? Let them know,” Latinus.]? Let them know how to seek salvation, so that you may appear to have given it to one who asks.

For no lesser reason the unmarried too should be delayed, since temptation lies ready for them: for virgins through their maturity, and for widows through their wandering [Note: [Col. 1222C] “Freedom from occupation,” Wouw.], [Col. 1222A] until they either marry or become strengthened for continence. If any understand the weight of Baptism, they will fear its reception more than its delay. Unimpaired faith is secure concerning salvation [Note: [Col. 1221D] “Unimpaired faith is secure concerning salvation.” Above, he spoke of those to whom another’s fault denied the washing; now he speaks of those from whom, while they were coming to Christ, growing up in Christ, and learning heavenly things, some overpowering force took both life and the washing. Septimius commands that these too be secure: the faith that strives to become such is unimpaired, and therefore secure concerning salvation. RIG.].

## CHAPTER XIX

Passover provides the most solemn day for Baptism [Note: [Col. 1222C] “The most solemn day for Baptism.” No one, I think, is unaware that in ancient times two principal seasons were appointed for baptizing Christians: holy Passover and Pentecost; and that especially during the former the solemnity of baptizing was celebrated over eight days. LE PR.], since the Lord’s passion, in which [Note: [Col. 1222C] “Which,” codex Wouw.] we are baptized, was then fulfilled. Nor will anyone [Note: [Col. 1222C] Codex Wouw. adds “anyone.”] interpret the following incongruously as a figure: when the Lord was about to celebrate his last Passover, he sent the disciples to prepare it and said, “You will find [Note: [Col. 1222C] “You will meet,” Paris.] a man carrying water” (Mark 14:13). Thus he indicated by the sign of water the place in which the Passover was to be celebrated.

After that, Pentecost is the broadest period for arranging baptisms (Acts 2), during which the Lord’s resurrection was repeatedly manifested among the disciples, [Col. 1222B] the grace of the Holy Spirit was inaugurated, and the hope of the Lord’s coming was intimated. For when he had then been received back into heaven—during the Pentecost season, of course—the angels said to the Apostles that he would come in the same manner in which he had ascended into heaven (Acts 1).

But Jeremiah too, when he says, “And I shall gather them from the ends of the earth on the festal day” (Jer. 21:8), signifies the day of Passover and of Pentecost, which is properly the festal day. Yet every day belongs to the Lord; every hour and every season is suitable for Baptism. If solemnity makes a difference, grace makes none.
