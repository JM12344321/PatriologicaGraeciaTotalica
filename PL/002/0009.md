Hold fast to this conviction for the moment, while I repel the assault of the question [Note: [Col. 0088C] “The assault of the question”: its attack. RIG.]. For I already hear it said: “Many other things, too, were produced by those whom the world believed to be gods; yet today they are found in our own uses, in those of the saints of old, in the things of God, and in Christ himself—who, of course, discharged the functions of human life by no other means than these common instruments of human sustenance” [Note: [Col. 0088C] “Human sustenance”: that is, human life. In the same way legal writers speak of “the burdens of maintaining a wife” and of parents “to be maintained” by their children. RIG.].

Let it indeed be so; nor will I yet dispute more remotely about origins. Let Mercury have been the first to expound letters: I will acknowledge that they are necessary both for commercial dealings and for our studies concerning God. But even if he also first stretched strings to produce sound, I will not deny that this device has had dealings with the saints and ministered to God, since I hear David. Let Aesculapius have been the first to investigate remedies: I remember that Isaiah too prescribed something medicinal for the ailing Hezekiah (Isa. 38:21). Paul also knows that a little wine is beneficial for the stomach (1 Tim. 5:23). And let Minerva have been the first to fashion a ship: I shall see Jonah sailing [Note: [Col. 0088C] All the others read “Jonah.”] (Jon. 1:3), and the apostles (Luke 8:2). More than that (Acts 13:4), even Christ will be clothed, and Paul too will have a cloak (2 Tim. 4:13).

If they name some one of the gods of the world as the inventor of each item of furniture [Col. 0088A] and every individual vessel, they must acknowledge Christ reclining on a couch [Note: [Col. 0088C] “Christ reclining on a couch.” Our Sirmond discusses this passage in Epistle 2, Book I of Sidonius, and says that, because Christ reclined, John—who was perhaps the youngest in age—reclined next to Christ, in his bosom. For the arrangement on the semicircular dining couch was such that the first place was given to Christ; then the others reclined, while John was in the last place and therefore rested in Christ’s bosom. Thus, plainly, Christ’s supper was arranged not as painters now represent it, with Christ in the middle and the apostles crowding either side of him, [Col. 0088D] but with him truly at the right-hand end, the others surrounding him, and John bringing up that sacred company; for this reason John reclined at Christ’s side. LAC.]; and also when he brings a basin to his disciples’ feet (John 13:5), when he pours water from a pitcher, and when he girds himself with a linen cloth—the distinctive garment of Osiris [Note: [Col. 0088D] “The distinctive garment of Osiris.” Herodotus is the authority that among the Egyptians it was forbidden to bring woolen garments into sacred buildings; accordingly, they used linen ones. RHEN.].

To a question of this kind I give the same answer everywhere: I indeed admit the common use of practical objects, but appeal to the distinction between rational and irrational things, because passivity [Note: [Col. 0088C] Rhenanus reads “passibility.”] deceives [Note: [Col. 0088D] “Because passivity deceives.” *Passivitas*, that is, the multitude. RIG.], obscuring the corruption of creation by which it has been subjected to futility (Rom. 8:28). For we say that those things alone were suitable for our use, for the use of our predecessors, for the things of God, and for Christ himself, which provide sheer benefits, reliable assistance, and honorable comforts for the necessities of human life [Col. 0088B] [Note: [Col. 0088D] “The necessities of human life”: that is, needs. RIG.]; so that they may be believed to have been inspired by God himself, who first exercised foresight for his human creature, furnished him, and, when appropriate, gave him pleasure. But whatever things have exceeded this order are not suitable for our use—especially those which, precisely for that reason, are recognized neither in the world’s ordinary life, nor in the things of God, nor in Christ’s conduct.

## CHAPTER IX

Finally, what patriarch, what prophet, what Levite or priest or ruler, or, later, what apostle or evangelist or bishop, is found wearing a crown? I think that neither the temple of God itself, nor the ark of the covenant, nor the tabernacle of witness [Note: [Col. 0088D] “The tabernacle of witness.” Just as in the Old Testament it is called the “tabernacle of testimony,” so too in the New; for “testimony” is μαρτύριον (*martyrion*) in the Septuagint. That word is also used for martyrs’ basilicas. Thus the Council of Chalcedon was convened ἐν τῷ μαρτυρίῳ τῆς ἁγίας καὶ καλλινίκου μάρτυρος Εὐφημίας, “in the basilica of the holy and gloriously victorious martyr Euphemia,” as stated in Act 4 of that council. LE PR.], nor the altar, nor the lampstand (1 Kings 6 and 8) was crowned—though crowning would certainly have been appropriate to them both at the solemnity of their first dedication and at the celebration of their second restoration [Note: [Col. 0089C] The Paris edition reads “prior establishment.”]—if it were worthy of God (1 Esd. 5:11; 1 Cor. 10).

Yet if they were figures of us—for we are the temples of God, his altars, lights, and vessels—they also symbolically portended this: that human beings belonging to God ought not to be crowned. The reality must correspond to the image. If perhaps you raise the objection that Christ himself was crowned, for the moment you will hear this brief reply: “Then crown yourself in that manner too; it is permitted.” Yet even that crown of insulting impiety was not decreed by the people [Note: [Col. 0089C] “Yet even that crown of insulting impiety was not decreed by the people.” “The people,” that is, the Jews. RIG.]. It was an invention of Roman soldiers, drawn from a worldly custom which the people of God never admitted, either in the name of public rejoicing or of innate extravagance. They returned from the captivity of Babylon more readily with drums, pipes, and harps than with crowns (1 Esd. 2 and 3); and after eating and drinking they rose up to play without crowns (Exod. 23). For neither a description of rejoicing nor a denunciation of extravagance would have remained silent about the honor or dishonor of a crown. Indeed, Isaiah too says (24:9): “For they drink wine with drums, pipes, and harps.” He would also have said “with crowns,” if this custom had ever existed in the things of God.

## CHAPTER X

Thus, when you advance the claim that ornaments belonging to the gods of the world are also found in the presence of God, in order to vindicate among them the crown of the head for common use, you have already laid down a rule against yourself: that what is not found in the things of God must not be admitted into common use [Note: [Col. 0089C] Semler inserts “among us.”]. For what is so unworthy of God [Note: [Col. 0089C] “For what is so unworthy of God?” The form of this argument is more compact in the manuscript: “For what is so unworthy of God as what is worthy of an idol? As what is also worthy of a dead man?” RIG.] as what is worthy of an idol? [Col. 0089C] And what is so worthy of an idol as what is also worthy of a dead person? For it is characteristic of the dead too to be crowned in this way [Note: [Col. 0089D] “It is characteristic of the dead to be crowned in this way.” Pliny writes that crowns were an honor paid to tombs and the spirits of the dead. RHEN.], since they immediately become idols through the attire and ritual of consecration, which among us is a second idolatry.

Therefore it will belong to those who lack perception to make use likewise of something of which they lack perception—as though they wished to misuse it, if they did not lack perception. But there is no distinction concerning misuse when the reality of use ceases with the cessation of the nature capable of perception. By what faculty could anyone wish to misuse a thing when he has no faculty with which to use it? We, however, are not permitted by the apostle to misuse; he teaches us more readily not to use—except that those who perceive nothing do not misuse either. But the whole matter is empty and is itself a dead work insofar as idols are concerned; it is, however, decidedly alive insofar as demons are concerned, to whom the superstition belongs.

“The idols of the nations,” says David (Ps. 113:4), “are silver and gold; they have eyes and do not see, nostrils and do not smell, hands and do not touch.” Yet it is by these faculties that flowers are enjoyed. But if he declares that those who manufacture idols will become like them, those who use anything in accordance with the adornment of idols are already like them. “All things are clean to the clean” (Titus 1:15); likewise, all things are unclean to the unclean—and nothing is more unclean than idols.

Substances, however, are clean, as things belonging to God, and in virtue of their created condition are available for common use; but the manner in which the use is conducted makes the difference. For I too slaughter a chicken for myself, no less than Socrates did for Aesculapius [Note: [Col. 0089D] “Than Socrates did for Aesculapius.” He ordered this while he was in prison, shortly before he was to drink the poison. RHEN.]; and if the odor of some place offends me, I burn something from Arabia [Note: [Col. 0089D] “I burn something from Arabia.” When speaking about himself, he is even afraid to use the word “incense.” He therefore said, “I burn something from Arabia,” that is, some incense or Arabian fragrance. In the book *Against the Jews*, too, he interprets “the spoils of Arabia” as gold and perfumes. An ancient author cited by Festus says, “It smells Arabian,” that is, of Arabian fragrances. RIG.]; but not with the same rite, attire, or ceremonial apparatus with which it is done before idols.

For if a created thing is polluted by a mere statement [Note: [Col. 0089D] “By a mere statement.” I believe this man, highly skilled in law, was alluding to formulas occurring in jurisprudence, where one frequently reads: “bare possession,” “bare ownership,” “bare claim,” “bare delivery”—that is, respectively, possession unaccompanied by ownership; ownership from which usufruct is absent; a claim in which some debt is entered in the accounts although no cause of indebtedness preceded it; and delivery lacking a just and adequate cause for transferring [Col. 0090D] ownership. Thus a “bare statement” will be one by which a person is admonished by someone who has no authority to admonish, as though that statement were bare of all authority and support. LAC.], as the apostle teaches—“But if anyone says, ‘This has been sacrificed to an idol,’ do not touch it” [Note: [Col. 0090D] “This has been sacrificed to an idol; do not touch it.” This is from the Apostle, 1 Cor. 10:28, where he retained the Greek word εἰδωλόθυτον (*eidōlothyton*), “idol-sacrifice,” in place of what our translator renders “sacrificed to idols.” It is surprising, however, that he reads “Do not touch it,” since the Greek is μὴ ἐσθίετε (*mē esthiete*), which cannot be translated otherwise than our translator does: “Do not eat it.” PAM.]—how much more is it contaminated when you have danced about [Note: [Col. 0090C] “Have danced about” is absent from Pamelius and the Leopold edition.] in the attire, rite, and ceremonial apparatus of idol-sacrifices? So too a crown becomes an idol-sacrifice. For by this rite, attire, and ceremonial apparatus it is sacrificed to an idol by those who originated it; its use is so peculiarly theirs that something not found in the things of God cannot be admitted into common use.

Therefore the apostle cries out: “Flee idolatry” (1 Cor. 10:14)—all of it, certainly, and in its entirety. Survey the forest and see how many thorns lie concealed. Nothing must be given to an idol, and likewise nothing taken from an idol. If reclining in an idol’s temple is foreign to faith, what of appearing in an idol’s attire? “What fellowship has Christ with Belial” [Note: [Col. 0090D] “What fellowship has Christ,” etc. Drogo, bishop of Ostia, expands this with other related passages. Others attribute it to Cassian, *Conferences* XII, chapter 16. LAC.]? And for that reason, flee. For he commands a far-reaching separation from idolatry: one must conduct nothing in proximity to it. Even an earthly dragon [Note: [Col. 0090D] “Even an earthly dragon swallows human beings from afar with its breath.” Pamelius published: “Even an earthly dragon no less swallows birds from afar with its breath.” These words seem to have been taken from what Isidore reports of the basilisk, though they are wholly inapplicable to the dragon. Rhenanus had published something quite different: “Even an earthly dragon swallows the breaths of a human being from afar.” This comes close to the true reading, which we have restored from the manuscript of Agobard: “Even an earthly dragon swallows human beings from afar with its breath.” For here Tertullian is dealing with fleeing idolatry in accordance with Paul’s command to the Corinthians, and he says it must be fled as far away as possible, because the earthly dragon—that is, infernal Satan—swallows human beings even from afar with his breath and blast. He then adds from the First Epistle of John: “John goes further: Little children, guard yourselves from idols.” That is, John traces the matter more deeply and declares that not only idolatry must be fled, but even the idols themselves. RIG.] swallows human beings from afar with its breath.

John goes further [Col. 0091A]: “Little children,” he says, “guard yourselves from idols” (1 John 5:21)—no longer merely from idolatry as an activity, but from their very image. For it is unworthy that you, the image of the living God, should become the image of an idol and of a dead thing [Note: [Col. 0091C] Semler reads “should become.”].

Thus far we have vindicated the peculiar ownership of this attire for idols, both by the title of its origin and by the practice of superstition; and additionally from the fact that, since it is not mentioned among the things of God, it is more and more reckoned an image of those in whose antiquities, solemnities, and rites it is encountered. Finally, even their doors, victims, and altars, and their ministers and priests themselves [Note: [Col. 0091D] “Their ministers and priests themselves.” Pliny relates that crowns initially claimed their designation only among sacred rites and military honors. RHEN.] are crowned (Baruch 6:9). In Claudius you have the crowns of all the priestly colleges.

But we have also inserted that distinction between rational and irrational things, to answer those who defend common use in every case on the occasion of certain examples. For this part, then, it remains to examine the actual grounds for wearing crowns, so that, by showing them to be foreign—indeed, contrary—to our discipline, we may prove that none of them is supported by the protection of reason whereby this kind of attire, and its use too, could be vindicated for common use, even though there are some whose examples are raised against us.

## CHAPTER XI

Indeed, to take up the very case of the military crown, I think it must first be investigated [Note: [Col. 0091D] “It must be investigated whether military service is altogether suitable for Christians.” Since he does not permit a Christian to lend his assistance to a pagan waging war, would he permit a Christian to join himself as a soldier to a Christian who was going to persecute his brothers? Tertullian undoubtedly never believed that Christians would one day clash with one another in arms. RHEN.] whether military service is altogether suitable for Christians. Otherwise, what sense is there in reconsidering incidental matters when the fault lies in what precedes them?

Do we believe that a human oath of allegiance [Note: [Col. 0091D] “Do we believe that a human oath of allegiance…” He alludes to the sacrament and response, or profession of faith, made at baptism. PAM.] may be superimposed upon the divine one, and that after Christ one may pledge allegiance to another lord? May one renounce [Note: [Col. 0091D] “And renounce father and mother,” etc. The word *ejerare* means to disown or renounce. The author’s meaning is that another oath, the military one, must not be superimposed upon the sacrament of baptism. For when a Christian binds himself by the military oath, he appears to renounce God’s law, by which parents are commanded to be loved in accordance with God. LE PR.] father and mother and every neighbor, whom the Law commands to be honored and loved after God (Exod. 20; Lev. 19) [Note: [Col. 0092C] “Whom the Gospel, making none of them dearer than Christ, honored in the same manner.” The old copy reads: “Whom the Gospel, making Christ alone dearer … also honored.” Hence, whether we read “making Christ alone dearer,” or “making none dearer than Christ,” the meaning will be the same. RIG.], and whom the Gospel too, making none of them dearer than Christ, honored in the same manner [Note: [Col. 0092C] “Honored in the same manner.” That is: the Gospel commanded that parents and every neighbor be honored no less than had been prescribed by the Mosaic Law. For the Law ordered them to be honored immediately after God, and the Gospel immediately after Christ. RIG.] (1 Cor. 6; Matt. 5; 1 Cor. 8)?

Will it be permitted to occupy oneself with the sword when the Lord declares that whoever has used the sword will perish by the sword? And will the son of peace take part in battle, when even litigation is unsuitable for him [Note: [Col. 0092D] “When even litigation is unsuitable for him.” He alludes to 1 Cor. 6:7: “Now therefore it is already altogether a fault among you that you have lawsuits with one another. Why do you not rather suffer injury?” Consequently Rhenanus is not wrong in interpreting *litigare*, a jurists’ term, as bringing an action against a person or concerning property before a judge. Yet it must be noted that the Apostle immediately adds in the same passage: “But you commit injury,” etc. PAM.]? Will he administer chains, imprisonment, tortures, and punishments, when he is not even the avenger of his own injuries?

Will he now perform guard duties for another more than for Christ? Or even on the Lord’s Day, when he will not perform them for Christ? Will he keep watch over temples which he has renounced? Will he dine in a place of which the apostle disapproves? Will he defend by night those whom he has put to flight by exorcisms during the day, leaning and resting upon the spear [Note: [Col. 0092D] “Resting upon the spear.” The books published thus far read “upon the *lunchus*.” Livy, Book XLIV: “They stood helmeted, holding their shields upright before them; then, when weary, leaning on their spears and placing their heads upon the rim of their shields, they slept standing.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0092C] The Frankfurt and Paris editions read *lunchus*.] with which Christ’s side was pierced (John 19:34)?

Will he also carry a standard rivaling Christ? Will he request the watchword from a commander after he has already received one from God? When dead, will he be disturbed by the trumpeter’s trumpet, though he expects to be raised by the trumpet of the angel? And will a Christian be burned in accordance with military regulations, when he was not permitted to burn incense [Note: [Col. 0092D] “When he was not permitted to burn.” Incense, that is, to an idol. The Glosses of Isidore define *cremutium* as a whole burnt offering. RIG.], and when Christ remitted for him the deserved punishment of fire?

How many other acts can be observed among the offenses involved in military duties, to be interpreted as transgression? The very act of transferring one’s name from the camp of light to the camp of darkness is transgression [Note: [Col. 0092D] “Is transgression.” The manuscript of Agobard reads “belongs to transgression.” RIG.].

Certainly, if faith finds some men already committed to military service when it comes to them afterward, their case is different: it was so with those whom John admitted to baptism (Luke 3:3), and with the most faithful centurions, the one whom Christ commends (Matt. 8) and the one whom Peter catechizes (Acts 10). Nevertheless, once faith has been accepted [Col. 0092C] and sealed, military service must either be abandoned immediately [Note: [Col. 0092D] “Once faith has been accepted and sealed, it must either be abandoned immediately.” That is, military service must be abandoned. RIG.], as many have done; or every kind of evasion must be employed to prevent anything from being committed against God—which military regulations do not permit either [Note: [Col. 0093C] “Which military regulations do not permit either.” What the manuscript reads, “outside military service,” seems less consistent with Tertullian’s meaning. For this is what he intends: the Christian whom faith finds in military service must either abandon it immediately upon accepting the faith—which military law, that is, the regulations governing military service, does not permit—or must guard by every possible means against doing anything contrary to the Christian faith, which likewise is scarcely permitted under Roman military law; or must endure every extremity for God, to which every Christian pledged himself when he pledged faith to Christ. RIG.]; or, finally, every extremity must be endured for God, something to which civilian faith equally binds one [Note: [Col. 0093C] “Something to which civilian faith equally binds one.” To death, that is. For a Christian civilian—that is, one who is not a soldier—has pledged this to Christ equally with a soldier. What follows suggests this interpretation. RIG.].

For military service will promise neither impunity for sins nor immunity from martyrdom. A Christian is nowhere anything else. There is one Gospel and the same Jesus, who will deny everyone who denies, confess everyone who confesses God, save the life lost for his name’s sake, and, conversely, destroy the life gained in opposition to his name (Matt. 10:39). Before him, a faithful civilian is as much a soldier as an unfaithful soldier is a civilian. The state of faith admits no pleas of necessity [Note: [Col. 0093D] “The state of faith admits no pleas of necessity.” Outside the state of faith, however, they are admitted (*Apology* XLV; *Exhortation to Chastity* VI). Necessity is reckoned as an excuse (*On Idolatry* X). Necessity is put forward as a kind of advocate pleading for pardon (*Apology* IV, VII), and shortly afterward here: “The plea of necessity is exceedingly familiar.” RIG.]. There is no necessity to sin for those whose sole necessity is not to sin.

For a person is also pressed by the necessity of torture or punishment to sacrifice and directly deny the faith. Yet discipline does not connive even at that necessity, because the necessity of fearing denial and undergoing martyrdom is stronger than that of escaping suffering and fulfilling one’s official duty. Otherwise, an excuse of this kind subverts the whole substance of the sacrament, so that it loosens the fastening even for voluntary sins. For even the will could be maintained to be a necessity [Note: [Col. 0093C] Semler reads “could have been.”], since it plainly has something by which it is compelled.

I would lay down this same argument in advance for the other grounds for official crowns, in connection with which the plea of necessity is exceedingly familiar: official posts must either be avoided, lest we fall into sins, or martyrdoms must be endured, so that we may break free from those posts. I shall say no more [Note: [Col. 0093D] “I shall say no more.” The manuscript of Agobard reads “They do no more”; Ursinus, “Let them do no more.” RIG.] concerning the first form of the question, namely the unlawfulness of military service itself, so that the second may receive its answer—lest, if I drive military service out by every means, I have already appealed in vain concerning the military crown.

## CHAPTER XII

[Col. 0094A]

Suppose, then, that military service is permissible up to the question of the crown. But let us first speak about the crown itself. That laurel is sacred to Apollo or Liber: to the former as the god of missiles [Note: [Col. 0093D] “To the former as the god of missiles.” Relevant here is Plato’s derivation of Apollo from ἀπὸ τοῦ πάλλειν (*apo tou pallein*), that is, “from hurling”; hence also the custom of forming his images with bow and arrows. PAM.]; to the latter as the god of triumphs. Claudius teaches this, while also saying that soldiers were customarily wreathed with myrtle [Note: [Col. 0093D] “Also with myrtle.” Myrtle likewise served in military affairs. When Postumius Tubertus, during his consulship, triumphed over the Sabines—the first of all men to enter the city in an ovation—because he had carried out the affair easily and without bloodshed, he proceeded crowned with the myrtle of Venus Victrix and made that desirable tree acceptable even to enemies. This afterward became the crown of those celebrating an ovation. RHEN.]. For myrtle belongs to Venus, the mother of Aeneas, and not laurel—to her who was also the mistress of Mars, Mars being Roman through Ilia and the Romuli. But I do not believe Venus to be Roman in this respect together with Mars [Note: [Col. 0094C] “But I do not believe Venus to be Roman in this respect together with Mars.” He says: I might believe Mars to be Roman insofar as he begot the Romuli by Ilia; but I do not think Venus Roman insofar as she perceived Ilia as her rival and grieved over her. RIG.], insofar as this involves resentment toward a rival.

When military service is crowned with olive too, the idolatry is directed toward Minerva [Note: [Col. 0093C] The Frankfurt edition reads “Minerva.”], likewise a goddess of arms, but also one crowned from this tree upon making peace with Neptune [Note: [Col. 0094C] “Peace with Neptune.” Since Minerva made peace with Neptune while wearing an olive crown, the olive is therefore a sign of peace. LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 0093C] The Frankfurt and Paris editions read: “Indeed, that military service is crowned with olive derives from Minerva, goddess not only of the arts but also of arms; and that she made peace with Neptune because of this tree,” etc. Junius reads: “But also that she was crowned from this tree after peace was made with Neptune.”]. Among these things the superstition of the military wreath will be both polluted and polluting; and everything is now polluted by its very grounds.

Look at the annual declaration of vows: what does it seem to be? Its first stage is in the headquarters, its second on the Capitol. Hear, after the places, the words: “THEN WE VOWED TO YOU, JUPITER, THAT THERE WOULD BE A BULL WITH ITS HORNS ADORNED WITH GOLD” [Note: [Col. 0094C] “Then we vowed to you, Jupiter, that there would be a bull with its horns adorned with gold.” This is the reading of the old manuscript, which may also be confirmed by the ancient inscription of the Arval Brothers: “WE VOWED THAT THERE WOULD BE A GILDED BULL.” RIG.]. Whose utterance is this? Surely an utterance of denial. Even if the Christian is silent there with his mouth, he has answered with his crowned head.

The same laurel is proclaimed at the distribution of the donative—idolatry that is plainly not free of charge, selling Christ for a few gold coins, as Judas sold him for silver (Matt. 26). Will this be what is meant by, “You cannot serve God and mammon” (Matt. 16:24): to hand your hand over to mammon [Note: [Col. 0094C] Leopold reads “is.”] and withdraw from God? Will this be, “Render to Caesar what belongs to Caesar and to God what belongs to God” (Matt. 22:21): neither rendering the human being to God [Note: [Col. 0094D] “Neither rendering the human being to God.” Yet the human being is God’s coin and image. He therefore says that the Christian, when both a soldier and crowned, does not render the human being to God, because he walks about wearing an ornament dedicated to idols; and that he takes the denarius away from Caesar, because he carries home the donative received from Caesar. Thus he renders neither what is God’s to God nor what is Caesar’s to Caesar. He signifies that military service and the business of battles are unsuitable for a Christian. Battles must be left to Caesar, the crown to the world and Satan; the human being himself, God’s image, must be rendered to God—but pure, free, the servant and soldier of no one but God. RIG.], nor leaving Caesar his denarius?

Is the laurel of triumph constructed from leaves—or from corpses? Is it adorned with metal plates [Note: [Col. 0094D] “Adorned with metal plates.” The old copy reads *lemnis*, “with ribbons.” RIG.]—or with funeral pyres? Is it anointed with perfumes [Note: [Col. 0095C] Hence the Paris edition reads: “with contrasting ribbons, gilded with little plates, with perfumes”; C. Wouwer: “constructed from leaves, adorned with ribbons”; Junius, from a manuscript: “The laurel of triumph is constructed from leaves, on one side with contrasting ribbons, on the other gilded with little plates; it is anointed with perfumes.”]—or with the tears of wives and mothers? Perhaps the wives and mothers of some Christians too, for Christ is also among the barbarians [Note: [Col. 0095C] “For Christ is also among the barbarians.” Trypho in Lucian gave the same answer when Critias asked whether the affairs of the Scythians were a concern to God: “Certainly, if perhaps Christ is also among the nations” (εἰ τύχοι γε Χηστὸς καὶ ἐν ἔθνεσι). RIG.]. Has not the man who has carried upon his head this cause [Note: [Col. 0095C] “Who has carried upon his head this cause”: the triumphal crown—the cause of corpses, funeral pyres, and tears. RIG.] himself also fought?

There is also another military service, that of the imperial households. For they too are called *castrenses*, palace soldiers, and are bound to the duties [Note: [Col. 0095C] “They too are bound to the duties of imperial solemnities.” In Festus we read that soldiers who were not exempt but performed service for the emperor were called *munifices*; those exempt from service were called *beneficiarii*. In Vegetius too, *munifices* are soldiers who perform service, from which those called *immunes* are free. Interpret *munificae*, therefore, as “bound and obligated to the duty of imperial solemnities.” A later age used *munificum* for *munifice*. Paul the Jurist too, in the chapter *Munus*, *On the Meaning of Words*, teaches that *munus* also signifies “official duty”; hence “military duties” and the designation of certain soldiers as *munifices*. RHEN.] of imperial solemnities. But you in like manner are the soldier and servant of another. Even if you are the servant of two, God and Caesar, you certainly cease to be Caesar’s whenever you owe yourself to God—who, I believe, has the stronger claim even in matters common to both.

## CHAPTER XIII

Public occasions crown the public orders with laurels, while magistrates additionally receive golden crowns [Note: [Col. 0095C] Semler inserts “as at Athens, as at Rome,” and says that these words were perhaps added by some interpreter.]. Etruscan crowns are ranked above [Col. 0095B] even these [Note: [Col. 0095D] “Etruscan crowns are ranked above even these.” Thus reads the old copy: *superferuntur illis*, that is, they are considered more noble even than golden crowns. RIG.]. This is the name for crowns distinguished with gemstones and golden oak leaves in honor of Jupiter, which magistrates wear with palm-embroidered robes when escorting the sacred chariots [Note: [Col. 0095D] “When escorting the sacred chariots.” Festus teaches that the *tensa* was a silver vehicle on which the emblems of the gods were carried to their cushioned seats in the circus during the Circensian games. RHEN.]. There are also provincial golden crowns, now seeking heads larger than those of men—the heads of statues.

But your orders and your magistracies, and the very name of your senate, are the Church of Christ. You are enrolled [Note: [Col. 0095D] “Enrolled in the books of life.” Lucian’s Trypho had also heard something of these books, for he says to Critias: “Therefore leave everything alone, so that you too may be enrolled among the good in the heavenly books” (Ὥστε ἔασον ἅπαντα ὡς καί σε ἐν ταῖς ἐπουρανίοις βίβλοις τῶν ἀγαθῶν ἀπογράψωνται). RIG.] in the books of life (Phil. 4; Rev. 13). There your purple is the Lord’s blood, and your broad stripe (*clavus latus*) is in his cross (Matt. 27). There the axe is laid to the trunk [Note: [Col. 0096C] Junius reads “to the roots.”] of the tree (Matt. 3:10); there are the rods [Note: [Col. 0096C] “There are the rods from the root of Jesse.” He says “rods,” alluding to the fasces of Roman magistrates; so it is read in the manuscript of Agobard. RIG.] from Jesse (Isa. 11:1).

Let public horses look to themselves and their crowns. When your Lord wished to enter Jerusalem according to Scripture (Matt. 21), he did not even possess a donkey of his own. “These trust in chariots and these in horses, but we shall call upon the name of the Lord our God” (Ps. 19). We are removed from even residing in that Babylon in the Apocalypse of John (*Apology* 18), much more from its magistrate’s platform [Note: [Col. 0096C] Junius reads “to the platform.”].

The populace too is crowned, now in exultation over the rulers’ successes, now through the particular customs of municipal solemnities; and extravagant display eagerly seizes upon every public rejoicing. But you are a stranger to this world, a citizen of the Jerusalem above. “Our citizenship,” he says (Phil. 3:20), “is in heaven.” You have your own civic rolls [reading *census* for the apparent OCR *sensus*], your own calendars [Note: [Col. 0096C] “Your own calendars.” The *fasti* were books containing the scheme and festivals of the whole year; Tertullian speaks with reference to these. In the Church too there are *fasti*, so do not concern yourself with the calendars and solemnities of municipalities. The passage should indeed be noted as evidence of the Church’s custom of entering the “birthdays”—that is, the deaths—of martyrs in calendars, which in ecclesiastical terminology we call martyrologies. There is an excellent passage on this matter in Cyprian, Epistle 83. LAC.], your own calendars. You have nothing to do with the world’s rejoicings; indeed, you owe the contrary: “For the world will rejoice, but you will mourn” (John 16:20). And, I think, he calls those who mourn blessed (Matt. 5:5), not those who are crowned.

Weddings too crown bridegrooms [Note: [Col. 0096D] “Weddings too crown bridegrooms.” Relevant here is the custom by which a bride, while preparing to go to her husband, would weave a garland from vervain, which was dedicated to Venus, and from flowers and herbs she had gathered herself, and carry it with her; for it was considered a fault to wear purchased wreaths. Likewise, the bridegroom, while awaiting her at home, would crown the door with great ceremony and decorate many things with laurel, as Alexander ab Alexandro attests. PAM.]. Therefore let us not marry pagans, lest they lead us all the way into idolatry, from which marriage among them begins. You have the law from the patriarchs (Gen. 24:3; Gal. 3), and you have the apostle commanding marriage “in the Lord” (1 Cor. 7).

Worldly freedom too crowns its recipients. But you have already been redeemed by Christ—and at a great price. How will the world manumit another’s slave? Even if it appears to be freedom [Note: [Col. 0096C] The Wouwer manuscript reads “may seem.”], the former slavery likewise only appeared to be slavery. All things in the world are imaginary, and nothing is real. For even then, when redeemed by Christ, you were free from human ownership; and now you are Christ’s slave, though manumitted by a human being. If you regard the world’s freedom as genuine, so that you authenticate it with a crown [Note: [Col. 0096C] Latinus, Rhenanus, and the Frankfurt edition read “the crown.”], you have returned to human slavery, which you regard as freedom; you have lost the freedom of Christ, which you regard as slavery.

Surely the occasions of athletic contests must not also be debated [Note: [Col. 0096D] “Surely the occasions of athletic contests must not also be debated.” That is, the games. “Surely the games will not also come into dispute,” he says, “so that one must further ask concerning them whether a Christian ought to avoid them or not?” RIG.], when their very titles immediately condemn them as sacred or funerary? This alone remains: that Olympian Jupiter, Nemean Hercules, poor little Archemorus [Note: [Col. 0096D] “And poor little Archemorus.” Archemorus, the son of Lycurgus, died while still an infant after being injured by a serpent, through the negligence of his nurse Hypsipyle. She had left him in the grass while going to show the spring Langia to the Greeks, who were suffering from thirst. An annual contest was instituted in memory of the boy; and because he died while still an infant, Tertullian calls him “poor little” rather than simply “wretched.” RHEN.], and unhappy Antinous [Note: [Col. 0097C] “Unhappy Antinous.” The emperor Hadrian kept him as his favorite and loved him so tenderly that he named after him the Egyptian city where he also instituted an annual contest in his honor. Hegesippus elegantly committed this affair to writing, as the blessed Jerome reports. RHEN.] should be crowned in a Christian, so that he himself becomes a spectacle—the very thing he ought to watch [Note: [Col. 0097B] Rhenanus reads: “which ought not to be watched.”].

We have, I think, enumerated every occasion. Not one belongs to us: all are foreign, profane, and unlawful, once for all renounced in the profession of the sacrament. For these were the pomp of the devil and the offices of his angels: the world’s honors, solemnities, public favor, false vows, human servitudes, empty praises, shameful glories—and idolatry in all of them, even in the mere conception of the crowns with which all these were wreathed.

Claudius will indeed begin by saying that in Homer’s poems even heaven is crowned with stars—certainly by God, certainly for the human being; therefore the human being himself must be crowned by God. In the world, however, brothels [Note: [Col. 0097C] “Brothels too are crowned.” He appears to mean the crowns with which public or private household gods were crowned in individual houses, and therefore in brothels too, etc. PAM.], latrines, bakeries, prisons, gladiatorial schools, the amphitheaters themselves, the stripping-rooms, and the undertakers’ establishments themselves are crowned. How sacred and holy, how honorable and pure [Note: [Col. 0097C] “How sacred and holy, how honorable.” The reading of the Dijon manuscript is attractive: “How sacred and holy, how honorable,” etc. RIG.] this attire may be, judge not from one poetic heaven, but from the practices of the whole world.

A Christian, moreover, will not disgrace even his own door with laurels, if he knows how many gods the devil has attached even to doorways: Janus from *janua*, “door”; Limentinus from *limen*, “threshold”; Forculus [Note: [Col. 0097C] Some read “Forcula,” others “Foreculus.” RIG.] and Carda from *fores*, “doors,” and *cardines*, “hinges”; and among the Greeks, Apollo Thyraeus, “Apollo of the Door,” and the Antelian (*Antelioi*, “facing the sun”) demons.
