Arbiters of the faith. Some interpreters take considerable pains over this expression by comparing Tertullian’s own usage; [Col. 1162C] for in the *Apology against the Gentiles*, ch. 7, he seems to use *arbitri* for witnesses who are present at the performance of some action, saying: “Impious initiations likewise always exclude the profane” (that is, those who are strangers to those very impious superstitions) “and guard against arbiters” (namely, lest they be present as witnesses). And in the same work, ch. 16, he says: “It feared outside arbiters” (witnesses or observers) “of its vain worship.” And in *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 24, speaking of Christ transfigured, he says that “in the seclusion of the mountain, within the circuit of the cloud, he shone brightly before three arbiters.” But in the same book, ch. 21, he seems to take *arbiter* in the sense of “Author,” speaking thus of Christ: “The Arbiter and Master of this gracious discipline, the Illuminator and Guide of the human race, was proclaimed to be the Son of God.” See also what we said above concerning this word *arbiter*. In this passage, however, he seems to take “arbiters” for witnesses, perhaps alluding to 1 John 5:7: “There are three who bear witness in heaven: the FATHER, the WORD, and the HOLY SPIRIT.”

Guarantors of salvation. This is, of course, in the sense in which, in the Epistle to the Hebrews 7:22, Jesus is said to have become [Col. 1162D] “the Guarantor of a better covenant.” Therefore the FATHER also, who sends the SON, and the HOLY SPIRIT, who was sent by the Son, will be Guarantors; for the Father through the Son, and the Son himself in the Holy Spirit, promised salvation to us.

Of the divine names, that is, of the Divine Persons—lest anyone attempt to wring Sabellianism from this phrase, as though there were merely names, with no distinction of realities in the Godhead. Now, just as the number of Persons is threefold, so too is the number of names, namely in the grammatical sense; but in the theological sense there is one Name, that is, one and the same dignity and authority in those three Persons. Accordingly, Baptism conferred “in the three names of the Father, the Son,” etc., would be invalid; it is therefore necessary that it be performed “in the Name of the Father and of the Son,” etc. On this matter the theologians should be consulted where they treat the form of Baptism.

Are pledged. This is not badly written *pignerentur* for *pignorentur*; for even in Cicero, *Philippic* IV, and indeed in an active sense, I find: “Mars himself is accustomed to claim as a pledge every most valiant man” [Col. 1163A] (that is, to claim him for himself and take him, as it were, in pledge). Here, however, our author takes it in a passive sense, for “to be given in pledge,” so that the meaning is: both the attestation of faith and the guarantee of salvation are confirmed through Baptism—to which these two things are, as it were, attached—as by a pledge now given.

Mention of the Church. Perhaps this should be understood to mean that a mention of the Church is necessarily made by implication as well, since it is to her that this pledge is given.

The body of the three exists, namely, united in the three Divine Persons and in faith in them.

### Notes on Chapter VII

With an anointing. He is by no means speaking here of the anointing of Confirmation (for he treats that in the following chapter), but of the rite which even today immediately follows Baptism. For after this the kiss of peace is given, and then the baptized person is anointed with chrism on the crown of the head. Indeed, St. Jerome says in his *Dialogue against the Luciferians* that presbyters are not permitted to baptize without the accompanying anointing with chrism.

I cannot refrain from transcribing here the rite of this [Col. 1163B] anointing as it is found in an ancient Pontifical of the local Metropolitan Church, cited by our Edmund Martène in *On the Ancient Rites of the Church*, bk. I, ch. 1, art. 13, § 6: “When the infants have been lifted from the font—let the bishop or presbyter, after obtaining the chrism, make the sign of the cross with his thumb upon the crown of their heads, saying thus: ‘May God the Father Almighty—himself anoint you with the chrism of salvation in Christ Jesus our Lord (C. J. D. N.) unto eternal life. Peace be with you.’”

There was also another anointing preceding Baptism, by which those about to be baptized were anointed with oil upon the breast and shoulders. The Author of the *Recognitions*—whoever he may have been, certainly a very ancient writer—already mentions this anointing in bk. III: “Let each one of you be baptized in living waters, with the name of the Threefold Blessedness invoked over him, having first been thoroughly anointed with oil sanctified by prayer.” St. John Chrysostom also mentions this anointing in Homily 6 on Colossians 2, saying: “The person about to be baptized is anointed in the manner of athletes who are just about to enter the contest.” See [Col. 1163C] also the Author of *On the Sacraments*, bk. I, ch. 2, and bk. II, ch. 7, among the works of St. Ambrose. This anointing, however, was performed not with chrism but with the oil of catechumens. On this twofold anointing, the same Martène, in the place cited, will supply abundant material from the ancient so-called Latin *Ordines* and Rituals, as well as from the Greek Euchologia.

Moreover, the fact that the anointing appended to Baptism did not possess the character of sacramental Confirmation is evident even from this: Confirmation was conferred only by bishops—at least among the Latins—whereas that anointing, although performed with chrism, was administered even by presbyters, and not upon the forehead but upon the crown of the head.

He was anointed by Moses. For concerning the consecration of Aaron as priest, God himself commanded Moses in Exodus 29:7: “You shall pour the oil of blessing upon his head,” etc. Moreover, the ancients formerly used horns as vessels. Thus the anointing of kings was also performed with oil from a horn. “Fill your horn with oil,” etc., God said to Samuel in 1 Samuel 16:1, sending him to anoint David as king. “Samuel therefore took the HORN OF OIL [Col. 1163D] and anointed him in the midst of his brothers” (ibid., v. 13). Solomon likewise had been anointed by Zadok the high priest (1 Kings 1:39).

From chrism. *Christus* derives from κέχρισται, the third-person perfect passive of the verb χρίω, “I anoint”; while χρῖσμα is said to derive from the first-person perfect of the same verb, κέχρισμαι; and from ἀπὸ τοῦ χρίσματος, “from the anointing,” comes χριστός, “anointed.” Hence Isaiah 45:1: “Thus says the Lord to my ANOINTED”—from “he anointed”—“to my Christ Cyrus,” etc.

Anointing. Elsewhere χρῖσμα signifies the ointment itself with which an anointing is performed; hence Latin theologians also distinguish, in the Sacrament of Confirmation, between the chrism and the chrismation or anointing itself—that is, the application of the chrism—which the Greeks otherwise properly call χρῖσις. Yet I find that the Fathers use τὸ χρῖσμα indiscriminately for both the ointment and the anointing. Thus Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, bk. I, says concerning the ancient Lacedaemonians that they rightly regarded and called deceitful both deceitful garments and “anointings” (τὰ χρίσματα). [Col. 1164A] And in the *Paedagogus*, bk. II, after having said shortly before, οὐ θέμις δολερὰ εἵματα, καὶ χρίσματα εἰς τῆς ἀληθείας εἰσιέναι πόλιν, “It is unlawful for deceitful garments and ointments to enter the city of truth,” he writes shortly afterward: “Let a woman always be anointed together with the divine anointing of chastity” (τῷ τῆς σωφροσύνης χρίσματι συναλειφέσθω), “delighting in the holy ointment” (μύρῳ), namely, the Spirit. And in bk. IV he calls τὸ χρῖσμα τῆς εὐαρεστήσεως “the anointing of good pleasure,” that is, of a mind pleasing to God. But why multiply examples, when even in the New Testament τὸ χρῖσμα is taken for anointing? Thus 1 John 2:20: “And you have χρῖσμα, an anointing, from the Holy One.” And again in v. 27: “And the χρῖσμα, the anointing which you received from him, abides in you.”

By God the Father. Christ was anointed as Prophet, Priest, and King; for these alone were the three classes of men customarily anointed in antiquity. This anointing, however, was performed not with any visible chrism, but spiritually, by the Holy Spirit himself, namely when Christ, [Col. 1164B] having been baptized in the Jordan, immediately came up from the water; “and behold, the heavens were opened to him, and he saw the SPIRIT OF GOD descending like a dove and coming upon him. And behold, a voice from heaven said: THIS IS MY BELOVED SON, IN WHOM I AM WELL PLEASED” (Matthew 3:16–17). This, however, must not be understood as though the Holy Spirit anointed Christ at his Baptism with the fullness of his grace—for he already possessed it from the first instant of his conception, and it was incapable of any further increase—but as meaning that the Holy Spirit wished to display by some visible sign the anointing of Christ, consisting chiefly in the GRACE OF HEADSHIP and the HYPOSTATIC UNION, as something already accomplished long before. It is in this sense that Christ applied to himself in Luke 4:18 the words of Isaiah 61:1: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me.” Thus Peter too preached concerning Christ in Acts 10:38: “How God anointed him with the Holy Spirit,” etc.; St. Augustine, *On the Trinity*, bk. XV, ch. 26, explains this as follows: “Certainly not with visible oil, but with the gift of grace, [Col. 1164C] which is signified by the visible ointment with which the Church anoints the baptized. Nor indeed was Christ then anointed with the Holy Spirit when the Spirit descended upon him, after his Baptism, in the form of a dove. For then he deigned to prefigure his body, that is, his Church, in which the baptized especially receive the Holy Spirit; but he must be understood to have been anointed with that mystical and invisible anointing when the Word was made flesh,” etc. See the same author further.

We are delivered, though with this distinction: Baptism is a Sacrament washing away sins as stains of the soul, whereas that anointing belongs only to the rite following this Sacrament. It merely presents, as in a symbol of a past event, the deliverance effected by the power of baptismal grace, which spiritually anoints; it does not itself confer a new increase of grace.

A parallel passage occurs in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 8, where our author says: “The flesh is washed, so that the soul may be cleansed of stains. The flesh is anointed, so that the soul may be consecrated. The flesh is signed, so that the soul may be fortified. The flesh is overshadowed by the imposition of hands, [Col. 1164D] so that the soul also may be illuminated by the Spirit.” But these matters will presently be explained more fully.

### Notes on Chapter VIII

Is imposed. Two impositions of hands were formerly celebrated in connection with Baptism. One preceded not only Baptism but also the very anointing that belonged among the preliminary rites of Baptism—see the preceding chapter, note “With an anointing”—and it is frequently mentioned in the Latin baptismal *Ordines* presented by Edmund Martène in *On the Ancient Rites of the Church*, bk. I, ch. 1, § 8. The other followed Baptism and was to be performed only by a bishop.

Here it must be understood that in the first centuries of the Church solemn Baptism—namely, that which was given only on Holy Saturday and the vigil of Pentecost—was administered by the bishops themselves, and that they immediately appended the SACRAMENT OF CONFIRMATION as a kind of completion. Hence St. Paulinus writes in his *Life of St. Ambrose*: [Col. 1165A] “He was also most vigorous in carrying out divine duties, so much so that five bishops after his death could scarcely perform what he had customarily accomplished for those being baptized.” That custom persisted for many centuries in the Church. But when the multitude of the faithful had grown excessively in the individual churches, and bishops had become exceedingly necessary for preaching the Gospel, the principle expressed by Paul in 1 Corinthians 1:17 also began to prevail: “Christ did not send me to baptize, but to PREACH THE GOSPEL.” Hence it came about that bishops could not always be present even at solemn Baptism to confirm the baptized immediately. For this reason, a manuscript Pontifical of the Church of Apamea in Syria, written in 1214 and reproduced by our Martène in *On the Ancient Rites of the Church*, bk. I, ch. 2, art. 1, § 3, prescribes after all the rites of Baptism that, “IF THE BISHOP IS PRESENT, he must immediately confirm him”—the baptized person—“with chrism,” etc. Indeed, as early as the seventh century, few bishops performed the office of Baptism, being excessively occupied with other cares. Meanwhile, the same Martène, in the place cited, § 1, testifies that this practice of [Col. 1165B] confirming the newly baptized immediately was continued, at least in some churches, including Latin ones, nearly down to our own age. Among the Greeks the same custom persists even now: the Sacrament of Confirmation is appended immediately to Baptism, though it is administered not by a bishop but by the very presbyter who baptizes. On this matter Juenin should be consulted, Dissertation VII, *On Confirmation*, q. V, art. 2, § 1 ff.

Through a blessing, that is, a certain fixed form of words, such as must ordinarily be used in almost all the Sacraments at the Savior’s command, and which our author above, in ch. VI, likewise expressed by the word “blessing.”

Invoking the Holy Spirit. Moreover, this entire passage seems to favor the opinion of those who maintain that the imposition of hands alone—and not also anointing with chrism—is the matter of Confirmation. On this issue the dogmatic-scholastic theologians engage in much controversy, especially Tournély, *Treatise on the Sacrament of Confirmation*, q. 1, art. 3.

[Col. 1165C] Device, that is, a contrivance devised with skill. Thus our author says in *On the Spectacles*, ch. 10, that the demons, foreseeing their own interests from the beginning, inspired, among the other pollutions of idolatry, those ingenious devices of the arts belonging to spectacles. This is precisely the sense in which, in *On the Pallium*, ch. 1, he says that the Carthaginians marveled at the Roman battering ram, a machine of war, as a new and foreign “device,” that is, invention.

A spirit may be permitted. Certainly not a good spirit; for he is speaking of hydromancers, who drew predictions of future events from water. King Numa Pompilius is said to have been exceedingly devoted to this art. When he was preparing to regulate the religious institutions and rites of his Romans, since he appeared unable to seek counsel for himself in any other way, he was compelled to practice hydromancy, so that in the water he might see images of the gods—or rather, deceptions of demons—from whom he might hear what should be established and observed in sacred matters. So says St. Augustine, *The City of God*, bk. VII, ch. 35; and in the same place, after certain other remarks: “Because Numa Pompilius therefore *egesserit aquam*—that is, drew or carried out water—from which to practice hydromancy, he is said to have had as his wife the nymph Egeria—a decidedly chilly nomenclature. [Col. 1165D] For this is how events are customarily transformed into fables by being sprinkled with lies.”

Their incorporation, that is, their intimate conjunction—the conjunction, namely, of the water and that spirit.

With hands suitably placed above. That is, with hands imposed from above; for this was the rite of the hydromancers, who enchanted the waters and, as it were, surrendered them to the power of an evil spirit.

To animate. This entire sentence has the flavor of irony and alludes to the magical rites by which hydromancers customarily employ water for magical purposes. He says that they “animate” it with another—that is, with some higher and more powerful—spirit of such great splendor (just as hydromancers customarily boast that such glorious and illustrious effects are produced by that spirit); that is, they stir it up and render it, as it were, alive.

As an organ. That is, as a minister or instrument [Col. 1166A] subject to that spirit “obedientially,” as the Scholastics say, for the production of whatever effects it wishes.

Through holy hands, namely, through their imposition.

To fashion. *Modulari* means to arrange something according to a definite mode and measure, as we have already said above in ch. III, note “Fashioners.” By “spiritual sublimity,” moreover, he readily means the power of producing the sacramental grace of Confirmation, as though that power had been imparted to and inhered in the hands of the confirming bishop.

I shall relate here something that does not seem entirely foreign to our subject. Eunapius of Sardis, a pagan who around A.D. 380 was a sophist of no little renown among his own people, tells it concerning Iamblichus, a distinguished adherent of Platonic philosophy, in his *Lives of the Philosophers*: “Some time afterward, all agreed to go at the appointed season of the year to Gadara, a place of hot springs in Syria,” etc. “While Iamblichus was bathing there and the others were likewise occupied in bathing, a discussion arose concerning the baths, and Iamblichus said with a smile: ‘Although religious scruple prevents me [Col. 1166B] from disclosing these things, I shall nevertheless do so for your sake.’ At once he ordered his disciples to inquire of the local inhabitants by what names two of the smaller hot springs—though more elegant and beautiful than the others—had long ago been called. When they had carried out his command, they reported that one was named Love and the other Anteros, the god who avenges wrongs done to lovers. Immediately, after touching the water with his hand and murmuring a few little words, he summoned from the bottom of the spring a small boy, fair-skinned and of pleasing stature, with hair tinged like ruddy gold and the skin of his back gleaming, whose whole appearance was like that of one bathing or freshly bathed. His companions were astonished by the novelty of the event. ‘Let us pass,’ he said, ‘to the neighboring spring’; and rising at once, he went before them, absorbed in thought and with his face intent. There, performing the same actions as before, he called forth Love, who resembled the former boy in every respect except that his hair was darker, auburn, and spread across his neck. Both little boys crowded around Iamblichus, clinging to him in close embraces as though to their natural father. He immediately restored them to their own abodes, finished bathing, and departed,” [Col. 1166C] etc. Thus Eunapius.

These were, of course, fleeting apparitions of infernal spirits. No matter how much they pretended to have been washed and baptized, and though they were drawn from the waters by the hands of that magician, he was nevertheless unable to impose upon them the hands by which he might confirm them in some stable state.

Shaping, for “preshaping,” “figuring,” or simply “forming.” It is certainly in this last sense that I also find the verb used by Vitruvius, bk. VI: “The tragic stage is shaped”—this clearly should not be taken in a pejorative sense, but rather as “adorned”—“with columns, statues,” etc. Moreover, this entire passage is drawn from Genesis 48:13 ff., where: “He”—Joseph—“placed Ephraim at Israel’s left”—that is, Jacob’s, who had received the name Israel from the Angel with whom he had wrestled, Genesis 32:28—“but Manasseh at his own left, and thus at his father Jacob’s right, and brought them both to him. Jacob, stretching out his right hand, placed it upon the head of Ephraim, the younger brother, and his left hand upon the head of Manasseh, [Col. 1166D] who was the elder, crossing his hands. And Jacob blessed the sons of Joseph,” etc. Joseph, namely, placed both his sons before his father Jacob: the firstborn, Manasseh, at Joseph’s left, which directly faced Jacob’s right; but Ephraim at Joseph’s right and consequently at Jacob’s left. Joseph did not doubt that Jacob would place his right hand upon the one who had also been positioned on his right side—namely, Manasseh—and his left hand upon the other, namely Ephraim. But when Jacob placed his hands crosswise, that is, intersecting one another, and thus placed his right hand—which by nature has greater dignity because it is stronger—upon Ephraim, the second-born, and his left upon the firstborn Manasseh, Joseph attempted to correct what he thought an error by removing his father’s right hand from Ephraim’s head and transferring it to Manasseh’s. Jacob, however, undoubtedly conscious of a [Col. 1167A] higher Mystery, persisted in his intention and completed, in the same rite, the blessing he had already begun.

Which was to come to pass unto Christ. Those hands crossed in the form of a cross therefore foreshadowed the very Cross of Christ. MANASSEH was a figure of the Old Testament, as the firstborn—namely, in time; and because the Old Testament was itself a figure of the New Testament, Manasseh was thus A FIGURE of Christ himself, who in Colossians 1:15 is called “the Firstborn of every creature.” EPHRAIM, the second-born, represented the New Testament and was A FIGURE of Christ himself and of the Church, as something later—though again only in time—and, as it were, born second in Christ, but far greater in merits and privileges. In the Church there would be not the figure of the Cross, but the Cross itself: CHRIST CRUCIFIED. Therefore, although others read “which was to come to pass IN CHRIST,” I earnestly retain “which was to come to pass unto Christ”: namely, that which was to redound UNTO CHRIST, or to his Church THROUGH CHRIST.

Cleansed. Namely, through the washing that takes place in the Baptism of Christ.

[Col. 1167B] Blessed, namely, both sanctified through Baptism and its sacramental form, and thereafter confirmed through the imposition of hands. Moreover, this sentence may perhaps reasonably be seen as containing a tacit comparison, so that it can be explained thus: Just as the Holy Spirit descended in a certain manner upon the sons of Joseph, Ephraim and Manasseh, who had been blessed by Jacob through the imposition of hands, so too the grace of Confirmation descends upon the baptized when the bishop imposes his hands upon them.

Descends. The connective “and” appears to have dropped out and should therefore be supplied here.

Dove. Some of the ancients believed this; but Aristotle, *History of Animals*, bk. II, and Pliny, bk. XI, ch. 27, who were themselves ancients, as well as all more recent anatomists, assert the contrary. Although doves, like other animals, also possess gall—the source of all anger and bitterness—it is well established that this gall is very temperate and boils up only when greatly provoked. In this sense St. Augustine says in Tractate VII on the Epistle of St. John: “The dove has no gall, yet it fights with beak and wings for its nest: it rages without bitterness.”

[Col. 1167C] Of the figure. For that descent of the Holy Spirit was prefigured by another dove, Noah’s dove in the Old Testament; and this dove was at the same time a certain preliminary figure of our Baptism.

Of the world. Thus he elegantly calls Noah’s Flood, which St. Gregory Nazianzen, Oration 40, *On Baptism*, aptly calls “a flood of sin.” For the whole world, previously filled and, as it were, inundated with sins and crimes, was cleansed from the filth of sin when the Flood came upon it under Noah and nearly the whole human race was miserably suffocated in the waters. In like manner, through the Baptism of Christ we are washed clean from original sin and from all other sins present before Baptism. That this same Baptism of ours was prefigured by that Flood is indeed evident from Holy Scripture itself, 1 Peter 3:20–21: “In [Col. 1167D] the days of Noah, while the Ark was being built, in which a few—that is, eight souls—were saved through water. The like form of this”—the Apostle continues—“now saves you also: BAPTISM, not the removal of the filth of the flesh, but the appeal of a good conscience to God through the Resurrection of Jesus Christ.” St. Augustine explains this further in *Against Faustus*, bk. XII, ch. 14: “Noah with his family is delivered through water and wood”—the ark—“just as the family of Christ is signed through Baptism by the Passion of the Cross.” And in the same book, ch. 17, he says: “The Flood came seven days after Noah entered the ark because we are baptized in hope of the future rest signified by the seventh day.” St. Basil the Great also compares Baptism to the Flood in Homily I on Psalm 28:10, saying: “The Prophet therefore calls the grace of Baptism a Flood, through which the soul, washed and purified from sins and having cast off the old man, thereafter becomes a dwelling fit for God in the Spirit.” He has similar remarks in Homily II on the same Psalm.

Herald of heavenly wrath. This passage is exceedingly difficult [Col. 1168A]: for how could the dove bearing the olive branch of peace have been the herald of heavenly wrath? Some therefore think that “grace” should be restored in place of “wrath.” Since the abbreviation *grae* is found in certain manuscripts, they say that this word was deformed into *irae* (“wrath”) by an ignorant scribe. Yet I think that “wrath” can also be maintained if we read it thus: “the dove announced to the earth peace from heavenly wrath”—that is, the wrath of heaven, which had formerly been angry but was now pacified—“as the herald” of that wrath now appeased, namely, announcing it and proclaiming it like a herald to the whole world.

Is held forth. Nothing is more widely known than that among the gentiles of old the olive branch itself was a symbol of peace. Especially pertinent here is the line of Virgil:

> And in her hand she holds forth the branch of the peace-bearing olive.

Thus, on a certain coin of the emperor Hadrian, a figure is seen with bent knee, extending its right hand to the Emperor himself and holding forth an olive branch in its left, with the [Col. 1168B] inscription: “TO THE RESTORER OF SPAIN,” namely, to the one who restored it to peace. Likewise, a certain coin of Severus Pius Augustus depicts a togate figure, similarly bearing an olive branch, with the added inscription: “TO THE FOUNDER OF PEACE.” No less do coins of Maximian and Philip the Elder display a figure of this kind; the former is inscribed “THE PEACE OF AUGUSTUS,” and the latter “ETERNAL PEACE.” Very many examples of this kind could be assembled here if space permitted.

By the same disposition, meaning “in the same manner” or “according to the same arrangement.”

To our flesh. For because it was formed from the earth—“the Lord God formed man from the slime of the earth,” Genesis 2:7—it is rightly called “earth.”

Bringing the peace of God. Concerning this dove, St. Ambrose says in *On Luke*, bk. II, ch. 3: “The grace of the laver requires simplicity, so that we may be simple as doves. The grace of the laver requires peace, which in the ancient type a dove once conveyed to that Ark which alone was immune from the Flood. [Col. 1168C] He who has now deigned to descend IN THE FORM OF A DOVE taught me of whom that dove was a type. He taught that in that branch and in that Ark there was a type of the peace of the Church: that amid the very floods of the World the HOLY SPIRIT should bring fruitful PEACE to his Church.” Thus St. Ambrose. That dove, therefore, brought an olive branch when the earth had already been cleansed from its filth; now, to those washed through Baptism, the Holy Spirit brings peace and grace of soul in the Sacrament of Confirmation and, by anointing the soul with oil, strengthens it for the struggle.

The Ark as a figure. In this allegorical meaning of the Ark, the ancient Fathers and interpreters agree almost universally. Of these I shall cite only two, Origen and St. Augustine. The former confirms it at length in Homily II on Genesis, from which I excerpt only a few words: “Just as it was then said to that Noah that he should build an Ark and bring into it not only his sons and relatives but also animals of various kinds, so too, at the consummation of the ages, the FATHER said to our NOAH, OUR LORD JESUS CHRIST, that he should make for himself an ARK of [Col. 1168D] squared timbers and give it dimensions filled with heavenly Sacraments,” etc. St. Augustine, moreover, speaks beautifully in *The City of God*, bk. XV, ch. 26: “God commands Noah, a righteous man, to build an ark in which he and his family might be delivered from the devastation of the Flood. This is undoubtedly a figure of the City of God journeying as a pilgrim in this world—that is, of the CHURCH—which is saved through the WOOD upon which hung the Mediator between God and men, the Man CHRIST JESUS.” See the same author further, both in this chapter and in the following ch. 27; likewise *Exposition of the Gospel of St. John*, Tractate VI; *Questions on the Gospels*, bk. II, q. 44; *On Catechizing the Uninstructed*, ch. 19; but especially *Against Faustus*, bk. XII, ch. 14 ff., where he explains the Ark most fully as a figure of the Church. On the same subject one may also read St. Gregory the Great, Homily XVI on Ezekiel; Rupert of Deutz, *Commentary on Genesis*, bk. IV, ch. 71 ff.; and many others everywhere.

By a flood. Junius considers this passage corrupt and accordingly believes it should be restored thus: “If the world has also sinned again, in respect of which Baptism is badly compared, a flood [Col. 1169A] of fire is therefore appointed,” etc. He thus assigns the following meaning to the passage: “But if, after the singular grace of God was offered in Baptism, the world is carried into sins and plunges headlong into them altogether, by which deed it behaves most unjustly toward Baptism, and Baptism has been badly matched; therefore—that is, for this reason—a flood of fire is appointed for the world, so that just as it once perished when the waters overflowed, it may likewise one day be consumed by the fire of divine judgment.”

For my part, however, I would prefer to retain the text. It is admittedly somewhat obscure, but can be understood in this sufficiently suitable sense: “But the world nevertheless sinned again,” relapsing into its former sins—especially in the offspring of Ham, who treated his father so insultingly and whose son Canaan and his descendants, the Canaanites, were cursed by God himself (Genesis 9:25)—“in respect of which,” that is, by which circumstance or so that, “it is badly compared.” This does not mean that the earlier comparison was bad or unsuitable. Rather, not only is Baptism well compared to the Flood, since it cleanses all the filth of sins from the soul of each person just as the Flood cleansed it from the entire [Col. 1169B] world, but the world should also rightly be compared “in evil” to fire—that is, to the other flood which is to come upon it at the Last Judgment. “Therefore”—or for that reason—“a flood of fire is appointed,” etc. Recall what we said above in the note “Of the world.”

Is appointed for fire. The world, namely. On this matter St. Justin Martyr says in *Apology* II, p. 51 of my edition: Καὶ Σίβυλλα δὲ καὶ Ὑστάσπις, γενήσεσθαι τῶν φθαρτῶν ἀνάλωσιν διὰ πυρὸς ἔφασαν. Οἱ λεγόμενοι δὲ Στοϊκοὶ φιλόσοφοι, καὶ αὐτὸν τὸν Θεὸν εἰς πῦρ ἀναλύεσθαι δογματίζουσι, καὶ αὖ πάλιν κατὰ μεταβολὴν τὸν κόσμον γενέσθαι λέγουσι; that is: “Both the Sibyl and Hystaspes said that corruptible things would be consumed by fire. Those called Stoic philosophers teach that God himself will be dissolved into fire, and they say that the world will come into being anew through transformation.”

What, moreover, of Josephus, who in *Jewish Antiquities*, bk. I, ch. 3, affirms that Adam foretold a universal destruction of things, one by FIRE and the other by FLOOD? I willingly pass over the rest [Col. 1169C] concerning the two pillars, one of brick and the other of marble, of which at least one would escape either the fire or the waters. It is all too widely repeated and rests, so far as is known, almost entirely upon the testimony of this Josephus alone.

Meanwhile, it is certain that this opinion concerning the world’s destruction by fire permeated nearly all antiquity and was propagated even by the pagans Zeno, Pythagoras, Heraclitus, Sophocles, and others.

But see how this matter is supported by weighty testimonies of Holy Scripture. Of these I cite only one, clearer than the rest, from 2 Peter 3:5: “The heavens existed long ago, and the earth, standing out of water and through water by the word of God; through these the world that then existed perished, flooded by water. But the heavens and earth that now exist have been stored up by the same word and reserved for fire until the day of judgment and the destruction of ungodly men.” And shortly afterward: “The Day of the Lord will come like a thief; on that day the heavens will pass away with a great rushing sound, the elements will be dissolved by heat, and the earth and the works that are in it will be burned up.” [Col. 1169D] Our most learned Calmet treats this subject in his introductory Dissertation *On the End of the World*, etc.

Restores. For thus it will be at the consummation of the age: “The Son of Man will send his angels, and they will gather out of his kingdom all scandals and those who practice iniquity, and cast them into the furnace of fire” (Matthew 13:40–42).

What must be received. Those very figures of our Baptism—namely both the FLOOD and FIRE—should admonish us of what awaits in the next life those who fall after BAPTISM and are not afterward restored through Penance. For one must by no means agree with Montanus or Novatian that sins committed after Baptism are likewise incapable of forgiveness.

### Notes on Chapter IX

Supports supplied by nature, namely, the natural qualities that commend water above the other elements, which were discussed throughout ch. III.

[Col. 1170A] Privileges of grace. Gratuitous gifts superadded to that same nature, which were likewise discussed throughout ch. IV.

Solemn observances of discipline, namely, the rites customarily employed in Baptism by ordinance of the Church, as above in chs. VII and VIII.

Preliminary constructions, that is, preparations and antecedent ceremonies. Our author uses this word in this sense in *Against Hermogenes*, ch. 16: “Therefore, in the preliminary construction of this proposition,” etc. No less so in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 4: “Then the heretics begin immediately from that point, and from that point construct their preliminary argument.” See also *On Prescription against Heretics*, ch. XV.

Prayers, the formulas of prayer customarily employed especially in the blessing of the baptismal font or in Baptism itself. See above, ch. IV, note “God having been invoked.”

The sanctity of the water. Here he calls *religio* that sanctity which inheres in the baptismal water as a sacred thing destined for the Sacrament of Baptism.

Unencumbered. We properly call those persons “unencumbered” whose feet have been released and freed from chains as impediments.

[Col. 1170B] By the sacrament? Whatever Junius may object, the sentence must be read interrogatively.

From the world. Here “world” is taken in the pejorative sense, as St. James uses it in 4:4 of his Epistle: “Whoever wishes to be a friend of this world makes himself an enemy of GOD.” And St. Paul says of Christ in Galatians 1:4: “He gave himself for our sins, that he might rescue us from this present evil world.”

Their former ruler, as though another and far more merciless Pharaoh, who through sin exercised the cruelest dominion over the human race and still rules over individual sinners until they have been delivered from his tyranny through the water of Baptism. “When you were slaves of sin,” says the Apostle in Romans 6:20, “you were free from righteousness.” Read this entire chapter of the Apostle.

They leave behind, namely, by renouncing Satan and his pomps in Baptism. Moreover, the Apostle himself sufficiently declares in 1 Corinthians 10:1 ff. that the crossing of the Red Sea was a figure of our Baptism: “Our fathers [Col. 1170C] were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea, and all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea.” Commenting on these words, St. Augustine says in *Against Faustus*, bk. XX, ch. 29: “The cloud and the Red Sea are assuredly Baptism. The enemies pursue from behind: past sins die.” St. Ambrose writes similarly in *On Those Who Are Initiated*, etc., ch. 3: “Do you observe that in that crossing of the Hebrews a figure of sacred Baptism was already present, in which the Egyptian perished and the Hebrew escaped? For what else are we taught daily in this Sacrament but that guilt is drowned and error abolished?” Thus Christians pass through Baptism as through the Red Sea into a new life. “The sea,” says Theodoret, as cited by Calmet on 1 Corinthians 10:2, “represented the sacred LAVER which those to be baptized enter; the cloud, the GRACE OF THE HOLY SPIRIT; Moses, the PRIEST who baptizes; the rod of this legislator, the CROSS OF JESUS CHRIST; the Israelites in the sea, the CHRISTIANS who are baptized; the drowned Egyptians and their leader Pharaoh, the demons cast down from the dominion they exercised over us.”

[Col. 1170D] Advantage. Latinius reads “for advantageous use,” and I do not oppose him. The common reading, however, can also be retained, although it is rather harsh, and “for its own advantage” can be understood as “for its own usefulness”—the usefulness that water customarily affords human beings through its pleasantness and clarity.

Of Moses, *Mosei* being used for *Mosis*, the genitive case.
