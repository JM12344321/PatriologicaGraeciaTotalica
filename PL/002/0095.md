If the man is the head of the woman, he is surely also the head of the virgin, from whom comes the woman who has married—unless, that is, a virgin is some monstrous third kind, with a head of her own. If it is disgraceful for a woman to be shaved or shorn (1 Cor. 11:5), it is surely so for a virgin as well. Accordingly, let the age that emulates God see to it [Note: [Col. 0897D] “The age that emulates God.” He means the Christian age. No one doubts, however, that girls ought to be unshorn and young men shorn. Canon 17 of the Council of Gangra seems to me worthy of attention; its words are: εἴ τις γυναικῶν διὰ νομιζομένην ἄσκησιν ἀποκείροιτο τὰς κόμας, ἃς ἔδωκεν ὁ θεὸς εἰς ὑπόμνησιν τῆς ὑποταγῆς, ὡς ἀναλύουσα τὸ πρόσταγμα τῆς ὑποταγῆς, ἀνάθεμα ἔστω: “If any woman, thinking thereby to adopt a stricter discipline, cuts off the hair that God gave as a reminder of subjection, as though dissolving the ordinance of subjection, let her be anathema.” This is repeated in *Decretum*, distinction 30, *Quaecumque*. The pursuit of ornaments in women was, of course, condemned, as was every kind of cosmetic artifice: *De consecratione*, distinction 5, *Fucare*, and 33, question 5, *Mulier*. If a woman must have authority upon her head [Note: [Col. 0899C] “If a woman must have authority upon her head.” [Col. 0899D] That is, the sign of the authority to which God subjected woman. For if it is “because of the angels” (1 Cor. 11:8, 10)—namely, those whom we read to have fallen from God and heaven through lust for women [Note: [Col. 0899D] “Because of the angels.” This again pertains to Tertullian’s error, of which he has made mention above in the books *On Idolatry*, *On the Apparel of Women*, and *On the Dress of Women*. [Note: [Col. 0899D] “Whose bloom refuses even human lust.” That is, “rejects”; just as elsewhere he says “to expose a garment” for “to take it off.” A virgin refuses lust when she rejoices in the bloom of her virginity and does not wish it to be plucked, like that untouched and inviolate flower of Catullus:

> Unknown to the flock, bruised by no plough,

For Scripture also presents the matter in this way: “And it came to pass,” it says, “when men began to become numerous upon the earth, and daughters were born to them, that the sons of God, seeing that the daughters of men were beautiful, took wives for themselves from among all [Col. 0899B] whom they chose.” Here the Greek term for women has the sense of “wives,” because marriage is being mentioned. When, therefore, it says “the daughters of men,” it plainly indicates virgins who were still reckoned as belonging to their parents. Married women, after all, are named after their husbands; Scripture could instead have said “the wives of men.” Likewise, it does not call the angels adulterers but husbands, since they took the unmarried daughters of men, whom it had said above had been born. Thus too, by saying above that they had been born, it signified virgins. Therefore so dangerous a face, which hurled stumbling blocks all the way to heaven, must be overshadowed [Note: [Col. 0899D] “Therefore it must be overshadowed,” etc. That is, veiled. For the unveiled face of a virgin [Col. 0900C] could offer beauty as an occasion of sin; for this reason Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus*, book III, chapter 2, calls beauty μοιχικὸν κάλλος, “beauty that incites adultery.” LE PR.], so that, when she stands before God—to whom she is answerable for the banishment of the angels—she may also blush before the other angels, and may restrain that wicked former freedom of her head, which must now no longer be presented even to the eyes of men [Col. 0899C].

But even if those angels had desired women already defiled, women should all the more have been veiled because of the angels, inasmuch as the angels could all the more have sinned because of virgins. Moreover, if the Apostle adds the precedent of nature [Note: [Col. 0900D] “The Apostle adds the precedent of nature.” Let no inattentive person suppose that Septimius contradicts St Paul. For although abundant hair is, as it were, a natural ornament of woman, nevertheless—even if women are shorn (although, as we have shown elsewhere, there was a contrary custom)—women must always be veiled after their hair has been cut, so that the veil takes the place of the hair. This is customarily done among nuns even in our own times. LE PR.], namely, that an abundance of hair is a woman’s honor, because her hair is given as a covering [Col. 0900A], surely this is preeminently the distinction of virgins. Their very arrangement of the hair [Note: [Col. 0900C] “Wherefore,” Semler.] [Note: [Col. 0900C] “Itself,” the manuscripts, Wouwer and Rigault.] is properly of such a kind that, gathered up upon the crown, it covers the very citadel of the head with a circuit of tresses.

## CHAPTER VIII

The opposites of all these considerations assuredly have the effect that a man must not veil his head: namely, because he has not naturally acquired an ambitious profusion of hair; because it is not disgraceful for him to be shaved or shorn; because the angels did not go astray on his account; because he is the glory and image of God; and because Christ is his head (1 Cor. 11:2, 7).

Accordingly, since the Apostle discusses man and woman—why she ought to be veiled, but he ought not—it is apparent why he also remained silent about the virgin: namely, because by the same reasoning he allowed the virgin to be understood under “woman” [Note: [Col. 0900C] “Woman,” Semler.] by which he also did not name the boy, since the boy was to be reckoned under “man” [Col. 0900B]. He encompassed the entire order of each sex under their proper terms, “woman” and “man.”

Thus Adam, even [Note: [Col. 0900C] Semler deletes “even.”] while still sexually intact, is called “man” in Genesis: “She shall be called woman,” he says, “because she was taken from her man” (Gen. 2:23). Thus Adam was a man before marital intercourse, just as Eve was a woman. On both sides the Apostle has pronounced sufficiently upon the entire class of each sex, both briefly and fully, by so comprehensive a definition: “Every woman,” he says (1 Cor. 11:5). What is “every” unless every kind, every rank, every condition, every dignity, every age? For “every” denotes a whole, complete and lacking no part of itself. A virgin, moreover, is a part of woman.

Likewise, concerning a man’s not being veiled, he says, “Every man” (1 Cor. 11:4). [Col. 0900C] See: two distinct terms, “man” and “woman,” and “every” applies to each. There are two laws corresponding to one another: on the one side, veiling; on the other, uncovering. Therefore, if, because “every man” was said, the term “man” is common even to one who is not yet a man—an unbearded male [Note: [Col. 0900D] “An unbearded male.” I have seen it observed by others, before we applied our hand to these brief annotations, that *investis* means “prepubescent,” and *vesticeps* “pubescent.” Lucretius, book V, should perhaps be understood in that sense:

> To become pubescent with the soft garment of puberty.]—then, since the term is common according to nature, the law of not being veiled is also common to him who, according to discipline, is a virgin among males [Col. 0901A]. Why, then, should it not likewise have been settled beforehand that every virgin woman is included under the term “woman” by sharing the name, so that she is also included by sharing the law? [Note: [Col. 0901C] “Why should it not have been settled beforehand,” etc. He continues in that manner of arguing which he employs throughout this work: the term “woman” is used indiscriminately of a virgin and of one joined to a husband. LE PR.]

If a virgin is not a woman, neither is an unbearded boy a man. If a virgin is not covered because she is not a woman, let the unbearded boy be covered because he is not a man. Let the same allowance be made for the same virginity. Just as female virgins are not compelled to be veiled, so boys should not be commanded to be unveiled.

Why do we acknowledge the Apostle’s definition as absolute on one side [Note: [Col. 0901C] “The Apostle’s definition on one side.” Why does the heretic adhere to one part of that command rather than the other? For when Paul wrote “every woman,” he included females of every kind, condition, and age. LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 0901C] *Diffinitionem*, Semler.] with regard to every man, without reconsidering [Note: [Col. 0901C] *Detractamus*, Semler.] why he did not also name the boy, yet on the other side violate the equally absolute definition concerning every woman? “If anyone,” he says, “is contentious, we have no such custom, nor does the Church of God” (1 Cor. 11:16). He shows that there had been some dispute [Col. 0901B] concerning this class; to extinguish it he employed a complete brevity: not naming the virgin, to show that there was no room for doubt about her being veiled, and naming “every woman,” when he might have named the virgin. The Corinthians themselves understood it in this way. Indeed, to this day the Corinthians veil their virgins; by approving what they learned, they show what the Apostles taught [Note: [Col. 0901C] “They approve,” Semler.].

## CHAPTER IX

Let us now see whether, just as we have shown that the arguments from nature and causation also apply to a virgin, the prescriptions of ecclesiastical discipline concerning a woman likewise extend [Note: [Col. 0901C] *Spectant*, “extend,” Semler and Leopold; Junius perhaps reads *speciant*, “refer.”] to a virgin.

A woman is not permitted to speak [Col. 0902A] in church (1 Cor. 14:34; 1 Tim. 2:12), nor to teach, baptize, [Note: [Col. 0901C] “A woman is not permitted to speak in church.” It was accepted in Roman custom that women should not exercise public offices—not because they lack judgment, but lest they involve themselves in masculine duties contrary to the modesty proper to their sex. For that reason the Church too judged that they should be excluded [Col. 0901D] from masculine functions. For the decree of the Apostle contains the words: “I do not permit women to speak in church.” Since this expression is general, it embraces every masculine function, including priestly ones. A woman is not permitted to speak in church—that is, neither to inquire nor to dispute, for these are masculine functions and are granted only to men. Nor may she teach, baptize, or offer, since these are indeed the duties of a priest, president, or overseer of a church. The Author of the *Questions from the Old Testament*, question 45, volume IV of the works of Augustine, says that it is absurd to assert that it was granted to woman that she, like man, should be the image of God by right of dominion: “For how can it be said of woman that she is the image of God, when it is agreed that she is subject to the dominion of man and possesses no authority? For she can neither teach, nor be a witness, nor give a pledge, nor judge; much less can she command!” In short, public and masculine functions are not permitted to a woman, and priestly functions much less. RIG.] nor to offer, [Note: [Col. 0901D] “Nor to offer.” Following Rhenanus, Pamelius explains this [Col. 0902C] as “to sacrifice,” which I would scarcely admit. For what is meant by an interpretation according to which a woman is not permitted to celebrate Mass? Who ever permitted it? “To offer” should be understood of vows and prayers. LE PR.] nor to claim for herself a share in any masculine function, much less [Note: [Col. 0902C] Semler reads *ne duo sacerdotalis officii*; the reading is uncertain.] in priestly office. Let us ask whether any of these things is permitted to a virgin.

If it is not permitted to a virgin [Note: [Col. 0902C] “But if it is not permitted to a virgin,” Semler.], but in every matter she is subjected under the same condition, and the necessity of humility is assigned to her together with woman, whence shall this one thing be permitted to her which is not permitted to any female? What prerogative against her own condition does she deserve merely because she is a virgin and has resolved to sanctify her flesh? [Note: [Col. 0902C] “To sanctify her flesh.” Below: “to consecrate nature itself.” These words are found in Isidore, but are most absurdly twisted into another sense in book II of *On Ecclesiastical Offices*. RIG.]

Is an exemption from the veil therefore granted her, so that she may enter the church as a conspicuous and distinguished person and display the honor of sanctity in the freedom of her head? [Note: [Col. 0902C] “So that she may display the honor of sanctity in the freedom of her head?” Those who sought human glory from virginity repeatedly claimed that the exemption from the veil had rightly been granted to them and that a prerogative granted to sanctity ought not to bring them disadvantage or resentment. For in this way, they said, being conspicuous and distinguished by that honor when they entered the church, they would encourage [Col. 0902D] the others toward the same work of sanctifying the flesh. But Tertullian contends that such an honor is inappropriate for them. He refutes it by proposing other, indeed greater, honors, but ones even more inappropriate for a virgin: “She could,” he says, “have been honored more worthily by some prerogative of masculine rank or office.” It is evident from what precedes that these words are spoken in mockery: “A woman is not permitted to claim for herself a share in any masculine function, much less in priestly office.” Likewise from what follows: “Thus nothing by way of position has been permitted to a virgin as an honor, nor anything by way of insignia.”—“That she may display the honor of sanctity.” Isidore reads: “the honor of a sanctified body.” RIG.] She could have been honored more worthily by some prerogative of masculine rank or office.

Indeed, I know that somewhere a virgin less than twenty years old [Col. 0902B] was placed in the order of widowhood. [Note: [Col. 0902D] “Placed in widowhood.” Namely, by the bishop; he says “in widowhood” just as one says “in the diaconate.” From this it is gathered that at that time there was in the Christian Church an order or dignity of widows. Thus, in book I *To His Wife*, he does not permit a widow to be chosen into the order unless she has been the wife of one man. Later the Councils of Orange I and Epaon resolved that no widows at all should be ordained. RIG.] If the bishop owed her some relief, he could surely have provided it in another way while preserving respect for discipline, lest such a prodigy—not to say a monstrosity—should now be marked out in the Church: a virgin widow, made all the more portentous because, precisely as a widow, she does not cover her head. She denies herself in both respects: both as a virgin who is reckoned a widow and as a widow who is called a virgin. Yet she sits there uncovered [Note: [Col. 0902C] “Covered,” Semler; Latinius perhaps reads *non texta*, “not covered.”] by the same authority by which she is a virgin.

For that seat, women are sometimes chosen only after sixty years of age (1 Tim. 5:9), and not merely women who have had one husband—that is, married women—but also mothers, and indeed women who have reared children. Thus, equipped by experience of every affection [Col. 0903A], they may readily know how to aid others with both counsel and consolation, and may also have passed through all those things by which a woman can be tested. Thus nothing by way of position has been permitted to a virgin as an honor.

## CHAPTER X

Nor, similarly, has anything been permitted by way of insignia. Besides, it is quite inhuman if women, though in every respect subject to men, display an honor-bearing mark of their virginity by which [Note: [Col. 0903C] “As though,” Venice.] they are admired, gazed upon, and magnified by the brethren, while men—so many virgins, so many voluntary eunuchs—walk about with their good unseen, carrying nothing that would also make them illustrious.

They too will therefore have to claim certain insignia for themselves: either the feathers of the Garamantes, or the headbands of the barbarians, [Note: [Col. 0903C] Perhaps *ut*, “so that,” Junius.] [Note: [Col. 0903C] “The headbands of the barbarians.” In the ancient manuscripts and Rhenanus’ editions, *Scrobulos* or *Strubulos*. RIG.] or the twisted locks [Col. 0903B] of the Germans, [Note: [Col. 0903D] “The twisted locks of the Germans.” Their hair was twisted into a knot. > And on the bloodless forms he reads the figures  
> marked by iron, as the painted man lies dying. RIG.]

or else the opposite should be done, and they should remain hidden in church with veiled heads.

We are certain that the Holy Spirit could more fittingly have endorsed some such thing for males, if he had endorsed it for women. For, besides the authority of their sex, males ought rather to have been honored in the name of continence itself: the more avid and ardent their sex is toward women, the more laborious their continence is because of its greater passion, and therefore the worthier of every display—if virginity derives its dignity from display.

Does not continence, moreover, take precedence over virginity, whether that of widowers or of those who by mutual consent have now renounced their common degradation? [Note: [Col. 0904C] “Their common degradation.” This belongs among Tertullian’s blemishes. He burned with so great a love of virginity and continence that in this passage—and below, chapter 2—he calls the lawful intercourse of husband and wife by that name. As an antidote, however, it may serve that in *On Modesty* he calls it “the intercourse of blessing.” PAM.] For virginity consists in grace, but continence in virtue. Not to desire what you have grown accustomed to desiring is a mighty contest. But what you have never known the pleasure of desiring [Col. 0903C], you will readily refrain from desiring, since you have no adversary—the desire for that pleasure. [Note: [Col. 0904D] “The enjoyment of desiring you have never known.” Those who explain this otherwise than of forbidden desire waste their oil and labor. It cannot be said of children, even though the children born of love are called “the rewards of Venus” by Anna, Dido’s sister, in the Latin poet, and δῶρ’ Ἀφροδίτης, “gifts of Aphrodite,” in Homer. How, therefore, would God not more fittingly have endorsed some such distinction for men as an honor, either because the male is more akin to him—namely, as his image—or because he labors more? But if nothing is granted to the male [Col. 0904A], much less is it granted to the female.

## CHAPTER XI

But we shall now dispose of by an answer the point that we omitted above because of the discussion that followed, lest we break its coherence.

When we took our position upon the Apostle’s absolute definition—that “every woman” must be understood as including every age (1 Cor. 11:5)—it was open to reply on the other side that a virgin must therefore be covered from birth and from the first designation of her age. But this is not so. Rather, she must be covered from the time when she begins to understand herself, to enter the register of her own nature, to depart from the status of a virgin [Note: [Col. 0903C] “Virgin qualities,” François.], and to undergo that new experience which belongs to another age.

For Adam and Eve, the founders of the race (Gen. 2), went naked as long as they lacked understanding. But when they tasted of the tree of knowledge, the first thing they perceived was that they had cause to be ashamed. Each therefore [Note: [Col. 0904C] *Ita*, “thus,” Semler.] marked the understanding of his or her sex by a covering. Yet even if she must be veiled because of the angels, the law of the veil will undoubtedly begin to operate from that age at which the daughters of men could provoke desire for themselves and become capable of marriage. [Note: [Col. 0904D] “Become capable of marriage.” This expression frequently occurs in Tertullian concerning a virgin who is now mature and marriageable. Petronius, *Satyricon*: “at years eager for sexual experience.” LAC.]

For a virgin ceases to be such from the moment when she is capable of not being one. And therefore [Note: [Col. 0904D] “Among Israel.” Pamelius asks where this is written in the sacred Scriptures; Junius observes that it does not derive from the law of God, but only from Jewish custom founded upon natural law and with reference to what is said in Deuteronomy 22. LAC.] among Israel it is unlawful to give a girl to a man unless her maturity has been attested by blood: before this indication she is an unripe thing. Therefore, if she is a virgin only so long as she is unripe, she ceases to be regarded as a virgin when she is mature [Note: [Col. 0904C] “By nature,” Paris.], and is brought under the law as one who is no longer a virgin, just as she is brought under marriage.

Betrothed women, indeed, have the example of Rebecca (Gen. 24:64). While she was being conducted, still unknown, to a bridegroom still unknown to her, as soon as she recognized him as the man whom she had seen [Col. 0904C] [Note: [Col. 0904C] “Observed,” Rhenanus.] from afar, she did not await a struggle of right hands, [Note: [Col. 0904D] “She did not await a struggle of right hands.” That is, she did not wait. RIG.] an encounter of kisses, or an exchange [Note: [Col. 0904C] “Communion,” François.] of greeting. Instead, confessing what she felt—that is, that in spirit she was married—she denied herself to be a virgin by veiling herself there and then. O woman already formed by the discipline of Christ! For she showed that marriage, like fornication, is effected even by sight and intention. Except that some still veil Rebecca. [Note: [Col. 0905C] “Some still veil Rebecca.” That is, some still veil betrothed virgins, but by no means the others. Tertullian, however, contends that the others too must be veiled. RIG.]

As for the others—that is, those who are not betrothed—let the parents’ postponement, arising from poverty [Note: [Col. 0905B] Most editions read *viderint*, “let them see to it.”] [Note: [Col. 0905C] “From poverty.” That is, from slender means or fortune. For, as the satirist says:

> Those whose virtues are obstructed by straitened means at home  
> do not easily rise.

Poorer girls of this sort also find husbands with greater difficulty. RHEN.] or excessive scrupulousness, [Note: [Col. 0905D] “Or excessive scrupulousness.” That is, from the fastidiousness of parents whom no one pleases. RHEN.] see to that; and let the vow of continence itself see to it as well. [Note: [Col. 0905D] “The vow of continence.” This passage should be noted most carefully. Here an author of the greatest antiquity mentions the vow of continence, which believers were already accustomed to make at that time. LAC.] None of this concerns age as it runs through its own stages and pays the dues belonging to maturity. Another mother in secret—nature—and another father in concealment—time—have married their daughter according to their own laws.

Look at that virgin of yours, already married: married in soul by expectation and in flesh by transformation, [Note: [Col. 0905B] “Expectation, transformation,” Semler; Rigault reads *ex spectatione*, “by reason of expectation.”] for whom you are preparing a second husband. [Note: [Col. 0905D] “Look at that virgin of yours, already married.” “That virgin of yours,” he says, “is already married: married in soul by the expectation of a husband, married in flesh by the transformation of puberty.” She is married in flesh and soul, just as “in face and shoulders she is like a god.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0905D] “For whom you are preparing a second husband.” The sense is: your virgin, whom you still consider a virgin, has ceased to be one because of her age—not indeed corrupted by a man, but changed by age. Consequently, the husband to whom you give her will be her second. RHEN.] Her voice has already changed, her limbs are fully developed, her private parts are clothed everywhere, HIL [Gl. 11.5, 3.14.10, 5.12] [Note: [Col. 0905D] “And modesty is clothed everywhere.” Tertullian himself says in *On the Soul*: “The sex is more fully clothed.” RIG.] and the monthly periods pay their tribute. [Note: [Col. 0905D] “And the monthly periods pay their tribute.” So the Fulvio Orsini manuscript reads. But the Dijon manuscript and Rhenanus’ editions read “defend.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0905B] “Defend,” Semler. “Pay”: this conjecture of Ciacconius is confirmed as probable by the authority of the manuscript, according to Rigault.] Yet you deny that she is a woman whom you say undergoes the experiences of women [Col. 0905B]!

If intercourse with a man makes a woman, let them not be covered until after the actual consummation of marriage. Yet even among the pagans women are led to their husbands veiled. If, however, they are veiled at betrothal because they have been united to a male in both body and spirit by a kiss and the joining of right hands—through which they first unsealed the modesty of the spirit [Note: [Col. 0905C] “In spirit,” Semler.]—and by the mutual pledge of conscience through which they covenanted the whole experience of shame, [Note: [Col. 0906C] “Through which they covenanted the whole experience of shame.” He came close to saying: “They covenanted the entire shamelessness of the degradation of marriage!” RIG.] how much more will time veil them? Without time they cannot become betrothed, and, when time presses upon them, they cease to be virgins even without betrothal.

Even the pagans observe the authority of time, so as to render to each age its rights according to the law of nature. For they send females to conduct legal business from the age of twelve, but a male two years later [Col. 0906A], determining puberty by years, not by betrothal or marriage. A female is called *materfamilias*, “mother of a household,” though a virgin, and a male *paterfamilias*, “father of a household,” though still unbearded. We, however, do not observe even the things of nature [Note: [Col. 0906B] “According to natural principles,” Venice.], as though the God of nature were other than our God.

## CHAPTER XII

Acknowledge her as a woman; acknowledge her also as married, from the testimonies of both body and spirit which she undergoes in both conscience and flesh. These are the first tablets recording her natural betrothal and marriage. Place a veil upon her outwardly, since she has an inward covering. Let the upper parts also be covered in one whose lower parts are not naked.

Do you wish to know the authority possessed by age? Place side by side a girl prematurely violated but dressed as a woman, and one who, though advanced in maturity, remains in virginity and retains her own mode of dress: the former will more easily be denied to be a woman than the latter will be believed to be a virgin. So great is the credibility of age that it cannot even be obstructed by clothing [Col. 0906B].

What of the fact that these virgins of ours also acknowledge by their dress the change of age, and, as soon as they understand themselves to be women, are led out of the class of virgins? [Note: [Col. 0906B] “What of the fact that they already,” Junius.] [Note: [Col. 0906C] “Are led out of the virgins.” They lead themselves out of the register of virgins. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0906B] “Are led out,” others.] Beginning with the head itself, they lay aside what they formerly were: they alter their hair [Note: [Col. 0906C] “They alter their hair.” I would not venture to affirm that this means the same as twisting the hair. For when I read in *On the Dress of Women*, book II, chapter 6, “to alter heads with saffron,” it recalls the care the ancients devoted to changing and dyeing the hair. Herodian says of Antoninus: κόμας τε τῇ κεφαλῇ ἐπετίθετο ξανθάς, “he even placed blond hair upon his head”; and Clement of Alexandria says, τρίχας ξανθὰς ποιοῦν, “making the hair blond.” Capitolinus says in *Verus*: “He is indeed said to have taken such care over the blondness of his hair,” etc. So strong was the fashion for blond hair that St Jerome warns Laeta: “Do not dye your hair red.” LE PR.], insert tresses with a more wanton hairpin, and, by parting their hair from the forehead, openly profess their womanhood.

They now seek from the mirror advice about their beauty, soften a more unyielding face by washing it, perhaps adulterate it with some cosmetic, toss a mantle over themselves, furnish themselves with shoes of many forms, and carry more equipment to the baths. [Note: [Col. 0906D] “They carry more equipment to the baths.” Clement, *Paedagogus* III.5: τὰ τε ἀργυρώματα μεθ’ ὧν ἐμπομπεύουσιν ἀπειροκάλως ἐν τοῖς βαλανείοις προτιθέασι, “They display in the baths, with vulgar ostentation, the silver articles with which they make their procession.” RIG.]

Why pursue each detail? They alone, by the conspicuousness of their attire, carry their entire womanhood about with them. [Note: [Col. 0906D] “They alone, by the conspicuousness of their attire, carry their entire womanhood about with them.” This is precisely how the Dijon manuscript reads, and I do not doubt that such was Tertullian’s own wording. “These virgins of ours,” he says—that is, Christian virgins—“also acknowledge by their dress the change of age; and as soon as they feel puberty rising within them, they understand that they are now women and have departed from the class of virgins. Accordingly, beginning with the head itself, they lay aside virginal dress, alter their hair, insert tresses with a more wanton hairpin, and, by parting their hair from the forehead, openly profess their womanhood.” He then concludes: “Indeed, virgins alone, by the conspicuousness of their attire, carry their entire womanhood about with them.” For they go about with uncovered heads, the principal part of conspicuous feminine attire, which women are accustomed to govern. Thus they display more conspicuous womanhood than women themselves. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0906B] *Solemnem*, “customary,” François and Paris; *solae enim*, “for they alone,” Latinius and Junius; *sola enim manifesta paratura*, “for by conspicuous attire alone,” Ciacconius; *solae*, “they alone,” in the Dijon manuscript, [Col. 0906C] which Rigault prefers.] Yet by the mere nakedness of the head they wish to play the virgin, [Note: [Col. 0907C] “But they wish to play the virgin.” They wish to be regarded as virgins. Useless and shameless seems to me the labor of those who immediately explain *devirginari*, “to be deprived of virginity,” as its contrary and thrust forward a thousand obscenities merely to hunt for that little glory by which they may be thought learned. I therefore leave it to investigators of sexual matters and ancient impurity. *Devirginare* is διαπαρθενεύειν, “to diminish, to dishonor,” as the glosses teach—an interpretation wholly irrelevant and most unfitting to this passage. But I do violence to you, modesty! LE PR.] denying by one article of dress [Note: [Col. 0907D] “By one article of dress.” Namely, the uncovered head. RIG.] what they profess by their entire equipment.

## CHAPTER XIII

If they misuse clothing because of men, [Note: [Col. 0907D] “If they misuse clothing because of men.” This entire passage should be read and punctuated as follows: “If they misuse clothing because of men, let them fulfill it in this respect also; and, as they veil their heads among the pagans, let them certainly hide in church the virginity that they conceal outside the church. They fear strangers; let them also respect their brethren. Or else let them consistently dare to be seen as virgins in the streets, just as they dare in the churches.” Christian virgins, although bareheaded in church, nevertheless went about among the pagans and in the streets with their heads veiled. They said that they “misused clothing”—that is, that they walked about in clothing not properly their own—lest, if they wore their virginal dress, the pagans should desire them more ardently. Tertullian praises their prudence, but further requires them to fulfill that dress, which was not their own, even when they attended church—namely, because of the brethren, who are also men. RIG.] let them fulfill that clothing in this respect also, by veiling their heads among the pagans. At least in church let them hide the virginity that they conceal outside the church. They fear strangers; let them also respect their brethren. Or else let them consistently dare to be seen as virgins in the streets, just as they dare in the churches. I shall praise their resolution if they also market some portion of their virginity among the pagans. [Note: [Col. 0907D] “If they also market some portion of their virginity among the pagans.” If only they possess enough constancy in their virginity to dare to set forth examples of it from which the pagans may take something to imitate. RIG.]

Their nature is the same outside as inside; their rule of life among men and before the Lord is established by the same freedom. Why, then, do they hide away their good outside but publish it in church [Col. 0907B]? I demand a reason: is it so that they may please their brethren, or so that they may please God himself?

If God himself, he is as capable of seeing what is done in secret as he is just in rewarding what is done for himself alone. Accordingly, he commands us not to trumpet any of the things that will earn a reward with him, nor to obtain compensation for them from men.

Now if we are forbidden to perform even the gift of a single *victoriatus*, [Note: [Col. 0908C] “A single *victoriatus*.” A kind of coin introduced from Illyricum, which circulated as merchandise; concerning it, see Pliny, book XXXIII, chapter 3. LE PR.] or any act of almsgiving whatever, with the left hand aware of it [Note: [Col. 0908C] “With the left hand aware.” The reading of the ancient manuscripts should be restored as follows: *sinistra conscientia*, “with left-hand awareness.” He said *sinistra conscientia* for *sinistra conscia*, just as in the book *On the Pallium* he says “priestly equipment” in place of “the equipment of a priest.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0907C] *Sinistra conscientia*, “with left-hand awareness,” Semler.] (Matt. 6), how much darkness ought we to spread around ourselves when we offer so great an offering to God—our very body and our very spirit—when we consecrate that very nature itself? [Note: [Col. 0908C] “When we consecrate that very nature itself.” The Dijon manuscript reads: “When we consecrate nature to him himself.” I would prefer: “When we consecrate nature itself to him.” RIG.]

Therefore, because this cannot be regarded as done for God—since God does not wish it to be done in this way—it follows that it is done for the sake of men. It is thus illicit from the outset, as being lustful for glory. For glory is illicit for those whose probation consists in every humiliation. [Note: [Col. 0907C] “In every humility,” Rhenanus.] And if the virtue of continence is conferred by God, “Why do you boast as though you had not received it?” (1 Cor. 4:7). But if you have not received it, what do you possess that was not given to you? By this very fact, moreover, it is established that it was not given to you by God: you do not render it to God alone. Let us therefore see whether that which is human is firm and genuine.
