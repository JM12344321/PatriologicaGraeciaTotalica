But this point too will certainly be seized upon: that Scripture signifies only of heaven and this earth that God made it in the beginning, but says nothing of the kind concerning the aforementioned forms; and therefore those which are not said to have been made belong to uncreated matter. We shall answer this difficulty as well. Divine Scripture would have spoken sufficiently if it had declared that the principal wholes of things—the heaven and the earth—were made by God, since they necessarily had their own proper contents, which could be understood as included within those principal wholes. Now the contents of heaven and earth at that first time were darkness, the abyss, spirit, and waters. For beneath the earth were the abyss and darkness. If the abyss was below the earth and darkness over the abyss, then undoubtedly both darkness and the abyss were below the earth. Beneath the heaven, however, lay the spirit and the waters; for if the waters which covered the earth were above the earth, and the spirit was above the waters, then both spirit and waters were likewise above the earth. But what is above the earth is necessarily below the heaven. And just as the earth rested upon the abyss and darkness, so the heaven rested upon the spirit and waters, and they were embraced within them.

[Note: [Col. 0225C] Thus Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür. Rigault and the Venetian edition read *Scripturarum*.]

[Note: [Col. 0225C] Rhenanus conjectured that the reading should be *disserit* (“speaks”), and shortly afterward *commendans et coelum*. Others, following Junius, read *si summas*, which is better.]

[Note: [Col. 0225D] Others, according to Junius, give *contempta dantur* from the Vatican codex.]

Thus it is nothing novel that only the containing whole should be named as the sum, while what is contained within it is understood as a part. Suppose, for example, that I say: “The city constructed a theater and a circus; the stage, however, was of such and such a kind, and statues stood above the *euripus*, and an obelisk towered above everything.” Because I did not also expressly declare that these particular forms were made by the city, will they therefore not have been made by it together with the circus and theater? Or did I refrain from adding that these particular forms too were made because they were contained within the things which I had previously said were made, and it could be understood both that they were contained and within what they were contained?

[Note: [Col. 0225D] “And statues above the *euripus*.” The Euripus, the strait at Chalcis, gave its name, by resemblance, to the trenches by which the games were surrounded in place of barriers. It afterward came about that *euripus* began to be used for any trench or channel. Pliny the Younger, in his letter to Caninius Rufus: “What of the green and jeweled *euripus*?” Vitruvius, Book VII: “For harbors, promontories, shores, rivers, springs, channels, shrines, groves, mountains, cattle, and shepherds are painted.” RHENANUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0225D] “Above the *euripus*.” In the Circensian games, the *euripus* was a pool or strait. For Hesychius states in these words that it is a receptacle for waters: he calls the *euripus* (Εὐρίπους) a “receiving-place for waters” (δόξας ὑδάτων) [the Greek reading is materially uncertain]. Cassiodorus, Book III, Letter 51: “The *euripus* presents an image of a glassy sea,” whence the expression: “Dolphins swim amid the waters of the sea.” “New shores and artificial rivers” are called *euripi* by Manilius, *Astronomica*, Book IV. See Suetonius, *Julius*, chapter 39. LE PRIEUR.]

[Note: [Col. 0226D] “And an obelisk above everything.” No one doubts that the column called an obelisk stood in the circus. Augustus erected in the circus an obelisk cut by King Semnesertes, 125¾ feet high, apart from the base of the stone. Another stood in the Campus Martius, nine feet shorter, carved by Sesostris. Today the celebrated Vatican obelisk at Rome is of ophite stone; its height is 170 feet, apart from its base, which measures 37 feet. By the work of Domenico Fontana of Como, it was moved from the left side of the Vatican basilica into the middle of the square in the year 1586. Moreover, in *On the Shows*, chapter VIII, its “monstrous mass” is called “the obelisk.” Pliny discusses it in Book XXXVI, chapter 9. LE PRIEUR.]

But let this example be set aside as merely human; I shall take another from the authority of Scripture itself. “God,” it says, “made man from the earth, breathed into his face the breath of life, and man became a living soul.” It does indeed name his face here, but did not say that even this was made by God. Later, however, it speaks of his rib, bones, flesh, eyes, sweat, and blood (Gen. II.21, 23; III.5, 29; IV.10), although it did not then signify that these too were made by God. What will Hermogenes answer? Will the members of the human being also belong to matter because they are not individually reported as having been made? Or are these too reckoned as included in the making of the human being? In the same way, the abyss and darkness, spirit and waters, will be members of heaven and earth. For within bodies the members were made, and within bodies the members were named. No element fails to be a member of the element by which it is contained; and all elements are contained either by heaven or by earth.

[Note: [Col. 0226C] Rhenanus read *cui tam vero*, for he conjectured *vero et ossa*. But the Paris edition has *cutem vero*, while the Frankfurt edition, Rigault, Junius, and Pamelius have *costam vero*.]

[Note: [Col. 0226D] “In the making of the human being.” No one should be troubled by the word *factitatio*, derived from a frequentative verb. For in the preceding book as well as in this work, he uses *factitatio* for creation or making. It has an adversative force: “Therefore,” he says, “he understood both that it was made and that whoever made it was the maker of the thing made.” RHENANUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0226C] Others read “were.”]

## CHAPTER XXXII

I would offer this answer concerning the present passage of Scripture, insofar as it appears here to declare only the making of the bodies of heaven and earth. Scripture knew that there would be those who would of their own accord recognize the members within the bodies, and it therefore used brevity. Yet it also foresaw the dull-witted and the captious, who, while ignoring the tacit meaning, would demand a word signifying the making of the members themselves as well. Accordingly, for their sake too, it teaches in other passages that the individual forms were made.

[Note: [Col. 0226C] Rhenanus suspected *commentari*; the Frankfurt, Paris, and Rigault editions read *commendare*, Semler *commentari*.]

[Note: [Col. 0226C] The Frankfurt edition and Pamelius read *sciit*; Rigault and Latinius *scivit*; Semler and Oberthür *scit*.]

[Note: [Col. 0226C] Semler and Oberthür read *significatorum*.]

[Note: [Col. 0226C–D] Semler and Oberthür, without any sense, read: “in other places. You have Wisdom.”]

You have Wisdom saying, “Before the abyss I was begotten”: so that you may believe the abyss too to have been begotten—that is, made—for we too “make” children, although we beget them. It makes no difference whether the abyss was made or born, provided that a beginning is assigned to it, which would not be assigned if it were subject to matter. Concerning darkness, however, the Lord himself says through Isaiah: “I am he who constructed the light and made the darkness” (Isa. XLV.7). Likewise concerning the spirit, Amos says: “He who establishes the thunder and creates the spirit, and announces his Christ among men” (Amos IV.13), showing that the spirit was created which was assigned to the created earth, which moved over the waters, balancing, breathing upon, and animating the universe—not, as some think, signifying that the Spirit is God himself because God is spirit.

[Note: [Col. 0227C] Semler, concerning darkness, has *si materiae subjiciatur, esset*, and proposes reading *subjiciatur esse*.]

[Note: [Col. 0227D–0228D] “For the waters would not have been capable of sustaining the Lord.” At this point he tests the opinion of those who think that the Lord himself is signified by “the Spirit of God.” As though, he says, it were fitting for God’s Spirit to be borne over the waters because of the fluid and unstable nature of water, and God could not have fixed his own waters so that they stood still—which he accomplished when he led Israel across—or made them solid so that they could be trodden upon—which he brought about for the instruction of his disciples’ faith. Yet he supports that common opinion more strongly in *On Baptism*, where he says: “The liquid alone, always a perfect, flowing, simple substance, pure in itself, offered itself as a worthy vehicle for God.” Such is Septimius’s usual practice: he seizes upon every passage of Scripture capable of edification as though divinely inspired. Indeed, he presents this approach in the first book of *On the Dress of Women*, where he excuses his citation of the Book of Enoch; and in *On the Shows* he says broadly that divine Scripture is always distributed wherever, in accordance with the sense of the matter at hand, discipline is also fortified. Yet the truest interpretation of all would seem to be that the words “The Spirit of God was borne over the waters” signify that a certain vital force was present in the waters when they were first created: it was not merely water, but living water, which did not lie inert in sluggish stupor but, quickened by an enduring spirit, was always in motion, lest it ever be said to rest. Our author certainly intimates this on the page of *On Baptism* cited above. Chrysostom, however, speaks most plainly in his *Homily on Genesis* CXI: “What does this statement mean: ‘The Spirit of God,’ he says, ‘was borne above the water’? It seems to me to signify that a certain life-giving energy was present in the waters, and that the water was not simply standing still and motionless, but moving and possessing a certain vital power” (Τὶ βούλεται τοῦτο τὸ εἰρημένον, Πνεῦμα, φησί, θεοῦ ἐπεφέρετο ἐπάνω τοῦ ὕδατος; Ἐμοὶ δοκεῖ τοῦτο σημαίνειν, ὅτι ἐνέργειά τις ζωτικὴ προσῆν τοῖς ὕδασι, καὶ οὐκ ἦν ἁπλῶς ὕδωρ ἑστὼς καὶ ἀκίνητον, ἀλλὰ κινούμενον, καὶ ζωτικήν τινα δύναμιν ἔχον). RIGAULT.]

For the waters would not have been capable of sustaining the Lord; rather, he means that spirit from whom the winds also came into being, as he says through Isaiah: “Because the spirit went forth from me, and I made every breath” (Isa. LVII.26). Likewise concerning the waters the same Wisdom says: “And when he established the firm springs of that which is beneath heaven, I was arranging all things with him” (Prov. VIII.28–29).

[Note: [Col. 0227C] Rhenanus wished to read: “the springs of the waters which are beneath…” Pamelius and the Frankfurt edition read “the springs of that which is beneath heaven”; others, according to Junius, “which are beneath heaven.”]

When, therefore, we prove that these forms too were made by God, even though in Genesis they are merely named without mention of their making, perhaps an answer will be made from the opposite side: “Certainly they were made, but from matter”; so that Moses’ wording, “And darkness was over the abyss, and the Spirit of God was borne over the waters,” denotes matter, while the other Scriptures show in scattered passages the forms that were made from matter. Thus, just as earth was made from earth, so the abyss came into existence from abyss, darkness from darkness, and spirit and waters from spirit and waters. And, as we said above, matter could not have been formless if it possessed forms from which other forms were fashioned—except that they were not other forms but those very forms fashioned from themselves. For one cannot admit that they were different when they are presented under the same names; otherwise the divine operation could now appear pointless if God made things that already existed, whereas it would have been nobler if things which did not exist were made.

[Note: [Col. 0227C] Rhenanus and Semler read “are.”]

[Note: [Col. 0227C] Semler and Oberthür omit “from spirit and” and read only “from waters.”]

[Note: [Col. 0227C–0228C] Semler, following Rhenanus, reads *si qua ferant*; all the others accept Rhenanus’s conjecture, “if the things which existed received…”]

[Note: [Col. 0228C] The Frankfurt and Paris editions, following Rhenanus’s conjecture, write “if the things which did not exist were made.”]

Therefore, to conclude: either Moses signified matter when he wrote, “And darkness was over the abyss, and the Spirit of God was borne over the waters”; or, when these forms are elsewhere afterward shown to have been made by God, they should likewise have been shown to have been made from the matter which Moses had previously introduced; or, if Moses signified these forms and not matter, I ask where matter has been shown.

[Note: [Col. 0228C] Rhenanus and Semler read “Moses writes”; the Paris edition, “when he writes.”]

## CHAPTER XXXIII

But while Hermogenes finds matter among his own pigments—for he could not find it among the Scriptures of God—it is enough that everything is established to have been made by God, while nothing is established to have been made from matter. Even if matter had existed, we would have believed that it too was made by God, because, by laying down the rule that nothing is unbegotten except God, we would have carried the point.

[Note: [Col. 0228C] Rhenanus, Pamelius, and Semler read “could not”; Paris reads “had not been able”; Frankfurt, “could not sufficiently.”]

Up to this point there is room for reconsideration, until, once the appeal has been made to the Scriptures, the production of matter fails. The summary is straightforward: I find nothing made except out of nothing, because whatever I find to have been made, I know did not exist. Even if anything was made from something, it derives its origin from something already made: thus grass, fruit, animals, and the formation of the human being himself came from the earth; and swimming and flying creatures came from the waters. I shall be able to call such origins “materials” of the things brought forth from them—but materials which were themselves made by God.

## CHAPTER XXXIV

Moreover, that everything came into existence from nothing will finally be confirmed by that divine arrangement which will reduce everything to nothing. For the heaven will be rolled up like a book (Isa. XXXIV.4); indeed, together with the earth with which it was made in the beginning, it will cease to exist altogether (Matt. XXIV.21, 35). “Heaven and earth will pass away,” he says. “The first heaven and the first earth departed, and no place was found for them” (Rev. XXI.1), because, of course, whatever comes to an end also loses its place.

[Note: [Col. 0228C] “Divine” is absent from the Venetian edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0228D] “The first heaven and the first earth departed.” He translated this idiomatically into Latin rather than word for word, because in Revelation XXI one reads παρῆλθεν, that is, “passed away”; then he reads, “and no place was found for them,” instead of the Greek and Latin text, “And the sea is no more.” RHENANUS.]

Thus David also says: “The heavens are the works of your hands, and they themselves will perish” (Ps. CI.26–27). For even if he changes them like a covering, and they will be changed, nevertheless to be changed is to perish with respect to their former state, which they lose while they are being changed.

[Note: [Col. 0229C] Junius reads “and thus he will change.”]

[Note: [Col. 0229C] Semler and Oberthür read “which, while…”]

“And the stars will indeed fall from heaven, just as a fig tree, shaken by a powerful wind, loses its unripe figs” (Rev. VI.13).

[Note: [Col. 0229D] “And the stars will fall from heaven, like a fig tree.” These words are taken from Revelation, where the text reads: καὶ οἱ ἀστέρες τοῦ οὐρανοῦ ἔπεσαν εἰς τὴν γῆν, ὡς συκῆ βάλλει τοὺς ὀλύνθους αὐτῆς ὑπὸ μεγάλου ἀνέμου σειομένη, that is: “And the stars of heaven fell to the earth, as a fig tree casts off its unripe figs when shaken by a great wind.” The Greek ὀλύνθοι are what the Latins call *grossi*, that is, figs not yet ripe. Tertullian translated *grossi* as “its unripe fruit”; the Vulgate edition has “its *grossi*,” but the interpretation translated by Tertullian is virtually the same. For an ὄλυνθος or *grossus* is nothing other than a fig which is still hard and unripe. Theodorus Gaza, in his work *On Plants*, calls the wild fig an ὄλυνθος. RHENANUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0229D] “A fig tree loses its unripe fruit.” Rigault reads “its *grossi*.”]

“The mountains, however, will melt like wax before the presence of the Lord” (Ps. XCVII.5), when he rises, namely, to shatter the earth (Isa. II.19). “I shall also dry up the marshes,” he says, “and they will seek water and will not find it” (Isa. XCI.17; XCII.15); even the sea shall be no more.

[Note: [Col. 0229C] The Frankfurt, Pamelius, and Paris editions read “the Ocean.” Rigault, following Rhenanus, retains *hactenus*; so do all later editors.]

[Note: [Col. 0229D–0230D] “And now the sea—thus far,” that is, “It will exist no longer.” So above concerning heaven: “It will cease to exist altogether.” So in *On the Pallium*: “Thus far Sodom.” RIGAULT.—“Even the sea—thus far.” Others have “Ocean” in place of *hactenus*, in which case the sense would be: “Even the Ocean will fail them.” But the word *hactenus* is not unusual in Tertullian, who uses it to mean “it no longer is,” or “will be.” LE PRIEUR.]

Even if someone thinks that all these things should be interpreted spiritually, he will nevertheless be unable to remove the reality that they will occur just as they are written. For if any of them are figures, they must be made from things that exist, not from things that are absent, because nothing can furnish a likeness of something else from itself unless it itself exists as that which furnishes such a likeness.

[Note: [Col. 0229C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read: “nothing can furnish toward a likeness from itself.”]

I therefore return to the case, which determines that all things produced from nothing will pass into nothing. For God would have made nothing perishable from something eternal—that is, from matter—nor would he have created lesser things from greater, since it is more fitting for him to produce greater things from lesser: that is, something eternal from something perishable, as he also promises for our flesh. He wished already to establish in us this pledge of his power and might, so that we might believe that he also raised the universe from nothing, like something dead—which, of course, it had been, in that it did not exist—so that it might exist.

[Note: [Col. 0229C–D] Rhenanus, Pamelius, and Oberthür read “his powers and authorities”; Junius, “by his power and authority.”]

[Note: [Col. 0230C] Rhenanus and Semler read “will be.”]

## CHAPTER XXXV

As for the remaining condition of matter, although it ought not to be reconsidered—for the first requirement was that its existence should be established—nevertheless, as though it had been established, the sequence must be pursued, so that it may be all the more firmly established that matter does not exist, since not even its remaining condition can be sustained, and at the same time so that Hermogenes may recognize his own contradictions.

“At first sight,” he says, “matter appears to us to be incorporeal; but when investigated by right reason, it is found to be neither corporeal nor incorporeal.” What kind of “right reason” is this, which reports nothing right—that is, nothing certain? For unless I am mistaken, everything must necessarily be either corporeal or incorporeal—granting for the moment that there is anything incorporeal among substances strictly so called, although substance itself is the body of each particular thing. Certainly, after corporeal and incorporeal there is no third thing.

[Note: [Col. 0230C] The last two words are absent from Rhenanus and those who follow him.]

Come, then: let there even be a third thing, which that right Hermogenean reason has discovered, making matter neither corporeal nor incorporeal. Where is it? What is it like? What is it called? How is it described? How is it understood? Reason has reported only this: that matter is neither corporeal nor incorporeal.

## CHAPTER XXXVI

But see: he adds the contrary—or perhaps another reason occurs to him—reporting that matter is partly corporeal and partly incorporeal. Therefore, lest it now be neither, must matter be regarded as both? For it will be both corporeal and incorporeal, contrary to the pronouncement of that “right reason,” which plainly gives no reason for its own judgment, just as the other gives none either.

[Note: [Col. 0230C] Pamelius, Frankfurt, Paris, and Rigault read thus; others wrongly have *reciprocationis* or *reparationis*.]

For he holds that the part of matter from which bodies are produced is corporeal, but that its unregulated motion is incorporeal. “For,” he says, “if it were merely body, nothing incorporeal would appear in it—that is, motion. But if it had been wholly incorporeal, no body would be made from it.”

How much “righter” this reasoning is! Except that, if you draw lines as straight as this reasoning, Hermogenes, no painter is more stupid than you.

[Note: [Col. 0230D] “How much righter this reasoning is! Except that, if you draw such straight lines, Hermogenes…” Here one should note the proverbial expression, borrowed from the painter’s art—which was Hermogenes’—“to draw straight lines”; another such expression is “beyond the line.” PAMELIUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0230C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür—or rather a follower of their errors—read “sufficient for the reasoning”; Junius reads “reasoning”; others, “this is the reason.”]

For who grants you the right to reckon motion as a second part of substance, when it is not a substantial thing, precisely because it is not corporeal, but rather, if anything, an accident of substance and body—like action, impulse, sliding, falling, and likewise motion? For if something is moved by itself, motion is its action; it is certainly not part of the substance, as you make motion the incorporeal substance of matter.

In short, all things are moved either by themselves, as living beings, or by others, as non-living things. Yet we shall not say that either a human being or a stone is both corporeal and incorporeal because it has both body and motion; rather, we assign to all of them the single form of corporeality alone, which is a substantial thing. If any incorporeal things are present in them—whether their actions, passions, functions, or inclinations—we do not reckon them as portions.

[Note: [Col. 0230D] “As non-living things.” The Vatican manuscript has *inanimata*, but I was unwilling to alter the former reading, since the same word is found below in chapter XCIV and the author habitually uses obsolete expressions of this sort, such as *corporalitas* and *incorpora*. PAMELIUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0230C] Pamelius, from the Vatican codex, reads *inanimata*.]

[Note: [Col. 0230D] “Or their inclinations.” By *libidines* he means appetites and inclinations. Thus in *On Patience*: “Although they disagree among themselves through the various inclinations of sects and rivalries of opinions.” *Libidinibus* means “pursuits.” RHENANUS.]

To what end, therefore, does it serve to arrange one portion of matter as motion, which belongs not to substance but to the condition of substance? For what if it had pleased you to present matter as motionless? Would immobility therefore have appeared to be a second part of its form? In the same way, motion is not such a part. But it will be possible to discuss motion elsewhere as well.

[Note: [Col. 0231D] “But it will be possible concerning motion elsewhere as well.” The verb “to discuss” is understood, unless *licebit* stands for *liquebit*, just as in the preceding book *licuit* stood for *liquit*, that is, “became clear.” RHENANUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0231C] Rhenanus reads “will become clear.”]

## CHAPTER XXXVII

For now I see you returning once more to another kind of reasoning, which is accustomed to report nothing certain to you. For just as you infer that matter is neither corporeal nor incorporeal, so you allege that it is neither good nor evil; and then you add the following argument:

“If,” you say, “it were good, as it had always been, it would not desire God’s ordering; if it were evil by nature, it would not admit of transformation for the better; nor would God have applied any of his ordering to something of such a nature, for he would have labored in vain.”

[Note: [Col. 0231C] Rhenanus reads “that”; the Frankfurt and Paris editions read “just as.”]

[Note: [Col. 0231C] Semler reads “infers.”]

[Note: [Col. 0231C] One reading has “had alleged.”]

[Note: [Col. 0231C–D] Semler and Oberthür read “had added a further argument”; another reading has “you add a further argument.” Rhenanus reads “but if.”]

[Note: [Col. 0231D] The Frankfurt edition reads “in vain” (*in vanum*).]

These are your words, which you ought to have remembered elsewhere as well, lest you introduce anything contrary to them. But since in what has gone before we have already discussed something of the ambiguity of good and evil in relation to matter, I shall now answer only your present proposition and argument. Nor shall I say that here too you ought to have pronounced something definite—either that matter was good, or evil, or some third thing—but merely that you ought to have preserved what it pleased you to pronounce concerning these matters.

[Note: [Col. 0231D] Rhenanus and Semler read “reconsidered.”]

[Note: [Col. 0231D] The same editors read “either good or evil.”]

[Note: [Col. 0231D] Rhenanus and his followers read “not here.”]

For you undo your pronouncement that it is neither good nor evil. When you say, “If it were good, it would not desire to be ordered by God,” you foreshadow that it is evil; and when you add, “If it were evil by nature, it would not admit transformation for the better,” you indirectly reveal that it is good. Thus you have made it akin to both good and evil, although you pronounced it neither good nor evil.

[Note: [Col. 0231D] Rhenanus edits “by adding, he shows,” with the same sense.]

[Note: [Col. 0232C] Rhenanus, Semler, and others add “and.”]

But to refute the argument by which you thought you would confirm your proposition, I also oppose this: if matter had always been good, why would it not have desired to be reformed for the better? What good thing does not desire, wish for, or admit advancement, so that it may become better from good?

[Note: [Col. 0232C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “But if.”]

[Note: [Col. 0232C] The Wouwer manuscript wrongly reads “desires.”]

Likewise, if it had been evil by nature, why could it not have been converted by God, as by one more powerful, by him who is capable of converting even the nature of stones into children of Abraham (Matt. IV)? You therefore plainly not only compare God with matter, but even subordinate him to it, as though the nature of matter could not have been conquered and subdued for the better by him. But elsewhere you will not deny having admitted that the matter which here you refuse to call evil by nature was evil.

[Note: [Col. 0232C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Pamelius read “the Lord.”]

[Note: [Col. 0232C] Rhenanus and Semler read “could better have been.”]

## CHAPTER XXXVIII

Concerning the position of matter, I discuss the same point as concerning its motion, so that it may expose your perversity. You place matter beneath God, and thereby certainly assign it a place below God. Matter is therefore in a place. If in a place, then within a place; if within a place, then bounded by the place within which it is; if bounded, it has an outermost line, which—as befits a painter—you recognize as the limit of every thing that has such an outermost line.

[Note: [Col. 0232C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “movement.”]

[Note: [Col. 0231D–0232D] “Which, as befits a painter…” This is certainly the passage’s meaning; for *quantum* is used for *in quantum* (“insofar as”), so there is no need to omit that word. PAMELIUS.]

Matter, therefore, will not be infinite, since, while it is in a place, it is bounded by that place, and while it is bounded by it, it admits the place’s outermost line. Yet you make it infinite, saying: “It is infinite because it always exists.”

[Note: [Col. 0232D] Rhenanus and his later followers read “in the place in which…”]

[Note: [Col. 0232D] Junius adds “in which it is.”]

And if one of your disciples should wish to argue that by “infinity” you mean infinity in duration and not in the extent of the body, the statements that follow show, on the contrary, that you mean it to be corporeally infinite, corporeally immense, and uncircumscribed. “Hence,” you say, “it is not fashioned as a whole, but only parts of it.” Thus it is infinite in body, not in time.

[Note: [Col. 0232D] Rhenanus and Pamelius read “infinite.”]

[Note: [Col. 0232D] The Frankfurt edition reads “in duration, not motion.”]

[Note: [Col. 0232D] Rhenanus and the Frankfurt edition read “as corporeally.”]

And you are caught: for you make it infinite in body, although by assigning it a place you enclose it within a place and within the place’s outermost line.

[Note: [Col. 0232D] “And you are caught.” We have noted more than once above that in Tertullian *obducere* and *obduci* mean “to refute” and “to be refuted,” or to catch or be caught asserting contrary and mutually conflicting claims. RHENANUS.—“And you are caught”: so that you cannot escape. RIGAULT.]

Yet why God did not form it all, I do not know, unless because he was either weak or envious. Accordingly, I seek the half of it which was not wholly formed, so that I may recognize what the whole had been like. For God ought to have displayed it openly as a specimen of antiquity for the glory of his work.

[Note: [Col. 0232D] Some add “that.”]

[Note: [Col. 0232D] “A specimen of antiquity.” He means an exemplar. So shortly afterward: “And you want better things to bear the exemplar of worse things.” Similarly, against the Valentinians he uses *sigillarium* in place of *sigillare*. RIGAULT.]

## CHAPTER XXXIX

Let it now be finite, as seems more correct to you, through its transformations and changes; and let it also be circumscribable, since, you say, it is fashioned by God, because it is convertible, changeable, and divisible. “For its transformations,” you say, “show that it is divisible.”

[Note: [Col. 0233D] “They show that it is divisible.” That is, capable of division, though this does not agree with Hermogenes’ earlier words. For he said that God made matter from himself, although God was eternal and indivisible; yet now he thinks that matter divisible, and thus contradicts himself. LE PRIEUR.]

Here too you have strayed from your lines, those which you employed concerning the person of God when you laid down the rule that God did not make matter from himself, because one who is eternal and remains forever, and is therefore immutable and indivisible, could not be divided into parts.

[Note: [Col. 0233D] “You have strayed from your lines.” You have missed the mark. Nor do I think that he was alluding here to that man’s art as a painter; since the words can be understood quite easily without recourse to that allusion, I would think it unworthy of Tertullian to dwell upon it continually. LE PRIEUR.]

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus and Semler read “that God.”]

If matter too is reckoned by the same eternity, having neither beginning nor end, by the same reasoning it will be incapable of undergoing division and transformation, just as God is. Placed in fellowship with eternity, it must necessarily share with him the powers, laws, and conditions of eternity.

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus wished to read “which.”]

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus and Semler read “him with it.”]

Likewise, when you say, “Its parts, however, possess all things together from all things, so that the whole may be discerned from the parts,” you plainly mean those parts which were brought forth from it and which are seen by us today. How, then, do they possess all things from all things—that is, from their former conditions—when the things seen today possess a different condition from what the former things possessed?

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Pamelius read “And.”]

## CHAPTER XL

You say that matter was reformed for the better, necessarily from worse conditions; and you wish better things to bear the exemplars of worse things.

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus and Junius read “necessarily from worse things.”]

It was a confused thing, whereas now it is ordered; and you wish the unordered to be represented by the ordered. No thing is the mirror of another thing—that is, of one that is not equal to it.

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus and Semler read “is of another,” that is…]

No one at a barber’s has looked upon a mule in place of a human being—unless someone thinks that in this construction of the world, matter, now arranged and adorned, corresponds to matter that is formless and uncultivated.

[Note: [Col. 0233D–0234D] “No one at a barber’s,” etc. He is referring to the custom by which barbers use a mirror; for he adds that the world is the mirror of matter. From this the author also derived the new word *speciatum*, meaning that which appears beautiful in a mirror. For Varro too says in *On the Latin Language*, Book IV: “A mirror (*speculum*) is so called from looking (*speciendo*), because people look there.” PAMELIUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus reads “already arranged and despised,” but thought the reading should be “conceived.”]

What in the world today is formless? What in matter formerly possessed visible form, so that the world should be matter’s mirror? Since among the Greeks the word “world” (*kosmos*, κόσμος) denotes adornment, how does it reflect the image of unadorned matter, so that you may say the whole is known through its parts?

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “gloomy” in place of “formerly.”]

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Semler notes κόσμος.]

[Note: [Col. 0233C] Rhenanus alone and his followers read “display.”]

Certainly that part too which did not undergo formation will belong to that whole. And above you declared that it was not all fashioned. Therefore, at least this rude, confused, and unordered portion cannot be recognized in the polished, distinct, and ordered things. Nor is it proper to call them parts of matter, since, divided from its form through change, they have departed from it.

[Note: [Col. 0233C] The same source reads “fashioned” in the feminine.]

[Note: [Col. 0233D–0234D] Rhenanus read “and I fled and counted for myself”; he recognized the corruption and proposed the reading which all editors adopted.]

## CHAPTER XLI

I return to motion, to show how slippery you are everywhere. Matter’s motion was unregulated, confused, and turbulent. For thus you also apply the analogy of a pot boiling over on every side.

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “you will oppose.”]

And how is it elsewhere affirmed by you to be something different? For when you wish to present matter as neither good nor evil, you say: “Thus matter lying beneath had a motion of equal weight, inclining no more toward good than toward evil.” If it was of equal weight, it was no longer turbulent or pot-like, but ordered and balanced—a motion which, being driven by its own choice between good and evil, nevertheless inclined and plunged toward neither side, but was carried with a balanced impulse at, as they say, the middle pivot of the balance.

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Paris reads *cacabatius*; Frankfurt *cacabaceus*.]

[Note: [Col. 0234C] The manuscripts and Junius read “is balanced.”]

[Note: [Col. 0234D] “At, as they say, the middle pivot of the balance.” That is, a motion so balanced that it inclines in no direction and neither side weighs more heavily. For Festus teaches that the *agina* is the hole into which the beam of a balance is inserted and within which it turns. LE PRIEUR.]

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read, contrary to all the manuscripts, “plunging mediates what they call…”]

You will say: “This is not—this is no turbulence and passivity”; but rather moderation, restraint, and the evenness of a wavering motion which inclines toward neither side.

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Rhenanus, contrary to all the manuscripts, reads “turbulent”; Semler conjectures that the words should be deleted.]

[Note: [Col. 0234D] “This turbulence and passivity.” The old copies read “turbulent.” But “turbulence” is preferable. We have noted in many places, as the subject required, what Tertullian means by “passive.” Here by “passivity” he means dissolution, or disorder and confusion. RHENANUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0234D] “The evenness of motion.” Thus at the beginning of *On the Pallium* he spoke of “the justice of the pallium.” RIGAULT.]

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Junius reads “wavering”; Rhenanus “changing.”]

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Semler and Oberthür read “judging.”]

To be sure, if it inclined this way and that, or leaned more toward one side, it would then deserve to be marked by disarray, inequality, and turbulence.

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Semler, contrary to all the manuscripts, reads “inclined.”]

Furthermore, if its motion was inclined neither more toward good nor more toward evil, it was necessarily conducted between good and evil. From this too it becomes apparent that matter is capable of being bounded: because its motion, inclined toward neither evil nor good, hung within the two and between them, since it tended toward neither; and on this account it was bounded by both.

[Note: [Col. 0234D] “Inclined neither toward evil nor toward good.” That is, inclined neither to evil nor to good. *Pronus* takes the dative. RHENANUS.]

[Note: [Col. 0234C] Rhenanus and Semler read “turned.”]

[Note: [Col. 0234D] Rhenanus and his followers, contrary to all the manuscripts, read “judged.”]

But you also place good and evil in a location when you say that matter’s motion was inclined toward neither of them. For matter, which was in a place, in inclining neither here nor there did not incline toward the places in which good and evil were. By assigning a place to good and evil, however, you make them corporeal by making them local; for things which possess a place must first be corporeal. In fact, incorporeal things would possess no place of their own except in a body, since they occur as accidents of a body.

But matter, in not inclining toward good and evil, was not declining toward things that were either corporeal or local. You therefore err if you wish good and evil to be substances. For you make substances of the things to which you assign places; and you assign places when you suspend matter’s motion between the two regions.

## CHAPTER XLII

[Col. 0235B]

You scattered all your statements so that their mutual contradictions would not shine out from close proximity. But I shall gather and compare them one by one. You assert that matter’s motion is unregulated, and add that matter pursues formlessness; then elsewhere you say that it desires to be ordered by God. Does that which pursues formlessness desire formation, or does that which desires formation pursue formlessness?

You do not wish to appear to equate God with matter, yet you add that matter possesses fellowship with God. “For it is impossible,” you say, “that matter should be adorned by him unless it had something in common with God.” Yet if it had something in common with God, it did not desire to be adorned by him, since through that fellowship it was, of course, a part of God.

Or God too could have been adorned by matter, since he likewise possessed something in common with it. And now in this respect you subject God to necessity, if there was something in matter on account of which he formed it.

You make this common feature between them: that they are moved by themselves and always.

[Note: [Col. 0235D] Rhenanus adds “they are moved” and reads it twice.]

What less do you ascribe to matter than to God? The whole partnership in divinity will amount to this: freedom and eternity of motion. But God moves in an ordered manner, matter in an unregulated manner. Nevertheless it is equally divine, with equally free and eternal motion. Indeed, you grant more to matter, to which it was permitted to move in a way in which God was not permitted to move.
