In various ways the devil has rivaled the truth. At times he has sought to shake it by defending it. He champions one Lord, the almighty Creator of the world, so that even from the doctrine of one God he may make a heresy. He says that the Father himself descended into the Virgin [Note: [Col. 0153D] “Virgin,” Semler.], that he himself was born from her, that he himself suffered, and, finally, that he himself is Jesus Christ. RHENANUS.] at Rome from Asia; a man in other respects also restless, and furthermore puffed up by boasting of martyrdom merely because of the simple and brief discomfort of imprisonment. For this same man at that time [Note: [Col. 0155C] “The Roman bishop, who was already acknowledging the prophecies of Montanus.” What are called Tertullian’s heresies prescribed scarcely anything other than sterner martyrdoms, drier fasts, holier chastity—that is, either one marriage or none. In these matters, whatever his fault may have been, he seems to have erred entirely through a more vehement love of virtue. And indeed that most learned cardinal rightly judged that Anicetus, then Roman pontiff, acknowledged these gifts of the divine Spirit in people who as yet dissented in nothing from the rule of faith, granted peace to their churches, and, as Tertullian says, sent letters of peace. compelled the Roman bishop—who was already acknowledging the prophecies of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, and, on the basis of that acknowledgment, bringing peace to the churches of Asia and Phrygia—both to revoke the letters of peace already issued and to desist from his intention [Col. 0156A] of receiving the spiritual gifts, by making false assertions concerning those prophets and their churches and by defending the authority of the bishop’s predecessors.

Thus Praxeas managed two pieces of the devil’s business [Note: [Col. 0156D] “Business.” He plays upon the name Praxeas, which could be derived from the Greek πράσσειν (*prassein*, “to do, transact”): thus Semler. ## CHAPTER II

Accordingly, it is now proclaimed that after a certain time the Father was born and the Father suffered: that God himself, the Lord Almighty, is Jesus Christ. We, however, have always believed—and now believe all the more, as persons [Note: [Col. 0156D] “Better instructed by the Paraclete.” Rhenanus correctly notes that he is speaking of Montanus’s Paraclete, to whom he also wrongly applies John 16:13, “the guide into all truth.” PAMELIUS.] better instructed by the Paraclete, that is, the guide into all truth—in one God indeed, yet under this dispensation, which we call the οἰκονομία (*oikonomia*, “economy”): namely, [Note: [Col. 0156D] “That the one God also has a Son, his Word, who proceeded from him.” He attributes the word “proceeding” to the Son, though the Church made it proper [Col. 0157D] to the Holy Spirit. RIGAULT.] that the one God also has a Son, his Word, who proceeded from him, through whom all things were made, and [Col. 0157A] without whom nothing was made (John 1:3); that this Son was sent by the Father into the Virgin and was born from her, man and God, Son of Man and Son of God, and was named Jesus Christ; that he suffered, died, and was buried according to the Scriptures; that he was raised by the Father and taken back into the heavens [Note: [Col. 0157C] “Into heaven,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.]; that he sits at the right hand of the Father and will come to judge the living and the dead; and that from there, according to his promise, he sent from the Father the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete, the sanctifier of the faith of those who believe in the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit.

That this rule has run its course from the beginning of the Gospel, even before all the earlier heretics—to say nothing [Note: [Col. 0157D] “Before yesterday’s Praxeas.” Thus Persius said “yesterday’s Quirites,” that is, new, recent ones. RIGAULT.] of yesterday’s Praxeas—will be proved both by the very posteriority of all heretics and by the sheer novelty of yesterday’s Praxeas. From this point onward it may equally be prejudged against [Col. 0157B] all heresies [Note: [Col. 0157D] “That whatever is first is true”—namely, taking as the starting point the place where truth began. Thus in the book *On the Prescription of Heretics*: “And so from the order itself it is manifest that what was handed down earlier is the Lord’s and is true.” RIGAULT.] that whatever is first is true, and whatever is later is adulterated.

But while preserving this prescription, a place must nevertheless also be given to renewed examinations, for the instruction and fortification of certain persons, lest every perversity appear to have been condemned without examination, merely by prejudgment—especially this one, which thinks itself to possess the unalloyed truth because it believes that the one God cannot be believed in otherwise than by saying that the very same one is Father and Son and Holy Spirit. It is as though all were not in this way also one, since all are from one, namely through unity of substance, while nevertheless the mystery of the economy is preserved, which arranges the unity into a Trinity, setting forth three: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Three, however, [Col. 0157C] not in condition but in degree; not in substance but in form; not in power but in aspect; yet of one substance, one condition, and one power, because there is one God from whom these degrees, forms, and aspects are reckoned under the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. How, however, [Note: [Col. 0157C] Semler, following Rhenanus, omits “however.”] they admit of number without division, the ensuing examination [Note: [Col. 0157C] “Discussions,” Semler.] will demonstrate.

## CHAPTER III

For all the simple people—not to call them unwise and uneducated, who always constitute the greater part of believers—are alarmed at the economy. The very rule of faith transfers them from the many gods of the age [Col. 0158A] to the one true God, but they do not understand that he is indeed to be believed as one, yet together with his economy. They assume that the number and arrangement of the Trinity is a division of the unity, whereas the unity, deriving the Trinity from itself, is not destroyed by it but administered through it. And so they now loudly accuse us of preaching two and three Gods, while presuming that they themselves are worshipers of one God—as though a unity irrationally gathered into one did not create heresy, while a Trinity rationally considered constitutes truth.

[Note: [Col. 0157D] “We maintain the monarchy,” they say. Μοναρχικήν (*monarchikēn*), that is, “we affirm one God”—rightly indeed, for otherwise we fall εἰς τὴν ἄθεον πολυθέοτητα (*eis tēn atheon polytheotēta*), “into godless polytheism.” Jerome, in his book *On Ecclesiastical Writers*, states that Irenaeus composed a book bearing this title: *On the Monarchy, or, That God Is Not the Author of Evils*. Tertullian himself used the word “monarchy” to signify God in Book II of *On the Apparel of Women*: “We must put on this incorruptibility for the new house that the monarchy promises.” RIGAULT.] “We maintain the monarchy,” they say. And even Latins—and even *Opici*—pronounce the sound of the word so precisely [Note: [Col. 0157C] “Throne,” Semler, Rhenanus, Junius.] that you would suppose they understood “monarchy” as well as they pronounce it.

[Note: [Col. 0158D] “Even *Opici*.” That is, barbarians, because they spoke *opice*, that is, barbarously, not in Latin, according to Pliny, Book XXIX, chapter 1. PAMELIUS.—I know that Juvenal said in Satire 6:

> Nor should men heed the words corrected by an Opican crumb.

But here the exemplar reads: “And even Latins, even craftsmen, pronounce the very sound of the word so precisely,” which seems most appropriate to what follows, so that you would suppose they understood “monarchy” as well as they pronounce it. Septimius speaks in just this way in the *Apology*: “Any Christian craftsman both finds God [Col. 0159D] and makes him known.” RIGAULT.]

[Note: [Col. 0157D] “And so skillfully,” Semler; “even craftsmen,” Rigault.]

But Latins strive to pronounce “monarchy,” while even Greeks refuse to understand “economy.” [Col. 0158B] Yet if I have gleaned anything from either language, I know that “monarchy” signifies nothing other than a sole and single sovereignty. Nevertheless, because it belongs to one, monarchy does not prescribe that the one to whom it belongs either must have no son, must have made himself his own son, or must not administer his monarchy through whomever he wishes. Indeed, I say that no dominion belongs so entirely to one, is so singular, is so monarchical [Note: [Col. 0158C] “A monarchy,” Junius.], that it is not also administered through other persons closely associated with it, whom it has itself provided as its officials.

Moreover, if the one whose monarchy it is also has a son, it is not immediately divided, nor does it cease to be a monarchy, if the son too is taken into participation in it. Rather, it continues primarily to belong to him by whom it is shared with the Son [Note: [Col. 0158C] “Himself,” Franeker, Paris.]; and while it belongs to him, it therefore remains a monarchy, since it is held by two who are so united.

Thus, if even the divine monarchy is administered through so many legions and armies of angels, as it is written, “A thousand thousands [Note: [Col. 0158C] “A thousand times ten thousand hundreds of thousands,” Semler; “a thousand thousands, hundreds of thousands,” Latinus.] stood before him, and a thousand times a hundred thousand appeared before him,” it has not thereby ceased to belong to one, so as to cease being a monarchy merely because it is managed through so many thousands of powers. How, then, can God be thought to undergo division and dispersal in the Son and the Holy Spirit, who have obtained the second and third places and are such sharers in the Father’s substance, when he does not undergo this in so great a number of angels, who are indeed so alien to his substance [Note: [Col. 0158D] “The Father’s substance,” Semler; Franeker adds “of aliens.”]?

You reckon as the monarchy’s overthrow its members, its pledges, its instruments, its very strength, [Col. 0159A] and the whole sum of its resources. That is not right. I would rather have you exercise yourself upon the meaning of the reality than upon the sound of the word. For you must understand that the overthrow of a monarchy occurs when another dominion is brought in—one possessing its own origin and independent condition and therefore a rival—when another God is introduced in opposition to the Creator. Then it is an evil, when there are several gods, as among [Note: [Col. 0159D] “Valentinians and Prodicans.” Better than “Proclians,” as Rhenanus has it, although he adduces the Proclianites from Philastrius and Augustine, who say that angels made souls out of fire and spirit, which was also a doctrine of the Seleucians. LE PRIEUR.] the Valentinians and Prodicans [Note: [Col. 0159D] “Prodigals,” Semler; “Proclians,” Paris.]; then it is an overthrow of the monarchy, when it tends toward destruction of the Creator.

## CHAPTER IV

But as for me, who derive the Son from no other source than the Father’s substance, who represent him as doing nothing without the Father’s will and as having received all power from the Father—how can I destroy in matters of faith the monarchy that I preserve in the Son, since it was delivered to the Son by the Father? Let this also be said by me concerning the third [Col. 0159B] degree, because I hold that the Spirit comes from no other source than from the Father through the Son.

See, therefore, whether it is not rather you who destroy the monarchy, since you overthrow its arrangement and dispensation, established in as many names as God willed. Indeed, it so remains in its own condition, although the Trinity is introduced, that it is even to be restored to the Father by the Son. For the Apostle writes concerning the final consummation: “When he delivers the kingdom to God and the Father. For he must reign until God puts his enemies beneath his feet”—that is, in accordance with the Psalm: “Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.” “But when all things have been subjected to him”—assuredly [Note: [Col. 0159D] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür omit “assuredly.”] except him who subjected all things to him—“then the Son himself will also be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, so that God may be all in all.”

We see [Note: [Col. 0159D] “We seem,” Venetian edition.], therefore, that the Son does not harm the monarchy, although today it is in the Son’s possession, because it both remains in its own condition while in the Son’s possession and will be restored with its condition to the Father by the Son. Thus no one will destroy it on this account if he admits the Son [Note: [Col. 0159D] “Destroy it. The Son,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.], to whom it is established that the monarchy was delivered by the Father and by whom it is one day to be restored to the Father.

By this single passage [Note: [Col. 0159D] “Passage,” that is, a saying, according to Semler. EDITORS.] of the apostolic Epistle, we could already have shown that the Father and the Son are two—not only from the names “Father” and “Son,” but also because the one who delivered the kingdom and the one to whom he delivered it, and likewise the one who subjected and the one to whom he subjected [Note: [Col. 0159D] “And the one to whom he subjected” was absent from Rhenanus.], must necessarily be two.

## CHAPTER V

But because they wish the two to be one, so that the same person may be regarded as both Father and Son, the entire question concerning the Son must be examined: whether he exists, who he is, and how he exists. Thus the reality itself, with the Scriptures and their interpretations supporting it, [Col. 0159D] will vindicate its own form.

Some indeed say [Note: [Col. 0160D] “Some,” Petrus Ciacconius.] that Genesis [Col. 0160A] begins thus in Hebrew: “In the beginning God made a son for himself.”

[Note: [Col. 0159D] “In the beginning God made a son for himself.” Blessed Jerome taught me, at the very beginning of his *Hebrew Questions on Genesis*, that this passage, which he cites there, can be understood of Christ more according to its sense than according to a literal translation. For the Seventy translators, Symmachus, and Theodotion translated “In the beginning”; and in Hebrew the text reads [Hebrew absent in the supplied text], which Aquila interprets “in the chief part,” and not [Hebrew absent in the supplied text], which is interpreted “in the son.” LE PRIEUR.]

Other arguments, however, derived from God’s own arrangement—from the state in which he existed [Note: [Col. 0160D] “In which he existed”—namely God. SEMLER.] before the establishment of the world until the generation of the Son—lead me not to regard this reading as established.

Before all things, God was alone, being for himself his own world, place, and everything. He was alone because there was nothing else [Note: [Col. 0160D] “Else” is absent from Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] external besides him. Yet even then he was not alone, for he had with him that which he had within himself, namely his own Reason. For God is rational, and Reason was first within him; and thus all things were from him. This Reason is his consciousness. The Greeks call it λόγος (*logos*), a term by which we also designate “Word.” Hence it has now become customary among our people, through the simplicity of the translation, to say that the Word was in the beginning with God, although Reason is more properly regarded as prior, because God was not “word-bearing” from the beginning but rational even before the beginning; and because the Word himself, consisting in Reason, thereby shows Reason to be prior as his substance.

Yet even so, there is no difference. For although God had not yet sent forth his Word, he nevertheless had him within himself, together with and within Reason itself, silently thinking and arranging with himself what he would soon say through the Word. For while thinking and arranging with his Reason, he was making it into Word, which he treated by means of Word.

To understand this more easily, recognize it beforehand [Note: [Col. 0160D] “From me,” Semler.] from yourself, as from the image and likeness of God (Gen. 1:26). You too possess reason within yourself, since you are a rational creature, made by a rational craftsman and not only made by him but also animated from his substance. Observe that when you silently converse with yourself, this very activity is conducted by reason within you, as reason meets you together with word at every movement of your thought and every impulse of your consciousness. Whatever you think is word; whatever you perceive is reason. You must speak it within your mind; and while you speak, you experience the Word as your interlocutor, in whom is this very Reason through which, thinking with him, you speak, and through whom, speaking, you think. Thus in a certain sense the Word within you is a second, through whom you speak by thinking and through whom you think by speaking. The Word himself is another.

How much more fully, then, does this take place in God, whose image and likeness you also are reckoned to be: that even while silent he possesses Reason within himself, and the Word within Reason? I can therefore maintain that I did not rashly lay down beforehand that even then, before [Col. 0160D] the establishment of the universe, God was not alone, since he possessed [Col. 0161A] Reason within himself and, within Reason, the Word, whom he made second to himself by setting him in motion within himself.

## CHAPTER VI

This power and this ordering of the divine consciousness is also shown in the Scriptures under the name of Wisdom. For what is wiser than God’s Reason or Word? Accordingly, hear Wisdom herself as also having been established as a second person. First: “The Lord created me as the beginning of his ways for his works, before he made the earth, before the mountains were established; before all the hills he begot me” (Prov. 8:22)—that is, establishing and begetting her within his own consciousness.

Then recognize her as standing beside him by reason of her distinctness: “When he prepared the heaven,” she says, “I was there together with him [Note: [Col. 0161D] “Together” is absent from other copies.]; and when he made firm the things above the winds, the clouds on high; and when he made secure the fountains of that which is beneath heaven, I was with him, fitting things together; I was the one in whom [Note: [Col. 0161D] “Whom,” Rhenanus.] he delighted, and daily I rejoiced in his presence.”

For as soon as God willed to bring forth into their own substances and forms the things that he had arranged within himself together with the Reason and Word of Wisdom, he first brought forth the Word himself, who had within himself his inseparable companions [Note: [Col. 0161D] “His inseparable companions,” that is, attendants. As near the end of the book *On Patience*: “When, therefore, the Spirit of God descends, patience, his inseparable companion, accompanies him.” RIGAULT.], Reason and Wisdom [Note: [Col. 0161D] “Reasons, Wisdom,” Rhenanus.], so that through him all things might be made through whom they had been thought and arranged—indeed, through whom they had already been made, insofar as they existed in God’s consciousness. This alone was lacking to them: that they should also be openly recognized and apprehended in their own forms and substances.

## CHAPTER VII

Then, therefore, the Word himself also assumes his form and adornment—sound and voice—when God says, “Let there be light.” This is the perfect nativity of the Word, as he proceeds from God: first established by him for thought under the name of Wisdom—“The Lord established me as the beginning of his ways”—and then begotten for effect: “When he prepared the heaven, I was there together with him.”

Thereafter, by making him equal to himself, from whom by proceeding he became the Son, God made him “the firstborn” (Col. 1:15), as begotten before all things, and “the only-begotten” (1 John 4:9), as alone begotten from God—properly from the womb of God’s own heart, in accordance with the Father’s own testimony: “My heart has uttered the best Word” (Ps. 44:1). Then, rejoicing in him who in turn rejoiced [Note: [Col. 0161D] “To the rejoicing one,” Semler.] in his presence, he said: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you” (Ps. 2:7); and: “Before the morning star I begot you.”

So too the Son, speaking in his own person under the name of Wisdom, confesses the Father: “The Lord established me as the beginning of his ways for his works. Before all the hills he begot me.” For although Wisdom here appears to say that she was established by the Lord for his works and ways, elsewhere it is shown that all things were made through the Word, and that without him nothing was made; as again it is written (Ps. 33:6): “By his Word the heavens were established, and by his Spirit all their powers”—assuredly by that Spirit which was within the Word. It is therefore apparent that the one and same power is designated now by the name “Wisdom,” now by the title “Word”: the power that received the beginning of the ways for God’s works, that established the heaven, through which [Note: [Col. 0161D] “Whom,” Franeker.] all things were made, and without which [Note: [Col. 0162D] “Whom,” Franeker.] nothing was made (John 1:3).

No more need be said on this point, as though the reference under the names of Wisdom, Reason, and every divine mind and spirit were not to him who became the Son of God by being begotten through proceeding from God.

“Therefore,” you say, “you grant that the Word is some kind of substance, constructed from spirit and the transmission of Wisdom?” Certainly. For you are unwilling that he should possess substantial existence in reality through the distinctive property of substance, so that he may be seen as a certain reality and person, and may thus, being established by God as second, make two: the Father and the Son, God and the Word.

[Note: [Col. 0161D] “For what, you will say, is the Word except the voice and sound of the mouth and air that has been struck?” So too in the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, where he discusses the teeth, which, he says, man received so that the articulations of the voice might be marked by their impact. RIGAULT.]

“What is the Word,” you will say, “except the voice and sound of the mouth and—as the grammarians teach—air that has been struck, intelligible to hearing, but otherwise some empty, insubstantial, incorporeal thing?” Yet I say that nothing insubstantial and empty could have proceeded from God, since it was not produced from what is insubstantial and empty; nor can that which proceeded from so great a substance and made such great substances itself lack substance. For the Word himself made the things that were made through him.

How could he himself be nothing, without whom nothing was made? How could one who is insubstantial have produced solid things, one who is empty have produced full things, and one who is incorporeal have produced corporeal things? For although something can at times be made different from that by which it is made, nevertheless nothing can be made through that which is empty and insubstantial.

Is the Word of God, who was called the Son and himself named God, an empty and insubstantial thing—“And the Word was with God, and the Word was God” (John 1:1)? It is written: “You shall not take the name of God in vain” (Exod. 20:7). He surely is the one who, “being established in the form of God, did not consider equality with God to be robbery” (Phil. 2:6). In what form of God? Surely in some real form, not in none at all.

[Note: [Col. 0162D] “For who will deny that God is a body?” See on this passage Dom Le Nourry, *Dissertation on Tertullian’s Apologeticum*, chapter VII, article 3, volume I of this edition, p. 811 ff. EDITORS.]

For who will deny [Note: [Col. 0162D] “Denied,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] that God is a body, although God is spirit? For spirit is a body of its own kind, in its own form. But even all those things that are invisible possess before God their own body and their own form, through which they are visible to God alone. How much more, then, will that which was sent forth from his very substance [Note: [Col. 0162D] “Substance,” Rhenanus.] not be without substance!

Whatever, therefore, the substance of the Word was, that I call his person, and for him I claim the name of Son; and when I acknowledge the Son, I maintain that he is second to the Father.

## CHAPTER VIII

If anyone [Note: [Col. 0162D] “This,” Latinus.] supposes [Note: [Col. 0162D] “That I am introducing some προβολή.” By this word, or “prolation,” he explains what the holy Fathers call “generation” when speaking of the Son or Word, who is γενητός (*genētos*, “begotten”), whereas the Holy Spirit is ἐκπορευτός (*ekporeutos*, “one who proceeds”). [Col. 0163D] RIGAULT.] that I am introducing some προβολή (*probolē*), that is, the prolation of one thing from another, as Valentinus does when he produces one Aeon after another from an Aeon, I shall first tell you that truth does not therefore refrain [Note: [Col. 0163D] “Refrain,” Franeker, Paris; “will refrain,” Latinus.] from using this word, and the reality and meaning belonging to it, merely because heresy instead borrowed from truth the material with which it might construct its lie.

The Word of God was brought forth, was he not? Here take your stand with me. If he was brought forth, acknowledge the *probolē* of truth, and let heresy see to it whether it has imitated anything from the truth. The question now is who uses a given reality, and its name, and how.

Valentinus distinguishes and separates his probolai from their author, and places them so far from him that an Aeon does not know the Father. Indeed, the Aeon desires to know him but cannot; it is even almost consumed and dissolved into the remaining substance. Among us, however, the Son alone knows the Father [Col. 0163B] (Matt. 11:27); he himself has revealed the Father’s bosom (John 1:18); he has heard and seen all things with the Father; and he speaks the things [Note: [Col. 0163D] Latinus inserts “to.”] that he was commanded by the Father to speak (John 8:26). He fulfilled not his own will but the Father’s (John 6:38), which he had known from close at hand—indeed, from the beginning. “For who knows the things that are in God except the Spirit who is within him?” (1 Cor. 2:11).

Now the Word was constructed from Spirit, and, so to speak, the Spirit is the Word’s body. The Word, therefore, is always in the Father, as he says, “I am in the Father” (John 14:11); and always with God, as it is written, “And the Word was with God” (John 1:1); and never separated from the Father or other than the Father, because “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30).

This will be the *probolē* of truth, the guardian of unity, whereby we say that the Son was brought forth from the Father, [Note: [Col. 0163D] “But not separated.” Hence arise so many questions about whether he is a different thing from the Father—which is not conceded—although he is another person. RIGAULT.] but not separated [Note: [Col. 0163D] “Prepared,” Semler.]. For God brought forth the Word, as the Paraclete also teaches, as the root brings forth the shrub, the spring the river, and the sun the ray. For these forms too are *probolai* of the substances from which they proceed.

Nor would I hesitate to call the shrub the son of the root, the river the son of the spring, and the ray the son of the sun, because every origin is a parent, and everything brought forth from an origin is offspring—much more the Word of God, who has also properly received the name of Son. Yet the shrub is not separated from the root, nor the river from the spring, nor the ray from the sun, just as neither is the Word separated from God.

Therefore, in accordance with the pattern of these examples, I profess that I speak of two: God and his Word, the Father and his Son. For the root and the shrub are two realities, but joined; and the spring and the river are two forms, but undivided; and the sun and the ray are two configurations, but coherent. Everything that proceeds from something [Col. 0163D] must necessarily be second to that from which it proceeds; it is not, however, therefore separated from it. But where there is a second, there are two; and where there is a third, there are three.

For the Spirit is third from God and the Son, just as the fruit from the shrub is third from the root; the stream from the river is third from the spring; and the tip of the ray is third from the sun. Yet nothing is estranged from the source from which it derives its distinctive properties. Thus the Trinity, flowing down from the Father through interwoven and connected degrees, neither disturbs the monarchy nor fails to protect the condition of the economy.

## CHAPTER IX

Hold everywhere to this rule that I have professed, whereby I testify that the Father, the Son, and the Spirit are inseparable from one another; and thus you will recognize what is said and in what sense.

For observe: I say that the Father is one, the Son another, and the Spirit another. Every uneducated or perverse person takes this statement wrongly, as though it signified diversity and, from that diversity, extended the separation [Note: [Col. 0164D] “Maintained,” Junius.] of the Father, the Son, [Col. 0164B] and the Spirit. I say this, however, under the compulsion of their contention that the same person is Father, Son, and Spirit, as they flatter the monarchy in opposition to the economy.

Yet I say that the Son is another than the Father not by diversity but by distribution; another not by division but by distinction, because the Father and the Son are not the same, but differ from one another in measure. For the Father is the whole substance;

[Note: [Col. 0163D] “The Son, however, is a derivation.” Rhenanus correctly noted in the margin that Tertullian must be read cautiously here, [Col. 0164D] since, having written long before the Arian heresy, he did not weigh his words with complete precision. Yet his meaning—that the Father, Son, and Spirit are of the same substance—is manifest both from what precedes and from what follows in this book, chapter [number absent in the supplied text]. PAMELIUS.]

the Son, however, is a derivation and portion of the whole, as he himself declares: “Because the Father is greater than I” (John 14:28). By him, too, the Son is sung of in the Psalm (8:6) as having been made less [Note: [Col. 0164D] “Younger,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.]: “A little [Note: [Col. 0164D] “By a little,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] below the angels.”

Thus the Father too is another than the Son, insofar as he is greater than the Son; insofar as the one who begets is one and the one who is begotten another; the one who sends is one and the one who is sent another; the one who acts is one and the one through whom the action is performed another.

It is well that the Lord, when using this word in the person of the Paraclete, signified not division but arrangement: “For I shall ask,” he says (John 14:16), “the Father, and he will send you another Advocate, the Spirit of truth.” Thus he made the Paraclete another than himself, just as we make the Son another than the Father, so that he might show the third degree in the Paraclete, just as we show the second in the Son, for the preservation of the economy.

Does not the very fact that they are called Father and Son mean that one is distinct from the other? Assuredly, whatever all things are called, that they will be; and whatever they will be, that they will be called. The diversity of names cannot possibly be confounded, because neither can the realities whose names they are. “Yes” is yes, and “no” is no; for whatever is more than this comes from evil (Matt. 5:37).
