[Col. 0199B] Two good, ancient, and weighty witnesses and authorities, Lactantius and Jerome, readily persuaded me both to entitle this work—whether booklet or dialogue—which for many centuries has falsely been believed and called Arnobius’s eighth book *Against the Nations*, and to affirm plainly and unequivocally that it is the *Octavius* of Marcus Minucius Felix. For Lactantius, in Book I, chapter 11, of his *Institutes*, cites “Minucius Felix in that book which,” as he says, “is entitled *Octavius*.” And he quotes from it certain words which do not permit us to imagine either some other *Octavius* or an *Octavius* belonging to anyone else. The same author, in Book V, chapter 1, when speaking of the eloquence of the Christians whom he had read, praises this Minucius first: “Minucius Felix,” he says, “held no undistinguished place among advocates. His book, [Col. 0199C] entitled *Octavius*, shows how capable a champion of the truth he could have been, had he devoted himself wholly to that pursuit.”

The other witness is Jerome, who, following Lactantius in this matter, as he himself acknowledges, says in his catalogue of ecclesiastical writers: “Minucius Felix, a distinguished advocate at Rome, wrote a dialogue between a Christian and a pagan engaged in debate, which is entitled *Octavius*.” Likewise, in his letter to Magnus, the Roman orator, he says: “Minucius Felix, an advocate of the Roman forum, in the book entitled *Octavius*, what part of pagan literature did he leave untouched?”

Now that this book is not by Arnobius is sufficiently evident, since it appears to be by Minucius. Indeed, what Jerome adds in the same letter could otherwise have shown that it was not Arnobius’s, since [Col. 0199D] he testifies that Arnobius wrote only seven books *Against the Nations*, the same seven that still survive. And since Jerome relates that another book had been falsely inscribed with the name of our Minucius, I am all the more astonished and grieved that this work, which was his sole and authentic work (*καὶ γνήσιος*, “and genuine”), should have been stolen from him [Col. 0200B] and unjustly attributed to another, who was not its owner; thus our Minucius appears to have suffered a double injury, both by plagiarism and by the substitution of a spurious offspring. But this is a frequent complaint concerning the misdeeds of copyists, who rashly mix up everything. Perhaps a certain resemblance of style and argument—which very often deceives the unlearned—caused some to attribute this booklet to Arnobius; and when they read “Octavius,” they immediately dreamed that it was some “eighth” book. Ridiculous and foolish indeed! At least they ought to have considered that a dialogue should not rashly be attributed to Arnobius, who employs continuous discourse. But ignorance possesses an audacity as blind as it is confident.

Jerome, indeed, writes that his Nepotian was so diligently versed in ecclesiastical writers that, if anything from them were produced, [Col. 0200C] he immediately recognized and distinguished what belonged to Tertullian, what to Cyprian, what to Lactantius, what to Minucius, what to Victorinus, and what to Arnobius. All the more, therefore, am I astonished that Erasmus—in that passage, I mean Erasmus, a man endowed with extremely acute judgment and by no means an obtuse critic of such writers—observed that nothing by this Minucius now survived.

Under which emperor our Minucius lived at Rome is not yet sufficiently clear to me. In his catalogue, in which he appears to enumerate ecclesiastical writers in the order in which they lived, Jerome obscurely indicates that Minucius was close in time to Origen and earlier than Cyprian. Yet elsewhere he indicates neither his century nor his period of life. Certainly, in Cyprian’s booklet *On the Vanity of Idols*, many things are found which are read in precisely the same [Col. 0200D] words in Minucius’s *Octavius*; it must therefore be the case either that Minucius took them from Cyprian or that Cyprian took them from Minucius. Jerome, however, in his letter to Magnus, when speaking of the Latin writers of the Church, although he praises Minucius before Cyprian, nevertheless appears to attribute that discourse on idols [Col. 0201A] to Cyprian. Yet in his *Apology for His Books Against Jovinian*, he mentions Tertullian first, Cyprian second, and Minucius third.

Sabellicus, though without citing a witness, affirms that Minucius flourished at Rome when Urban was bishop there—that is, in the reign of the emperor Alexander Severus. If that were so, Minucius would be earlier than Cyprian, contemporary with Tertullian, and thus also contemporary with our jurists Ulpian and Paul. But although I believe him to have been later, I nevertheless think that he followed closely upon that period, whereas he does not approach our own except across the vast interval of nearly thirteen hundred years.

I recall that the Minucian clan was once noble at Rome, and that within it four consuls named Minucius Augurinus may be counted within forty years. I also read of the Minucii Rufi [Col. 0201B] and the Minucii Thermi in the consular fasti. Finally, Minucius Fundanus was a distinguished governor of Asia, to whom the emperor Hadrian sent a law concerning the Christians that was not unjust—to say nothing now of the Minucius whom Pliny mentions in his *Letters*, or the one praised by our Julian, whose books on civil law addressed to Minucius Natalis are commended.

Yet before all these I readily place one Minucius Felix, even if he was not of the same clan or family: later than they in age, certainly, but far superior in learning, virtue, and—what must be reckoned first—in piety. For he was not only a great jurist and an outstanding orator in his time; what is greater, he was a Christian in earnest. I suspect that he was African, although I dare not affirm it. [Col. 0201C] Certainly, he mentions Fronto of Cirta—Cirta was a most celebrated city of Numidia, also mentioned in the *Pandects*—in such a way as to indicate that he himself had spent time in Africa. Moreover, he adopted the kind of speaking and writing characteristic of those Africans Tertullian, Cyprian, and Arnobius—not to speak now of later authors nourished by that same Africa: that happy and fruitful Africa, I say, which, just as it was said always to produce something new, also produced the most learned and vigorous champions and defenders of our religion, and sent them even to support and defend the Roman Church itself. For from that same Africa also came enemies who were by no means inactive, and slanderers no less depraved than violent, such as—to go no farther—the Fronto whom I have just mentioned. From Minucius we shall learn that he was as shameless a rhetorician as he was an impious adversary of the Christians. [Col. 0201D] I should not like this man to have been Papirius Fronto, the jurist praised in the *Pandects*. I rather suspect that he was the rhetorician Cornelius Fronto, whom Capitolinus relates to have been the teacher of Marcus Antoninus, the philosopher-emperor. From his school of rhetoric we are also told that our [Note: [Col. 0201D] Bishop of Sardis and author of an *Apology for the Christians*. Jerome, *Catalogue*.] Melito emerged; he turned his eloquence to the defense of the Christians, so that by replying the good pupil might wipe away the reproach which his impious teacher had cast upon them through abuse.

[Col. 0202A] That our Minucius pleaded cases at Rome has already been stated above from Lactantius and Jerome. Nor does he himself conceal it, since he relates that during the vintage holidays he had been released from judicial business. Thus Ulpian too, in the *Pandects*, Law I, *On Holidays*, writes that it was expressly provided by a speech of the emperor Marcus Antoninus “that no one should compel an opponent to come to court during harvest or vintage.” Similarly, Suetonius (chapter 35) relates that Augustus ordained “that it should not be necessary” to attend the Senate “in September or October.” Indeed, Cyprian himself, as though another Minucius, in his *Letter to Donatus* (Oxford edition, treatise *On Grace*, p. 1), recalls that season in which, as he says, “with the vintage granting indulgence, the mind, released into repose, obtains the customary and appointed respite from the weariness of the year.” [Col. 0202B] At that time, he says, he sought pleasant retreats in which to reflect on divine matters. The narrative of our Minucius is not dissimilar.

Happy indeed were those holidays by which that most distinguished advocate, led away from the forum and its benches, was brought to religious reflection or debate. Cicero, reporting what he learned from our Scaevola, relates (*On the Orator*, II.6) that “Laelius was accustomed to spend time in the country with Scipio, and that they used incredibly to become boys again when they had flown from the city into the countryside as though from chains;” that they were even accustomed “to gather shells and smooth pebbles at Caieta and Lake Lucrinus, and to descend to every kind of mental relaxation and play.”

This example immediately came to mind when I heard that Minucius, having left the din of the forum, went into the country with his Octavius [Col. 0202C] and pleasantly watched on the shore at Ostia the childish game which he describes. But when I then hear how weighty and serious a debate concerning religion took place in that retreat, and what sort of council was held within a deserted vault, I perceive that no senate was ever convened at Rome concerning a greater matter. I would prefer no forum, no city, and no business to that leisure or solitude; and I am repeatedly compelled to wish that jurists might sometimes be willing to take holidays, retire, and go into the country in this manner. Can no holy and religious meditation on divine things, worthy of their privacy, enter their minds?

Lactantius intimates that Minucius did not devote himself wholly to the study of ecclesiastical matters. Yet all the nobler for that reason was his jurisprudence: it existed in the commonwealth as jurisprudence ought to exist, and united concern for and knowledge of divine and human things. For that reason, I commend his example all the more to men of our profession, so that at least on days when legal business is forbidden they may cultivate in leisure the calendar of a better religion and give and dedicate themselves to it.

When the Romans took holidays, they once had their “Tiber excursion,” mentioned by Cicero (*On Ends*, V.24), and also what our laws [Note: [Col. 0202D] Under the title *On the Maiuma* in both Codes. The *Suda* adds that these games were celebrated at Ostia; their chief feature was “to revel voluptuously in the waters of the sea, throwing one another in” (*ἡδυπαθεῖν ἐν τοῖς θαλαττίοις ὕδασιν, ἀλλήλους ἐμβάλλοντες*).] call their “Maiuma” at Ostia. But how completely free from idleness or play was the excursion of our Minucius! It was assuredly worthy [Col. 0203A] of having Augustine gladly behold its traces and salute the retreat in which the debate had taken place when he buried his mother Monica in the church at Ostia. For my part, whenever I read this Minucian dialogue, I am accustomed to recall the religious conversation which that holy matron held with her son at those mouths of the Tiber, as he himself recounts in Book XI of the *Confessions*.

What the condition of the Roman forum was when Minucius practiced there, and what appearance the Church then presented in that city, are plainly shown by the record of his age; and what I once wrote on the edicts of the ancient Roman emperors concerning the Christians illuminates the matter. Nor is the history of the times in which Decius or Valerian ruled unknown.

It is truly gratifying that a Christian advocate succeeded jurists whose opinions on civil law we read, men not only alien to religion but also its bitterest adversaries; and that the Roman forum or college of the toga and advocacy, which for so many years had been sprinkled with the blood and ashes of pious men, was at last purified and consecrated by the voice and footsteps of a Christian legal expert. [Col. 0203B] Arnobius boasts in his first book that both orators and jurists, men endowed, as he says, with great talents, had passed over to the Church. I indeed believe that, when he wrote this, he was thinking of his Minucius. He could perhaps scarcely have enumerated many of that profession. Yet I believe that some followed Minucius’s example, just as Minucius had men whom he himself followed [Col. 0203C] in the same course.

At any rate, when praising Minucius in his letter to Magnus, Jerome mentions two senators of the city of Rome, Hippolytus and Apollonius, who deserved to be included among the ecclesiastical writers. What shall I say of that [Note: [Col. 0203D] A martyr at Lyons under Marcus Antoninus and Lucius Verus. The witnesses are Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, V.1, and Gregory of Tours, I.29.] Vettius Epagathus of Lyons, who lived many years before Minucius and was publicly called—partly as a term of reproach and partly as an honor, since judgments about names arising from religion then varied—[Note: [Col. 0203D] *παράκλητος Χριστιανῶν*, “advocate of the Christians.” Eusebius, passage cited.] THE ADVOCATE OF THE CHRISTIANS?

Whether Minucius ever pleaded their cause in the Roman forum I do not know. Tertullian certainly indicates that this was scarcely permitted. So hateful then was their religion or Church, to which the constitution of the Roman state and empire was opposed, and so thoroughly had it already been pierced by public prejudgments, that the mere confession of the Christian name was sufficient for condemnation. So far was the defense of this religion from enjoying the defense of liberty. Meanwhile, I marvel all the more that a pious advocate could maintain his position in so profane and hostile a forum.

But it is astonishing that, when for nearly two hundred years the Roman world had openly, publicly, and freely given its allegiance to Christ, under the emperors’ own authority, a law was thought necessary—and was at last enacted—providing that in no court should anyone be an advocate or pleader, or possess the right to plead, unless he were a Christian. (Law VIII, Code, *On Pleading*.) [Col. 0204A] How noble, therefore, was the example of our Minucius, who was at Rome a champion of the good cause so far as he could be, at a time when this was not yet permitted with impunity! Christians were then certainly barred from the forum, and the praetor did not grant them the right to plead.

Yet although they were generally men of the cloak rather than of the toga, because the commonwealth often could not do without them, pagan emperors from time to time permitted them to wear the toga and some of them to perform public office. Christian emperors were afterwards far more indulgent, for they sometimes appointed as consuls, prefects of the city, and even praetorian prefects men whom they otherwise knew to be the bitterest enemies of religion, as may be understood even from the circumstances of Symmachus alone. But at last they became more severe, thoroughly purged the Roman Senate and forum, and ordered that all who either administered justice or pleaded cases should be Minuciuses, as it were.

They had long before overthrown the altar of Victory which stood in the vestibule of the senate-house and at which the Senate was accustomed to swear and sacrifice, despite the protests of both Symmachus himself [Note: [Col. 0204D] Symmachus, Book X, Letter 54 in Juretus’s edition, or 61 in Scioppius’s, to the Augusti Valentinian, Theodosius, and Arcadius.] and the Senate. But in the end they brought it about not only that all wearers of the toga were free from such superstition, but also that no jurisprudence existed in the forum except that Christian jurisprudence of which some likeness had already appeared long before in our Minucius.

[Col. 0204C] The title of this Minucian booklet and its dialogue form reproduce an ancient and learned manner of writing. Its style is pointed, keen, and vehement; compressed, dense, vigorous, compacted with frequent maxims, filled with the memory of every kind of antiquity and history, and such that one may count more thoughts than periods in it. For the early Christians were not verbose, since they had not yet learned to deceive with words; and their defenders were learned men—in a word, polymaths. As for the false charges against them, they refuted their adversaries not so much by words and pamphlets as by their life and by the reality itself.

The question proposed here and the case at issue are great: namely, whether religion is true or false. The pleading and contention on both sides are extremely sharp. [Col. 0204D] The outcome and victory are what they ought to be when truth conflicts with falsehood. Caecilius Natalis is the vehement advocate of the bad cause and a formidable enemy of the good. Octavius Januarius, by contrast, is the champion of the good and the most vigorous and just accuser of the bad. Between them sits Minucius Felix, the good judge and arbiter.

The occasion of the dispute was this: while Minucius was walking with the other two men I have named on the shore at Ostia, the pagan Caecilius encountered an image of Serapis and indicated his worship of it; for this he was reproved by the Christian Octavius. Long before, in the consulship of Piso and Gabinius, [Col. 0205A] the rites of Serapis had been expelled from the Capitol and the city; but they were afterwards restored and easily took root in the suburbs. Caecilius, having been reproved, fumes and, as though provoked, launches a fierce attack upon the Christian religion; while simultaneously defending his own superstition, he challenges Octavius, as it were. But unwittingly he encounters a strong and keen adversary, and, as the poet says:

> Desiring to drive his tooth into something fragile,  
> He broke it upon something solid.

(Horace, *Satires*, II.1.)

The posture of the case is not a single one. Caecilius argues variously and confusedly about many matters. Octavius repels most of them by simple denial and turns them back by a just counteraccusation. Some things he candidly admits, but legally defends. Thus this dispute consists partly of questions of fact and partly of questions of law. [Col. 0205B]

Tertullian before him—and before Tertullian, Justin and Athenagoras, whose apologetic booklets survive—and afterwards Cyprian, partly *Against Demetrian* and partly in his booklet *On the Vanity of Idols*, and later, much more extensively, Arnobius and Lactantius, pleaded this same case. They generally employed not merely the same thoughts and arguments, but even the same words and formulas. For thus our forefathers were accustomed to say, as that ancient author put it, “the same things about the same subjects.”

Would that posterity had observed such an example and ambitious novelty had not defiled sound antiquity. I should certainly like the *Apologies* of the authors whom I have named to be compared with this Minucian booklet, so that it may not now be necessary to compile another commentary. Many things from the disputations of ancient philosophers, the fables of poets, and Roman and Greek history [Col. 0205C] are subtly and learnedly rehearsed here. But it would be foolish to heap up many idle annotations on those subjects in this place.

I would rather explain fully the matters pertaining to the record of Christian antiquities. But for commentaries upon these too, another, freer occasion will be given, for I shall at some point devote myself to completing those which I have already begun. For now, besides the booklets I have named, I should very much like the opposing orations of Symmachus and Ambrose also to be read and compared here. Thus we shall hear from Minucius that this case was argued sharply but privately, at a time when Christians were scarcely permitted to exist openly; and we shall hear the same case argued publicly many years later, again and again, in the imperial consistory. In it, the distinguished champion of the Roman rites, who was both pontifex maximus and prefect of the city, engaged in combat with the greatest and keenest prelate of the Christian religion, at a time when our people were free and enjoyed a fair and equitable condition. [Col. 0205D] Most importantly, the judge and arbiter who sat over the case was not merely as religious as Minucius but possessed far greater authority, and could execute a sound judgment and restrain and punish the insolence of the slanderous adversary.

Finally, if anyone should add here Augustine’s books *On the City of God*, he would add much light and splendor to Octavius’s disputation. I shall not linger now over the writing out of such commentaries, but shall merely touch briefly upon certain chapters of the Minucian booklet, to prepare the reader.

[Col. 0206A] At the outset Caecilius praises some sort of Academic *ἀκαταληψία* (“inapprehensibility”) or Pyrrhonian *ἐποχή* (“suspension of judgment”), as though there were no knowledge of religion; and with Epicurean audacity he attacks the providence of God, almost as that [Note: [Col. 0206D] The names have been transposed and confused. For Gaius Velleius, a senator, is the Epicurean in Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book I, chapter 6, who attacks Providence from chapter 8 to chapter 21; Gaius Cotta, the Academic, replies to him from chapter 21 to the end of the book. Add the beginning of Book II. (LINDNER.)] Cotta does in Cicero’s books *On the Nature of the Gods*. Octavius defends Providence; nor does he disdain to use even those arguments by which Velleius in the same work of Cicero refutes Cotta’s vanity.

Caecilius reproached the Christians for assigning to God, as some assign to Fate, whatever is done or comes to pass. Octavius does not shrink from the word “Fate,” although it was odious because of the follies of the Stoics, who misused that term. But he postpones a longer discussion of the matter to another occasion, and cautiously avoids it as though it were a reef.

Jerome, in his *Catalogue* and in his letter to Magnus (Letter 84), testifies that a certain book *On Fate* was once inscribed with Minucius’s name, but warns that the attribution was false. [Col. 0206B] I believe that some attributed it to him because he appeared here to have promised something of that kind.

Arnobius, whom I am accustomed to call Minucius’s disciple and imitator, at one moment accuses his opponents concerning fate and at another shows that the Christians themselves were accused under that same name; yet in both cases, when he comes to the heart of the matter, he falters and somehow, as though stunned, *ἐπέχει* (“suspends judgment”). For—it is helpful, on account of Minucius and certain others, to note this here in passing—after attacking fate in Book VII, especially as the Stoics fashioned it, he says: “Yet these aspects, because the subject requires an excessively long and extensive discourse, we pass over unexplained and untouched.”

Likewise in Book II, when writing that the Christians were pressed with the question whether anything happened without God’s will, he feels himself so constricted on both sides that, in order to escape, he appeals to a kind of plea of ignorance:

> If all things are accomplished by his will, and nothing in the world can either arise or fail without his assent, it necessarily follows that all evils too must be understood to spring from his will. But if, rejecting from him the causes of the worst things, we wish to say that he is conscious of evil but the author of none, the worst things will begin to appear to occur either against his will or—what is monstrous to say—without his knowing them, while he is ignorant and unaware.

[Col. 0206C]

What, then, does he finally answer to the question when confined between these, as it were, clashing rocks? “We must answer,” he says, “that we do not know these things, etc.”

Before him, Justin in his *Apology* had not hesitated openly to deny and wholly reject what he calls *τὴν τῆς εἱμαρμένης ἀνάγκην* (“the necessity of destiny”), wishing thereby to escape one reef which he thought otherwise threatened him. Yet he then runs into another which Minucius and Arnobius seem to have feared no less.

At last Augustine, in that same Africa, wishing, as it were, to discharge what Minucius was said to have promised, and to pay in full what Minucius might perhaps scarcely have been able to furnish, [Col. 0207A] subtly expounded a middle position on this question, by which he judged that both difficulties could be satisfied.

Both Minucius and Arnobius knew what dispute and disagreement had once existed between Chrysippus and Diodorus *περὶ δυνατῶν καὶ εἱμαρμένης* (“concerning possibilities and destiny”), or *On Fate*. For we too possess some knowledge of it from Cicero and Plutarch. Yet even among its judges and arbiters our authors found nothing which they could follow unreservedly. Therefore, so long as the matter was unclear, they preferred to pronounce that further inquiry was needed rather than rashly define something else at once.

I return to Caecilius. He defends as far as he can the antiquity of his deities and their authority, which had been received everywhere. He attacks the true and unique God of the Christians, while abusing them as *ἄθεοι* (“atheists”). [Col. 0207B] Octavius wittily exposes the fiction and emptiness of the idols, even using the same words which Cyprian employs in the same argument. Nor was the refutation difficult, since Cicero’s own books *On the Nature of the Gods* seemed, as it were, to cast their vote in its favor. For Arnobius too, near the beginning of Book III, testifies that on this account certain enemies of the Christians had once “muttered that those books ought to be abolished and burned by order of the Senate.”

What more? The thing itself, which even its own worshippers mocked—for no tale belonging to any superstition was ever more foolish or absurd—refuted itself of its own accord. Moreover, Minucius could easily have copied what was relevant here from Tertullian, Justin, Athenagoras, and Theophilus, just as Arnobius, [Col. 0207C] Lactantius, Ambrose, and Augustine afterwards drew from Minucius when pleading the same cause. Certainly, Lactantius repeats what Octavius relates about Saturn, citing Minucius himself as his authority, and expounds it more fully in Book I, chapter 11.

Just as Octavius wittily mocks the entire fiction of Roman superstition, so too he elegantly demonstrates the emptiness of the very oracles in which the pagans so greatly gloried. He indicates that even before his own time the oracle of Pythian Apollo had ceased, just as Plutarch acknowledges that in the reign of the emperor Hadrian it had utterly failed. For as the truth of the Christian religion grew strong, the deception of those *χρηστήρια* (“oracles”) necessarily vanished.

Furthermore, Octavius speaks of demons and of their trembling when adjured by Christians, just as Justin and Tertullian had previously done in their *Apologies*, Cyprian in *Against Demetrian*, and Lactantius later in Book IV, chapter 27. [Col. 0207D] Thus he readily refutes Ulpian himself, who in Law I, *On Extraordinary Proceedings*, mocks Christians as impostors because of what is called this exorcism. That exorcisms were not ineffectual even in later times, and were frequent in the Church, may also be understood from Optatus the African and Augustine, writers by no means remote from Minucius.

Octavius also repeatedly presses many points concerning the one God, not unlike those collected by Justin *περὶ μοναρχίας* (“On the Sole Sovereignty [of God]”) and Clement of Alexandria. For our author could have read them both. Nor does he overlook what Cyprian emphasizes: that even the common people naturally call God one and singular when they invoke the Deity. Similarly, Lactantius says in Book II, chapter 1, that when the pagans swore, they named “God” rather than Jupiter.

In the ancient Roman formula of oath, Diespiter is named. But Cicero, in Book VII of his *Letters to Trebatius*, said, “to swear by Jupiter the Stone.” [Col. 0208A] It is also established that those taking oaths in ancient times often mentioned Jupiter; among the Greeks he was called *Ζεὺς ὅρκιος* (“Zeus, guardian of oaths”).

It is astonishing that Caecilius boasts that the Romans erected altars even to unknown deities. For what is this but an admission of error? Paul says that he saw such an altar at Athens and from it draws an argument for convicting this ignorance and instructing the Athenians. Pausanias testifies that altars bearing such an inscription existed not only in Attica but also in Elis. [Note: [Col. 0208D] Pausanias, *Elis* I.14: *ἀγνώστων θεῶν βωμός*, “an altar of unknown gods.”] Perhaps, when Octavius afterwards undertook to instruct Caecilius, he likewise followed Paul’s example and derived from this the beginning of his catechesis concerning knowledge of the true God. [Col. 0208B]

It might also seem astonishing that Caecilius boasts that “all the deities worshipped individually among other peoples are worshipped at Rome,” so that nothing remains except for him proudly to display his Pantheon. Yet Romulus’s law stood in the way, for it forbade the worship of foreign gods. Cicero’s judgment also stood in the way, for he declared that otherwise a confusion of religions would arise. But perhaps they did not regard them as foreigners, because they had now been officially invited and, as it were, granted citizenship.

Thus that city which Athenaeus (I.17) aptly called *ἐπιτομὴ τῆς οἰκουμένης* (“an epitome of the inhabited world”) was wittily called by our Theophilus, before Minucius’s age, *ἐπιτομὴ τῆς δεισιδαιμονίας* (“an epitome of superstition”). For Tertullian too (*On the Spectacles*, chapter 6) had said that “an assembly [Col. 0208C] of demons” and a senate-house of gods had settled there. Meanwhile, he did not rashly add that in that city it was lawful to worship anything whatever except the true God.

What Augustine writes in Book I, chapter 18, of *The Harmony of the Evangelists* is certainly memorable: why did the Romans, who worshipped all the others, not worship that one God? Because, he says, he wished to be worshipped alone and forbade the worship of those gods of the nations whom they were already worshipping. When the question concerned Christ himself, moreover, there was another reason for the Senate’s opposition.

And just as Augustine in that book repeatedly presses many points which might seem to have been taken from our *Octavius*, so too his elegant answer concerning the destruction of Jerusalem confirms Octavius’s answer on the same matter. But there is no reason now to set out every particular. I return to the Minucian courtroom.

[Col. 0208D] Caecilius accuses the Christians because they “despise temples as though they were funeral pyres.” Octavius admits it. For Clement of Alexandria had earlier proved in his *Protrepticus* that they were [Note: [Col. 0208D] Clement, *Protrepticus to the Nations*, p. 58: *δεικνύντες, τῶν εἰδώλων τὰ τεμένη, τάφους τινὰς ἢ δεσμωτήρια*, “showing that the sacred precincts of the idols are certain tombs or prisons.”] tombs. Arnobius, however, says in Book VI that “temples had been raised above funeral pyres.”

Yet Christians afterwards, on a different principle, made not only churches but also altars from the tombs of their martyrs. Minucius himself could well have seen such things. The matter is certainly thus: although our forefathers did many things in the name of religion which outwardly resembled what the pagans did, [Col. 0209A] nevertheless, because the purpose and use were different, they knew that their own sacred practices could be defended even while they condemned the *κακοζηλία* (“misguided imitation”) of others. How vigorously they mock the rites of Roman sacrifices! Yet their form was once not unlike that of the rites commanded to the Jews by divine law.

Although the Christians in the age of our Minucius could not proudly raise their own temples after the pagan model, they nevertheless possessed at least their crypts and even certain sacred buildings and houses in open and elevated places, as Tertullian says. They gladly assembled at the tombs of their martyrs, which they called *κοιμητήρια* (“sleeping places,” cemeteries). Eusebius writes that the emperor Gallienus, who was separated from Minucius’s age by no long interval, permitted them to have these.

Caecilius mocks the Christians because they said and believed that their people who had departed this life were not so much dead as asleep, and because they vaunted the resurrection of bodies. [Col. 0209B] Yet in his own Homer (*Iliad* XXIV.344) he had read *ὑπνώοντας ἐγείρειν* (“to awaken those who sleep”), and in Virgil (*Aeneid* IV.244), “to take away sleep,” when the subject is recalling the dead to life. What Octavius further answered here may be compared with what Theophilus, Athenagoras, Justin, and Tertullian had previously written on the same matter, for Minucius followed them.

Caecilius mocks the Christians (chapter 12) because they anoint corpses but do not crown them. Yet the Romans too, although they did not understand what they were doing, possessed funeral ointments. That little verse of Ennius is well known:

> A good woman washed and anointed the body of Tarquinius.

And although the Laws of the Twelve Tables abolished the anointing of corpses by slaves, as Cicero says (*On the Laws*, II.24), they nevertheless allowed another kind of anointing; and the jurists themselves include ointments among the legitimate expenses of a funeral (Law 37, *On Religious Matters and Funeral Expenses*). [Col. 0209C]

As regards “crowns for corpses,” Octavius admits that they seem foolish to Christians and that Christians therefore refuse to imitate the pagans in this practice. Tertullian, in *On the Crown* (chapter 13), says that “even the undertakers’ establishments are crowned by the world.” Cicero himself, in *For Flaccus* (chapter 31), mentions “a golden crown placed upon the dead body” of a certain Castricius. What the ancient Roman laws ordained concerning crowns for corpses, however, I have explained in my work on the Twelve Tables.

Finally, Caecilius mocks and resents the fact that among Christians “corpses are buried,” not “burned.” But that ancient saying is true: “Nothing is more foolish than foolish laughter.” What does Octavius say? [Col. 0209D] He answers simply that his custom of burial is ancient and better. Arnobius too gave this answer, for even after Minucius the question was raised again.

Yet the Romans could at least have read the second book of their own Cicero’s *On the Laws* (chapter 22), in which he defends and confirms this custom. Pliny too says in Book VII, chapter 54: “Cremation itself was not an ancient institution among the Romans; bodies were buried in the earth.” Tertullian, in *On the Crown* (chapter 12), says that it was not lawful for a Christian to cremate a body; and at the beginning of *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* he mocks those who, as he says, “burn the dead with the utmost cruelty.”

Furthermore, Augustine in Book XI of the *Confessions* indicates that some peculiar custom for the burial [Col. 0210A] of bodies existed in the church at Ostia. Whether it was already in use when Minucius was active there, I do not know.

It is ridiculous that Caecilius, a stern censor indeed, accuses the Christians because they were neither drenched in perfumes nor wreathed with garlands. It is astonishing that he does not meanwhile marvel that they nevertheless already used oil and chrism. The pagans themselves were accustomed to praise a sober, austere, and frugal life—that is, one free from perfumes. In the ideal commonwealth which Plato fashioned according to his own judgment, he expels Homer only after having drenched him in perfume, bound him with ribbons, and, as our Octavius says here, “crowned” him; by this he indicated that he did not wish to have citizens so effeminate.

And, not to go farther afield, Vespasian turned away from a certain man drenched in perfumes who was thanking him for obtaining a prefecture, and said: [Col. 0210B] “I would rather you smelled of garlic.”

But what would Caecilius not make into an accusation, since he even charges the Christians with holding flowers to their noses rather than placing them upon their heads as crowns? Yet Lucian himself, in his *Nigrinus*, more justly mocks the Romans themselves because they do not imitate the Christians in this respect, but act contrary to nature and plainly possess neither taste nor sensation. Add Tertullian, *On the Soldier’s Crown*.
