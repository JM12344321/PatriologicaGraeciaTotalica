[Col. 1379C] **UTILITY.** I, 1309 B, “The utility of these things has been so arranged.”

**UTHINENSIS**, the bishop of Uthina, a city in Africa that was a Roman colony. He did not, it seems, acknowledge the new prophecy of Montanus. II, 947 C, “That man of yours from Uthina, of course.”

**USEFUL**, meaning “in use.” II, 1039 B, “But the passive part is useful to everyone, like this cloak.”

## V

**VACARE.** II, 1030 C, *Cum ita vacat ac juvat*, “when one thus has the opportunity and inclination,” i.e. while it is permitted and pleasing. II, 1041 A, *vacuerat* for *vacaverat*. “To be absent”: II, 1047 A, “The torment of the belt is absent.” I, 276 A, “And if they are forestalled by confession, they will cease,” i.e. they will stop and no longer be applied—namely, the tortures. II, 226 A, “But let this example be set aside as merely human.” II, 229 A, “They must be made from things that exist, not from things that are empty.” I, 380 A, “Lest knowledge be wanting,” i.e. so that [Col. 1379D] they themselves might understand the sacred books. II, 22 B, “It was of no effect toward us,” i.e. it was empty, idle, and useless. II, 968 B, “Those who invalidate these things as though they were of no effect,” i.e. as though they were useless and worthless. II, 256 B, “There is no question concerning what is known.” II, 479 A, “Therefore both were without effect in Christ.” II, 815 B, “Although the diversity of the things makes the example inapplicable,” i.e. although the example is utterly dissimilar and in no respect comparable with this. II, 760 B, “Because, as a phantom, he lacked the sensation of them,” i.e. he was without sensation of them.

**EMPTY.** II, 654 B, “How, then, can solid things impel an empty thing?”—i.e. the soul, if in Tertullian’s view it is incorporeal and empty.

**POWER**, ἰσχύς (*strength*). II, 238 A, “The power of him who made the earth.” II, 619 B, “And he will gather all the strength of the peoples.”

**EMPTINESS.** I, 1246 A, “And by the wasted appearance of the face from fasting.” For through fasting the face’s natural vigor and form fade away. He alludes to Matt. VI, 16: “They go about with a faded countenance.”

[Col. 1380A] **VANITIMUM**, an ancient word derived from *vanus* (“empty”), meaning that which pertains to emptiness and is situated in it. So too *aeditimus*, *maritimus*, *medioximus*, and *finitimus*. II, 224 C, n. (12).

**TO VARY, CHANGE.** II, 1031 A, “The urn has changed the course of worldly affairs,” i.e. the vicissitudes of affairs have changed. II, 558 A, “Because they vary in this way concerning the sex of the father,” i.e. they differ in sex altogether, as does the father.

**VARIETY.** II, 610 C, “Even the varieties of the Gaetuli,” meaning the various Gaetuli; Pomponius Mela, *On the Description of the World*, book I, ch. 4: “The Gaetuli are a populous and manifold nation.”

**VEHICLE.** I, 1202 A, “He placed beneath him a vehicle worthy of God.” Others read *vectabulum*, perhaps more correctly, on the basis of Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book XX, ch. 1, and Nonius Marcellus IV, 321: “under the designation of a failing vehicle.”

**TO BE BORNE.** II, 670 A, “Asclepiades is also carried along by that line of argument.” II, 1002 A, “While the Holy Spirit bears you along.” Irenaeus: “Walking in the Holy Spirit”; and ancient copies of Acts: φερόμενοι ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ, “being borne along in the Holy Spirit.”

**TAX.** I, 381 A, “Tax-purchased liberty is commonly resorted to,” [Col. 1380B] i.e. the liberty that the Jews purchase by a tax or annual payment, so that they may freely assemble in their synagogues and practice their worship.

**VEDIUS POLLIO.** One of his slaves had broken a crystal vessel. Vedius ordered him seized and condemned to die no ordinary death: he was to be thrown to the moray eels, enormous specimens of which were kept in a fishpond. Seneca, *On Anger*, book III, ch. 40; Pliny, *Natural History*, book IX, ch. 23; II, 1048 B.

**VELIFICARE**, “to set or make sail.” II, 122 A, “Its savagery sets sail upon the south and southwest winds,” i.e. scorpions, “the dreadful scourge of Africa,” as Pliny calls them (*Natural History*, book V, ch. 7). Pliny accordingly says in book XI that even the south winds make this flying scourge of Africa; Tertullian explains that this occurs when the south and southwest winds blow.

**TO SELL.** I, 308 A, “To sell crimes” means to conceal them after receiving payment.

**TO BE PUT UP FOR SALE**, to be venal or regarded as such. I, 512 A, 513 A, “Plato puts himself up for sale to Dionysius for his belly’s sake.”

**BELLY, WOMB.** II, 908 B, “They are compelled to conceal their wombs by the ruin of weakness.” By “wombs,” as legal authors commonly do, he means wombs made pregnant through intercourse with a man. Thus “to conceal wombs” means to hide pregnancies. [Col. 1380C] I, 1274 A, “Workers for the belly,” i.e. parasites.

**TO FAN.** II, 1044 B, “He fans his silk,” i.e. he catches the wind in a loose and flowing silken garment and walks with the silk billowing. II, 82 B, “He wafted something fragrant before his nostrils.”

**WIND.** II, 123 A, “But most men become Christians according to the wind, if it please”—“according to the wind,” i.e. as the wind carries them, with a favorable wind, as occasion affords and counsels. “If it please,” namely, Caesar, the proconsul, the governor, etc.

**TRUTH.** II, 295 A, “An image will not correspond in every respect to the truth.” II, 795 B, “Or contending that it possessed no truth,” i.e. contending that Christ’s flesh was not real. I, 1264 B, “In the possession of the truth,” i.e. in Christ’s possession.

**NATIVE.** I, 388 A, Josephus is called a native historian because, being himself a Jew, he described the origins of the Jews and their wars with the Romans, though in Greek. I, 421 A, [Col. 1380D] the Trojan race is called native to Cybele. I, 454 A, “And the common people themselves, native to the seven hills.” I, 1341 B, “Among certain barbarians, because gold is native to them.” II, 737 B, “I know a certain woman native to the Church,” i.e. a Christian.

**WART.** I, 1341 A, “Like some hard and rounded wart of that shell.” He calls pearls “warts” of the shell because healthy offspring of shellfish consist of many layers of skin, so that they may not improperly be regarded as a callus of the body; they are therefore customarily cleaned by experts. For they arise within the oyster’s flesh itself, just as hailstones are said to arise in pork. See Athenaeus, book III, ch. 8, and Pliny.

**VERSURA.** “To make and discharge a *versura*” is to repay one person what was owed to him with money received from another. II, 1035 A, “It is τὸ ἀντίστροφον or ἡ ἀντιστροφή”—“the reciprocal or counter-turn”—“that repays another by balancing the *versura*.”

**TO CHANGE.** II, 906 B, “They change the hair” does not seem [Col. 1381A] to mean the same as curling the locks. The words at I, 1322 A, “I see some also changing their hair with saffron,” warn us to understand this of altering and dyeing the hair among the ancients. Herodian, on Antoninus: Κόμας τε τῇ κεφαλῇ ἐπετίθετο ξάνθας, “and he placed blond locks upon his head”; and Clement of Alexandria: Τρίχας ξάνθας ποιοῦν, “making the hair blond.” Capitolinus, in his *Verus*: “He is indeed said to have taken such care over his golden hair.” So strong was the affectation of golden hair that Jerome admonishes Laeta: “Do not dye the hair red.” II, 705 B, “But the condition of souls changes,” with an intransitive meaning.

**SUMMIT.** I, 262 A, “The summit of the city is the Capitol.”

**VERTIGINARE.** II, 1037 B. Its meaning can be understood from Aristotle’s words on the chameleon: Στρέφει δὲ τὸν ὀφθαλμὸν κυκλῷ, καὶ τὴν ὄψιν ἐπὶ πάντας τοὺς τόπους μεταβάλλει, “It turns its eye in a circle and shifts its gaze toward every place.”

**BLADDER.** I, 521 A, “There the bladder is sought.” Havercamp reads: “Immediately there the bladder complains,” i.e. as though distended with wind, their breast is most violently tormented. Havercamp regards it as a proverb drawn from little boys who can no longer endure the need to urinate when their bladder is full of urine and who, often fearing the rod, cry out. [Col. 1381B] So these men, upon hearing the Christians’ discourse on the resurrection of the dead, immediately leave the lecture hall as though pressed by the need to urinate.

**VESPERUGO**, a bat. II, 703 B.

**VESTICEPS.** II, 746 B, “A pubescent boy.”

**CLOTHING-WORKSHOP**, a place where garments were made. II, 1039 A. Rhenanus, however, interprets it as “clothes-makers or tailors.”

**TO CLOTHE.** II, 1031 A, “So too, recently, the sister city was clothing [someone or something].” II, 1031 B, “And whatever else condition, rank, or circumstance clothes,” is said ἀμεταβάτως, “without change.” Turnebus proposes *vertit*, “changes”; Junius, *gestit*, “desires,” i.e. ὀρέγεται. At II, 50 A it is said of baptism: “It clothes with the Holy Spirit.”

**CLOTHED.** II, 716 A, “From these years onward the sex is both more suffused and more clothed”—more clothed with the hair that grows upon the body. See **HAIRLESS**.

**VETERNUS** is a disease arising from cold humors that afflict the brain and abolish reason, commonly called lethargy. [Col. 1381C] II, 1048 A, “I spare no lethargies.” Metaphorically, “vices.”

**OLDNESS.** II, 1022 A, “But if, after abolition, anything of it revives into oldness,” i.e. into the old man, into a pagan.

**WAY.** I, 634 B, 635 A. For they call the horizontal divisions of belts “hinges” and the vertical divisions of tunics “ways.” See **BELT**.

**TO VIBRATE.** I, 1196 B, “They look up to heaven, not idly, with their mouths, vibrating, to move by their breath.” The passage is corrupt; perhaps it should be restored thus: “They look up to heaven with no idle mouth, vibrating their breath in their own fashion.” That is: the lowing of cattle, bleating of sheep, and cries of beasts and birds are prayers directed toward the Parent of all, who provides prey for lions seeking it by their roaring and feeds the young ravens that call upon him. “To vibrate the breath” means to expel the breath forcefully, as animals do when they cry out and make a noise. Prudentius says of the Λόγος (*Word*), *Apotheosis* 49:

[Col. 1381D] This is the Word which, sounded from the kindly Father’s mouth,

Assumed a frail form from a virgin body.

**RETURN, RECOMPENSE.** I, 499 A, “To allow the return of an injury” means to repay an injury. II, 437 C, “Who could not repay in full the return for this humane act of his.” II, 551 B, “In the manner of seed.” Irenaeus: κάθαπερ σπέρμα, “just like seed.”

**VICTORIATE.** II, 907 B, a coin stamped with an image of Victory, discussed by Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXXIII, ch. 3. It was worth two sesterces.

**TO SEE.** “Never mind the form,” i.e. the form is immaterial and contributes nothing to the matter; let the wood be fashioned in this way or that—as the sign of a cross or as a rough stake—provided that it is wood. II, 198 A, “But never mind the person,” i.e. why linger over the person? What is the person to us, when doctrine is the question before me? II, 555 A, “Never mind the solecism.” II, 898 C, “Never mind the age that rivals God,” i.e. let this impious age, hostile to God, judge even under nature’s guidance.

**TO BE SEEN.** I, 386 A, “In which it is seen,” i.e. it is beheld or discerned; it must not be interpreted as “is thought.” [Col. 1382A] II, 608 B, “Nor if you come to be seen by me,” i.e. to appear.

**TO WIDOW.** II, 953 A, “The priestesses of Ceres are widowed even while their husbands live and consent to an amicable separation.” II, 680 B, “Things that deprive the mind of understanding even for a brief time.”

**IN WIDOWHOOD.** II, 902 A, “I know very well that somewhere a virgin not yet twenty years old has been placed in widowhood,” ἐν χηρείᾳ, “in the sacred ministry of widows,” concerning which see Acts VI and 1 Tim. V.

**WIDOWED.** I, 1204 B, “But they deceive themselves with widowed waters”—waters widowed, namely, of the angel and the Holy Spirit, and therefore barren. But upon our waters the Spirit comes from heaven, sanctifying them from himself; thus sanctified, they conceive the power of sanctifying and consequently give birth—namely, to Christians destined for eternal life.

**TO INVIGORATE.** II, 984 A, “Practices that effeminate discipline rather than invigorate it.”

**STEWARD**, properly one who presides over the administration of an estate, οἰκονόμος (*household manager*). II, 40 B, “The steward, Christ’s deputy, has neglected God’s office.” He means Paul.

[Col. 1382B] **FLEECE** is wool. II, 913 A, “They place either a fleece or any sort of thread upon the head.”

**TO VINDICATE.** I, 889 A, “And thus it is vindicated against the truth,” i.e. asserted, in a forensic sense.

**THE VERGILIAE**, or rather *Vergiliae*, are the seven stars in the constellation Taurus, so named either because they rise in spring (*vere*) around the equinox or because their rising signals the spring season, as Servius observed on book I of the *Georgics*; Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, book II, ch. 44; II, 772 A.

**TO PLAY THE VIRGIN.** II, 907 A, “But they wish to play the virgin,” i.e. they wish to be regarded as virgins.

**VIRGIN.** I, 413 A, “Virgin continence,” through which they always remain virgins. I, 413 A, “The heavenly virgin” is Juno. II, 549 A, “Of virgin old age,” i.e. of one who remained unmarried until old age. II, 960 B, “While the saliva is still virgin,” i.e. while our saliva remains untouched by food and drink and we are fasting. II, 782 A, “The earth was still [Col. 1382C] virgin, not yet pressed by labor,” etc.

**VIRIAE**, bracelets worn by women upon their arms. II, 1042 B, “She occupied the traces of boxing-straps with bracelets,” i.e. she put bracelets upon her arms in place of boxing-straps. Turnebus proposes: “A bracelet occupied the traces of boxing-straps.”

**VIRILE.** II, 542 A, “An image of the male member is revealed.” Junius thinks *membri viralis* should be read, so that *viralis* is derived from *vira*, “woman,” just as *virilis* is derived from *vir*, “man.” For the male member belonged to the Bacchic orgies, whereas the female member belonged to the Eleusinian rites of Ceres.

**POWERFUL.** II, 570 A, “And by use he confused powerful things,” i.e. things of great strength, ἰσχυρός, δυνατός, “strong, powerful.” II, 681 B, “And indeed he will embrace it more powerfully.”

**POWER**, a miraculous gift. I, 378 B, “Their utterances and likewise the powers that they displayed as proof of divinity,” concerning the prophets. II, 41 A, “So many powers,” like the Greek δύναμις (*power, miracle*). II, 619 B, “The power of the East,” that is, its forces.

**FORCE.** I, 275 A, 276 A, “Lest any force lie hidden in secret,” is understood of the devil.

[Col. 1382D] **DISTRIBUTION OF MEAT** was a distribution of flesh customarily made either at a solemn sacrifice or at the funeral of some distinguished person. I, 641 A, “As though they were enjoying a certain distribution of meat”; II, 331 B.

**VISUALITY.** II, 699 B, “For we too shall oppose the contrarieties of the born and unborn, of visuality and blindness.”

**VICE, DEFECT.** II, 1034 A, “He introduced a new kind of vice into the sea,” i.e. a new vice. Junius prefers the reading “the name of vice.” It signifies Charybdis, which Tertullian calls “Vice,” because it cannot be sailed past without danger. I, 47 A, “And the defect of a single aeon”: shortly before, in ch. 7, he called it ἔκτρωμα, “an abortion.”

**GLASS.** I, 626 B, “If glass is worth so much, how much is a pearl worth?”—a proverb signifying an immense inequality and distance between things. Jerome, *To Laeta* 7: “If glass is worth so much, why should a pearl not be of greater value?”

**BURNING ALIVE.** II, 648 A, “But let it exhaust every device of cruelty upon the gibbet and by burning alive.” [Col. 1383A] II, 706 C, “Gibbets, burnings alive.”

**CALLED.** II, 220 A, “It likewise departs from the designation derived from his calling.”

**VOLATICA.** II, 1050 A, “One who observes the *volatica*.” Some interpret this of geometry, from *vola*, the palm, by which geometers measure everything. Salmasius understands it of divination from entrails.

**FLYING, FLEETING.** I, 1281 A, “Temporal and fleeting desires.” II, 1217 A, “And all this for the fleeting joy of a single year.”

**TWEEZERS**, an instrument for plucking out hairs. II, 1040 A, “Tweezers so thievish upon the chin.”

**WILL.** I, 1285 A, “Finally, to endure the will of God,” i.e. to receive judgment on the last day.

**TO ROLL.** I, 1302 B, “At the same time they pray, at the same time they roll about.” So in book III *Against Marcion*: “To commend prayer with hands beating the breast and with the face rolling upon the ground.”

**VORACIOUS GULLET.** I, 470 A, “Nor to ungrateful voracious gullets.” These are gluttons, profligates, abysses and whirlpools of charitable money, who are as ungrateful to God, the Church, and individual beneficent brethren as they are to others.

[Col. 1383B] **VOTIVE.** I, 315 A, “He exposed them upon votive crosses.” Havercamp explains this as meaning that Tiberius made votive crosses from votive trees, upon which those criminal priests hung splendidly like offerings dedicated in fulfillment of a vow. Junius derives the designation from the vows for public safety [Col. 1384A] made by Augustus. See Suetonius, *Augustus*, ch. 97.

**TO WOUND.** I, 1252 A, “The Lord’s patience was wounded in Malchus.”

**VOLSINII**, once the wealthiest people of the Etruscans, whose entire city was consumed by lightning. II, 1034 B.

## X

**XEROPHAGY**, ξηροφαγία, “the eating of dry foods.” II, 955 A, “That we may also observe xerophagies.” II, 960 A, “And they too were displeased by the xerophagy of angelic bread.”

**XYSTARCH**, the superintendent of the *xystus*, or athletic stadium, who supplies everything needed by the competitors. I, 624 B, “The Holy Spirit, the xystarch.”

**XYSTIC**, proper to athletes. II, 1010 A, “Xystic cleanliness.” For athletes and wrestlers exercised naked in the *xysti* and porticoes; hence they took the greatest care of their bodies and cleanliness. [Col. 1384B] II, 993 B, “And xystic vanities.”

## Z

**ZONE.** II, 519 B, “By a sort of wall of that fiery zone.” He calls the ether the fiery zone.

## SACRED WRITERS

[Col. 1383D]

**AMOS** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**ARITHMI**, or Numbers, II, 129 A, 418 B, 431 C.

Under the name of **BARNABAS** the Epistle to the Hebrews is cited, which Tertullian declares to have been received among the Churches, II, 1021 A.

**BARUCH** is cited under Jeremiah’s name, II, 137 B.

The four books of the **BASILEIA**, that is, Kings, II, 350 B, 386 B, 410 A. They are also called the Annals of the Kings, II, 129 C.

The Annals of the **CRITAE**, or Judges, II, 129 B.

The weeks of **DANIEL**, II, 612, 614.

Words from the books of **ENOCH** are cited, I, 665 C, 666 A, 2307 A. They are not admitted into the Jewish archive, ibid. [Col. 1384D] In both places they possess testimony in the Apostle Jude, ibid. in both places; I, 1328 B.

**EZRA** restored the entire corpus of Jewish literature, I, 1307 A. Certain passages from the fourth book of Ezra are also cited, II, 15 A, 395 A.

**EZEKIEL** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**HERMAS**, whose writing is entitled *The Shepherd*, I, 1172 G. It is judged apocryphal and false by every council of the Churches, II, 1000 B, 1021 A.

Whatever is now contained in the book of **BARUCH** was formerly attributed to **JEREMIAH**, II, 137 B.

**ISAIAH** is frequently cited.

**JAMES.** II, 600 B, 922 B.

[Col. 1385A] The Apocalypse of **JOHN**, and the First and Second Epistles of the same author, II, 1020 A. His Gospel was published before Marcion, II, 767 B. The Apocalypse is attributed to the apostle John. As an apostle, he commends the faith to us through his Gospel, II, 363 C. “And even if Marcion rejects his Apocalypse, nevertheless the succession of bishops, traced back to its origin, will establish John as its author,” II, 112 A, 147 A–B, 366 C ff.

**JOB** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**JOEL** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**JONAH** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**JUDE.** See the Index of Scriptures.

Certain passages from the book of **JUDITH** are cited, II, 253 C, 952 B.

The *Acts of the Apostles* by **LUKE**, the follower of the apostle Paul, is an authentic writing among the apostolic documents, II, 34 C, 35 B; the arrangement of his Gospel, II, 363 ff., [Col. 1385B] which they also customarily ascribe to Paul, II, 366 C. Paul confirms the writing of the apostolic authors in the Epistle to the Galatians; these writings are therefore wrongly rejected by Marcion.

The books of the **MACCABEES** are cited by Tertullian, II, 26 B, 606 C.

**MALACHI** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**MARK.** As an apostolic man he restores the faith to us through his Gospel, II, 363 ff., 366 C; although what Mark published is also affirmed to belong to Peter, whose interpreter Mark was.

**MATTHEW**, a most faithful expositor of the Gospel as a companion of the Lord, II, 788, 790; as an apostle, he guarantees the faith to us, II, 363 ff.

**MICAH** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**MOSES**, author of the Jewish corpus, was contemporary with Inachus of Argos; he preceded Danaus by almost three hundred years, the destruction of Priam by about a thousand, and Homer by more than five hundred. His original document, [Col. 1385C] Genesis, I, 382, 386 A; he was about nine hundred years earlier than Saturn, II, 697 A; author of the Pentateuch, II, 257 B; earlier than every Lycurgus and Solon, II, 305 A.

**NAHUM** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

**HOSEA** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

Tertullian observed that heretics made extensive use of the **EPISTLES OF PAUL** to raise questions, II, 35 A–B. His Apocalypse is rejected, ibid. and 37 A; likewise the *Acts*, or *Journeys*, of Paul and Thecla, I, 1219 A. Marcion reduced the number of his Epistles, II, 470 C, omitting the Epistles to Timothy and Titus; he also mutilated his other Epistles by removing passages, II, 471 A. The Epistle to the Galatians is the principal Epistle against Judaism, ibid. The Epistle that we possess under the title “To the Ephesians” is entitled by the heretics “To the Laodiceans,” II, 500 B, 512 B. They were written chiefly from imprisonment, II, 111 C.

[Col. 1385D] **PETER.** The Epistle to the people of Pontus, which we now call the First Epistle, II, 146 B, and frequently elsewhere.

Under **SOLOMON’S** name are cited not only Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs, but also the book of Wisdom, II, 20 B, and Ecclesiasticus, II, 265 A, 950 C.

The **SEVENTY-TWO TRANSLATORS**, at the command of Ptolemy Philadelphus, translated the Jewish corpus of Hebrew literature into Greek style. The philosopher Menedemus admired their agreement in judgment; Aristeas attested these things, I, 378, 379. Their translation is displayed in Ptolemy’s library at the Serapeum together with the Hebrew writings themselves, ibid.

**ZEPHANIAH** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.

Tertullian sometimes follows **SYMMACHUS’S** edition, II, 403, 404.

**ZECHARIAH** is frequently cited; concerning him, see the Index of Scriptures.
