[Col. 0448B] As for the three rivers mentioned by Minucius, if you seek the testimony of writers other than Cicero, many examples can quickly be produced for you. First, Diodorus Siculus clearly shows not only how the Nile moderates Egypt’s aridity, as Minucius says, but also how it renders the country fruitful and fertile. Since his Greek words are rather lengthy, we shall give them only in the Latin translator’s rendering:

> “Its inundation,” he says, “beginning at the summer solstice, increases until the autumnal equinox. Meanwhile, continually bringing in fresh silt, it waters both untilled ground and soil cultivated with crops and plants for as long as the farmers wish. For they easily divert the gently advancing waters with modest embankments; and, by opening these, they let the water in again with no great labor, if that seems useful. [Col. 0448C] It brings so great a saving of labor and benefit to the inhabitants that most of the country people, after casting seed upon the fields once the ground has dried, drive in their livestock to tread it down and return after four or five months to reap the harvest. Others, lightly breaking the upper surface of the soil with small plows after it has been moistened, gather from it enormous heaps of produce without great expense or the hardships of labor” (Diodorus, *Library*, book I, p. 22).

Saint Ambrose likewise writes no less clearly:

> “In springtime among the Egyptians,” he says, “the Nile overflows, so that the earth may be softened for the sowing of seed and the seed cast upon it may be received in a gentler fold and a more kindly bosom” (Ambrose, *On Noah and the Ark*, ch. 14, § 48, p. 247).

Anyone who desires more may consult Strabo, Mela, [Col. 0448D] Pliny, Solinus, and others (Strabo, *Geography*, book V, p. 694, and book VII, p. 788; Mela, *On the Situation of the World*, book I, ch. 9; Pliny, *Natural History*, book I, ch. 9, p. 557, and book XVIII, ch. 8, p. 490; Solinus, ch. 32).

The same authors testify that, just as Egypt is made fertile by the Nile, so Mesopotamia, as Minucius says, is made fertile by the Euphrates. Concerning this river Pliny relates:

> “It too rises, like the Nile, on fixed days, though with a slight difference, and inundates Mesopotamia when the sun occupies the twentieth degree of Cancer. It begins to diminish in Virgo, after the sun has passed through Leo. It returns completely to its bed at the twenty-ninth degree of Virgo” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book V, ch. 27, p. 584).

Solinus, Pliny’s intermediary and imitator, says:

> [Col. 0449A] “It enriches Mesopotamia by the overflow of its annual inundation, covering the land after the fashion of the Egyptian river—that is, the Nile—and carrying fertility into the soil at almost the same times as the Nile” (Solinus, ch. 38).

See Strabo, Arrian, Quintus Curtius, and other geographers (Strabo, *Geography*, book XVI, p. 740; Arrian, *Expedition of Alexander*, book VII, pp. 453 and 489; Q. Curtius, book V, shortly after the beginning).

Furthermore, after the same Strabo has first said certain things about the rivers that flow into the Ganges and the Indus, he confirms on Eratosthenes’ authority what Minucius recorded in writing about the Indus:

> Ἐκ δὲ τῆς ἀναθυμιάσεως τῶν τοσούτων ποταμῶν, καὶ ἐκ τῶν ἐτεσίων, ὡς Ἐρατοσθένης φησι, βρέχεται τοῖς θερινοῖς ὄμβροις ἡ Ἰνδικὴ καὶ λιμνάζει τὰ πεδία· ἐν μὲν οὖν τούτοις τοῖς ὄμβροις λίνον [Col. 0449B] σπείρεται, καὶ κέγχρον· πρὸς τούτοις σησάμην, ὄρυζα, βοσμορον· τοῖς χιμερινοῖς δὲ καιροῖς πυροὶ, κριθαὶ, ὄσπρια, καὶ ἄλλοι καρποὶ ἐδώδιμοι, ὧν ἡμεῖς ἄπειροι.
>
> “Eratosthenes states that, through the exhalations from so many rivers and the blowing of the Etesian winds, India is drenched by summer rains and its plains are turned into standing pools; during these rains flax and millet are sown, together with sesame, rice, and *bosmoron* [the precise crop-name is uncertain]. In wintertime, however, wheat, barley, pulses, and other edible crops with which we are unacquainted are sown” (Strabo, *Geography*, book XV, p. 690).

Do you see how rightly Minucius concludes from all these things that all things, collectively and individually, are governed by God’s Providence? For it makes lands naturally barren, scorched by the sun, and not moistened by rain fertile through the inundations of these rivers. In precisely the same manner, [Col. 0449C] he had previously shown that, through this divine Providence, the fertility of other parts of the world was wonderfully provided for by the regular succession of the four seasons of the year:

> “How great, moreover, is the providential care,” he says, “that autumn and spring have been inserted as temperate intervals, lest winter alone should burn with frost or summer alone scorch with heat, so that, as the returning year retraces its course, the transitions too may return unnoticed and harmless.”

There, moreover, he said, “Winter burns with frost,” just as Virgil wrote:

> . . . Or the piercing cold of Boreas burn.  
> (Virgil, *Georgics*, book I, v. 93.)

## ARTICLE II. Caecilius the pagan’s arguments against divine Providence, drawn from the prosperity of the wicked and the adversity of the good, are examined; with a discussion of the tyrants Dionysius and Phalaris, Rutilius, Socrates, Camillus, and the misery of the Jews and Christians

[Col. 0449D]

Caecilius raises several objections against divine Providence, some of which concern all human beings universally, others particular individuals, others the Gentiles, others the Jews, and still others the Christians. First, he asked, if God’s Providence exists, how is it that sacred and profane places, just like pious and impious people, are struck equally and indiscriminately by lightning and other accidental disasters? Why are the guilty as well as the innocent, the good and the wicked, overwhelmed without any distinction by storms and shipwrecks? Finally, why are the most wicked men so very often more prosperous and fortunate than the best?

[Col. 0450A] He then undertakes to prove by certain examples that it is false that the world is governed by divine Providence, or that provision is made for each and every thing. But it is better to hear Caecilius himself presenting them:

> “For if the world,” he says, “were ruled by divine Providence and by the authority of some deity, Phalaris and Dionysius would never have deserved a kingdom, Rutilius and Camillus would never have deserved exile, nor Socrates poison.”

By precisely the same argument, Cotta the Academic attempted in Cicero to undermine God’s Providence (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, book III [the printed “V” is evidently erroneous], p. 252, line 4 ff.). He treated as certain what Telamon repeatedly declared about the gods in this single verse:

> For if they cared, it would go well with the good—which now it does not.

That Academic endeavors to confirm this by the same examples as Caecilius and by several others, [Col. 0450B] although omitting Camillus. There he presses them as follows:

> “Why is my uncle, P. Rutilius, a man of perfect innocence and also of the greatest learning, in exile? . . . For thirty-eight years Dionysius was tyrant of a most wealthy and fortunate city. How many punishments before him did Pisistratus, Phalaris, and Apollodorus suffer amid the very flourishing of Greece, though they had first tortured and killed many? . . . What shall I say about Socrates, whose death makes me weep whenever I read Plato?”

Finally, after recounting many impious and sacrilegious deeds of Dionysius—later also noted by Valerius Maximus (book I, ch. 1, Foreign Examples, § 3)—he concludes his narrative with these words:

> “Thus neither did Olympian Jupiter strike him with lightning, nor did Aesculapius destroy him by wasting him away through a miserable and lingering disease. He died in his own bed, was borne to the pyre of Tympanidis, and handed on to his son as though just and legitimate, by way of inheritance, the power that he himself had obtained through crime” (Cicero, passage cited).

[Col. 0450C] Yet neither Caecilius nor the other Gentiles could conceal the fact that this Dionysius—whose various and extraordinary crimes both Cicero and Valerius Maximus also recount elsewhere—was utterly miserable amid such blessed and prosperous circumstances (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations*, book V, p. 186, line 3 ff.; Valerius Maximus, book IX, ch. 13, Foreign Examples, § 4). For Cicero himself recorded the following about him in another book:

> “What are we to think of that elder Dionysius? By what torment of fear was he habitually distressed, when, fearing the barber’s razor, he used to singe his own hair with a glowing coal?” [Col. 0450D] (Cicero, *On Duties*, book II, p. 377, line 17 ff.).

Valerius Maximus relates in the following manner how Dionysius paid the penalty for his crimes in another way:

> “Although he did not discharge the punishments he owed, nevertheless, after death, through the disgrace of his son, he paid the penalties that he had escaped while alive. For the divine wrath advances with a slow step toward its vengeance, and compensates for the delay of punishment by its severity” (Valerius Maximus, book I, ch. 1, Foreign Examples, § 5).

Concerning the death of Phalaris, Cicero handed down the following to posterity:

> “Phalaris is a witness, whose cruelty has become notorious above that of all others. He did not perish by treachery, as did the man whom I just mentioned, Alexander; nor at the hands of a few, as did this man of ours; rather, the entire multitude of the Agrigentines launched an attack against him.”

From Valerius Maximus [Col. 0451A] you may learn on whose advice and in what manner this tyrant was killed by the Agrigentines (Valerius Maximus, book III, ch. 3, Foreign Examples, § 2).

The story of P. Rutilius Rufus, whom Paterculus calls the best man not merely of his own age but of every age, is known to everyone (Paterculus, *History*, book II). For who does not know that he was driven into exile by a conspiracy of the tax-farmers, whose depredations he had restrained, although, as Cicero testifies, he was entirely innocent (Cicero, Oration II, *For Fonteius*, p. 227 ff., and Oration 37, *Against Piso*, p. 544)? Valerius Maximus speaks of him as follows:

> “What is more wretched than condemnation, what more wretched than exile? Yet when P. Rutilius was struck down by a conspiracy of the tax-farmers, they could not deprive him of his authority. When he made for Asia, all the cities of that province [Col. 0451B] sent envoys to meet him, awaiting his arrival among them. Would one more rightly call this exile or a triumph?” (Valerius Maximus, book II, ch. 10, § 5.)

Seneca said similar things more than once (Seneca, *Why Misfortunes Befall Good Men*, etc., ch. 3; *On Benefits*, ch. 17; and Letters 24 and 69). Nor should these verses of Ovid concerning the same Rutilius be omitted:

> And marvel at the stern resolve of great-souled Rutilius,  
> Who did not accept the offered terms of return.  
> Smyrna detained the man, not Pontus and an enemy land.  
> (Ovid, *Letters from Pontus*, book I, Elegy IV, v. 65.)

Finally, Vossius mentions him; if you wish to consult him, you will have no difficulty doing so (Vossius, *On the Greek Historians*, book I, ch. 22).

[Col. 0451C] The book of Plato concerning the death of Socrates, which Cicero testifies he never read without tears, is the very work entitled *Phaedo, or On the Soul*. At its conclusion Plato laments, with no less eloquence than grief, how Socrates ended his life after drinking poison (Plato, *Phaedo*, vol. I, p. 116 ff., and *Apology of Socrates*, vol. I). Concerning the killing of so great a philosopher, however, one may read his *Apology*, composed by Plato, Xenophon, and Apuleius, as well as the books and discussions of Seneca, Origen, Augustine, and many others (Xenophon, *Apology of Socrates*, p. 705, and *Memorabilia*, book II; Apuleius, *Apology of Socrates*; Laertius, *Life of Socrates*, book II, § 42; Seneca, *On Providence*, p. 272; *On the Happy Life*, book I, chs. 26 and 27; and Letter 74; Origen, *Against Celsus*, book II, p. 71; Augustine, *City of God*, book VIII, ch. 3, [Col. 0451D] p. 192; Laertius, passage cited; Origen, *Against Celsus*, book I, p. 5; Seneca, *On Providence*, ch. 3, p. 495). Moreover, the writers we have cited relate how false and fictitious were the charges for which he was condemned to death by utterly unjust judges. Lastly, Laertius and, later, Origen recorded that the Athenians repented of their unjust condemnation as soon as he was dead. Accordingly, Seneca asserts that this death was as glorious for him as the life of many others was infamous:

> “Do you judge Socrates badly treated because he drank the publicly mixed potion just as though it were a medicine of immortality, and discussed death right up to death itself? Was he badly dealt with because his blood [Col. 0452A] congealed and, as the cold gradually spread, the vitality of his veins ceased? How much more is he to be envied than those who are served from jeweled cups, for whom some effeminate youth, trained to submit to every abuse, melts snow suspended in gold? Whatever these men drink, they will measure out again in vomit, wretched and tasting their own bile once more; but he will drain the poison gladly and willingly.”

Concerning Camillus, finally, whom Caecilius—but not Cicero—mentions, you will find the following written by Valerius Maximus:

> “Furius Camillus, both the most joyful source of the growth of Roman power and its surest safeguard, was unable to protect his own security in the City whose safety he himself had established and whose prosperity he had increased. For L. Apuleius, tribune of the plebs, charged him with embezzlement of the booty of Veii, and he was sent into exile by harsh and, so to speak, [Col. 0452B] iron sentences. This happened, moreover, at the very time when he had been deprived of his excellent young son and ought rather to have been relieved by consolations than burdened with disasters. But his homeland, forgetful of so great a man’s immense services, joined the father’s condemnation to the son’s funeral. ‘Yet,’ he says, ‘the tribune of the plebs complained that fifteen thousand asses were missing from the treasury, for his penalty was fixed at that amount—an unworthy sum, on account of which the Roman people were deprived of such a leader’” (Valerius Maximus, book V, ch. 3, § 2).

Plutarch described these events at greater length; you may consult him, as also Seneca, Diodorus Siculus, Livy, Florus, and others (Plutarch, *Life of Camillus*, vol. I, p. 334 ff.; Seneca, *On Benefits*, book IV, ch. 27, and book V, ch. 17; Diodorus Siculus, *Library*, book XIV, p. 456; Livy, book V, § 32).

[Col. 0452C] Caecilius passes from the Gentiles to the Jews and Christians, and thus attempts to abolish divine Providence by their example:

> “I know indeed,” he said, “that one God is openly and publicly worshiped by the Jews. But what assistance or aid has he brought them? Is not that nation utterly miserable, since it and its God are captives of the Roman deities?”  
> (*cod. reg.* “names”; Rigault’s edition, “men.”)

He presses the Christians still more sharply:

> “Behold, the greater and better part of you, as you say, are needy, cold, and afflicted by toil and hunger; and God permits and disregards it. He either does not wish or is unable to assist his own; thus he is either powerless or unjust.”

“But these things,” he continues, “are few and are shared with others. In addition, however, we see that you always have evils peculiar and proper to yourselves: threats, punishments, tortures, [Col. 0452D] crosses, and fires. Where, then, is your God? Why does he not come to your aid and assist you?”

But not only Caecilius: all the pagans were accustomed to assail Christians with this very complaint and remonstrance. For, as Clement of Alexandria reports, they objected to them:

> Εἰ κήδεται ὑμῶν ὁ Θεὸς τὶ δήποτε διώκεσθε, καὶ φονεύεσθε; ἢ αὐτὸς ὑμᾶς εἰς τοῦτο ἐκδίδωσι;
>
> “If God cares for you, why are you persecuted and put to death? Or does he himself surrender you to this?” (Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, book IV, p. 504.)

And shortly afterward:

> Διατί δὲ οὐ βοηθεῖσθε διωκόμενοι . . . .
>
> “Why, then, when you are persecuted, is no assistance brought to you?” (*Ibid.*, p. 505.)

In Eusebius too, the Church of Lyons and Vienne wrote:

> Οἱ [Col. 0453A] δὲ ἐπιεικέστεροι, καὶ κατὰ ποσὸν συμπαθεῖν δοκοῦντες, ὠνείδιζον πολὺ, λέγοντες ποῦ ὁ Θεὸς αὐτῶν, καὶ τί αὐτοὺς ὤνησεν ἡ θρησκεία, ἣν καὶ πρὸ τῆς ἐαυτῶν εἵλοντο ψυχῆς;
>
> “Some who were rather more moderate, and who seemed in some measure to sympathize with our plight, repeatedly reproached us, saying: ‘Where is their God, and what benefit did they receive from this religion, which they preferred even to their own lives?’” (Eusebius, *History*, book V, ch. 2, p. 165.)

Thus Caecilius himself, as well as very many other Gentiles—especially Epicureans and Academics—used all these arguments, as we have shown from Cicero’s books *On the Nature of the Gods*, to attack divine Providence. By them they attempted to prove that God, if there is one, enjoys supreme repose and happiness, does nothing, is distracted by no occupations, undertakes no works, and [Col. 0453B] is blessed because he holds it as certain and established that he will always enjoy the greatest and eternal pleasures. Accordingly, they rejected the Providence of God asserted by the Stoics as though it were a prophetic old woman, and denied that God cared for anything or that the world was governed by any Providence of his. But they raised these objections as impiously as they did in defiance of the promptings of their own conscience, so that they might sin with impunity and do whatever they pleased. But let us hear Octavius, if you wish, refuting and overturning those various arguments.

## ARTICLE III. How Octavius weakens and demolishes Caecilius’s preceding arguments

Octavius answers the arguments raised against divine Providence by Caecilius with as much ease as solidity, and utterly demolishes them. [Col. 0453C] For you have already observed that the examples advanced by the pagans—the tyrants Phalaris and Dionysius, and also Socrates, whom they called the greatest and most admirable of philosophers—were overturned by the pagans themselves.

To the argument drawn from lightning bolts, which strike sacred and profane places, and pious and impious people, either with vain and ineffectual strokes or without any distinction, Octavius replies that we are so far from denying God on that account that, rather:

> “In all the movements of the world,” he says, “we behold his power always present: when it thunders, flashes, and hurls lightning, and when it clears the sky.”

For just as we recognize God and his omnipotent power in the serenity of the heavens, so too do we recognize them amid their greatest and most turbulent storms. And surely, although no one then sees God with bodily eyes, [Col. 0453D] who can doubt that the one whom he then fears and dreads is the first author of such great movements? Thunder therefore roars, Tertullian says, “so that human beings may be terrified,” and so that through this terror the wicked may return to a sounder mind and implore God’s mercy (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 17, p. 18). But we shall discuss Tertullian in his proper place.

Caecilius’s second argument against God’s Providence—repeated rather frequently and tediously by other pagans and atheists—is drawn from the prosperity of certain wicked people and the misery of the good. For how, they ask, could a God equally just and powerful have tolerated these things? But Minucius easily and clearly shows that only the person who knows the true [Col. 0454A] God, and serves and obeys him piously and faithfully, is happy. To weaken Caecilius’s argument, he generally employs the same reasons and words as Seneca, although Seneca was a pagan (Seneca, *Why Misfortunes Befall Good Men*, etc., p. 488 ff.).

First, therefore, he utterly denies that those who abound in riches or have attained the most exalted ranks of honor and dignity are happy. For they are raised higher so that they may fall more heavily; or, as Claudian sang:

> They are raised on high  
> So that they may fall with a heavier plunge.  
> (Claudian, *Against Rufinus*, book I.)

Moreover, like victims they are fattened for punishment and, like sacrificial animals, garlanded for execution. What is more, do not flatterers very often misuse the power of others through feigned blandishments [Col. 0454B] and obsequiousness?

Furthermore, is not the rich man, as Minucius says, “wrongly entrusted to fortune” and committed to her inconstancy? Is he not less enriched than burdened by his wealth, as though it were traveling money for completing life’s short journey? A king, moreover, is as much afraid as he is feared, and he alone stands exposed to danger. For Labienus, quoted by Seneca, rightly sang:

> He whom many fear must fear many.

The man who proclaims the nobility of his family praises not himself but his ancestors. Finally, the man who glories in the fasces and purple is sordid in mind. For all human beings, whether poor or rich, [Col. 0454C] are born with an equal lot and are distinguished by virtue alone.

Cyprian, in his Letter to Donatus, pursues this matter far more fully than our author (Cyprian, Letter 2 to Donatus, pp. 7–8). We do not venture to reproduce his rather lengthy discourse here in full, though you will doubtless not regret having read it. We shall therefore add what we find written more briefly by Apuleius:

> “Is he nobly born? You are praising his parents. Is he rich? I do not trust fortune. I count such things as disadvantages instead. Is he strong? He will be worn down by sickness. Is he swift? He will pass into old age. Is he handsome? Wait a little while, and he will not be” (Apuleius, *On the God of Socrates*, near the end).

See, if you wish, the book of Seneca cited by us, *On Providence, or Why Misfortunes Befall Good Men Although Providence Exists*, together with Muretus’s *Disputation* on this book [Col. 0454D] and the *Observations* of Morellius and others.

Minucius proceeds and similarly rejects the argument drawn from the poverty and adversities of upright people. For that poverty, he says, is neither disgrace nor misery, but glory and an opportunity to acquire and establish virtue. For “just as our spirit is weakened by luxury, so it is strengthened by frugality.” Moreover, amid want, the person who desires nothing is rich; conversely, the person who abounds in riches yet desires still more is poor. Accordingly, Pomponius Rufus said in Valerius Maximus:

> “He who desires nothing possesses everything, and more surely than one who possesses all things” (Valerius Maximus, book IV, ch. 4, preface).

[Col. 0455A] To this is added that no one, as Minucius says after Seneca, “lives so poor as he was born.” Seneca infers from this that poverty should be despised (Seneca, *On Providence*, ch. 6, p. 504). Then, so far as Christians are concerned, Minucius unhesitatingly declares that they could obtain riches and resources from God if these were useful to them, but that riches are disdained by them and innocence and piety preferred.

The point Caecilius presses from the utterly miserable condition of the Jews is of far less—or rather, of no—weight. For as long as they worshiped God piously and devoutly, and as long as they diligently obeyed his commandments, it is established that they enjoyed favorable circumstances and every kind of prosperity, accompanied by great miracles. But after they abandoned God through their impiety and [Col. 0455B] crimes, they were in turn abandoned by him and paid the penalties owed for their impiety and crimes. Hear, I ask you, the words of Minucius:

> “As long as they worshiped chastely, innocently, and devoutly, and as long as they obeyed his saving precepts, from a few they became innumerable, from needy they became rich, and from servants they became kings. Though few, they overwhelmed many; though unarmed, they overwhelmed the armed; while fleeing, they overwhelmed their pursuers, at God’s command and with the elements assisting them. . . . Thus you will understand that they first deserted him before they were deserted; that they were not taken captive together with their God, but were surrendered by God as deserters from his discipline.”

Neither Caecilius nor anyone else could reply that Minucius had fabricated this without foundation, since it was handed down to everlasting remembrance in the writings of these same Jews and is read everywhere throughout them. For they wrote nothing in them against themselves [Col. 0455C] and their own ancestors except what they could neither suppress nor conceal. Minucius adds that the Jews were all the more guilty because all these events had been foretold in those same books long before they occurred.

Moreover, he names two witnesses explicitly: Josephus and Antonius Julianus. The former was certainly beyond all suspicion in this matter. For he was a Jew, and anyone can very easily read the matters reported by Minucius in the books that he wrote about his own nation and the Jewish War, works that are in everyone’s hands. The latter too, as Minucius indicates quite plainly, had published commentaries concerning the Jews. But we lost them so long ago that scarcely anyone besides our author can be found who remembers either those works or Julianus himself. [Col. 0455D] Yet no one who is not altogether incredulous can deny that the things advanced by our Minucius are true.

If anyone more exacting than Caecilius should demand that another witness be cited to him in place of Julianus, we shall immediately give him Tertullian, whose words are as follows:

> “So great was the favor enjoyed by the Jews before God, on account of the extraordinary righteousness and faith of their original ancestors, that among them both the greatness of their nation and the elevation of their kingdom flourished; and so great was their happiness that they were forewarned by God’s utterances how to merit God’s favor and not offend him. Yet how greatly they sinned—swollen with confidence in their fathers and deviating from his discipline in a profane manner—even if they themselves would not confess it, their present condition would prove. Scattered, wandering, exiles from both their heaven [Col. 0456A] and their land, they roam the world without man, without God as king; they are not permitted, even under the law accorded to strangers, so much as to greet their native land with the sole of their foot” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. I, p. 20).

You will find the same things in Cyprian, who, excerpting this entire passage of Tertullian, sometimes borrowed his very words as well (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 15). But these matters must be treated more fully in another of our dissertations.

Caecilius nevertheless insisted that God’s Providence was proclaimed in vain, since God permits upright people to be afflicted with every kind of adversity without punishment, and especially permits Christians to be tormented, mangled, and killed by the greatest and most exquisite tortures. Minucius admits that this is indeed true, but contends that these things are the surest indications not only [Col. 0456B] of God’s Providence, but also of his compassion—indeed, of his love and benevolence. For God, as he says after Seneca, tests and examines each person in adversity; or, to use Seneca’s own words:

> “Those whom God approves and loves he hardens, scrutinizes, and trains. But those toward whom he seems indulgent, whom he seems to spare, he keeps soft for evils still to come. For you are mistaken if you believe anyone exempted. His own share will come to the person who has long been fortunate: whoever seems to have been released has merely been deferred” (Seneca, *On Providence*, ch. 4, p. 498).

Accordingly, just as gold is tested by fire, say both Seneca and Minucius, so a brave, pious, and upright man is tested by miseries and calamities. He may therefore appear wretched, but in reality he is not wretched. For all bodily evils and torments [Col. 0456C] are not punishments but military service, and quite often the training of virtue—or, as Seneca puts it, “It is not cruelty; it is a contest,” and an opportunity for virtue (*Ibid.*, ch. 5, p. 501; ch. 3, p. 494; ch. 4, p. 498). Minucius further adds what is confirmed not only by the same Seneca but also by Plato and Xenophon: that without the exercise of labor our powers become altogether sluggish (Plato, *Theaetetus*, vol. I, p. 153; Xenophon, *Expedition of Cyrus*, book VII, p. 198).

Above all others, moreover, we praise Seneca here and have quoted his words because Minucius drew many things from him, as we showed above. On all these matters you may also read Cicero’s *Paradoxes*; Valerius Maximus, book IV, ch. 4, *In Praise of Poverty*; Justin Martyr, *Apology* 76, [Col. 0456D] p. 43 ff.; Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, book IV, p. 504 ff.; Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 41; Cyprian, treatise *On Mortality*, p. 158; Arnobius, *Against the Gentiles*, book II, p. 97; Lactantius, book V, chs. 21–22, p. 529 ff.; Ambrose, Sermon 11 on Psalm 118, p. 1110 ff., and *On Jacob and the Happy Life*, book II, ch. 9, p. 473 ff.; Augustine, *City of God*, ch. 29, p. 27; Theodoret, Oration 6, *On Providence*, p. 378 ff., and *Cure of the Greek Maladies*, Discourse 6, likewise *On Providence*, p. 573 ff.; and very many others. Meanwhile, however, let us follow our author as he turns this feeble weapon of the pagans back against them with the utmost force.

## ARTICLE IV. How Octavius answered Caecilius’s argument drawn from the sufferings and death of the martyrs; with an exposition of the examples of Scaevola, Regulus, and Aquilius, and a discussion of the great number of martyrs and the necessity of divine grace for undergoing martyrdom

[Col. 0457A]

Minucius proceeds and clearly shows Caecilius and the other pagans that the cruelest tortures of the Christian martyrs not only do no injury to God’s Providence, but commend it in a truly wonderful manner. For he is so far from regarding a Christian who is mangled by the most dreadful agonies and torments for professing his religion, and who gives up his life amid them, as miserable [Col. 0457B] and unfortunate, that he considers him a spectacle worthy of God. For is not that most holy martyr worthy of the gracious gaze of the supreme Majesty, and most acceptable to him, who, because of his public confession of the Christian faith, has endured innumerable and exceedingly atrocious punishments and, after finally shedding his blood, triumphs over executioners and tyrants?

Certainly no pagan, however obstinate, can rightly deny this if he has read Seneca’s words concerning the brave man:

> “Behold a spectacle worthy of God’s looking upon while intent upon his work. Behold an encounter worthy of God: a brave man matched against evil fortune, especially if he himself challenged it. I see, I say, nothing on earth more beautiful for Jupiter to behold, should he wish to turn his attention to it, than Cato standing upright amid the ruins of the commonwealth, although his party had already been [Col. 0457C] shattered more than once. . . . It is clear to me that the gods watched with great joy when that man, the sternest avenger of himself, provided for the safety of others and organized the escape of those departing; when even on his final night he pursued his studies; when he drove the sword into his sacred breast, scattered his entrails, and drew forth with his own hand that most holy soul, unworthy to be defiled by steel” (Seneca, *On Providence*, ch. 2, p. 492).

If Seneca could say these things about a brave pagan man placed amid so many adversities and uncertain of his lot after this life, what ought he to have said of a Christian martyr, who knows with absolute certainty that by his death he will attain eternal happiness?

Lest Caecilius or some other pagan object [Col. 0457D] that this was indeed Seneca’s opinion, or that of some Stoic philosopher, but not the opinion of others, Minucius presses his case further and sets before the eyes of Caecilius and the rest the examples of Mucius Scaevola, Regulus, and Aquilius. Although they appeared afflicted by calamity, all the pagans nevertheless extolled them to heaven with the highest praises because of their unconquered steadfastness amid the most bitter torments.

Minucius says nothing of the punishments endured by Aquilius and Regulus, doubtless because in his time they were unknown to no one. But lest they perhaps be unknown to someone, we shall recount them briefly. Concerning the ordeal of Mucius Scaevola, Arnobius added the following in the same place:

> “Since he had mistaken his target in attacking the king, he would have perished among the enemy had he not sacrificed his right hand.”

[Col. 0458A] For when Porsenna, king of the Etruscans, was besieging Rome in a severe and protracted war, Scaevola boldly entered the enemy camp without any hope of safety, as Cicero says, and there inadvertently killed the king’s secretary instead of the king himself, whom he intended to kill (Cicero, *Paradoxes*, p. 434). Consequently, when captured and brought before the king’s tribunal, he fearlessly thrust his right hand into a fire kindled for sacrifice. Terrified by such boldness, Porsenna granted him his freedom and released him (Livy, Decade I, book II, p. 22 ff.). We have, however, discussed this deed of his elsewhere.

Marcus Atilius Regulus, whom our author later mentions, was consul for the second time during the First Punic War. He was captured by enemies whom he had crushed and defeated in various disasters, and was sent [Col. 0458B] to Rome to negotiate an exchange of prisoners after swearing that he would return:

> “When he arrived,” says Cicero, “he advised in the senate that the captives should not be returned. Then, although his relatives and friends tried to detain him, he nevertheless preferred to return to certain punishment rather than violate the pledge he had given the enemy” (Cicero, *On Duties*, book I, p. 356, line 16 ff.).

Accordingly, after returning to them, he was killed by sleep deprivation and starvation. See the same Cicero, *On Ends*, book V, p. 112, and especially *On Duties*, book III, p. 401 ff.; Valerius Maximus, book I, ch. 1, § 14, p. 13; Eutropius, *Roman History*, book II; Aurelius Victor, *On Illustrious Men*, p. 605; Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book VI, ch. 4; Florus, book II, ch. 2; Seneca, *On Providence*, ch. 13, p. 272; Augustine, *City of God*, book I, ch. 13, p. 15; ch. 24, p. 23; book II, ch. 23, p. 52; and book III, ch. 18, p. 76. [Col. 0458C] On both Scaevola and Regulus, see Cicero, *On Duties*, book III, p. 252; Lactantius, book V, ch. 13, p. 496; Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, and *To the Martyrs*, ch. 4; and others. We shall note only one point: these writers held varying opinions about the punishments inflicted upon Regulus and the manner of his execution.

Moreover, the more frequent the mention of this Regulus and Mucius Scaevola among various writers, the rarer is the mention of Aquilius, whom our author records in the same place. For this reason some suppose that a copyist erred in writing his name and that *Atilius* or *Acilius* should be read there instead of *Aquilius*. After making this emendation, they believe this to be the man whom Dionysius of Halicarnassus called Ἀτύλιον (*Atylion*), or rather, as they prefer, Ἀκύλιον (*Akylion*) (Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Antiquities*, book III, p. 259 ff.). [Col. 0458D] But these conjectures are futile and vain. For Minucius is speaking about entirely innocent men who were killed by various kinds of exceedingly dreadful punishment. Atilius, however, according to the same Dionysius of Halicarnassus, paid the penalties deserved by his crime.

What, I ask you, prevents us from saying that Minucius is speaking of Marcius Aquilius, whom Appian describes as having been delivered over by Mithridates to a most cruel death in these words?

> “Not long afterward he took Manius Aquilius, the principal instigator of this embassy and war, and carried him around bound upon an ass, compelling him to proclaim in his own voice before the spectators that he was Marcius, until at Pergamum he poured molten gold into his mouth, reproaching the Roman [Col. 0459A] name for the corruptions caused by bribery” (Appian, *The Mithridatic War*, well after the beginning, p. 123).

Pliny likewise says:

> “And now, to the disgrace not of any single Roman citizen but of the Roman name as a whole, King Mithridates poured gold into the mouth of the captured commander Aquilius” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXXIII, ch. 3, p. 32).
