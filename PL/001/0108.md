[Col. 1016C] Moreover, the perversity of the pagans was all the greater because they could very easily and readily investigate and ascertain whether those crimes attributed to the Christians were true. “The origin of that teaching,” says Tertullian—that is, the beginning of this Christian teaching disseminated throughout the world—“dates from Tiberius” (ibid.); or, as he states more explicitly in another book: “Under the emperor Augustus this name, ‘Christians,’ arose; under Tiberius its teaching dawned.” Yet certainly, because the Christian religion is true, “it began together with hatred of itself”—these too are his own words—“as soon as it appeared, it was an enemy.” In our manuscript codices, however, the reading is “it is an enemy”; that is, from the very time when it began and appeared to the world, it was hostile and hateful to everyone; and “it had as many enemies as there were outsiders” (the same author, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 307), that is, absolutely everyone except the Christians themselves. For, as Tertullian further says, [Col. 1016D] their enemies were, first, “the Jews, from rivalry peculiar to themselves,” or from their envy of them, with which they were always inflamed because Christ had abolished their ancient rites; then “the soldiers, from extortion” (the same author, *Apology*, chap. 7, p. 308), which, as some understand it, means through the insidious accusations of men driven to frenzy, but, more properly, through cruel harassment; and finally even “the Christians’ own household members, by their very nature” (ibid.), since, impatient with their own condition, they customarily plotted against their masters and never ceased attempting to harm them.

“We are therefore besieged by all these persons every day,” Tertullian continues; “every day we are betrayed; very often we are seized in our very assemblies [Col. 1017A] and congregations” (ibid.); or, as he said elsewhere in addressing the pagans themselves: “You know even the days of our gatherings; and so we are both besieged and seized, and detained in our most secret congregations” (the same author, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 568). The pagans therefore could not have remained unaware of what Christians were accustomed to do in them.

But who, our Author argues (Tertullian, *Apology*, loc. cit.), ever came upon an infant wailing in this fashion while it was being killed by Christians? Who, after finding the Christians eating its flesh and drinking its blood, preserved for the judge to inspect their bloody mouths—like those of Cyclopes and Sirens—not even wiped clean? Who ever discovered upon their wives any filthy [Col. 1017B] traces of the adultery committed a little earlier in those same assemblies (ibid.)? In another book he pressed these points in other words as follows: “Who ever came upon a child half eaten? Who detected marks of teeth upon the bloodstained bread? Who, when sudden light burst into the darkness, observed anything filthy—to say nothing of incestuous?” (the same author, *To the Nations*, book I, loc. cit.). In the *Apology*, however, he alluded to the twofold fable of the Cyclopes and the Sirens. For both Greek poets, such as Homer (Homer, *Odyssey* VIII, past the middle), and Latin poets, such as Virgil (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book III, v. 617 ff.), represented the Cyclopes as one-eyed men who devoured their guests. No less familiar is the fable of the Sirens, who lulled sailors with the sweetness of their song and cast them into the sea (Pliny, *Natural History*, book X, chap. 49, p. 441).

[Col. 1017C] Tertullian therefore argues as follows: the Christians had as many enemies as there were pagans, Jews, soldiers, and household servants, by whom they were daily besieged and seized even in their own synaxes. If, therefore, they had perpetrated so many and such horrible crimes in them, they could very easily have been detected by so many enemies so fiercely hostile to them. And yet, out of the almost countless number of those enemies down to that time, not one had ever discovered the slightest trace of any crime. Therefore the charges brought against the Christians by their wicked slanderers were utterly false (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 569).

Nor could these men, however shameless, reply that witnesses to these crimes had ever been corrupted by the Christians with money or payment. For, says Tertullian, “who, after discovering such deeds, [Col. 1017D] concealed or sold them, even while dragging the persons themselves away?” (the same author, *Apology*, chap. 7, p. 308). That is, it could not have happened that any pagan, after learning of these crimes of the Christians, either concealed them of his own accord or, while dragging the Christians before the tribunals of the judges, sold his knowledge for some price and then became completely silent. In another book, however, he expresses this in other—and consider whether clearer—words as follows: “If by payment we prevail upon pagans who have witnessed our crimes to prevent us from being brought before the public and condemned as the persons they actually know us to be, why are we seized when no one has accused or convicted us? Can we not, after corrupting the witnesses with gold, also avoid being brought to trial altogether? For who, whether witness or judge, sells the betrayal”—that is, the disclosure and accusation—[Col. 1018A] “of some horrible crime without the crime itself, which might at some later time be discovered and punished by another; or who, being privy to it, buys back the secret?” (the same author, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 568).

Thus it had never been proved by anyone’s testimony that Christians were guilty of these crimes. For who, as Tertullian insists, could ever have disclosed them? Certainly not the Christians themselves. For if it was unlawful for pagans to disclose their Samothracian and Eleusinian rites, how much more were Christians bound to keep silent about their divine mysteries (Tertullian, *Apology*, loc. cit.; and *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 568)? If, therefore, they were concealed by the Christians themselves in the profoundest silence, “whence,” asks Tertullian, “did foreign pagans obtain knowledge of them, when even pious initiations always exclude the profane and dispense with witnesses, unless the impious have less fear?” [Col. 1018B] (ibid., loc. cit.). In our better manuscript codices and in most of the early editions, we read “even impious initiations.” And this is perhaps not a bad reading. For Tertullian could thus have argued, as they say, from the lesser to the greater: if the impious initiations of the pagans must be concealed, how much more the sacred and pious initiations of the Christians—unless impious pagans, by divulging them, had less reason to fear for themselves than Christians did.

Our Author presses the matter further, and demonstrates by the following argument that these execrable crimes could neither be committed nor believed by anyone. Christians who lived piously were promised a blessed eternity after this life. But what Christian, or what person about to be initiated into the Christian religion, could ever have been persuaded that it might be obtained by so many and such great crimes? [Col. 1018C] Could even one pagan who was not wholly insane ever have believed a man, even one of the highest authority, who said such things—or, if you prefer, swore to them—and with such ready credulity have allowed himself to be polluted by such atrocious abominations? Yet if pagans can neither believe nor perpetrate these things, how much less can Christians, whose nature is no different from that of other human beings (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 8, p. 312; and *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 569)? For, says Tertullian, “they are not Cycopennae or Sciapods”; or, as he writes in another book, “some Cynopennae, or Sciapods, or some antipodes from beneath the earth” (the same author, loc. cit., pp. 312 and 569). The first of these, if Ctesias, whom Pliny cites, is to be believed, “were called Monocoli,” he says, “and had a single leg, with marvelous speed in leaping; they were also called Sciapods because, in the greater heat, they lay upon the ground on their backs [Col. 1018D] and protected themselves with the shadow of their feet” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book VII, chap. 2, p. 11). On these see also Solinus (Solinus, *Polyhistor*, chap. 52, p. 80), Augustine (Augustine, *City of God*, book XVI, chap. 8, p. 422), Suidas (Suidas, under V. Σκιάποδες), and Apollodorus as cited by Tzetzes (Tzetzes, *Chiliades* 7, v. 662 ff.). The Cynopennae, however, are called “Cyclopes” in some printed editions and “Cynophanes” in others, but contrary to the authority of our manuscript codices. Vossius thinks that *Cynopanes* should be read, in Greek κυνοπάνες, “horned dogs” (Vossius, notes on Mela, *On the Situation of the World*, book III, chap. 9). Salmasius, however, approves the reading *Cynopennae* found in those same codices of ours, deriving it from the Greek word κυνοπεῖναι: “as though,” he says, “suffering from canine hunger” (Salmasius, notes on Solinus, [Col. 1019A] p. 1005). This certainly suits Tertullian’s purpose, since he is there speaking of Christians who were falsely accused by the pagans of eating the flesh of a murdered infant and suffering from hunger worse than a dog’s. But who these beings were is of little importance to know. For our Author is speaking of monsters which everyone freely acknowledges to have been entirely fictitious.

What, then, someone may ask: did he regard the antipodes also as monsters of this kind? Yet no mention of them is found in the *Apology*, but only in the book *To the Nations*. Did he not name them there because he himself, like many others whom we have named elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 21, art. 6), had persuaded himself that there could be no antipodes unless they walked, as was said, with their feet turned opposite to ours? Certainly [Col. 1019B] they are rejected by him as nothing other than monstrous, false, and fictitious men in these words: “Or some persons sprung up like mushrooms from the earth beneath the ground, who possess a nature different from that of the people of our earth.” But Christians were far removed from the savagery of these and other monsters; they differed from other human beings like ourselves only in surpassing them in humanity and charity. They could therefore never have been induced, under any appearance whatsoever of piety and religion, to eat the flesh of an infant whom they themselves had killed, and afterward to pollute themselves with fornications, adulteries, and incest.

The pagans, nevertheless, struck blind by obstinacy, insisted that either the Christians, being excessively credulous, or at least novices in their religion had been deceived and deluded. But nothing more foolish or absurd could be imagined. For it was the custom among Christians that those about to be initiated should be taught by the father of the sacred rites—that is, by the bishop or one of his deputies—what they must do to prepare for those rites, or what was to be done in them. But with what effrontery could a bishop or anyone else have said to a novice or a Christian: “When you come to the sacred synaxes, you will need an infant, still tender, one who knows nothing of death, who will smile beneath your knife, with which you will cut his throat; likewise bread, with which you may collect the juicy blood”—or, as it is better expressed in another book, “the fresh blood” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 8, p. 313; and *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 569). “You will also need dogs which, after a morsel has been thrown to them, will extinguish the lights; and finally you will need your sister and your mother, with whom in the midst of the darkness you will commit sexual violation.” But tell us, please: with what ears could any Christian [Col. 1019D] or novice have listened to these things? Could he do so without horror? Ought he not at least to have feared the laws, in which, as Cicero observes, it had been ordained: “Let the pontiffs punish incest with the ultimate penalty” (Cicero, *On the Laws*, book II, p. 335, line 15)? But if he thought he could evade this penalty through the Christians’ deception or influence, how was it that, when questioned by pagan judges, he preferred to be tortured with the cruelest torments and killed rather than confess crimes against which his conscience utterly rebelled? Such things are plainly credible to no sane person, nor will anyone ever be persuaded that they could have occurred.

The pagans insisted that they had learned by report that the Christians were in fact guilty of the aforesaid crimes. But, Tertullian replies, this answer is utterly foolish [Col. 1020A] and futile (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 7, p. 309; and *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7, p. 566). For they could not deny the truth of what the most celebrated poet sang:

> Rumor—an evil swifter than any other.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book IV, v. 174.)

For rumor is merely the name given to an uncertain matter, and it very often arises either from a disposition toward rivalry—that is, envy—or at the prompting of suspicion, meaning a suspicion invented without cause, or from the pleasure of lying. Arising, therefore, from so corrupt a source and from the mouth of only one person, it generally lies; or, if it ever reports anything true, it even then adds something, subtracts something, or alters something. It remains vigorous only so long as it proves nothing. For once the matter is proved, rumor ceases by that very fact and disappears. [Col. 1020B] A wise man therefore rightly places no trust in it, and all the less when it alone is the informer and messenger of wholly secret crimes which no person in possession of his faculties either dares or is able to commit. We have observed elsewhere that this reply of Tertullian is fully confirmed by Minucius Felix in fewer, indeed, but no less effective words (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 8, art. 2). Finally, Tertullian added that rumor, as swift as it is mendacious, ought not to be believed, but that the truth must be investigated, because, as a proverb current among the pagans declared, “Time reveals all things” (Tertullian, ibid., p. 310). Aulus Gellius (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book XII, chap. 11) cited this adage as celebrated in the following verses of Sophocles:

[Col. 1020C] Πρὸς ταῦτα, κρύπτε μηδὲν, ὡς ἅπανθ` ὁρῶν

Καὶ πάντ` ἀκούων, πάντ` ἀναπτύσσει χρόνος.

> Moreover, nothing is hidden, since time, seeing all  
> And hearing all, reveals everything.

Seneca, however, says: “Some falsehoods bear the appearance of truth. Time must always be allowed: the day discloses the truth” (Seneca, *On Anger*, book II, chap. 540). Erasmus, however, assembles more material concerning this proverb, and you may consult him (Erasmus, *Adages*, chiliad 2, century 4, adage 17). But what announces falsehoods under the appearance of truth more frequently than rumor? The pagans therefore ought not to have relied, in harassing the Christians, upon its testimony alone, which, if not false, was at least uncertain; rather, they ought to have allowed time for the Christians’ case to be examined and the truth ascertained.

## ARTICLE II. How invincibly Tertullian demonstrates that the pagans were themselves guilty of the homicide of which they falsely accused the Christians, since in Africa they sacrificed infants to Saturn down to the proconsulship of Tiberius, and since this custom continued in secret, although Saturn’s priests, suspended from the trees that shaded his temple, paid the penalty with death, as attested by the soldiery of our native country

[Col. 1020D]

Nothing more shameless can be conceived than falsely to accuse another of a most grievous crime of which the accuser himself is openly proved guilty. Yet such were the pagans, who most shamelessly charged the Christians with infanticide and incest. For “this very thing,” as Tertullian says in addressing them, [Col. 1021A] “is perhaps what led you to believe other things about us” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9, p. 314)—namely, that the far more grievous crimes we have recounted were committed by us. But he deliberately added “perhaps” there, because he had said shortly before that the crimes with which they charged the Christians were so atrocious that scarcely any person could have believed them, unless they themselves had done something similar. First, however, he shows in various ways that they were murderers, because “in Africa,” as he says, “infants were openly sacrificed to Saturn down to the proconsulship of Tiberius, who exposed those very priests upon the same trees that shaded their temple, upon crosses consecrated to their crimes, as is attested by the soldiery of our native country, which carried out that very task for that proconsul” (ibid. ff.). Concerning this detestable sacrifice of infants, contrary to every law of nature itself, [Col. 1021B] we have made many observations in our earlier dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 9, art. 2; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 34, art. 3). It remains, therefore, for us now to examine individually those points peculiar to our Tertullian.

First, then, he relates that this most cruel custom persisted down to the proconsulship of Tiberius. Some, however, have suspected that the passage should read “down to the proconsul of Tiberius”; that is, some proconsul either sent by the emperor Tiberius or himself surnamed Tiberius, who was entirely different from and later than the emperor Tiberius. But since this emendation, whatever its worth, was insufficient, they added another, and thought that what followed should be read “as attested by the military service of our father”; that is, as they conjectured, Tertullian’s father, [Col. 1021C] who was proconsul after the death of Tiberius Caesar. But they first ought to have proved that Tertullian’s father bore Tiberius as his personal name. Moreover, all the codices, both printed and manuscript, contradict this conjecture.

But what prevents us from believing that the emperor Tiberius prohibited that most dreadful sacrifice of infants in Africa? Did not Pliny record that in the year 647 from the foundation of the City, when Gnaeus Lentulus and Publius Licinius Crassus were consuls, a senatorial decree forbade the sacrifice of a human being? But because this most savage custom nevertheless persisted afterward among the Gauls, “the principate of Tiberius Caesar,” he says, “abolished their Druids. . . . . . [Col. 1021D] Nor can it adequately be estimated how much is owed to the Romans, who abolished monstrosities in which it was considered most religious to kill a human being, and even most beneficial to eat one” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXX, chap. 1, p. 728). Do you hear that in this most impious sacrifice the pagans not only killed human beings but even ate their flesh? Yet Tiberius Caesar is said to have abolished it in Gaul and elsewhere. Why, then, should he not also have done so in Africa, as Tertullian so expressly declares? If, moreover, a similar example can contribute anything toward confirming this opinion, Josephus supplies one for us (Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities*, book XVIII, chap. 4, p. 622). For he attests that the same Tiberius demolished the temple of Isis and had its statue cast into the Tiber because Paulina, a noblewoman, had been violated in that temple of Isis through the wicked artifices of the priests by the young Decius Mundus, who was pretending to be the god Anubis. [Col. 1022A] Why, then, would he have spared the still more horrible sacrifice of infants to Saturn in Africa, rather than in Gaul, as we have said, and in other regions?

But by what reasoning, you will ask, did Tertullian say “down to the proconsulship of Tiberius”? Had Tiberius been proconsul in Africa before he held the government of the Roman Empire? But what, I ask you, prevents him from having been appointed proconsul of Africa and sent there by Augustus Caesar, or even earlier? If, however, someone should properly prove that Tertullian must be understood as referring to another Tiberius, a proconsul of Africa, we shall readily agree with him.

Tertullian further added that the most cruel priests of Saturn were, by command of the same Tiberius, exposed upon the trees of that temple which had shaded so many crimes. [Col. 1022B] For trees were planted around pagan temples and consecrated to the particular god of each temple. Passing over other witnesses, Apuleius recorded the following concerning a certain shrine: “She sees splendid offerings and strips of cloth embroidered with gold, fastened to the branches of trees and to the doorposts, which, together with an acknowledgment of the favor received, bore witness to the name of the goddess to whom they had been dedicated” (Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, book VI, shortly after the beginning, p. 92). You see, therefore, that gifts were hung upon these trees and that the name of the god to whom the temple was dedicated was affixed to them. But from these trees that shaded Saturn’s temple, says our Author, were made votive and consecrated crosses upon which those most inhuman priests were suspended and compelled to end their lives by strangulation. Whether this is the true meaning of Tertullian’s words, [Col. 1022C] which have exercised the ingenuity of scholars to no small degree, I should like you to consider, or else to offer us a better interpretation. Yet you will doubtless acknowledge with us that some light may be shed upon this passage by the following verses of Ausonius, in which he sang of Love suspended from the trunk of a myrtle:

> In the mournful grove the best-known myrtle is chosen,  
> Hateful because of a god’s punishments. There once Proserpina,  
> Scorned by Adonis, tormented him in remembrance of Venus.  
> Upon the lofty trunk of this tree they suspend Love,  
> His hands bound behind his back and bonds tightened  
> Around his feet; as he grieves, they afflict him with punishment  
> Without restraint.  
> (Ausonius, *Idyll* 2, v. 55 ff.)

Furthermore, lest anyone should doubt the punishment inflicted upon those priests of Saturn, our Author calls as witness the soldiery of his native country, which “carried out,” he says, “that very task for the proconsul Tiberius” (Tertullian, *Apology*, [Col. 1022D] chap. 9, p. 315). By these words he doubtless means the soldiers of Africa who were then serving under the proconsul Tiberius and, while performing their duty, saw the priests suspended and strangled upon those trees. The pagans were indeed terrified by their example, but not entirely overcome. For they no longer dared openly to sacrifice infants to Saturn; “but now,” says Tertullian, “this sacred crime continues in secret” (the same author, ibid.). Thus, armed against their innocent children under the appearance of false piety, they did not cease to slaughter them secretly and in concealment, in contempt of the laws of their emperors. And so Saturn, “who did not spare his own children,” as our Author says and as we have seen elsewhere (ibid.; and *Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, [Col. 1023A] chap. 9, art. 2), did not abandon his former cruelty, but desired the children of other men still to be sacrificed to him.

Therefore, even if Christians had killed some infant in their synaxes—although this was utterly false—they ought not on that account to have been accused and condemned by pagans who, with the most savage inhumanity, sacrificed their own children as victims which they believed most pleasing to Saturn.

## ARTICLE III. It is shown that the Gauls were likewise guilty of homicide, since they sacrificed human beings to Mercury; as were the Taurians, who sacrificed them to Diana; likewise the Romans, who in the city of the descendants of Aeneas drenched Jupiter with human blood at their games; and others, who wrung the breath from their newborn children in water or exposed them to a death by no means entirely uncertain; and how greatly Christians abhorred every homicide, since they were not even permitted to destroy what had been conceived in the womb while the blood was still being formed into a human being

[Col. 1023B]

Tertullian proceeds and demonstrates that the pagans were guilty of parricide and homicide. For, he says, persons “older than infants were also slaughtered in the Gauls’ rites to Mercury”; and he repeats this in another book (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9, p. 316; and *Scorpiace*, chap. 7). We have made many observations elsewhere concerning this utterly execrable sacrifice (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 9, art. 2), where we also explained more clearly what he immediately adds: [Col. 1023C] “I leave the Taurian fables to their own theaters” (ibid.); or, as he himself says in another book: “Nothing glows there but savagery, namely that which furnished the tales about the sacrifices of the Taurians” (Tertullian, loc. cit.; and *Against Marcion*, book I, chap. 1), who, of course, sacrificed their guests to Diana.

He continues: “Behold, in that most religious city of the pious descendants of Aeneas there is indeed a Jupiter”—namely, Jupiter Latiaris—“whom at their circus and gladiatorial games they drench with human blood.” Elsewhere we read that he wrote: “In Latium, down to the present day, human blood is given to Jupiter to taste in the midst of the City; yet no one reconsiders the matter, or fails either to assume some reason for it or to presume upon the inestimable will of his god” (the same author, *Apology*, chap. 9, p. 317; *Scorpiace*, chap. 7). Who does not see that by the names “most religious city” [Col. 1023D] and “pious descendants of Aeneas,” he designates the Romans with considerable irony? For they boasted that they derived their origin from Aeneas, whom Virgil everywhere calls “pious father.” But who could have been more impious and less religious than those Romans who, at games and spectacles consecrated to Jupiter Latiaris, their “best and greatest” god, watched with eyes not merely dry but joyful as gladiators killed one another, or were horribly torn apart and killed by the beasts against which they fought? We have discussed elsewhere both this cruel homicide and this cruel spectacle (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 8, art. 2).

The pagans replied that blood was indeed shed on those occasions, [Col. 1024A] but it was the blood of men who were either beast-fighters or condemned criminals put to death (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9, p. 321). The former, as they said, were the vilest of men, who hired themselves out for profit and voluntarily offered their services to kill beasts. The latter, however, were compelled, because of their crimes, as will be said below, to fight against beasts by sentence of the judges. Therefore, the pagans said, there was nothing wrong in the slaughter of such men.

But Tertullian easily and quickly overturns that empty evasion (ibid.). For the beast-fighter, however vile and however much the lowest dregs of society, is nevertheless no less a human being on that account, and his life is taken by the same homicide as that of a richer man. But if a criminal has been condemned to death, certainly the more wicked he is, the more unworthy he is to be sacrificed to Jupiter; or, to use Tertullian’s own words: [Col. 1024B] “It is all the more shameful because it is the blood of an evil man” (ibid.) that is poured out in honor of the highest god, as they called him. For this reason Tertullian there exclaimed, not without wit, in mockery of the pagans and their Jupiter: “O Christian Jupiter!” (ibid.). For Jupiter was the cause and author of the homicide of which Christians were falsely accused, and by that title alone was he the son of his father Saturn, who had devoured his children—the son, that is, of his father’s cruelty, to which his own was similar.

Tertullian presses further and asserts that the crime of those who killed their newborn children was greater than that homicide. Would you like to hear him pressing and addressing the pagans? Here are his very words: “Surely it is more cruel when you wring out their breath in water, or expose them to cold, hunger, and dogs.” But you will ask why he calls extinguishing the breath of infants in water a more cruel kind of death. [Col. 1024C] Some reply that philosophers, joined by Servius, taught that the human soul is fiery and is therefore extinguished in water, or reduced to nothing, with greater difficulty (Servius on Virgil’s *Aeneid*, book I, v. 95). They think that this can be proved to be truly the cruelest of all deaths from the following verses of Ovid:

> I do not fear death; it is the manner of death that is pitiable:  
> Remove shipwreck, and death will be a gift to me.  
> (Ovid, *Tristia*, book I, elegy 2, v. 57 ff.)

But the poet sang this because of his desire for burial, of which one who died by shipwreck would be deprived. As for the philosophers, although some believed the human soul to be fire, others certainly thought it to be water, others a body, others breath, and others something else, as we have shown elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 23, art. 2). [Col. 1024D] Moreover, Tertullian did not have one philosopher’s opinion in view rather than another’s.

Thus, in our judgment, there is little substance in the answer of those who, by investigating the rationale of Tertullian’s words more curiously than was proper, looked for a knot in a bulrush. For he speaks of infants whose breath is extinguished in water in no other manner than he speaks of those exposed to cold, hunger, and dogs. He maintains that the killing of all these infants is more cruel than that of other persons because infants exposed to certain death were entirely deprived of every means of defense by which others might protect themselves, and therefore were killed more cruelly than older persons. That this is Tertullian’s meaning is indicated with sufficient clarity [Col. 1025A] by his following words: “For even a more advanced age would choose to die by the sword” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9, p. 319). For it would prefer to be killed by a single stroke of the sword rather than in the prolonged and lingering manner inflicted upon those pitiable infants. We have discussed this exposure of infants, and the force of this argument, in our dissertation on Minucius Felix’s *Octavius* and in the passages cited there (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 9, art. 12).

Moreover, every kind of homicide was so strictly forbidden to Christians that, in Tertullian’s words, “they were not even permitted to destroy what had been conceived in the womb while the blood was still being formed into a human being” (Tertullian, loc. cit.); that is, while the fetus in the mother’s womb had not yet received human form. For not all the members of the human body are formed at the same time, nor is the soul immediately infused into the fetus, but only many days after conception, as the philosophers believe and as we have shown elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 27, art. 2; and chap. 40, art. 6). [Col. 1025B] Yet if it was unlawful for Christians to induce abortions of this kind, how much more unlawful was it for them to kill any human beings! Who, then, would not be indignant that they were so rashly charged with homicide by pagans who so frequently killed other human beings with the utmost cruelty, as has already been said and remains to be said?

## ARTICLE IV. How certainly Tertullian asserts that human blood was drunk by pagans at public games dedicated to Jupiter, by those who sought a remedy for epilepsy, and by others who, like Catiline, entered into a compact with certain persons; likewise that in Africa those consecrated to Bellona were marked by drinking blood, and that others committed a crime still more execrable than these

[Col. 1025C]

With the same evident proof by which Tertullian demonstrated that nothing was more false than the charge that Christians drank the blood of an infant killed while they celebrated their sacred mysteries, he shows with equal certainty that pagans were in fact guilty of so great a crime. For at the Latin Games, which we discussed in the preceding article, “they drench him,” says Tertullian, “with human blood” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9, p. 317); or, as he writes in another book: “In Latium, down to the present day, human blood is given to Jupiter to taste in the midst of the city of Rome” (*Scorpiace*, chap. 7). The unknown author of the book *On the Public Shows*, printed after the works of Cyprian, [Col. 1025D] describes how that impious sacrifice was customarily performed: “What need is there,” he says, “to pursue the matter further, or to describe the monstrous kinds of sacrifices at the games? Among these, a human being is sometimes made the victim through the murderous robbery of a priest, when blood, still hot, is received from his throat into a bowl and, while it yet boils, is cruelly cast into the face of the idol as though offered for it to drink” (author of the book *On the Public Shows*). We have discussed this sacrifice, as absurd as it was cruel, in our dissertation on Minucius’ *Octavius* (*Dissertation on Minucius*, chap. 9, arts. 3 and 4), where we also dealt with those who, when suffering from epilepsy, sought a cure by drinking the blood of men killed at these spectacles. For these persons, as Tertullian says, “[Col. 1026A] at a public show in the arena carry away with greedy thirst the fresh blood of criminals whose throats have been cut, treating epilepsy” (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9, p. 321). Who, unless savage and ironhearted, would not shudder to preserve his own life by such a remedy and by the cruel death of other human beings?

Not far removed from their inhumanity were those savage peoples who struck and confirmed compacts among themselves by drinking blood drawn from their own limbs. Tertullian cites Herodotus as a witness to this plainly barbarous custom in the following words: “Read whether it is not reported somewhere—by Herodotus, I think—that certain nations contracted an alliance by tasting blood drawn from one another’s arms” (ibid. ff.). By these words, “by Herodotus, I think,” he indicates clearly enough that he was quoting the matter from memory, [Col. 1026B] and so was not entirely certain whether it had been recorded by Herodotus himself or by another writer. Nevertheless, he could certainly have cited Herodotus, for in his first book he relates the following about the Lydians: Ὃρκια δὲ ποιέεται ταῦτα τὰ ἔθνεα, τὰ πέρ τε ἕλληνες, καὶ πρὸς τούτοισι ἐπεὰν τοὺς βραχίονας ἐπιτάμωνται ἐς τὴν αἰμοῤῥοίην, τὸ αἷμα ἀναλείχουσι ἀλλήλων. “These nations make compacts with one another, both in other respects according to the custom of the Greeks and by bloodying their arms with self-inflicted wounds and licking one another’s blood” (Herodotus, book I, § 74). In another book, however, he says of the Scythians: Όρκια δὲ, ποιεονται Σκύθαι ὧδε πρὸς τῶν ἂν ποιέωνται· ἐς κύλικα μεγάλην κεραμίνην οἶνον ἐγχέαντες, αἷμα συμμίσγουσι τῶν τὰ ὅρκια ταμνομένων, τύψαντες ὑπέατι, ἣ ἐπιταμόντες μαχαίρῃ σμικρὸν τοῦ σώματος· καὶ ἔπειτα ἀποβάψαντες ἐς τὴν κύλικα ἀκινάκεα [Col. 1026C] καὶ οἰστοὺς, καὶ σάγαριν, καὶ ἀκόντιον· ἐπεὰν δὲ ταῦτα ποιήσωσι, κατεύχονται πόλλα, καὶ ἔπειτα ἀποπίνουσι αὐτοί τε οἱ τὸ ὅρκιον ποιευμένοι, καὶ τῶν ἐπομένων οἱ πλείστου ἄξιοι. “The Scythians make compacts with anyone in the following manner: after wine has been poured into a large earthenware cup, they mingle with it the blood of those making the compact, striking with a knife or cutting a small part of the body with a sword. They then dip into the cup a scimitar, arrows, an axe, and a javelin. When they have done this, they bind themselves by many words of imprecation; afterward, not only those who have made the compact drink the wine, but also the companions who hold the highest rank” (the same author, book IV, § 70).

Tertullian could therefore have invoked this ancient writer without hesitation as an authority for his position, and with all the greater right because he has Lucian as a supporting witness [Col. 1026D] (Lucian, *Dialogue Toxaris, or On Friendship*, p. 630). For Lucian attests that the Scythians allowed blood from their cut fingers to drip into a cup, dipped the points of their swords into it, and drank together; and that in this manner they established a compact which no one was thereafter permitted to dissolve. Concerning certain peoples neighboring the Axiacae, Pomponius Mela recorded the following: “They love wars and slaughter; and it is the custom of warriors to drink, directly from his wounds, the blood of the first man they kill. . . . . . Even their compacts are not bloodless. Those who make the agreement wound themselves; when they have mixed the blood thus drawn, they taste it. They regard this as the surest pledge of enduring fidelity” (Pomponius Mela, *On the Situation of the World*, book II, chap. 1, p. 27).

[Col. 1027A] In addition, Cornelius Tacitus, when speaking of the compact between Rhadamistus and Mithridates, says: “It is the custom of kings, whenever they enter into an alliance, to clasp right hands, bind their thumbs together, and fasten them tightly with a knot. Then, when the blood has flowed into the extremities, they draw blood with a slight cut and lick it from one another. This compact is held secret, as though consecrated by their mutual blood” (Cornelius Tacitus, *Annals*, book XII, past the middle).

Why say more? Valerius Maximus writes the following about Sariaster, or Tigranes the Younger, who deserted his father and went over to Pompey the Great, and then defected from Pompey back to his father: “Sariaster formed an agreement with his friends against his father Tigranes, king of Armenia, in such a manner that they all drew blood from their right hands and drank it from one another” (Valerius [Col. 1027B] Maximus, book IX, chap. 11, foreign examples, § 3).
