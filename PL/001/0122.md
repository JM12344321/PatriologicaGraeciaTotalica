## CHAPTER XIII

[Note: [Col. 1167C] Title in the Ambrosian manuscript: “On the Washing of Hands.”]  
[Note: [Col. 1167B] Chapter XI in other editions.]

[Col. 1167B]

Moreover [Note: [Col. 1167C] *Moreover, what reason is there?* Here he teaches that hands are not to be washed with water but kept clean from wicked deeds. G. PANCIROL.—*Moreover, what reason is there?* etc. The washing of the hands, and sometimes indeed of the whole body, customarily preceded prayer not only among Christians but also among pagans and Jews. Some evidently believed that, once the body had been washed and especially the hands cleansed—which generally contract dirt more readily—the soul too was washed and purified from the stains of guilt. But both reason and religion counseled that a person ought to approach God with a pure and purified soul. I shall prove that this custom prevailed among the gentiles by citing the most ancient witness of all. Homer, namely, presents Penelope to us in the *Odyssey* washing herself with water before her prayers. Her son Telemachus likewise:

> Ξεῖρας νιψάμενος πολιῆς ἁλὸς εὔχετ᾽ Ἀθήνῃ.

“Having washed his hands in the gray sea, he prayed to Athena.”

“His hands washed in the sea, he thus adores Pallas.” [Col. 1167D]

Macrobius, moreover, says in *Saturnalia*, book III, chapter 1: “It is established that one about to perform sacrifices to the gods above is purified by washing the body.” In the same place Macrobius supplies other examples of this rite from Virgil. Lactantius Firmianus likewise ridicules those same pagans who suppose that they have sacrificed piously if they have washed their skin, as though any rivers could wash away the lusts enclosed within the breast, or any seas purify them. Pseudo-Aristeas, in his book *On the Seventy Translators*, attests that the Jews had the same custom: “After consulting the king,” he says, “they withdraw to various places and, as is the custom of all Jews, first wash their hands in the sea and then pour out prayers to God.” See Spencer, *De Legibus Hebraeorum*, book III, dissertation 3, chapter 3.

This custom afterward passed to Christ’s faithful, and perhaps some believed it had been commanded by the Apostle himself in 1 Timothy, chapter II, where he orders us χεῖρας ὁσίους ἐπαίρειν, “to lift pure hands” to heaven, as though the Apostle were speaking also of the external purity and washing of the hands as a sign of inward purity. > Gleaming in the holy court, a fountain flows with water for the servants  
> And washes the hands of those entering . . . .

Chrysostom in several places, and especially in homily [Col. 1168C] 57, volume V, page 390, attests to the same fact in these words:

First of all, it must be noted that those who understand *modulantes* here as though it meant “singing” or “chanting psalms” are seriously mistaken. For since the subject is private prayer, which Tertullian says should be performed with a subdued and lowered voice, there was no need for musical melodies. We interpret *modulatum* as “image,” namely the figure or posture of a person praying. We therefore “extend the form derived from the Lord’s Passion”; that is, in prayer we do not merely raise our hands but also extend the pattern, the model—or, as Tertullian says, the *modulatum*—which our Lord Jesus Christ displayed when his hands were extended upon the cross. This rite is still observed among us today when the Lord’s Prayer is recited in public worship. In praying thus we confess Christ, because during prayer we profess by that very gesture that we are Christians, conforming our body to the figure of Christ crucified. Tertullian himself says in book I of *To the Nations*: [Col. 1170C] “If you set a man upright with his hands extended, you will have made the image of a cross.” Maximus of Turin, homily II *On the Lord’s Cross*: “Even the very posture of a man, when he lifts his hands, depicts a cross. We are therefore commanded to pray with hands raised, so that by the very gesture of our limbs we may confess the Lord’s Passion.” What Maximus calls “by the very gesture of our limbs,” Tertullian would have called “by the very configuration of the body.” So too in *On the Soul*, chapter 32, where he discusses the transference of souls into other bodies, he says: “Nor are they equal to the dimensions of bodies”; that is, they do not correspond to their measure, size, or capacity.
