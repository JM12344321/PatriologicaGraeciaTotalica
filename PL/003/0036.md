[Col. 0276A] Thus Caecilius spoke, and then, smiling—for the vehemence of his outpouring had relaxed the swelling of his indignation—said: “Does Octavius dare make any reply to this—a man of Plautine lineage, foremost among millers, but last among philosophers?”

“Refrain,” I said, “from applauding him; for you have no right to exult in the elegance of your discourse before both sides have argued their cases more fully, especially since your debate rests not upon praise but upon truth. And although your speech, with its subtle variety, has delighted me exceedingly, I am nevertheless more deeply troubled—not by the present pleading, but by the whole manner of conducting debate—because the standing even of manifest truth is generally altered according to the abilities of the disputants and the power of their eloquence.

“It is well known that this happens through the pliability of audiences: while the enticement of words distracts them from attention to the facts, they assent indiscriminately to everything that is said and do not distinguish falsehood from truth, [18] unaware [Col. 0276B] that truth may be found even in the incredible and [Col. 0277A] falsehood in the plausible. Accordingly, the more often they trust confident assertions, the more frequently they are refuted by those of greater expertise, and so are continually deceived by their own rashness. They transfer the judge’s fault into a complaint about uncertainty, preferring, after condemning everything, to suspend judgment universally rather than to pass judgment upon deceptive claims.

“We must therefore take care that we do not repeatedly become afflicted with hatred for every discourse, as many of the more simple-minded are carried away into loathing and hatred of humankind. For in their incautious credulity they are deceived by those whom they believed good; presently, through a similar error, they become suspicious of everyone and fear as wicked even those whom they might have recognized as excellent.

“We, therefore, are apprehensive because in every matter arguments are presented on both sides, and because on the one side the truth is generally obscure, while on the other [Col. 0277B] there is remarkable subtlety, which sometimes, through copious eloquence, [Col. 0278A] imitates the credibility of demonstrated proof. Let us weigh each point as carefully as possible, so that we may indeed praise ingenuity, but choose, approve, and embrace what is right.”

## CHAPTER XV

> **Note: [Col. 0278C]**
>
> **CHAPTER XV.—“If he can,” etc.** The Royal manuscript has *reputare* (“reconsider”). The passage does not appear sound; perhaps it was written: “Since Octavius has every point whole and unimpaired, if he can refute them. That which,” etc. HERALDUS.—I would punctuate it: “. . . has every point, if he can, to refute,” that is, “has points that must be refuted.” Unless perhaps the author used *habere* (“to have”) for *debere* (“to have to”), or—as I should prefer—we ought to read *debeat*. WOPKENS.—A more careful examination of this passage has led me to the following correction: “If he can, [Col. 0278D] he will refute the accusations which, unless I am mistaken, I have set forth in a summary for our common benefit.” Caecilius means those accusations that he had thus far directed against the Christians. “These accusations,” he says, “I have set forth in a common summary,” that is, “I have recited them briefly and concisely, thinking this would be equally convenient for you and for me.” HEUMANNUS.—For my part, I understand this passage thus: “If Octavius can refute what you, Caecilius, allege”—that is, what you charge as a crime against the Christians—“I have produced [or: benefited] a common summary for the three of us.” Yet the reading is not so perplexed that no meaning at all can be hammered out of it. It certainly seems to me capable of the following explanation: “If Octavius—who would have a more just cause for being angry with me—can reconsider those things, however diverse, that were said to you in chapter 16, even after my rather lengthy interruption, during which others might perhaps have forgotten the objections to which he had to respond; what you accuse me of and count as my fault, by declaring it a very serious injustice for me to weaken the force of your case through the interruption of a most weighty discussion, I have brought forward for our common benefit in summary form.” [Col. 0279B] The word *τὸ* (“the”)—I repeat—certainly shows that the discourse beginning with “if he can” belongs to Marcus Minucius Felix. The discourse would nevertheless be clearer if *inquam* (“I said”) were placed after *reputare*. LINDNER.
>
> **“In complete silence.”** I restore *tuto* (“safely”). Thus above, chapter XIII, §3: “safe doubt.” HEUMANNUS. But doubt is safe because of the danger of error. Yet what danger is there for the man who breaks silence with a word? Here *totus* means “entire” or “unbroken.” Thus below, chapter XXIII, §6: “We should have more gods than entire human beings”; and XXVIII, §8: “You consecrate entire asses in their stables along with your Epona.” Velleius Paterculus II.109.2: “And, because his rivalry was poorly concealed, he acted entirely as a rival.” LINDNER.
>
> **“Already eager.”** Eager to speak, that is. RIGALTIUS.—Showing by bodily movement that he was about to speak; for *gestire* properly means to indicate one’s feeling by a bodily gesture, as Donatus observes on Terence’s *Eunuch*, act III, scene V, line 7. Hence Cicero says oratorically in *For Marcellus*, chapter 3: “By heaven, as it seems to me, even the walls of this senate-house [Col. 0279C] are eager to give you thanks.” DAVIS.

**ARGUMENT.—**Caecilius retorts to Minucius, not without some indication that his feelings have been wounded, that Minucius is abandoning the duty of a conscientious judge by weakening the force of his argument. Octavius remains entirely free to refute everything Caecilius had brought forward.

“You are departing,” said Caecilius, “from the duty of a conscientious judge; for it is a very serious injustice for you to weaken the force of my case by interrupting a most weighty argument, when Octavius has every point whole [Col. 0278B] and unimpaired. If he can reconsider them—”

“What you charge me with,” I said, “I have, unless I am mistaken, brought forward in summary form for our common benefit, so that by scrupulous examination we may free our judgment from dependence, not upon the swelling of eloquence, but upon the solidity of the facts themselves. Nor need attention be distracted any longer, as you complain, [19] since in complete silence we may hear the reply of our friend Januarius, who is already eager to speak.” [Col. 0279A]

## CHAPTER XVI

> **Note: [Col. 0279C]**
>
> **CHAPTER XVI.—“As I shall be able, according to my powers.”** Joseph Wouwer suspects the first two words of being a gloss, and Heumannus the last two. LINDNER.—They do so unjustifiably, in my opinion, since African writers not infrequently duplicate and combine phrases of the same meaning. Arnobius, book II, p. 42: “Everywhere, without distinctions of any person,” where the latter words explain the former, as Desiderius Heraldus observed. So too book VII, p. 225: “And they ordered it to be among your customary practices, as part of your provisions.” Certainly the words printed in roman type could be removed with equal justification. DAVIS. Thus Florus II.2.5: “Immediately and without delay”; compare II.3.1. Likewise II.8.12: “Immediately and on the spot.” Cicero, *Letters*, VIII.4.4: “Because Caesar despised him not moderately . . . but greatly.” LINDNER.
>
> **“You must strive together with me.”** The expression is ambiguous: is Minucius or Caecilius to strive? But every ambiguity could have been removed by the speaker’s expression and gesture, if Octavius said this while turned toward Caecilius. This certainly appears to have happened. RIGALTIUS.—On the contrary, nothing seems more certain to me than that Minucius is meant; for apart from the fact that he spoke last, so that the discourse must necessarily refer to him, to whom but a Christian is it appropriate to wash away, with a stream of truthful words, the most bitter stain of the abuse that Caecilius had poured forth? Everyone, unless I am mistaken, will agree with me and acknowledge that Rigaltius slipped. DAVIS.
>
> **“Truthful words in the light.”** *In lumine* (“in the light”) is written in the Royal manuscript and the Roman edition. Gelenius had published *lumine*. Others conjecture *flumine* (“in the stream”). HERALDUS.—Fulvius Ursinus reads *flumine*, and correctly. For, first, a stain is washed away not in light but in a stream or water or some other liquid. Seneca, *Hippolytus* 890: “Our blood will wash away this stain upon our honor.” Second, a “stream of speech” or “of words” is well known among the best writers; by contrast, the “lights of speech” mean something different. Third, the nature of the verb *diluamus* (“let us wash away”) requires this; for colors and odors are *diluted* in a quite different sense. Fourth, there can be no doubt that he had in mind Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, book II, chapter [Col. 0280B] 7: “The faults exposed by criticism are washed away by the stream of eloquence.” “The most bitter stain of abuse” means “the stain of most bitter abuse.” LINDNER.
>
> **“Thus the judgment of my Natalis . . . .”** He addresses Caecilius Natalis in indirect discourse. RIGALTIUS.—Valesius wonders whether this Caecilius Natalis, who was converted to faith in Christ by Octavius’s disputation, is the same Natalis mentioned by the Roman Gaius in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 28. “The name,” he says, “and the time and profession certainly agree.”
>
> **“Wavered.”** Cicero uses this highly appropriate verb in *On the Nature of the Gods* I.43: “He seems to waver concerning the nature of the gods.” Martianus Capella, book I, p. 9:
>
> > An anxious judgment wavers amid troubled circumstances.
>
> So too Juvencus spoke of the “wavering step of faith” in his *History of the Gospels*, book III, p. 80; see also p. 93. Franciscus Juretus, however, in his note on book II of Ambrose’s *Against Symmachus*, p. [Col. 0280C] 330, restores from the Roman edition *mutasse* (“had changed”), that is, “that his opinion had changed.” It cannot indeed be denied that the ancients spoke in this manner, as Jacobus Palmerius also shows in his *Spicilegium on Sallust*, p. 300. But since the received reading is much more elegant, I should think that the alternative crept in through a typographical error, especially because those who carefully examined the sole manuscript do not record this variant at all. DAVIS.
>
> **“Whether your learning.”** Thus Heraldus and Rigaltius published from the Royal manuscript. It is an interchange of grammatical persons, by which indirect discourse is changed into direct discourse, as at Acts 1:4; this happens especially when the person addressed is present, as here in Minucius. GRONOVIUS. Thus Caesar, *Civil War* II.32: “After dismissing the council, Curio summons an assembly of the soldiers and reminds them of the zeal Caesar had received from them at Corfinium.” And shortly afterward: “‘For you and your deed,’ he says, ‘all the towns thereafter have followed.’” See Berger, *On the Natural Beauty of Oratory*, p. 208. Cellarius and others read *sua* (“his”), but even so it violates the rule governing reflexives, since it ought to be *ejus*, namely, Caecilius’s. LINDNER.
>
> **“Whether your learning was disturbed.”** Or read *fucata sit* (“was disguised”). Chapter XVI, §9: “It is not disguised by a display of eloquence and charm.” For learning that is disguised by cunning, subtlety, and deceit is contrasted with learning that wavers through error. Octavius recognizes the latter innocent kind, not the former crafty kind, in his Natalis. For it is characteristic of fraud to shift one’s position, so that the aim of the response may be founded upon a proposition’s uncertainty and rendered still more uncertain. But one who, bewildered at a crossroads, neither ventures to choose any single road nor to approve all of them, wavers through error. Or, as I prefer, mentally supply “through design and intention,” so that there is a proper contrast between learning disturbed by design and learning wavering through error. LINDNER.
>
> **“Or had wavered through error.”** Pagan error, that is. By almost transposing the letters, one might write *an cavillaverit* (“or had quibbled”). Thus guilty persons are said *cavillare* or *cavillari* when they do not make themselves available, but frustrate proceedings and evade them. In an old glossary *vacillator* (“waverer”) is defined as *συκοφάντης* (“false accuser, pettifogger”). RIGALTIUS.
>
> **“He varied.”** Livy XXVII.27: “If I wished to pursue everything in which the authorities vary concerning Marcellus’s [Col. 0281A] death.” In the same place: “Tradition varies in such a way that most authorities nevertheless relate,” etc. The same author, II.57: “Timon and anger alternately caused their judgments to vary.” Ovid, *Metamorphoses* XV.648: “Opinion is divided and varies.” From this it is apparent that Wopkens, *Miscellaneous Critical Observations*, vol. X, book I, p. 29, unjustly restores *declaravit* (“declared”) in place of *variavit*. HEUMANNUS.
>
> **“The more uncertain aim of our response.”** The manuscript reads *certio* for *certior*, since scribes were not accustomed to repeat the same letter, as is frequently evident from the Pandects. GRONOVIUS.—I add that *in* was omitted because it had already occurred immediately before in *in incerto propositionis* (“in the uncertainty of the proposition”), and I agree with Rigaltius, who restored the negative that had fallen out and that is required by Octavius’s meaning. Heumannus reads *nec certius* (“and no more certainly”), though that is scarcely plausible. LINDNER.—I have restored the negative compound that had fallen out and is necessary to Octavius’s meaning. For he says that Caecilius, through subtle wavering born of pagan error, sometimes believes in gods and sometimes doubts whether any exist, so that through this variation and the uncertainty of his proposition—or through his uncertain proposition—he renders uncertain the foundation of Octavius’s response. Octavius will shortly call this cunning. RIGALTIUS.—I do not agree with Rigaltius, though the manuscript reading does not appear entirely sound either; for, unless I am mistaken, it is wholly contradictory: “SO THAT upon the uncertainty of the proposition the surer aim of our response might be founded.” If the situation were otherwise, the variation of propositions ought to suggest stupidity, not cunning. These difficulties will be avoided [Col. 0281C] if only we read: “LEST upon the uncertainty of the proposition the surer aim of our response might be founded.” These particles are frequently confused by copyists. Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, book II, chapter 1: “SO THAT it may imitate that over which it ought to rule.” Other manuscripts have: “LEST it . . . rule,” etc. DAVIS.
>
> **“In my Natalis.”** I write “to my Natalis.” MEURSIUS.—Write *Natale*. Thus the ablative of *Juvenalis* and *Martialis* is *Juvenale*, *Martiale*. See Vossius, *On Analogy*, book II, chapter 11, p. 295. On a certain Roman consul, Gaius Bellicius Natalis, see Gorius, *Annotations on the Florentine Inscriptions*, part I, p. 259. Valesius thought he had found our Caecilius Natalis in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 28; but he rightly left the question undecided. HEUMANNUS.
>
> **“I will not have it; I do not believe it.”** Heumannus deletes the latter phrase as a gloss, because *nolo* (“I will not have it”) would mean the same as “I do not believe it; I do not maintain it.” Yet they are distinct. I will not have him guilty of cunning, insofar as I hope every good thing of him; and even if someone should affirm the contrary as emphatically as possible, I still do not believe it. LINDNER.
>
> **[Col. 0281D] “Simplicity.”** On this sense of *simplicitas*, see Jean Le Clerc on Albinovanus Pedo, *Elegy* II.22. Sigismund Gelenius, Laurentius Barrensis, and others indeed printed it thus—*simplicity* in place of the manuscript’s *subtlety, simplicity*—and the conjectures Nicolaus Rigaltius ventured upon this passage, writing “simple subtlety,” are by no means attractive. A drowsy copyist wrote an empty word but did not think it should be deleted, lest he disfigure the manuscript with erasures; this is the origin of many errors still remaining, as I showed on Caesar, *Gallic War* II.19. I shall now add a notable example from Theophilus of Antioch. In book II *To Autolycus*, p. 104B, he speaks as follows:
>
> > “And the two shall become one flesh,” something that is itself shown to be fulfilled in our own case. For who, when marrying lawfully, does not disregard mother and father and every relation and everyone else?
>
> These words disturb the sense in that place and are repeated shortly afterward; they must therefore be deleted altogether. Read instead:
>
> > And at the same time God made his wife, also so that their mutual affection might be greater,
>
> etc. The repetition of the same word caused certain material to be omitted; when the copyist noticed this, he inserted that material, but did not delete what remained, lest he mar the beauty of the book. DAVIS.—Jacobus Gronovius should also be noted; he restores *sinceritate* (“sincerity”) in place of *subtilitate* (“subtlety”). HEUMANNUS.
>
> **“Branches apart.”** This has correctly been restored in place of *diffunditur* (“spreads apart”). Our Felix undoubtedly had before his eyes Virgil, *Aeneid* V.540:
>
> > Where the road divides into two branches.
>
> Nor does Heraldus’s *diffinditur* (“is split apart”) displease me. Rigaltius and Davis, however, as well as Jacobus Gronovius, follow a blind guide when they defend *diffunditur* on the authority of that ancient copyist. In the same manuscript, after *diffunditur*, these three words are added: “because he does not know the road.” The critics were entirely right to reject this gloss, nor should Wopkens be heard when he is eager to restore “because he does not know the true road.” HEUMANNUS.—Since the author says “is scattered” in the consequent clause, “spreads apart” is also more appropriate in the antecedent. Pomponius Mela III.3: “The sea, wandering and widely spread, is scattered in the form of rivers.” GRONOVIUS.—Cicero, *On Invention* I.52: “Enumeration is the device by which matters stated separately and diffusely are collected into a single place.” Pliny XII.5: “In India the fig tree spreads out in enormous branches.” LINDNER.—Although “because he did not know the road” has the flavor of a gloss and appears to have flowed from the preceding words, “one who does not know the right road,” such a pleonasm can nevertheless be tolerated in our author, since it explains why the man remains at a loss. THE EDITORS.
>
> **“Seethes and is tossed by waves.”** According to Heraldus and Rigaltius, the manuscript has “is tossed in the tide and is driven by waves.” Meursius correctly restored it thus: “is tossed, seethes, and is driven by waves.” Cicero spoke of “seething with doubt” in *Against Verres*, book IV, chapter 30. HEUMANNUS.—To describe Caecilius as remaining in uncertainty of mind and revealing this in his ill-fated indignation, the author employs a sufficiently pleasing gradation. He first says “is tossed,” which is familiar enough but admits a great range of variation and therefore does not adequately express what he wished. Hence he adds, secondly, “seethes,” something ordinary in the Ocean but stronger than the preceding term. A third word is therefore added, “is driven by waves,” which, though itself familiar enough, certainly embraces the most violent agitations of storms, which are by no means perpetual. GRONOVIUS.
>
> **“Although the things said are diverse.”** The writing of the old book has been disturbed: “Although the diverse things that have been said have been confirmed and proved by one truth.” RIGALTIUS.—From this disturbed writing of the old book, it seems to me that the true reading can be gathered. I therefore think it should be restored as follows: “I shall convict and refute the diverse things that have been said, once a single truth has been confirmed and proved. Thus thereafter he will neither have to doubt nor wander.” With this correction admitted, the sentences cohere well; but if we retain the common reading, it becomes mere sand without lime. DAVIS.—With Davis I delete *sint* (“are”), which is found neither in the manuscript nor in the Roman edition. HEUMANNUS.—Yet *sint* does occur after *probataque*, although not in its proper place, as Rigaltius perceived very well; hence the critics rightly inserted it after *diversa*. LINDNER.
>
> **“Once a single truth has been confirmed and proved.”** Supply “nevertheless”: “nevertheless, once a single truth has been confirmed and proved,” etc. The preceding particle *quamvis* (“although”) requires it. Ovid, *Tristia* I.1.25:
>
> > And do not presume to defend them, although you will be assailed by words.
>
> That is: “Nevertheless, take care not to defend them.” Horace, *Odes* III.10.13:
>
> > O, although neither gifts nor prayers,  
> > Nor the violet-tinged pallor of lovers  
> > . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .  
> > Bends you, spare  
> > Your suppliants.
>
> That is: “nevertheless spare them.” Lucretius IV.157:
>
> > And although suddenly . . . whatever  
> > Object you place before a mirror, its image nevertheless appears.
>
> Because either Davis or Heumannus failed to observe this, they complained that the common reading did not cohere properly with the sentence, and therefore restored—Davis: “Thus thereafter he will neither have to doubt,” etc.; Heumannus: “So that he need not doubt.” LINDNER.
>
> **[Col. 0283C] “Let him know that all.”** Gelenius correctly published this, and it is so written in the Royal manuscript. The Roman edition has *sciet* (“he will know”). HERALDUS.
>
> **“Without distinction.”** Thus the Royal manuscript and the Roman edition, not *dilectu*. LINDNER.
>
> **“Were created capable and fit.”** There is no need to read with Meursius “were created to be capable,” since the infinitive *esse* (“to be”) is often understood in this manner. Thus our author above, chapter V: “Everything in human affairs [is] doubtful, uncertain, suspended, and more plausible than true.” Chapter VIII: “asserting that there [are] no gods.” Chapter IX: “he tells fabulous stories that their rites [are such].” Chapter X: “he declares that they [are] true.” Chapter XI: “Although I should grant that they [are] righteous.” Chapter XVII: “They maintain that it [was] perfected not by divine reason.” Section 13: “The beauty of our form acknowledges that God [is] its craftsman.” Chapter XVIII: “Those who maintain that Jupiter [is] supreme.” Chapter XIX: “Democritus says that intelligence [is] god.” In the same place: “He argued that reason [is] God”; “by interpreting Juno [as] air”; “we know that there [is] a God”; and six hundred further examples. LINDNER.
>
> **“But that wisdom is implanted in them by nature.”** I think we must read: “but that they possess it implanted in them.” Cicero, *On Ends* IV.2: “They also possess a certain implanted—or rather innate—desire.” The same author, *For Fonteius* 23.1: “To possess implanted enmities.” Or else read *insitam esse* (“is implanted”), which is the opinion of the distinguished Grüner. Scheffer thought that before the word *sapientiam* (“wisdom”) some words had fallen out that could not easily be supplied by conjecture. Wopkens wishes *indutos* (“clothed with”) to be restored in place of *insitos* (“implanted”). Heumannus thinks the following the true correction: “Nor is wisdom given by fortune, but is implanted by nature.” Others prefer *indeptos esse* (“have obtained”); others, *inditam esse* (“has been instilled”). Amid this medley of opinions, one may choose whatever correction yields good sense without straying far from the form of the letters or violating the rules of the language. LINDNER.
>
> **“To look up to.”** Thus it stands in the manuscript. The Roman edition has the same reading, *suspicere*. Compare chapter XIV. LINDNER.
>
> **“Our own people.”** Heumannus says, “I restore *contra* (‘on the contrary’),” though he does not have my agreement. For the word *nostratium* denotes parties to which each person is devoted, and sects. In this passage it embraces both the Jews and the early Christians, who devised wisdom, because from them the Gentiles, philosophers, and poets drew their own teachings. Kortholt demonstrates this in his notes on Justin, *First Apology*, p. 92, and Athenagoras, *Apology*, section 21, p. 94, note 1. LINDNER.
>
> **“By means.”** That is, “in proportion to their means.” Plautus, *Persian* IV.4.13: “This is given for sixty minae.” The same author, *The Ass-Dealer* I.3.20: “If it is given for money.” LINDNER.
>
> **“If anyone.”** The Roman edition has *quaecumque* (“whatever things”). Wrongly. HERALDUS.
>
> **“Should inquire, think, and declare.”** Davis restores *censeat* (“should form a judgment”). Wrongly, however; for “to think” and “to declare” are here contrasted with one another. It is neither pleasant nor permissible to declare everything we think. “Rare is the happiness of times,” says Tacitus, *Histories* I.1, “when one may say what one thinks.” HEUMANNUS.—This passage leads me to a correction that, though perhaps not entirely necessary, is nevertheless not forced. I read the passage thus: “If anyone should inquire into divine things and declare what he thinks.” The word *quae* (“what”) could have fallen out because of the preceding *quaerat*, which begins with *quae*. Ablancourt translates no differently: *S’enquérir des choses divines, et en vouloir dire ce qui leur en semble*—“To inquire into divine things and to wish to say what one thinks about them.” For this was chiefly what Caecilius had censured in the Christians: that they made definite pronouncements about the sum of things and the divine majesty, chapter V, and thus declared what they thought, whereas philosophers who had devoted themselves entirely to that study spoke only doubtfully and said that they wished to continue deliberating. Unless perhaps one prefers to supply by syllepsis: “If anyone should inquire concerning divine things, think [divine thoughts], and declare [divine things].” LINDNER.
>
> **“By this it is clearer.”** This can nowhere be said correctly. Yet the recent Ouzel edition, following Rigaltius, prints it so. The Roman, Sabaeus, Wouwer, Elmenhorst, and both of Heraldus’s editions, however, have: “By this the reasoning is clearer.” GRONOVIUS.

**ARGUMENT.—**Octavius therefore begins his reply and expresses confidence that he will wash away the most bitter stain of Caecilius’s abuse with a stream of truthful words. He then proceeds to weaken each of Caecilius’s arguments. [Col. 0280A] First, therefore, he maintains that no one should resent the Christians, however unlearned, for discussing heavenly matters: it is not the authority of the disputant but the truth of the disputation itself that must be considered.

Octavius then said:

“I shall indeed speak as best I can, according to my powers; and you must strive together with me so that we may wash away the most bitter stain of abuse in a stream of truthful words.

“At the outset, I shall not conceal that the erring, wandering, unstable judgment of my Natalis wavered in such a way that we must wonder whether your learning was deliberately disturbed or whether it wavered through error. For he alternated between believing in the gods and declaring himself still undecided, so that the more uncertain aim of our response might be founded upon the uncertainty of his proposition. [Col. 0281A]

“But I will not have cunning attributed to my Natalis; I do not believe it of him. Such subtle sophistication is far removed from his simplicity. What, then? When a man who does not know the right road comes to a place where, as often happens, a single road spreads into several, he remains anxiously at a loss because he does not know the road; he ventures neither to choose any one of them nor to approve them all. So too, when a man has no stable judgment of what is true, his doubtful opinion is scattered just as his unreliable suspicion spreads abroad. [Col. 0282A]

“There is therefore nothing surprising if Caecilius is repeatedly tossed, seethes, and is driven by waves amid contrary and mutually inconsistent positions. To prevent this from happening any longer, I shall convict and refute the things that have been said, however diverse they are; once a single truth has been confirmed and proved, he will thereafter have neither to doubt nor to wander.

“And [Col. 0283A] since my brother burst out that he resented, was irritated and indignant, and was distressed that the unlettered, the poor, and the inexperienced should discuss heavenly matters, let him know that all human beings, without distinction of age, sex, or rank, were created capable and receptive of reason and understanding. They have not obtained wisdom by fortune; rather, it is implanted in them by nature.

“Indeed, even the philosophers—or any other discoverers of arts [20] who have passed into recorded memory—were regarded as commoners, uneducated and half-naked before, through the ingenuity of their minds, they gained the renown of a name. So greatly have the rich, bound fast to their own means, grown accustomed to looking up to gold rather than heaven; whereas our poor people have both devised wisdom and handed down learning to others.

“From this it is apparent that talent is not bestowed by wealth or acquired through study, but is engendered together with the very formation of the mind. [Col. 0283B] There is therefore no ground for indignation or distress if anyone should inquire into divine matters, form an opinion, and express it, since what is required is not the authority of the disputant but the truth of the disputation itself. Indeed, the less skillful the speech, the clearer the reasoning, since it is not disguised by a display of eloquence and charm, but is sustained, just as it is, by the rule of what is right.”

>
> **CHAPTER XVII.—“He strove.”** Thus Heraldus from the Royal manuscript. CELLARIUS. The reading is *connixus est*. LINDNER.
>
> **“Because they, being bent downward.”** He imitates the very familiar verses of Ovid, *Metamorphoses* I.84:
>
> > And while the other animals look downward at the earth,  
> > He gave mankind an upraised countenance and commanded them

>
> **“They were born.”** I have no doubt that we should read “they are born.” HEUMANNUS.—The received reading may stand, because it can be explained as “they may appear to have been born to look forward to nothing.” For it does not follow that, because animals incline toward the earth, they were therefore born to look forward to nothing but food; yet this alone can be inferred from it with some appearance of truth. LINDNER. Moreover, note Minucius, who writes as though he had not said merely “wild beasts” but had also added “cattle” or “animals,” as in chapter XIX, “the race of cattle and every other kind of animal,” and hence expresses this whole phrase [Col. 0285B] in the neuter plural. Or perhaps the passage stood readily available in his language and had become familiar from his youth, whether from Sallust or Ovid, so that this habitual usage excluded other constructions. That these very words were constantly on the tongues of later writers is also proved by the agreement of the two authors whom Barthius cites in *Adversaria*, book XIX, chapter 7. Livy XXVI.27, at the end, provides a similar change of gender, and Paraeus, in his supplement to the *Critical Lexicon*, pp. 61–62, collected other examples. GRONOVIUS.
>
> **“Speech and reason.”** Perhaps: “were given, both speech and reason,” etc. Minucius, however, also delights in asyndeton. WOPKENS.—Heumannus severs the sinews of the discourse when he deletes the words “speech and reason, through which we recognize, perceive, and imitate God,” as though they had crept into the text from the margin. For there are three things by which we are distinguished from wild beasts: an upright posture, by which we recognize God as we raise our eyes toward heaven; speech, by which we perceive God, since every notion and awareness of divinity is instilled in us from infancy by means of speech; and reason, in which our likeness to God consists and by which we imitate him, since he is a Being who not only employs reason but is himself the author of reason. LINDNER.
>
> **“Through which.”** Correctly, by the customary syllepsis. Cicero, *Letters* VII.20.4: “A house, fields, and these in a pleasant location.” LINDNER.
>
> **[Col. 0285D] “The things below and around.”** Others read “above and around,” but contrary to the manuscript’s authority. LINDNER.
>
> **“From which.”** I restore *a quo* (“from which”) without hesitation. HEUMANNUS.—Unless the preposition has been omitted with equal legitimacy, as when Horace writes “descended [from] kings,” and as in all other authors. LINDNER.

> **“Or because.”** Thus the manuscript. The Roman edition has “or which”; understand “stars.” HEUMANNUS.

> **“Bring on.”** Some prefer “indicate the time,” but without being driven by any necessity they have abandoned the reading preserved by the manuscript. See Virgil, *Georgics* I.24. DAVIS.—Ovid, *Fasti* V.163:
>
> > Or together they bring on the obscure twilight and night.

> LINDNER.

> **“And of the crops.”** Thus it stands in the manuscript, but not in the Roman edition, which has *hac frugum* (“by this of the crops”), and wrongly so, as Heraldus also acknowledges. The frequency of the words by which the varieties both of seasons and of crops are explained shows that Minucius wrote exactly as the manuscript has it. HEUMANNUS.
>
> **[Col. 0286B] “The winter olive-harvest.”** Rigaltius missed the point here. But J. F. Gronovius correctly perceived, *Ecclesiastical Observations*, chapter 7, that Minucius uses “winter olive-harvest” for winter itself, just as poets use “harvest” for summer. For olives ripen in December, as Columella attests in book XII, chapter 50. DAVIS.
>
> **“To insert.”** I read *interserere* (“to intersperse”). HEUMANNUS.—Pliny, *Letters* III.19: “Estates adjoining my lands and even inserted among them are for sale.” Some copies read *inter serta*, but to no purpose. LINDNER.
>
> **“Hidden and harmless transitions.”** He speaks thus because philosophers believed that all bodies consist of the Empedoclean elements (*στοιχεῖα*, “elements”) and that, for the enjoyment of health, these elements must be present in the proper quantities and suitably blended together, something impossible amid an abrupt and excessive change of weather. See Apuleius, *Platonic Philosophy*, book I, p. 5, and Maximus of Tyre, *Dissertation* XXIII, p. 234. DAVIS.—If these words belonged to Caecilius, the observation might have some place. But since Octavius, a Christian, is speaking, it is scarcely plausible that he was thinking of Empedoclean elements, when the explanation may more conveniently be sought in obstructed bodily transpiration. LINDNER.
>
> **“Than.”** An inspection of what precedes and follows will show anyone that *quam* must be deleted. [Col. 0286D] HEUMANNUS.
>
> **“Suitably arranged.”** That is, “how suitably they have been placed.” LINDNER.
>
> **“The uprightness of the mountains.”** That is, “erect.” Lactantius, book II, chapter 1, §14: “But an upright posture has been given to us.” In the same place, §16: “Unless perhaps they think that the very fact that we stand upright was attributed to the human being without a cause.” There was consequently no reason for Meursius to restore *arrecta* (“raised”), Perizonius *erecta* (“erect”), or Wopkens *ardua* (“lofty”). LINDNER.
>
> **“Both based upon hooves and bristling.”** I have no doubt that this passage lies corrupted through the negligence of copyists. For Minucius does not mean that the same animals were both based upon hooves and bristling with spines, but that some enjoy the protection of hooves and others that of spines. We must therefore undoubtedly read: “EITHER based upon hooves OR bristling with spines,” which the following words fully confirm. DAVIS.—**“Bristling.”** The glosses say: *SPICO*, “I sharpen, I scrape to a point.” See Servius on Virgil, *Georgics* I:
>
> > And points the torches with sharp iron.
>
> WOUWER.—Dalecampius restores “fortified with hooves.” The reading “based upon hooves,” however, is correct. For Lucretius V.929 also said: “The human race is based inwardly upon solid bones.” In place of “based upon hooves,” Lactantius, *On the Workmanship of God*, chapter 2, says “supported by hooves.” HEUMANNUS.
>
> **“Grouped.”** Heumannus reads “distributed.” But the subject is not a diversity of locations and the order in which they are distributed, but the single place in which they are grouped. Soldiers are distributed when several approaches exist, on both sides, right and left; but they are grouped in a citadel. The senses could have been distributed throughout the entire body, but God preferred to group them in the head. LINDNER.

**ARGUMENT.—**He indeed acknowledges that a human being ought to know himself, but entirely denies that this knowledge can be acquired unless one has first come to know the totality of things and God himself. From the very constitution and ornament of the world, moreover, everyone endowed with reason knows with certainty that it was founded by God and is ruled and administered by him.
