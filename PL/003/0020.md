VIII. How frequently heavenly visions and prophetic revelations were granted to martyrs at that time is most fully attested by the authentic and genuine Acts both of James and Marianus, etc., and of the martyrs Montanus, Lucius, and their companions, which are filled with heavenly visions and prophetic predictions. I do not intend to recount them here one by one, for it would be necessary to transcribe almost the entire Acts, which may conveniently be consulted in the learned Ruinart. I shall cite only one example from the Acts of Montanus, which [Col. 0121B] bears more directly on the present argument. It is described in the Acts in these words:

> “But when the executioner was already at hand, and the sword hung poised above his neck, he—that is, Montanus—stretched out his hands toward heaven and prayed in a clear voice, so that its sound reached not only the ears of the people but even the pagans themselves, asking and beseeching that Flavianus, who had been separated from their company through the people’s intercession, might follow on the third day. And to provide confirmation of his prayer, he tore into two pieces the handkerchief with which his eyes had been bound, and ordered one half to be kept so that Flavianus’s eyes might be bound with it the day after the morrow. He also ordered that a plot of ground in the open area between them be reserved, so that they might not be deprived of fellowship even in burial. And before our very eyes there was fulfilled what the Lord promised in the Gospel: that whoever asked with complete [Col. 0121C] faith would obtain whatever he asked. For after two days Flavianus too was brought forth, and perfected his glory through suffering.”

It is no more astonishing that Perpetua foretold that a revelation concerning her impending martyrdom would be given before the following day than that Montanus prophesied that Flavianus’s martyrdom would occur after three days. For by the same prophetic spirit under whose impulse Montanus asked “that Flavianus might follow on the third day,” Perpetua asked that before the next day it should be shown to her whether her future held suffering or release. For, as is established by many examples from Scripture, both belong to the spirit of prophecy: to foresee future events, and to await definite revelations of future events.

IX. Concerning the preceding visions and prophetic predictions both of Cyprian [Col. 0121D] and of the contemporary martyrs, one point must be carefully observed: it is agreed by all that the abundance of charisms was far greater at the beginning of the Church than in later centuries. Thus Origen, who wrote his books *Against Celsus* several years before the persecution of Decius, bears witness that by his time only traces of the signs of the Holy Spirit had survived among a few persons distinguished by purity of character and conforming their conduct to the rule of the Gospel. For after saying that the truth of the Gospel is proved by miracles, he says that these can be demonstrated,

> “from many other things, and also from the fact that certain traces of them still survive among those who live according to the will of the Word.”

[Note: [Col. 0121D] “From many other things, and from the fact that traces—or perhaps *certain traces*—of them [Col. 0122D] still survive among those who live according to the will of the Word” (Ἐκ πολλῶν μὲν ἄλλων, καὶ ἐκ τοῦ ἴχνη δὲ [perhaps τινὰ] αὐτῶν ἔτι σώζεσθαι παρὰ τοῖς κατὰ τὸ βούλημα τοῦ Λόγου βιοῦσι). Book I, p. 5, Cambridge edition.]

[Col. 0122A] And in another book of the same work:

> “The signs of the Holy Spirit were shown in very great number at the beginning of Jesus’ teaching and after his ascension, but afterward in smaller number. Yet even now traces of him remain among a few who have purified their souls by the Word and by conduct consonant with it.”

[Note: [Col. 0122D] “The signs of the Holy Spirit were shown in greater number at the beginning of Jesus’ teaching and after his ascension, but afterward in smaller number; yet even now traces of him remain among a few who have purified their souls by the Word and by practices consonant with it” (Σημεῖα δὲ τοῦ ἁγίου Πνεύματος κατ᾽ ἀρχὰς μὲν τῆς Ἰησοῦ διδασκαλίας, μετὰ δὲ τὴν ἀνάληψιν αὐτοῦ πλείονα ἐδείκνυτο, ὕστερον δὲ ἐλάττονα, πλὴν καὶ νῦν ἔτι ἴχνη ἔστιν αὐτοῦ παρ᾽ ὀλίγοις, τὰς ψυχὰς τῷ Λόγῳ καὶ ταῖς κατ᾽ αὐτὸν πράξεσι κεκαθαρμένοις). Book VII, p. 337.]

He explains more clearly in the following words what those traces were:

> “Even now traces of that Holy Spirit survive among Christians. They cast out demons by incantation, perform many cures, and, according to the will of the Word, see certain things concerning the future.”

[Note: [Col. 0122D] “Even now traces of that Holy Spirit . . . survive among Christians: they cast out demons by incantation, perform many cures, and see certain things concerning the future according to the will of the Word” (Καὶ ἔτι ἴχνη τοῦ ἁγίου ἐκείνου Πνεύματος . . . παρὰ χριστιανοῖς σώζεται· ἐξεπᾴδουσι δαίμονας, καὶ πολλὰς ἰάσεις ἐπιτελοῦσι, καὶ ὁρῶσί τινα κατὰ τὸ βούλημα τοῦ Λόγου περὶ μελλόντων). Book I, p. 31.]

From these words it is evident that the exceedingly abundant fountain of heavenly charisms, which in the time of Irenaeus—as he himself testifies—was poured out upon all who were true disciples of Christ, [Col. 0122B] had not entirely dried up, though it no longer overflowed afterward with the same breadth and abundance. Now Perpetua and Saturus lived very close to the time of Irenaeus; whereas the last years of Cyprian, and the martyrs Montanus and his companions, were far removed from the period when Origen composed his books *Against Celsus*—namely, under Gordian or the Philips, after the Church had enjoyed a long and lasting peace. Consequently, if the holy martyrs were so filled with the Holy Spirit when only certain traces of his signs remained that they seemed able to make use of his illuminations at will, why should we wonder if the Holy Spirit granted Perpetua the same abundance of them, at a time when “no reckoning could be made of the charisms which [Col. 0122C] the Church received from God throughout the whole world”?

# CHAPTER V. NOTHING IS CONTAINED IN PERPETUA’S REVELATIONS THAT IS NOT ENTIRELY FITTING FOR A CATHOLIC MARTYR

I. Christ the Lord is not dishonored by the figure of a shepherd milking sheep.  
II. How Perpetua understood from her first revelation that death was imminent.  
III. Nothing absurd is contained in the revelation in which Perpetua seemed to herself to be transformed into a man and to wrestle with the Devil.  
IV. The inept comparison of Perpetua’s revelations with those of the Montanists.  
V. Perpetua’s vision of Dinocrates situated amid punishments.  
[Col. 0122D] VI. Basnage’s difficulties concerning the aforesaid vision.  
VII. The place in which Perpetua saw Dinocrates was purgatory. Basnage’s cavils are refuted.  
[Col. 0123A] VIII. The Montanists’ opinion concerning the receptacles of souls is not correctly presented by Basnage. Those heretics did not hold a single opinion on this matter.

## I

Basnage constructs another argument against the Martyrs from the very manner and character of the visions presented to Perpetua, which do not seem to him sufficiently compatible with dignity and decorum:

> “A man dressed as a shepherd gave Perpetua what was, as it were, a morsel from the cheese he was milking. From this she concludes that death is imminent for her.”

Basnage thus appears, with censorious authority, to find fault with two things in this vision of Perpetua: first, the humble figure of Christ in the garb of a shepherd milking sheep; second, the fact that Perpetua and Saturus took the morsel offered to her as a sign of her impending death.

But there was no reason for him to take offense at either of these things. [Col. 0123B] Concerning the first, the distinguished Holstenius should be heard in his *Notes*:

> “Christ was represented to Perpetua in the form in which he was depicted in the oratories and sacred vessels of those times. For since the Gospels especially assign to Christ the title and person of shepherd, the Christians of the earliest period delighted in setting before their eyes with great frequency this most lovable appearance of his, together with the faithful, who are everywhere designated by the name of sheep. In Bosio’s *Underground Rome*, p. 59 and following, you have many images reproduced from the monuments of the cemeteries, in almost all of which Christ is depicted as a shepherd with sheep. In the very first of them, reproduced here, he is represented precisely in this form: as a shepherd milking sheep. He was also often depicted carrying upon his shoulders the recovered sheep that had been lost, in accordance with the Gospel parable; [Col. 0123C] and this was done especially upon chalices, as Tertullian indicates in chapter 7 of his book *On Modesty*, written against the Catholics, in these words: ‘You may begin from the parables, where there is the lost sheep, sought by God and carried back upon his shoulders. Let the very pictures on your chalices come forward, if even in them the interpretation of that sheep is made manifest,’ etc.”

To this I add that in the Italian work of the most illustrious and learned senator Filippo Buonarroti, entitled *Observations upon Certain Fragments of Ancient Glass Vessels Ornamented with Figures, Found in the Cemeteries of Rome*, many reproductions may be seen of ancient chalices marked with this figure of Christ in the garb of the Good Shepherd: for example, plate 5, figs. 1 and 2, and plate 6, fig. 2. See also plate 1, fig. 1, and in plate 4 a rather large bronze coin, commonly called a *medallion*, engraved on both sides with the figure of the Good Shepherd; [Col. 0123D] all of which the learned author elucidates with highly erudite observations. But what he discusses concerning figure 2 of plate 6 is especially pertinent to our subject:

> “In this fragment the Good Shepherd is seen between two sheep or rams, wearing the customary shoes with bands crossed in a herringbone pattern. Beside him one must observe that little pail, or vessel for milk, which is also seen in the paintings of the cemeteries, [Note: [Col. 0123D] Arringhi, plate 6, chamber 3, Cemetery of Callistus.] and particularly in plate 3 of the sixth chamber of the Cemetery of Callistus in Arringhi. There it is seen attached to a shepherd’s crook held by a reclining lamb, as a symbol of the Good Shepherd. At the feet of that same [Col. 0124A] shepherd, in plate 1 of the second chamber of the Cemetery of Saint Agnes, one sees the crook with a vessel, though of a different shape and with a handle on the side. These shepherds’ vessels were called *mulctrae*, or *mulctralia* [milk-pails], [Note: [Col. 0124D] Servius on *Eclogue* 3.] and this kind of vessel was called in Greek πέλλα (*pella*). It resembled the *scyphus*, but had a somewhat broader bottom, and so was not very dissimilar to ours; it was used especially for milking. [Note: [Col. 0124D] Athenaeus, LXI, col. 14.] Another milk vessel was called *sino*, [Note: [Col. 0124D] Servius on *Eclogue* 7; Nonius, ch. 15.] which, judging from the force of the name, must have been larger than our little vessel,” etc., etc.

And below:

> “To return to our little milk vessel beside the Good Shepherd, I believe that it was fashioned not only as a continuation of the allegory of pastoral things, but furthermore I regard it as, in a manner, a figure of the most divine sacrament [Col. 0124B] of the altar. This seems to be inferred from the most ancient Acts of Saints Perpetua and Felicity, where it is said that Saint Perpetua received milk in a vision from the divine Shepherd: ‘And from the cheese that he was milking he gave me what was, as it were, a morsel; and I received it with joined hands and ate it, and all who stood around said, Amen.’ These were ceremonies and devotional acts customarily practiced by the ancient Christians when receiving Holy Communion. [Note: [Col. 0124D] Tertullian, *On the Shows*, ch. 25; Cornelius in Eusebius, *History*, book VI, ch. 43; Dionysius in Eusebius, book VII, ch. 9; St. Augustine, *Sermon to the Newly Baptized on the Sacraments*; St. Ambrose, book IV *On the Sacraments*, ch. 5; St. Cyril, *Catechesis* 5; the Council in Trullo, canon 102.] And the words of the Song, [Note: [Col. 0124D] Song of Songs V.1.] ‘I drank my wine with my milk,’ are applied by Saint Ambrose [Note: [Col. 0124D] *On the Sacraments*, book V, ch. 3.] to the sacrament of the Eucharist. Since, moreover, in Arringhi, among the paintings on the vault of the ninth chamber in the Cemetery of Saints Marcellinus and Peter, there appears a lamb with a palm branch, bearing a similar little vessel on its shoulders and surrounded by a nimbus, or diadem, and [Col. 0124C] since the same lamb is there painted no fewer than four times in the corners of the same vault, I have wondered whether perhaps in very ancient times they preserved the Holy Eucharist in a similar vessel placed upon a lamb, just as they later used for the same ministry vessels having the form of a dove. In such matters, however, it is good to observe, but one must suspend judgment until further corroboration is available.”

Thus the most illustrious senator. He also observes that the same shepherd’s tunic is ornamented in its lower part with two disks, or bosses; and that in these Acts of Perpetua and Felicity those ornaments are called *calliculae*, from the Greek word κάλλος (*kallos*, “beauty”). They were for the most part of gold or silver, as is established from the same Acts and from other ancient figures in Lambecius [Note: [Col. 0124D] *Imperial Library*, plate 4, Appendix, p. 301.], Pietro [Col. 0124D] Sante Bartoli [Note: [Col. 0124D] *Ancient Paintings of the Grottoes of Rome*, plate 4.], and the Vatican manuscript of Virgil copied in the time of Constantine the Great. [Note: [Col. 0124D] Before book II of the *Aeneid*.]

[Col. 0125A] But to return πρὸς τὸ πρόβλημα (*pros to problēma*, “to the problem”), to the points noted by these most learned men I further add that images of this kind were not especially pleasing to the Montanists, nor were they in use among them. I infer this most clearly from the same words of Tertullian: “Let,” he says, “the very pictures on your chalices come forward.” He does not say “ours,” but “yours”—that is, those which are in use among you, not among us. And in chapter 10 of the same book:

> “If perchance that shepherd whom you depict upon your chalice shall patronize him, he too is a corrupter of the Christian sacrament, deservedly both an idol of drunkenness and a refuge for the adultery that is to follow the chalice—the chalice from which you drink nothing more willingly than the sheep of a second repentance. But I drink in the Scripture of that Shepherd, which cannot be broken.”

Again, he does not say “whom we depict upon the chalice,” but “whom you depict upon the chalice.” [Col. 0125B] Moreover, he calls this pious picture “an idol of drunkenness and a refuge for adultery.” He would certainly not have assailed it with such insulting words if it had also been in use among the Montanists and if they had adorned with it the sacred vessels intended to receive Christ’s blood. Hence there arises a presumption that Perpetua was accustomed to Catholic rites, since she saw Christ, in the garb of a shepherd, administering to her the Eucharist or a symbol of the Eucharist.

Furthermore, if Christ did not disdain the appearance of a shepherd but so frequently applied it to himself, why would he disdain a shepherd’s duties, among which milking sheep occupies no insignificant place? Does this figure dishonor Christ more than that of a hen gathering her chicks beneath her wings and warming them—a figure that the most gracious Lord, accommodating himself to our capacity, by no means rejected? [Col. 0125C] And certainly no image could be more fitting for prefiguring impending martyrdom than that of a shepherd milking sheep. For with the same eagerness with which a shepherd draws milk from the udders of sheep, Christ the Good Shepherd received blood from the martyrs’ veins.

## II

Concerning the second difficulty, the same distinguished Holstenius has these few remarks:

> “Much could be said about the symbolic use of milk and honey customarily given to catechumens, from Hesychius, *On Exodus*, p. 257, and Tertullian, *On the Crown*, p. 121, Rigault’s edition, where he says, ‘We taste beforehand the concord of milk and honey.’ See Albaspinaeus, book II, final Observation.”

But Pierre Poussines adds much more on this point in his *Supplementary Notes* to the notes of Holstenius, and his observations contribute still more [Col. 0125D] toward resolving the difficulty raised against us by Basnage:

> “‘And from the cheese,’ etc. The observation that I found in Holstenius’s papers on the custom of giving milk to catechumens is insufficient to explain the meaning of this passage. That the author would have added other matters to it if he had been able to put the final hand to this work is shown by the blank space he left in his notebooks, after the words of Saint Perpetua that we have transcribed, obviously to be filled in during his final revision. It still remained to explain how, after awakening, Saint Perpetua understood from this presentation of a morsel administered in a vision—as did her brother after she had related the matter to him—that those held in prison were by no means to be released from it, [Col. 0126A] but were to be consummated in martyrdom. For after the words transcribed above, the following are added there: ‘And I immediately reported it to my brother’—the brother, namely, who had asked her to arrange for it to be shown her by divine means whether they were to die or to be released safely—‘and we understood that suffering was to occur, and we began to have no further hope in this world.’ It is rightly asked how they understood this. I think they did so because the rite of administering the Eucharist to Perpetua had been foreshadowed in the vision. Since by the very well-known practice of that time it was customarily given to those who were certain to die shortly, Perpetua and her brother correctly interpreted this to mean that the vision signified the confessors’ imminent death. That the rite of receiving the Eucharist is foreshadowed there is apparent from the fact that what was, as it were, a morsel is offered to Perpetua; [Col. 0126B] that she herself receives it with joined hands; that she then eats it; and that those standing around acclaim, ‘Amen.’ That all these things were customarily observed in the ancient administration of the Eucharist is too well known from the testimony of many ancient writers, and especially Saint Ambrose, to require further proof. Certainly, as regards the practice of the African churches in particular, it is established that they customarily administered the Eucharist to confessors preparing for martyrdom. Indeed, they regarded this as so necessary that they drew from it an argument for writing to Cornelius, the Roman pontiff—by the hand and pen of Saint Cyprian, but in the name of thirty-seven other African bishops as well—asking him to deem it just that dispensation should be granted from the law previously enacted, under which those who had once lapsed during persecution were never to be admitted to the communion of the Church and of the Eucharist. For at that time many of them, enrolled among the penitents, were endangered by new persecutors and, as matters stood, seemed likely soon to be put to death for confessing Christ. To deny those preparing for the contest the customary viaticum of Christ’s body was excessively inhumane. [Col. 0126C] From this letter, which is the second in book I of Cyprian, I shall append certain passages suitable for establishing the point with which we are dealing:
>
> “‘But since,’ they say, ‘we see that the day of another assault has again begun to draw near, and are warned by frequent and continual revelations to be armed and prepared for the conflict which the enemy declares against us; since we must also prepare by our exhortations the people entrusted to us by divine favor, and gather within the Lord’s camp absolutely all the soldiers of Christ who desire weapons and demand battle—compelled by necessity, we have judged that peace must be granted to those who have not withdrawn from the Lord’s Church, but who have not ceased from the very first day of their lapse to do penance, to lament, and to beseech the Lord; and that they must be armed and equipped for the battle that is imminent.’”
>
> And a little later:
>
> “‘But now peace is necessary not for the weak but for the strong, nor must communion be given by us to the dying but to the living, so that we may not leave unarmed and naked those whom we stir up and exhort to battle, but may fortify them with the protection of Christ’s body and blood. And since the Eucharist is made for this purpose, that it may be a safeguard to those who receive it, let us arm with the fortification of the Lord’s sustenance those whom we wish to be secure against the adversary. [Col. 0126D] For how do we teach or urge them to shed their own blood in confession of the Name if we deny Christ’s blood to those about to fight? Or how do we make them fit for the cup of martyrdom if we do not first admit them, by right of communion, to drink the Lord’s cup in the Church? For’—as is added somewhat later—‘he cannot be fit for martyrdom who is not armed by the Church for battle; and that mind fails which the Eucharist, when received, does not raise up and kindle.’”

[Col. 0127A] I do not deny that these arguments are brilliantly and excellently conceived, and that they can most fully satisfy Basnage’s scruple. Nevertheless, I shall add certain points unnoticed by the learned man which will remove every cause of offense from this passage.

[Col. 0127B] First, then, I observe that something similar occurs in the Acts of Saint Philip, bishop of Heraclea and martyr. In them Hermes, his deacon, who together with him consummated his martyrdom by fire, recounts that he learned through this revelation that death had certainly been prepared for him:

> “I had learned that I was to suffer this through an entirely certain revelation, God and the Lord having already announced it beforehand. For while I lay bound by sweet sleep, a dove appeared to me, shining with snowy whiteness. It entered that room and suddenly settled upon the crown of my head; then, descending from there to my breast, it offered me food of the most delightful kind. I immediately understood that the Lord had deigned to call me and had judged me worthy of suffering.”

[Note: [Col. 0127D] In Ruinart, *Authentic Acts*, § 13.]

Second, I observe that Perpetua does not say that she and her brother understood from the presentation of the morsel alone—as seemed to Poussines [Col. 0127C] and afterward to Basnage—that death would soon follow. Perpetua reported to her brother everything she had seen; but she had seen signs of her future suffering far clearer than this. Why, then, should they not have understood more plainly from these that “suffering was to occur”? She had seen a golden ladder of marvelous size extending all the way to heaven, with every kind of iron implement fixed upon its sides. There were swords, lances, hooks, and large blades. She had seen an enormous dragon lying beneath the ladder itself, which laid snares for those ascending and terrified them to keep them from climbing. She had seen Saturus ascending, reaching the top of the ladder, and from there inviting her also to ascend. She had seen herself, as soon as she stepped upon the first rung of the ladder, also tread upon that dragon’s head. Finally, she had seen Christ [Col. 0127D] in the garb of a shepherd milking sheep, etc. Were not all these the clearest signs of the death that was to follow? Why, then, do we insist that the holy martyrs understood from the presentation of the morsel alone that death was imminent for them?

It is true, as Poussines observes, that after the words “and from the cheese that he was milking,” etc., Perpetua adds: “And I immediately reported it to my brother, and we understood that suffering was to occur.” But just as she did not report to her brother only what she had seen last—namely, that a morsel of milk was offered to her—so they did not understand from this sign alone that “suffering was to occur.”

## III

[Col. 0128A] Basnage seeks to bring another of Perpetua’s visions into disrepute in these words:

> “More remarkable is what Perpetua recounts in chapter III, § 2: ‘A certain Egyptian, hideous in appearance, came out against me, intending to fight with me together with his assistants. And handsome young men also came to me, my assistants and supporters; and I was stripped, and I became a man.’ Perpetua therefore advances to fight the Egyptian with her sex changed, intending to receive as the prize of victory ‘a green branch on which there were golden apples. And he called for silence and said: “If this Egyptian defeats her, he will kill her with the sword; and if she defeats him, she will receive this branch.” And he withdrew. We approached one another and began to exchange blows. He sought to seize my feet, but I struck his face with my heels.’ Are these things worthy of the Holy Spirit? Did he ever present anything similar to the prophets? These, of course, are supposedly the visions to which Joel’s sacred oracle is to be applied. Dreams of this kind smell rather of Montanus’s workshop; and her ecstasy is very like the one by which Tertullian proves that the soul is ‘of an airy color and in every respect of human form.’”

[Col. 0128B] There are, then, three things in this vision of Perpetua that appear especially to offend Basnage:

1. Perpetua’s change of sex;
2. the prize of victory, a green branch with golden apples;
3. Perpetua’s struggle with the Egyptian.

Before responding to each of these points, I shall say in general that very many visions and revelations—not only those recounted in the authentic Acts of the martyrs, in histories of the saints, and in other monuments of unquestioned credibility, but also those found in the sacred books of both Testaments—[Col. 0128C] can easily be exposed to contempt and ridicule if the symbols are considered merely in themselves, without regard to their σχέσις (*schesis*), their relation and connection to divine and incorporeal realities. Indeed, not only in visions and inward revelations, but also in prophetic actions and the ceremonies of the Law, there are many things that vain and arrogant people once received with laughter and still ridicule, because they considered them in themselves and paid no attention to their mystical meanings.

Hence it has happened that what appeared to some unworthy of the Holy Spirit, inept, and ridiculous, others held in honor and admiration and treated with the highest veneration: the former directed their minds no further than what was presented to the senses, while the latter carefully investigated the mysteries also concealed in these figures. We see that this occurred also in the interpretation [Col. 0128D] of Perpetua’s revelation. For what now offends Basnage once delighted Augustine’s devout mind wonderfully:

> “It delights the devout mind,” he says, “to contemplate such a spectacle as blessed Perpetua relates was revealed to her concerning herself: that, having become a man, she fought with the Devil. For in that contest she too was advancing toward the perfect man, toward the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.”

[Note: [Col. 0128D] Sermon 281, otherwise *On Various Subjects* 104.]

And in another sermon, alluding to the same vision of Perpetua, he repeats similar words:

> “For what is more glorious than these women, whom men more readily admire than imitate? But this is chiefly the praise of him in whom they believed, and in whose name they strove together with faithful zeal: according to the inner person they are found to be neither male nor female, so that even in those who are women in body the virtue of the mind conceals the sex of the flesh, and one is ashamed to consider in their limbs what could not appear in their deeds.”

[Note: [Col. 0128D] Sermon 280, otherwise *On Various Subjects* 103.]

Why Basnage should have been displeased that the prize of victory was represented by a green branch adorned with golden apples [Col. 0129A] I cannot adequately understand. Did not John the Evangelist see palms in the martyrs’ hands? “And palms,” he says, “were in their hands.” If the Holy Spirit represented the martyrs’ victories by palms, why could a green branch not be a figure of the same victory?

Nor was there reason for Basnage to be so offended by Perpetua’s struggle with the Devil, since he had often read of Jacob’s struggle with the angel. For if we do not attend to the mystical meanings of that event, could it not appear more absurd for a man, not in dreams but while awake, to wrestle through the entire night with a good angel, than for a holy woman to wrestle in dreams with the Devil? If it was not unworthy of the Holy Spirit for Jacob’s struggle with the angel to foreshadow something mystical, why does Basnage ask whether the vision presented to Perpetua—in which she seemed to herself to contend against the Devil—was worthy of the Holy Spirit? [Col. 0129B] Why does he also ask whether the same Holy Spirit ever presented anything similar to the prophets? For he did present it, as we have seen. What difference does it make that Jacob and Perpetua wrestled while awake or asleep—the former with an angel, the latter with the Devil?

The green branch offered to Perpetua as the prize of victory also has, as we have seen, something similar in the canonical and [Col. 0129C] genuine revelations of the Holy Spirit: namely, the palms which John saw in the martyrs’ hands. Finally, the change of sex signifies nothing other than what Augustine observes the Apostle to have written: “In Christ Jesus there is neither male nor female”; and that not only males but females also, through the exercise of virtue, become “according to the inner person, a perfect man, unto the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ.” Was it, then, unworthy of the Holy Spirit to signify through a mystical vision what Paul had expressed in mystical words?

Did not the same Holy Spirit, moreover, later signify to the chief of the Apostles, through a linen sheet presented to him in a vision and filled with every kind of unclean [Col. 0129D] animal, what he had foretold through Isaiah concerning the conversion of the nations under the various figures of different animals and in metaphorical words? Has not the same Holy Spirit often foreshadowed through prophetic actions what he had sometimes foretold in words through the prophets—actions which at first sight may appear insufficiently joined with decorum and dignity? Thus Isaiah is commanded to walk naked; Ezekiel to eat bread either covered “with dung that comes out of a man” or prepared over cow dung; and Hosea to take for himself a wife of whoredom and beget children of whoredom.

What would Basnage answer if some atheist asked him: “Are these things worthy of the Holy Spirit? Do these things seem to you sufficiently compatible with dignity and decorum?” Whatever argument he might devise to defend from the ridicule of the impious these prophetic deeds undertaken at the command of the Holy Spirit, we too shall always and easily devise a similar argument to vindicate Perpetua’s vision from Basnage’s own cavils—unless perhaps he requires greater decorum and dignity in the dreams of a sleeping person, however prophetic they may be, than in the likewise prophetic actions of those who are awake. [Col. 0130A]

Yet another point must by no means be omitted: Basnage unreasonably demands that we produce from the sacred books another vision wholly similar to this vision of Perpetua. By this argument he could bring under suspicion almost all the visions of saints reported in ecclesiastical histories, for he will not find many wholly similar to those recounted in the sacred books.

Pontius the deacon relates in his *Life* [Col. 0130B] the revelation granted to Saint Cyprian concerning his future martyrdom, a revelation wholly unlike anything the Holy Spirit ever presented to the prophets. I shall therefore adduce it as an example:

> “On the day when we first remained at the place of exile—for his loving kindness had chosen me as a voluntary exile among his domestic companions, and would that this had been permitted also in his suffering!—there appeared to me,” he says, “when I had not yet sunk into the repose of sleep, a young man of extraordinary size. As he led me, as it were, to the governor’s residence, I seemed to myself to be brought before the tribunal of the proconsul then seated there. As soon as the proconsul looked upon me, he immediately began to write down upon a tablet a sentence unknown to me, for he had asked me nothing according to the customary interrogation.
>
> “But the young man who stood behind him read with great attention everything that had been written down. Since he could not express it from there in words, he showed by an explanatory gesture what was contained in the writing on that tablet. [Col. 0130C] Extending his hand and flattening it like a sword, he imitated the stroke of the customary execution, and expressed what he wished to be understood as clearly as spoken language. I understood that the sentence meant future suffering. I immediately began to ask and entreat that a postponement of even one day be granted me, until I might arrange my affairs in due order.
>
> “When I had repeatedly renewed my entreaties, the judge again began to write something—I do not know what—upon the tablet. From the serenity of his expression, however, I perceived that his mind had been moved, as it were, by my just petition. And that young man who had previously disclosed the indication of suffering by gesture rather than speech now hastened, by another secret sign, to indicate that the postponement requested until the morrow had been granted: he repeatedly twisted his fingers one behind another.” [Col. 0130D]

Basnage will seek in vain for anything wholly similar to this among the prophetic revelations of either the Old or New Testament. Pontius noted passages of Scripture with which this vision of Cyprian seemed to have some similarity; yet they are not so closely related as those which we have noted above in connection with the revelation of Perpetua now under discussion. For he writes as follows:

> “What could be clearer than this revelation? What could be more blessed than this favor? Everything was foretold to him beforehand that later came to pass. Nothing was diminished from God’s words; nothing was mutilated from so holy a promise. Examine each detail, finally, according to the manner in which it was shown.
>
> “He requests a postponement until the morrow while the sentence of suffering was under consideration, asking that he might arrange his affairs [Col. 0131A] on the day he had obtained. That one day signified the year which he was to live in this world after the vision. For, to speak more plainly, he was crowned on that same date, once the year had elapsed, on which this had been shown to him a year before. Although in the divine writings we read of the Lord’s day and not the Lord’s year, we nevertheless understand that period as owed to the promise of future things. Hence it makes no difference if now a single year was shown under the signification of a day, since that which is greater ought to be fuller.
>
> “As for its being explained by gesture rather than speech, the expression in speech was reserved for the time of its realization. For something is customarily uttered in words at the moment when whatever is uttered is fulfilled. And in truth no one understood why this had been shown to him until he was crowned afterward on the same date on which he had seen it. [Col. 0131B] In the intervening time, nevertheless, the impending suffering was known with certainty by everyone; yet the exact day of his suffering was passed over in silence by all of them, as though they did not know it.
>
> “Indeed, I find something similar in the Scriptures as well. For when the priest Zechariah did not believe the angel’s promise that he would have a son, he became mute, so that, when he was to write rather than speak his son’s name, he requested tablets. Rightly, therefore, in this instance too, when God’s messenger expressed the imminent suffering of the bishop by gesture rather than speech, he both admonished his faith and fortified the priest.”

Pontius had precisely these arguments with which to defend the credibility and dignity of Cyprian’s revelation; and I do not think that I have produced arguments less appropriate on behalf of Perpetua’s revelation. Basnage must therefore either reject both alike as wholly unworthy of the Holy Spirit, or certainly accept both [Col. 0131C] and acknowledge that they had the same Spirit as their author. This is especially so because what Pontius carefully demonstrates concerning Cyprian’s revelation, we too shall demonstrate in the following chapter concerning Perpetua’s revelations: namely, that they were confirmed by the realities themselves and by the events that followed.
