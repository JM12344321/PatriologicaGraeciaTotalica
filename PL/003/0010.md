**Such darkness.** Tertullian, *To the Martyrs*, chapter 2: “The prison has darkness, but you yourselves are light. It has chains, but you have been set free for God. A foul breath is exhaled there, but you are an aroma of sweetness.” Blessed Cyprian imitated this in Epistle 81: “O blessed prison,” he says, “which your presence has illuminated! O darkness brighter than the sun itself, and more radiant than this light of the world!” Tertullian, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 8: “Come now, what do you think of it”—that is, the flesh—“when it is wasted away in prisons, in a most loathsome exile from light, in deprivation of the world, filth, stench, [Col. 0021B] and degrading food; not even free in sleep, since it is chained even to its beds and mangled by the very bedding?”

There is a particularly remarkable passage in Libanius’ recently published *Oration on the Custody of Prisoners*, in which he describes the wickedness and greed of the prison officials, who would scarcely allow even a single lamp except at a high price. His words will more conveniently be quoted shortly below. HOLST.

**Heat.** That heat arises from the confinement of the place and the crowd of people, through which Libanius, in the oration I have just mentioned, complains that many suffocated in prison—and this through the magistrates’ negligence. I shall append his passage from page 68, slightly corrected by me and translated according to the author’s intention:

> ὅταν οὖν οὗτοι πέμπωσιν ἐπὶ τὰ δεσμωτήρια φάλαγγας, ἐῤῥῶσθαι δὲ φράσαντες τοῖς περὶ ἐκεῖνα νόμοις, ἐπ᾽ ἄλλοις διατρίβωσιν, ὡς μὴ φιλάνθρωποι δόξαντες εἶναι, τίς οὐκ ἂν ἀποπνιγείη;

That is: “When, therefore, these magistrates send companies of people into confinement and meanwhile, having told the laws enacted concerning such matters to go hang, occupy themselves with other business so as not to be thought humane, who would not [Col. 0021C] suffocate?” [The accompanying Latin paraphrase instead reads: “so that they may appear popular.”]

Lucian the confessor likewise describes the same hardships of prison in Cyprian, Epistle 22, in these words, also incidentally restored: “By command of the emperor, we were ordered to be killed by hunger and thirst; and we were shut up in two cells so that they might afflict us with hunger and thirst. But the fire too, from the constriction under which we labored, was so unbearable that no one could endure it.” Thus far he. There “fire” seems to mean what St. Perpetua calls “heat.”

From these passages it is understood that the sufferings of prisons were neither the least nor the last of the devil’s devices against the martyrs. The churches of Gaul acknowledge this in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 1, in these words:

> τὰς κατὰ τὴν εἱρκτὴν ἐν τῷ σκότει καὶ τῷ χαλεπωτάτῳ χωρίῳ συγκλείσεις, ὥστε ἀποπνιγῆναι τοὺς πλείστους τῇ εἱρκτῇ.

That is: “Confinements in a dark and most distressing place, so crowded that most were suffocated in the prison.” HOLST.

**Because of the crowds.** That is, “on account of the crowds,” or “the multitude of people.” A pure Graecism, τοῦ [Col. 0021D] ὄχλου χάριν, “for the sake of the crowd.” So shortly afterward, “because of me” (*mei beneficio*), that is, “on my account” or “because of me.” HOLST.

**The soldiers’ rough handling.** This seems to signify the violent shoving of soldiers as they thrust prisoners together, which Procopius calls ὠθισμός (“jostling”) on the last page of the *Secret History*. Note also the soldiers’ barbarous ferocity toward prisoners, attested even by St. Ignatius, who therefore calls them leopards and writes to the Romans that they become more savage when treated well.

Moreover, not only was a fee paid to them for custody, but gifts were also freely offered so that they might deal more mildly and gently with the prisoners; these were effectively payments extorted from them. Thus the *Apostolic Constitutions*, book V, chapter 1, commands alms to be sent to designated martyrs, both for their sustenance and to pay the soldiers’ fee, so that they might lighten their treatment and take care of the prisoners—that is, relax their customary cruelty toward them:

> ἵνα, ὅσον ἐφ᾽ ὑμῖν, μὴ θλίβηται ὁ μακάριος ἀδελφὸς ὑμῶν,

“so that, insofar as it lies with you, your blessed brother may not be violently handled”; that is, to buy relief from harassment and rough handling, for θλῖψις in this passage means rough handling. HOLST.

[Col. 0022A] **Finally, I was tormented by anxiety for my infant.** A most elegant and natural expression of maternal affection, whose example God himself employs in declaring his loving-kindness toward us. HOLST.

**Blessed deacons.** An εὐφημισμός (“honorific expression”) conveying affection or reverence for the person mentioned. It is more familiar today in the common Italian language than in ordinary Latin. It may have passed into African Latin from the Syriac or Hebrew tongue, which the ancient Tyrians and Phoenicians who colonized Carthage used—or one not greatly [Col. 0022B] different from it. For Syrians and Hebrews customarily add the epithet “Blessed” especially when mentioning God, as in Mark 13:61 and often elsewhere in Scripture, but also when addressing good people: “You are blessed by the Lord,” Psalm 123:15, and not infrequently elsewhere.

At the very beginning of his book addressed to those destined for martyrdom, Tertullian says: “Blessed martyrs.” Shortly afterward: “First of all, therefore, blessed ones, do not grieve the Holy Spirit.” And in chapter 2: “You, blessed ones, may perhaps consider yourselves transferred from prison into protective custody.” In the same place: “Thus now, blessed ones, prison is troublesome even to Christians”; and shortly afterward in chapter 3, and in book II of *On the Dress of Women*, he calls Christian women “blessed.” In *Prescription against Heretics*: “During the episcopate of the blessed Eleutherius”; and in *Exhortation to Chastity*: “the blessed patriarchs.”

Thus Lucian the confessor in Cyprian, Epistle 22: “When the blessed martyr Paul was still in the body.” And the Roman clergy in Epistle 2 to Cyprian: “We have learned that the blessed pope Cyprian has withdrawn.” HOLST.

**Who ministered to us.** The Church always took great care [Col. 0022C] of those held in bonds, especially for confessing Christ; in former times deacons were particularly assigned to minister and attend upon them. They supplied them with food and with means to soften the soldiers’ cruelty, as we reported a little above from the *Apostolic Constitutions*. They were present with counsel, encouragement, and consolation. Cyprian, Epistle 11: “It was always the practice under our predecessors that deacons visiting the prison should guide the martyrs’ desires by their counsel and by the precepts of Scripture.”

In short, as good angels stand ready to assist the people entrusted to them, so deacons stood ready for the confessors in labor, duty, and diligence. Hence Ignatius’ statement in the *Epistle to the Trallians*: “What are deacons? Surely imitators of the angelic powers.” See Council of Carthage IV, canon 43. The Council of Orléans V, canon 20, likewise imposes upon the archdeacon or provost of the church the duty of visiting the prison, and upon the bishop that of mercifully relieving the prisoners’ need by assigning them suitable food from the resources of the church. HOLST.

[Col. 0022D] **They arranged it for a payment.** For this purpose both the churches publicly and individual believers contributed from their resources, so that confessors and martyrs in prison might be supplied with food, other supports of life, and necessary relief; or, when need arose, these things might be wrung from the injustice of the guards by bribes, lest, overcome by suffering, they fail before the actual contest. This may be seen from Tertullian’s *To the Martyrs*, St. Cyprian’s Epistles 4 and 5, and other sources.

Eusebius testifies that the Roman Church’s particular solicitude and πατροπαράδοτος (“ancestrally transmitted”) piety in this matter—praised long before by Dionysius of Corinth, a most ancient writer—persisted constantly down to his own time, so that it cared for all martyrs throughout the world and all who suffered for Christ’s name, and provided them with the necessities of life.

Nor is it without reason that the most holy martyr writes that this privilege was “arranged for a payment,” that is, purchased for a price, so that they might be let out of the cramped work-prison for a few hours. For prison officials, to whom punishment was a source of revenue, according to Manilius in book V, customarily relaxed confinement only after being corrupted with payment. Law 8, *Digest*, *On the Custody of Prisoners*, restrains them. Manilius, just cited, plays elegantly upon these guards’ savagery in the passage beginning with the verse:

> Whoever, when Andromeda rises from the sea,  
> Is born, etc.

**That, being released for a few hours, we might refresh ourselves in a better part of the prison.** They had therefore been thrown into the “inner prison,” as it is called in Acts, chapter 16. What is here obtained as a favor from the commander of the guard, appeased by a payment, was later established by Emperor Constantius in Law 1 of the Code, *On the Custody of Prisoners*, where we read:

> Nor indeed ought one who is confined to suffer the darkness of the innermost chamber, but he should be revived and relieved by access to light; and when night has doubled the guard, he should be received in the vestibules of the prisons [Col. 0023D] and in wholesome places. When day returns, he should immediately, at the first rising of the sun, be brought back into public light, lest he be killed by the punishments of prison—a thing recognized as wretched for the innocent and insufficiently severe for the guilty.

This enactment seems to have mitigated a rescript of Antoninus Pius, who wished persons accused of graver crimes to endure the punishment of prison itself before their execution: book III, *Digest*, *On the Custody of Prisoners*. The added phrase “that we might refresh ourselves,” besides what has been said above, seems to render the Greek verb ἀναψύχειν (“to refresh”), although an old lexicon renders “to refresh” by ἀγαλλιᾶσθαι (“to rejoice”). HOLST.

**Already weakened by starvation.** That is, brought almost to death. Tertullian, book II *To the Nations*: “Why is not she a goddess who was more dutiful—the daughter who nourished from her own breasts her father, weakened by hunger in prison?” Seneca and Phaedrus also speak in this fashion. I marvel that a learned man suspected the latter’s passage, since it can be confirmed by innumerable other testimonies of classical authors readily encountered everywhere.

It is consequently apparent how elegantly [Col. 0024A] the common Latin translator rendered the Greek word ἐσχατογήρως in Ecclesiasticus 41:4 as *defecto aetate*, “spent with age.” I add, for good measure, a passage from the *Acts of the Vindication of Caecilianus*: “When I heard this, I became faint in mind.” And Suetonius Tranquillus, in *On Illustrious Grammarians*, concerning [uncertain: *Offio Care* in the supplied text]: “When he was now failing not only in gait but also in sight.” HOLST.

**The prison became a praetorium for me.** That is, not only did the prison’s discomforts no longer disturb me, but I even rejoiced in them as though I were living in splendid and comfortable premises. Properly speaking, the *praetorium* was the public palace in which the proconsul administered justice, as may also be understood from Pontius the deacon; nor is it understood differently in the history of the Lord’s Passion. Since it generally surpassed other private houses in the elegance of its structure and the splendor of its ornamentation, any palaces and magnificently furnished houses—even when privately owned and built in the countryside—were called *praetoria*. Martial:

> At the fourth milestone Torquatus has his praetorium.

The following words of Tertullian nevertheless seem pertinent to the meaning of this passage: “Outside prison the Christian has renounced the world; in prison, even prison itself.” For the mind, strengthened by divine grace, either does not feel the prison’s discomforts and hardships or disregards them and conquers them by constancy. HOLST.

**III.—You are now in great dignity.** Holstenius reads “favor” (*dignatione*) and adds: “For divine favor had already illuminated her with glorious beginnings,” as St. Cyprian says of the confessors held in prison in Epistle 4. The word *dignatio* (“favor”) was discussed above.

Here I add one remarkable and entirely apposite passage from Tertullian. In book I of *To His Wife*, chapter 4, after using the example of sisters who prefer holiness to marriage, he adds:

> For they prefer to marry God. They are beautiful for God; for God they are maidens. With him they live; with him they converse. They occupy themselves with him by day and night. They assign their prayers to the Lord as dowries; and from him they obtain favor, as though bridal gifts, whenever they desire it.

Believing that his sister had advanced into such a state through her endurance of imprisonment, her brother urges her to ask that God reveal to her what is to come, confident that she [Col. 0024D] will be heard. This is the meaning of the words “that I should ask for a vision,” etc. HOLST.

**Whether it is to be martyrdom or release.** Just as many other words from military discipline were transferred to Christ’s holy soldiery, so too was the word *commeatus*. According to Festus, it means “a period customarily granted by the emperor to soldiers, during which it is possible to go and return.” Hence Apuleius, book XI: “He granted a brief furlough from the warfare of love.” In the Code, soldiers are said “to be dismissed upon the grant of furlough” or “by furlough,” and those so dismissed are called *commeatales*, “men on leave.”

Thus Hesychius says that κομίατον means ἐξαίτησιν λαμβάνειν τοῦ ἀφεθῆναι, that is, “to receive permission, obtained by request, to depart freely.” And in the glosses: “*Commeatus*: ἐξουσία”—authority, as it were, or the ability to depart and go wherever one wishes. So Law 3, *Digest*, *On Punishments*: “No one can grant permission to depart and return to an exile except the emperor, for some cause.”

This word has remained in common Italian usage down to the present day, except that it extends beyond military affairs to anyone’s permission to depart from anyone. [Col. 0025B] Among Christians of that period, moreover, this word is found transferred so as to denote deliverance from danger to life, whether that danger threatened from illness or from a judge’s sentence.

Concerning illness, we have a remarkable passage in Palladius’ *Lausiac History*, chapter 86, where Melania obtains from God for the mortally ill Evagrius καιρὸν κομιατοῦ καὶ προθεσμίαν ζωῆς, “a period of reprieve and an extension of life.” In the same sense we read in Cyprian’s sermon *On Mortality*: “to pray for a reprieve for oneself,” that is, deliverance from the danger of plague. Thus Paulinus, in the *Life of St. Ambrose*, writes that Stilicho sent noblemen to the sick Ambrose to persuade him to ask the Lord for leave to go on living.

But in our passage the word *commeatus* signifies escape from danger threatened by a persecutor, as is clear from the fact that it is opposed to *passio*, “martyrdom,” that is, death for Christ. In the same way Cyprian, Epistle 8, opposed reprieve to martyrdom, or the consummation of victory; and in Epistle 52 says that when peace is granted by persecutors, reprieve is granted [Col. 0025C] by God. Hence those who, as sometimes happens, falsely boast of such intimacy are said by Misitheus, in his letter to Gordian preserved by Capitolinus, to “sell stories about the prince”: “They pose as persons privy to the prince’s secret counsels and arbiters of his intimate conversations, deceive others, and sell smoke.” HOLST.

**Every kind of iron implement.** The glosses explain *ferramenta* as ἐργαλεῖα σιδηρᾶ, “iron tools.” Properly, however, *ferramenta* [Col. 0025D] are weapons suited for harming, or anything possessing an edge or point. Thus Cethegus, questioned in Cicero’s *Catilinarians* about the swords and daggers found in his possession, replied that “he had always been a devotee of good ironware.” And Law 18, *On an Instrumentum Bequeathed*: “Iron implements prepared for cutting flesh are counted among a butcher’s equipment.” HOLST.

**Hooks.** In an old glossary the *hamus*, ἄγκιστρον (“hook”), is listed among bronze implements; but since here it is placed among iron implements and instruments of martyrdom, I would prefer to understand it as the *uncus*, the hook by which, as we read, the saints’ bodies were mangled or suspended and dragged into a river or the sewers. These hooks resemble fishhooks in shape, though they differ in size; hence the same name remained for both.

Alternatively, perhaps we ought to read *amae* here. These are mentioned, after Pliny the Younger in book X of his *Letters* and Juvenal in Satire XIV, by Law 3, [Col. 0026B] *Digest*, *On the Office of the Prefect of the Watch*, in these words: “It should be known that the prefect of the watch ought to keep watch throughout the entire night and make his rounds shod, with hooks and axes.” Those *amae*, as the word itself indicates, were sickles, but larger ones—that is, curved iron implements attached to very long poles. Implements of this sort, prepared for containing or extinguishing fires, are still kept in public places throughout the cities of Germany and Gaul; and the jurisconsult joins them with axes in this passage. Livy establishes that soldiers formerly used them to tear down a rampart or wall.

Law 12, *On an Instrumentum Bequeathed*, joins *amae* and grappling hooks, just as Vegetius, book V, has “sickles and grappling hooks.” Hence Nonius, chapter 18: “Sickles and grappling hooks, kinds of weapons suited to assaulting walls.” And Cicero, *For Milo*: “They fly about at the Temple of Castor with sickles, and throughout the whole Forum with swords.” HOLST.

**In the garb of a shepherd, milking sheep.** Christ was represented to Perpetua in the form in which he was depicted in the oratories and sacred vessels of that period. Since the Gospels attribute the title and character of shepherd preeminently to Christ, the Christians of the earliest times delighted in placing before their eyes with great frequency this most lovable image of him together with the faithful, who are commonly designated as sheep.

In Bosio’s *Subterranean Rome*, page 59 and following, you have many images copied from the monuments of the cemeteries, in almost all of which Christ is depicted in the form of a shepherd with sheep; and in the first he appears in precisely the form expressed here, that of a shepherd milking sheep. He was also not infrequently depicted carrying on his shoulders the recovered sheep that had been lost, in accordance with the Gospel parable, especially upon chalices. Tertullian indicates this in *On Modesty*, chapter 7, written against the Catholics:

> You may begin [Col. 0027D] with the parables, where the lost sheep is sought by the Lord and carried back on his shoulders. Let the very images upon your chalices come forward, if even in them the interpretation of that sheep will shine forth, etc.

**And standing around him were many thousands clad in white.** The joining of “many thousands” to the word *candidati* (“those clad in white”) without a conjunction has an example not only in the common Latin translator of Apocalypse, chapter 7: “forty thousand sealed,” but also in Trebellius Pollio’s *Life of Emperor Claudius*, where we read: “You will give him as an annual allowance from our private treasury three thousand modii of wheat, six thousand of barley, two thousand pounds of bacon, three thousand five hundred sextarii of old wine,” etc. Similar examples occur in other authors. HOLST.

**Welcome, teknon.** A greeting, as it were, from a parent to a daughter; for the Greek τέκνον (“child”) is applied to both sexes, as everyone knows. HOLST.

**And he called me.** As the Latin language had already begun to become corrupt in everyday use, this construction of the verb *clamare* (“to call”) had passed into popular speech; it was afterward received into vernacular Italian: *mi chiamo*. HOLST.

**And from the cheese he was milking, he gave me, as it were, a morsel.** Augustine, cited above, calls it “a morsel of milk.” Much could be said, from Hesychius on Exodus, page 257, and Tertullian, *On the Crown*, page 121 in Rigault’s edition, about the custom of giving milk and honey symbolically to catechumens. Tertullian says there: “We taste beforehand the concord of milk and honey.” See Albaspinaeus, book II, final Observation. HOLST.

**—And from the cheese he was milking, he gave me, as it were, a morsel; and I received it with joined hands and ate it, and all those standing around said, ‘Amen.’** Concerning these words, the observation I found in Holstenius’ papers about the customary giving of milk to catechumens is insufficient to explain the meaning of the passage. The author would have added other observations if he had been able to put the final hand to this work, as is shown by the blank space he left in his notes after the words of St. Perpetua that we have transcribed, evidently to be filled during his final revision.

It remained to be explained how, upon awakening from this presentation of a morsel administered in the vision, St. Perpetua—and likewise her brother, after she had related the matter to him—understood that it had been divinely signified that those held in prison were by no means to be released, but were to be consummated in martyrdom. For after the words transcribed above, the following are added there:

> And I immediately related it to my brother—who, of course, had asked her to arrange for it to be divinely shown to her whether they were to die or be released safely—and we understood that martyrdom was to occur; and we began now to have no hope in the world.

It is rightly asked how they understood this. I believe it was because the rite of administering the Eucharist had been foreshadowed in the vision for Perpetua. Since, by a very familiar custom of that time, it was ordinarily given to those known to be shortly about to die, Perpetua and her brother rightly interpreted the vision as signifying that the confessors’ death would soon follow.

The rite of receiving the Eucharist is seen foreshadowed in the following details: a morsel, as it were, is offered to Perpetua; she receives it with joined hands; she then eats it; and those standing around acclaim “Amen.” That all these observances were customary in the ancient administration of the Eucharist is better known from the testimony of numerous ancient writers, especially St. Ambrose, than to require extensive demonstration.

It is certainly established, with particular reference to the usage of the African churches, that they customarily administered the Eucharist to confessors standing ready for martyrdom. Indeed, they judged this so necessary that they derived from it an argument for writing to Cornelius, the Roman pontiff—by the hand and pen of Cyprian, but also in the name of thirty-seven other African bishops—asking him to approve an exception to the law previously enacted that those who had once lapsed during persecution should never be admitted to communion with the Church and the Eucharist. For at that time many of these, enrolled among the penitents, were in danger from new persecutors and, as matters stood, seemed likely soon to be killed for confessing Christ. To deny the customary viaticum of Christ’s body to people standing ready for the contest was excessively inhuman.

From this epistle—the second of book I in Cyprian, Epistle 57 in our edition; see below—I shall append some passages appropriate for establishing the point we are considering:

> But since, they say, we see that the day of another assault has begun once more to approach, and we are admonished by frequent and repeated revelations to be armed and prepared for the contest which the enemy announces against us; since we must also prepare by our exhortations the people entrusted to us [Col. 0029B] through divine favor and gather within the Lord’s camp absolutely all the soldiers of Christ who desire arms and demand battle; under the compulsion of necessity we have judged that peace ought to be granted to those who have not withdrawn from the Lord’s Church, but who, from the first day of their lapse, have not ceased to perform penance, lament, and entreat the Lord, and that they ought to be armed and equipped for the battle that threatens.

And a little afterward:

> But now peace is necessary not for the weak but for the strong, and communion must be granted by us not to the dying but to the living, [Col. 0029C] so that we may not leave unarmed and naked those whom we rouse and exhort to battle, but fortify them with the protection of Christ’s blood and body. Since the Eucharist is given for this purpose, that it may be a safeguard for those receiving it, let us arm with the defense of the Lord’s abundance those whom we wish to be secure against the Adversary. For how do we teach or urge them to shed their own blood in confession of the name if, when they are about to fight, we deny them Christ’s blood? Or how do we make them fit for the cup of martyrdom if we do not, by the right of communion, first admit them in the Church to drink the cup of the Lord?

Indeed, as is added somewhat later:

> He cannot be fit for martyrdom who is not armed by the Church for battle; and the mind that the received Eucharist does not raise and lift up fails.
