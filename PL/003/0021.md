IV. Basnage further adds that Perpetua’s dreams smell of Montanus’s workshop, and that her ecstasy is very similar to the one by which Tertullian proves that the soul is “of an airy color and in every respect of human form.” But the same claim could equally be applied to every vision in which either the soul, an angel, or God himself is represented under the forms of corporeal things. Did Perpetua actually believe that there is a distinction of sex in the human spirit? Did she believe that her soul had been changed into the male sex [Col. 0131D]? Did she suppose that the form of the Egyptian whom she saw was the demon’s own proper shape? Did she think that Christ truly was a trainer of gladiators, and that the angels were handsome young men? Did she judge that she had actually gone in glory to the Sanavivarian Gate, just as that deranged little woman of Tertullian’s truly believed and affirmed that the soul was “of an airy color and in every respect of human form”? What, then, does Perpetua’s vision have in common with the dreams of this delirious woman, that the former should be called very similar to the latter? For it is just as groundless to suppose that Perpetua believed there really to be a distinction of sex in our soul as it would be to judge that she supposed the other things she then saw to be the things themselves, rather than representations of things and of future events. [Col. 0132A] We should therefore listen to Augustine, a far more reasonable and sympathetic interpreter of this vision than Basnage [Note: [Col. 0132D] Book IV, *On the Origin of the Soul*, chap. 18]:

> Saint Perpetua seemed to herself in a dream to have been changed into a man and to wrestle with a certain Egyptian. But who could doubt that it was her soul, and not her body, that was present in that bodily likeness? Her body, of course, remained in its female sex, lying upon the bed with its senses asleep, while her soul wrestled in that likeness of a male body.

V. I cannot restrain myself here from also briefly refuting what Basnage remarks concerning that celebrated revelation of Perpetua about her brother Dinocrates. For although he does not derive from it an argument for attacking the orthodoxy of the holy martyrs, nevertheless his observations place the martyrs’ unblemished [Col. 0132B] and sincere faith in very great jeopardy. The vision is as follows:

> I see Dinocrates coming out of a dark place, where many others also were. He was very hot and exceedingly thirsty, with a dirty face and pale complexion, and with the wound on his face that he had when he died. . . . I had therefore prayed for him. Between him and me there was a great *diadema* (or *diastema*, “interval”), so that neither of us could approach the other. Then, in the very place where Dinocrates was, there was a pool full of water, whose rim was higher than the child’s stature; and Dinocrates stretched himself out as though about to drink. I was grieved because, although that pool contained water, nevertheless because of the height of its rim he would not be able to drink. I awoke and understood that my brother was suffering. But I trusted that my prayer [Col. 0132C] would benefit him in his suffering, and I prayed for him every day until we were transferred to the military prison. For we were to fight in the military games, which were then being held for the birthday of Geta Caesar. I prayed for him day and night, groaning and weeping, that he might be granted to me. But on the day when we remained in the stocks, this was shown to me. I see that place which I had seen as dark now filled with light, and Dinocrates with a clean body, well clothed, and refreshed, etc. Then I understood that he had been transferred away from punishment.

VI. This passage troubles Protestants, since by it the faith of the Roman Church concerning purgatory and the prayers of the living for the dead is most invincibly established. It is therefore hardly surprising that Basnage attempted to diminish its authority by numerous trifles and cavils [Col. 0132D]:

> Concerning this vision, he says [Note: [Col. 0132D] At the year 205, no. VII], Augustine must be heard [Note: [Col. 0132D] *On the Soul and Its Origin*, chap. 10]: “As for Dinocrates, the brother of Saint Perpetua, the writing itself is not canonical; nor did she—or whoever wrote it—write in such a way as to say that the boy, who had died at the age of seven, had departed without baptism. For him, as she faced martyrdom, she is believed to have been heard, so that he was transferred from punishments to rest. For children of that age can already lie and speak the truth, confess and deny. And therefore, when they are baptized, they already recite the creed and themselves answer the questions on their own behalf. Who knows, then [Col. 0133A], whether that boy, after baptism, had in time of persecution been estranged from Christ by his impious father through idolatry, on account of which he entered into the condemnation of death, and did not leave it unless, as a gift, through the prayers of his sister who was about to die for Christ?”

> That Dinocrates, who died at the age of seven, had not received baptism is evident from the fact that his sister, although already married and twenty-two years old, was nevertheless still among the catechumens, and that their father was alien to the Christian religion. In that case Dinocrates could not have been delivered by Perpetua’s prayers. Next, the fire of purgatory is extinguished by this vision. Perpetua saw Dinocrates dwelling not in flames but in a dark place, subject only to the punishment of loss, not to the punishment of sense. Thus this vision does nothing to help Ruinart prove that the dead can be delivered, by the prayers of the living, from the punishments they suffer [Col. 0133B]. For according to the Latins’ position, only those who have received baptism are delivered from the punishment of sense through the suffrages of the living. But if Dinocrates was defiled by idolatry after baptism, as Augustine suggests, he certainly could not have been exempted by anyone’s prayers from the punishments he deserved. Finally, that dark place in which Dinocrates was confined, entirely different from purgatory, signifies that common prison into which, as very many Fathers and the Montanists believed, all souls except those of the martyrs are cast. Hence Tertullian wrote: “The entire key to paradise is your blood.” And he thought that the patriarchs themselves were held below until they should be delivered by Christ. Dinocrates was thought to have been placed in that prison which more recent writers call Limbo.

VII. I do not think that the learned man would ever have poured forth so many trifles [Col. 0133C] had he not been driven headlong by his passion for attacking Catholic doctrines. First, contrary to Augustine’s opinion, he thinks it evident that Dinocrates, who died at the age of seven, had not received baptism; and he proves this both from his sister’s example and from the fact that their father was alien to the Christian religion. I, however, think it evident that Dinocrates had been washed in the saving waters. For since Perpetua’s whole family, as she herself plainly testifies, practiced the religion of Christ, it is not likely that they failed to take care that, before he died, the endangered boy should be assisted through the sacrament of baptism. Hence the argument he constructs from the sister’s example is utterly worthless. I admit that in former times baptism was postponed for many unless danger to life was imminent. But when a person’s salvation was in immediate peril [Col. 0133D], who could believe there to have been such negligence in a Christian family that they felt no concern for a boy at the point of death, and took no care whatever that, at least with his final breath, he should be washed in the laver of regeneration, even against his father’s wishes?

Next, he says that the fire of purgatory is extinguished by this vision of Perpetua. The distinguished Holstenius did not think so. On Perpetua’s words, “very hot and thirsty,” he added this quite appropriate note:

> These words accord perfectly with the Church’s doctrine concerning purgatory, where souls are tormented by heat and fire.

Furthermore, because Basnage makes these observations not only to extinguish the fire of purgatory but also to overthrow purgatory itself, it will be worthwhile to recall to his mind [Col. 0134A] a doctrine which, I believe, he has often read in the more eminent theologians and the keenest defenders of the Catholic side: it is not a dogma of faith that those who depart this life with something still to be purged in the future life are tormented by corporeal fire rather than by other punishments—darkness, for example, grief, or sorrow. Indeed, at the Council of Florence there was scarcely any controversy between Latins and Greeks concerning purgatory, although the Greeks considered “purgatory to be not fire, but a certain dark place full of sufferings”; and while admitting purgatory they thought it made no difference [Note: [Col. 0134D] Εἴτε πῦρ ἐστιν, εἴτε ζόφος καὶ θύελλα, εἴτε τι ἕτερον, οὐ διαφερόμεθα. “Whether it is fire, darkness and tempest, or something else, we do not dispute.” Session XXV] “whether it was fire, darkness, a tempest, or some other thing of that kind.” The holy synod itself likewise judged this question to be of no great consequence, since in the definition of faith, which the prelates of both Churches signed, only the following was decreed concerning purgatory [Col. 0134B] . . . . [Note: [Col. 0134D] Τὰς ψυχὰς καθαρτικαῖς τιμωρίαις καθαίρεσθαι μετὰ θάνατον· ὥστε δὲ ἀποκουφίζεσθαι αὐτὰς τῶν τοιούτων τιμωριῶν λυσιτελεῖν αὐταῖς τὰς τῶν ζώντων πιστῶν ἐπικουρίας· δηλονότι τὰς ἱερὰς θυσίας, καὶ εὐχὰς, καὶ ἐλεημοσύνας, etc. “Souls are purified after death by purgatorial punishments; and, so that they may be relieved of such punishments, the assistance of the faithful who are alive benefits them—namely, sacred sacrifices, prayers, and alms, etc.”]:

> Souls are purified after death by purgatorial punishments; and, so that they may be relieved from punishments of this kind, the suffrages of the living benefit them—namely, the sacrifices of Masses, prayers, and alms, etc.

Basnage’s observation would therefore do nothing to diminish the force of this passage, even if Dinocrates had not burned in any corporeal flames. For that dark place in which Perpetua saw him “very hot and thirsty” agrees perfectly with the purgatory admitted by the Greeks at the Council of Florence without contradiction from the Latins. And although the Latins’ opinion concerning the corporeal flames of purgatory is, as Bellarmine says [Note: [Col. 0134D] Book II, *On Purgatory*, chaps. 10 and 11], the most probable, nevertheless, as Bellarmine also teaches [Note: [Col. 0134D] Ibid.], the substance of the faith does not turn upon this point. Moreover, as Holstenius observed [Col. 0134C], Perpetua’s words accord perfectly with the Latins’ opinion.

More astonishing is what Basnage immediately adds: that Perpetua saw Dinocrates subject only to the punishment of loss, not to the punishment of sense. What, then? Do darkness, thirst, heat, a dirty face, a pale complexion, a wound on the face, and so forth seem to him to belong to the punishment of loss rather than to the punishment of sense? The punishment of loss is defined by theologians as deprivation of the divine vision. Was that alone what Dinocrates endured? If so, then when Perpetua later understood that he had been transferred away from punishment, he was refreshed by the divine vision. Basnage certainly will not admit this, since, as he notes, it was the doctrine of the Montanists—to whose impious sect he assigns Perpetua—that before the solemn day of judgment only martyrs are received into heaven.

Still more astonishing is his statement that this vision does nothing [Col. 0134D] to help Ruinart prove “that the dead can be delivered by the prayers of the living from the punishments they suffer.” Since Perpetua affirms in such explicit words that she obtained by her prayers the transfer of Dinocrates from the punishments by which he was tormented, it is truly astonishing how this vision does nothing to help Ruinart prove “that the dead can be delivered from punishments, etc.” Here, surely [Col. 0135A], either the authenticity (*γνησιότης*) of the Acts or the martyr’s trustworthiness is being called into question. Ruinart was touched by no suspicion either concerning the genuineness of the Acts or concerning the martyr’s credibility. But let us see by what arguments Basnage, who raises no question about the Acts, attempts to deprive Perpetua of credibility:

> According to the Latins’ position, only those who have received baptism are delivered from the punishment of sense through the suffrages of the living.

But we, with Augustine, hold it certain that Dinocrates was baptized, and we have demonstrated most clearly that he was subject to the punishment of sense.

> But if, he adds, Dinocrates was defiled by idolatry after baptism, as Augustine suggests, he certainly could not have been exempted by anyone’s prayers from the punishments he deserved.

But if, I say, Dinocrates was defiled by idolatry after baptism, as Augustine suggests, he could not have been subject only [Col. 0135B] to “the punishment of loss, not of sense.” Moreover, we are no longer dealing here with Perpetua, but with Augustine’s opinion. She did not write that he had been estranged from Christ through idolatry by his impious father and brought back to the sacrileges of the pagans, as Augustine suggests. There are many other sins, as Augustine himself observes, on account of which Dinocrates could have been consigned to purgatorial punishments: he could have lied, become angry, played in an unbecoming manner, and so forth. Whatever, according to Augustine’s opinion—which I have no desire to discuss at present—Dinocrates’s sins may have been, or by whatever punishments he was tormented, the holy Doctor does not doubt that “he left that place, as a gift, through the prayers of his sister who was about to die for Christ.” Accordingly, I do not see why this vision does not help Ruinart prove that the dead can be delivered, through the prayers of the living, from the punishments they suffer [Col. 0135C].

VIII. Finally, Basnage says that the dark place in which Dinocrates was confined, being entirely different from purgatory, signifies that common prison into which, as very many Fathers and the Montanists believed, all souls except those of the martyrs are cast. Concerning what are called the receptacles of souls, Tertullian’s opinion was not always one and the same. For in his book *On the Soul* [Note: [Col. 0135D] Chap. 57; see also chaps. 55 and 56], following the doctrine of the Paraclete—that is, of Montanus—he places “all souls in the lower regions,” and there assigns them punishments and refreshments in the meantime:

> For why should you not suppose that the soul is both punished and comforted in the lower regions in the meantime, while awaiting each judgment, in a certain provisional enjoyment and as its candidate [*candida*, reading uncertain]?

Elsewhere, however, he refutes this as Marcion’s opinion by the authority of Scripture [Note: [Col. 0135D] Book IV, *Against Marcion*, chap. 34] [Col. 0135D]:

> But Marcion drives matters in another direction. He determines that both rewards belonging to the Creator—whether torment or refreshment—have been appointed in the lower regions for those who obeyed the Law and the Prophets; but for Christ and his own god he appoints a heavenly bosom and harbor. We shall answer him, with this very Scripture refuting his eyes, since it distinguishes Abraham’s bosom, where the poor man was, from the lower regions. For the lower regions are one thing, as I think, and Abraham’s bosom another. For Scripture says that a great depth lies between those regions and prevents passage in either direction. Nor would the rich man have raised his eyes, and indeed from afar, unless he looked toward higher things and across the immense distance between height [Col. 0136A] and depth. Hence it is apparent to every wise person who has ever heard of the Elysian fields that there is some locally defined region called Abraham’s bosom, intended to receive the souls of his children, including those from the nations. . . . I therefore call that region Abraham’s bosom: though not heavenly, it is nevertheless higher than the lower regions and will meanwhile afford refreshment to the souls of the righteous, until the consummation of all things, through the resurrection of all, discharges the full measure of their reward, when the heavenly promise shall then appear, etc. Without prejudice to that promise, why should Abraham’s bosom not be understood as some temporary receptacle of faithful souls, in which the image of the future is already outlined and a certain preliminary prospect of each judgment is displayed?

According to this second opinion, it cannot be said that the dark place [Col. 0136B] in which Dinocrates was confined was some common prison into which, as very many Fathers and the Montanists believed, all souls except those of the martyrs are cast. For Tertullian teaches that Abraham’s bosom, destined to receive the souls of his children, was a region which, although not heavenly, was nevertheless higher than the lower regions and would meanwhile afford refreshment to the souls of the righteous.

According to the first position, however, just as both punishments and refreshments existed in the lower regions, so purgatory also existed in the lower regions. Accordingly, it cannot be established that the dark place in which Dinocrates was confined was entirely different from purgatory. For by the name “purgatory” we understand the punishments of the next life which, after some time, are to be exchanged for refreshment in the case of the righteous; nor does the question whether it is spatially separate from the lower regions pertain to the dogma of faith concerning [Col. 0136C] purgatory [Note: [Col. 0136D] See Bellarmine, Book II, *On Purgatory*, chap. 6]. Since, therefore, that dark place in which Perpetua saw Dinocrates very hot and thirsty was soon afterward changed into a place of light, in which his most holy sister beheld him “with a clean body, well clothed, and refreshed,” it still cannot in any way be affirmed, even according to the position of certain ancient Fathers and the Montanists, that the dark place in which Dinocrates was first confined was entirely different from purgatory.

If the distinguished Holstenius’s reading were established and certain, the theologians’ common position concerning the receptacles of souls would be splendidly confirmed by this vision of Perpetua, as Possinus observes [Note: [Col. 0136D] *Paralipomena* to Holstenius’s notes]. For his text reads:

> I see Dinocrates coming out of a dark place, where there were also [Col. 0136D] several dark places, etc.

The theologians teach, namely, that beneath the earth there are four distinct places in which the souls of the dead are detained: first, the lowest of all, where demons and condemned souls are held; above this, purgatory, where souls are purified for a time; next, the Limbo of Children, where the souls of those who died in original sin alone are detained; and lastly, the Limbo of the Fathers, now empty since the time of Christ’s death and resurrection, etc. These, Possinus adds, seem to have been the several dark places which Saint Perpetua testifies in the quoted words that she saw. But [Col. 0137A] Ruinart’s text reads: “where there were also many persons.” This is certainly more appropriately referred to the multitude of those confined there than to a diversity of places. Nevertheless, Perpetua does not by these words deny that there are various receptacles of souls beneath the earth. But we shall return to this vision of the holy martyr in the seventh chapter.

# CHAPTER VI

## THE VIRTUES OF OUR MARTYRS AND THE FRUITS OF THEIR VISIONS SHOW THAT THEY WERE INSPIRED BY THE DIVINE SPIRIT AND THAT THEIR REVELATIONS MUST BE ATTRIBUTED TO GOD

I. In distinguishing true prophets from false, account must first be taken of their morals. II. Certain passages of the Acts in which the virtues of our martyrs are vividly described. III. Nothing can be observed in their conduct that [Col. 0137B] smacks of the discipline of Montanus. IV. The example of Ignatius the Martyr demonstrates that Perpetua and her fellow martyrs by no means violated the laws of nature. V. The prayers and vows of other martyrs for the crown of martyrdom. VI. Besides Perpetua, other martyrs either provoked beasts against themselves or voluntarily employed instruments of cruelty to hasten their martyrdom. VII. The examples of ancient Christians who either offered themselves voluntarily or deliberately provoked the savagery of governors against themselves excuse Perpetua’s action. VIII. The memorable deed of Saints Andronicus and Tarachus. IX. The widely differing effects of each demonstrate how different the spirit of Perpetua and her fellow martyrs was from the spirit of the Montanists. [Col. 0137C] X. The honorable and excellent fruit of Perpetua’s and Saturus’s revelations.

I. In the preceding chapters I weakened the arguments that Basnage constructs against the martyrs from their own Acts. In the present and following chapters I shall bring forward arguments which I have gathered from those same Acts in favor of the holy martyrs, and by which, as I believe, their orthodoxy is most invincibly vindicated. Thus, just as our opponent assails the martyrs with arguments both external, as they are called, and internal, so we, after presenting in the first chapter of this dissertation the external arguments drawn from the authority of the Church and the ancient Fathers, shall contend for them in these final two chapters with the internal arguments most abundantly supplied to us by their own Acts.

[Col. 0137D] Let the first argument be that which spontaneously shoots forth and blossoms from the heroic virtues of Perpetua and her fellow martyrs: their patience, piety, moderation, charity, fortitude, and contempt for all human things. The ancients believed that the conduct of prophets carries great weight in distinguishing the true from the false. Hence that ecclesiastical writer Apollonius, according to Eusebius [Note: [Col. 0137D] Τῆς δὲ ΚΑΤΑ ΦΡΥΓΑΣ καλουμένης αἱρέσεως καὶ Ἀπολλώνιος ἐκκλησιαστικὸς συγγραφεὺς ἀκμαζούσης εἰσέτι τότε κατὰ τὴν Φρυγίαν ἔλεγχον ἐνστησάμενος, ἴδιον κατ᾽ αὐτῶν πεποίηται σύγγραμμα, τὰς μὲν φερομένας αὐτῶν προφητείας ψευδεῖς οὔσας κατὰ λέξιν εὐθύνων, τὸν δὲ βίον τῶν τῆς αἱρέσεως ἀρχηγῶν ὁποῖός τις γέγονε διελέγχων. “While the heresy called ‘that of the Phrygians’ was still flourishing in Phrygia at that time, the ecclesiastical writer Apollonius undertook its refutation and composed a special work against them, examining word for word the prophecies circulated by them and showing them to be false, while also exposing what sort of life was led by the leaders of that heresy.” Book V, *Ecclesiastical History*, chap. 18]:

> A writer who undertook to refute the Cataphrygian heresy [Col. 0138A], which was still flourishing in his own age, composed a special volume against them, both examining word for word the false prophecies circulated by them and investigating the life and conduct of those who were the authors of that heresy.

He then presents several fragments from this work in which Apollonius describes the depraved conduct of Montanus and his little women, and also that of Themison and Alexander. Afterward he adds [Note: [Col. 0138D] Δεῖ γὰρ τοὺς καρποὺς δοκιμάζεσθαι τοῦ προφήτου· ἀπὸ γὰρ τοῦ καρποῦ τὸ δένδρον γινώσκεται. “For the prophet’s fruits must be examined; the tree is known by its fruit”]:

> A prophet’s deeds must be examined as his fruits. For the tree is known by its fruits.

And a little later [Note: [Col. 0138D] Ἐὰν ἀρνῶνται δῶρα τοὺς προφήτας αὐτῶν εἰληφέναι, τοῦτο ὁμολογησάτωσαν, ὅτι ἐὰν ἐλεγχθῶσιν εἰληφότες, οὐκ εἰσὶ προφῆται, καὶ μυρίας ἀποδείξεις τούτων παραστήσομεν· ἀναγκαῖον δέ ἐστι πάντας καρποὺς δοκιμάζεσθαι προφήτου· προφήτης, εἰπέ μοι, βάπτεται; προφήτης φιλοκοσμεῖ; προφήτης, τάβλαις καὶ κύβοις παίζει; προφήτης δανείζει; ταῦτα ὁμολογησάτωσαν, πότερον ἔξεστιν ἢ μή; ἐγὼ δὲ ὅτι γέγονες παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς δείξω. “If they deny that their prophets accepted gifts, let them concede this: if they are proved to have accepted them, they are not prophets; and we shall present countless proofs of these things. It is necessary that every fruit of a prophet be examined. Tell me: does a prophet dye his hair? Does a prophet delight in adornment? Does a prophet play at tables and dice? Does a prophet lend money at interest? Let them acknowledge and answer whether these things are permissible or not. I shall show that they have occurred among them”]:

> If they deny that their prophets accepted gifts, let them therefore admit that they are not prophets if they are proved to have accepted gifts. Then at last we shall produce countless proofs and testimonies of the fact. Moreover, all the fruits and deeds of a prophet must be tested. [Col. 0138B] Tell me: does a prophet dye his hair? Does he paint his eyes with antimony? Does he strive to adorn himself? Does a prophet play at tables and dice? Does he lend money at interest? Let them candidly admit and answer whether it is permissible to do these things or not. I, for my part, shall prove that these things have been done among them.

II. But in these Acts of Perpetua, Felicitas, and their fellow martyrs, marvelous examples of every Christian virtue shine everywhere. Thus one may rightly affirm that for every vice Apollonius censures in Montanus, Maximilla, Themison, and Alexander, an equal number—and indeed many more—adornments of every virtue shine forth in these holy martyrs, and especially in Perpetua. As examples, I shall indicate several passages of the Acts that bear more directly on the matter.

Chapter I, § 2:

> While we were still with our persecutors, she says, and my father persisted in trying to turn me away and [Col. 0138C] cast me down because of his affection, etc., I said to him . . . “I cannot call myself anything other than what I am: a Christian.” Then my father, provoked by this word, threw himself upon me as though he would tear out my eyes; but he merely tormented me and went away, defeated along with the devil’s arguments. Then, because I was without my father for a few days, I gave thanks to the Lord, and his absence brought me relief. During that very interval of a few days we were baptized; but the Spirit instructed me to ask for nothing else in the water except endurance of the flesh. After a few days we were taken into prison, and I was terrified because I had never experienced such darkness. . . . I was nursing my child, who was already faint from hunger. In my anxiety for him, I spoke with my mother, encouraged my brother, and commended my son to them. I wasted away because I saw them wasting away on my account. [Col. 0138D] I endured such anxieties for many days, and obtained permission for my child to remain with me in prison. At once I recovered my strength and was relieved of my distress and anxiety for the child; and the prison suddenly became like a palace to me [Col. 0139A], so that I preferred to be there rather than anywhere else.

How many virtues shine here! Constancy against her father’s threats and entreaties; constancy likewise against every human affection; devotion toward her parents; a mother’s concern for her son; invincible patience in enduring the hardships of prison. She does not, after the fashion of hypocrites, conceal human affections beneath a Stoic solemnity, but overcomes and moderates them through Christian virtue. She gives thanks to the Lord, implores divine assistance, and grieves more over the distress of others than over her own—a marvelous testimony to her religious devotion toward God and charity toward her neighbor.

The same virtues shine in other passages also. Chapter II, § 1:

> After a few days a report spread that we were to be given a hearing. My father also arrived from the city, worn out with grief, and came up to me in order to cast me down, saying: “Have pity, daughter, on my gray hairs,” etc. [Col. 0139B] My father said these things out of his affection, kissing my hands, throwing himself at my feet, and with tears calling me not his daughter but his lady. And I grieved for my father’s gray hairs, because he alone of my whole family would not rejoice in my suffering. I encouraged him, saying: “On that platform, whatever God wills shall happen. For know that we have not been placed in our own power, but in God’s.”

Similar examples both of filial respect for her father and of Christian fortitude occur in § 2. Although the whole passage deserves to be transcribed, I shall append only these few words from it:

> And the procurator Hilarianus . . . said: “Spare your father’s gray hairs; spare the tender years of your child. Offer sacrifice for the safety of the emperors.” And I answered: “I will not.” Hilarianus said: “Are you a Christian?” And I answered: “I am a Christian.” And when my father stood there trying to cast me down [Col. 0139C], Hilarianus ordered him to be cast down, and he was struck with a rod. My father’s misfortune grieved me as though I myself had been struck; so deeply did I grieve for his wretched old age. Then he pronounced sentence upon us all and condemned us to the beasts, and we went joyfully down to the prison.

Saint Augustine commends Perpetua’s filial respect toward her father in these words [Note: [Col. 0139D] Sermon 281, otherwise *On Various Subjects* 104]:

> She grieved at the outrage done to her aged parent; and although she did not give him her assent, she preserved her affection for him. For in him she hated his folly, not his nature; his unbelief, not her own origin. With all the greater glory, therefore, did she resolutely reject the evil counsel of a father whom she loved so deeply that she could not see him beaten without sorrow. Accordingly, that sorrow neither diminished the strength of her courage nor failed to add something to the praises of her suffering:

“For all things work together for good for those who love God.”

[Col. 0139D] Another example of the same virtues occurs in chapter III, § 1:

> When the day of the games drew near, my father entered to see me, worn out with grief. He began to tear out his beard, cast himself upon the ground, prostrate himself upon his face, reproach his own years, and utter such words as would move the whole creation. I grieved for his unhappy old age.

Here Saint Augustine must again be heard [Note: [Col. 0139D] *Exposition of Psalm* XLVII, no. 13]:

> Through charity many have died to the world in order to live to God. The martyrs were set aflame by this charity—not pretenders, not people blown about by vainglory, not such as those of whom it was said, “If I hand over [Col. 0140A] my body to be burned,” etc., but such as were truly brought to suffering by love of Christ and of the truth. What did the persecutions of their raging assailants do to them? The eyes of their weeping loved ones exerted greater violence than the persecutions of those who pursued them. How many were held back by their children, lest they suffer! At the knees of how many did their wives prostrate themselves, lest they be left widows! How many parents did their children try to prevent from dying, as we know and read in the Passion of BLESSED PERPETUA? These things happened. Yet however many tears flowed, and however violently, when could they extinguish the ardor of charity?

Chapter V, § 2, presents us with numerous examples at once of mutual charity, hope, and magnanimity:

> As for Felicitas—for the Lord’s grace also befell her in this manner—she was already eight months [Col. 0140B] pregnant, since she had been pregnant when arrested. As the day of the spectacle approached, she was in great sorrow lest she be postponed because of her pregnancy, since pregnant women are not permitted to be presented for punishment, and lest she afterward shed her holy and innocent blood among some criminals. Her fellow martyrs also were deeply grieved lest they leave so good a companion behind, alone, as it were, upon the road of the same hope. Therefore, joining together in one united groan, they poured forth prayer to the Lord three days before the games. Immediately after the prayer, labor pains seized her. And when she suffered in childbirth because of the natural difficulty of the eighth month, one of the servants of the prison guards said to her: “If you are suffering so much now, what will you do when you are thrown to the beasts which you scorned when you refused to sacrifice?” And she answered: “Now it is I who suffer [Col. 0140C] what I suffer; but there another will be within me who will suffer for me, because I too am to suffer for him.” Thus she gave birth to a girl, whom one of the sisters raised as her own daughter.

No less elevated, and deserving of no less admiration, are the proofs of Perpetua’s and Saturus’s unbroken and exalted courage described in §§ 3 and 4. But chapter VI, § 1, must not be omitted, since it presents a connected tissue and variety of all the virtues:

> The day of their victory dawned, and they proceeded from the prison into the amphitheater as though into heaven—joyful and lovely in countenance, trembling, if at all, with joy and not fear. Perpetua followed with a serene face and measured step, like a matron of Christ, beloved of God, lowering the vigor of her gaze before the eyes of all. Felicitas likewise rejoiced that she had safely given birth and so could fight the beasts, passing from blood [Col. 0140D] to blood, from the midwife to the net-fighter, to be washed after childbirth in a second baptism. When they had been brought to the gate and were being compelled to put on costumes—the men those of the priests of Saturn, and the women those of women consecrated to Ceres—that noble woman resisted with steadfastness to the very end. For they said: “We came to this voluntarily precisely so that our freedom should not be taken from us. We pledged our souls to God so that we would do no such thing; this was our agreement with you.” Injustice acknowledged justice: the tribune permitted them to be brought in simply dressed as they were. Perpetua sang psalms, already treading upon the Egyptian’s head. Revocatus, Saturninus, and Saturus threatened the watching populace with judgment for this. When they came within Hilarianus’s sight [Col. 0141A], they began to say to him by gesture and sign: “You judge us,” they said, “but God will judge you.” At this the infuriated populace demanded that they be scourged by a line of beast-fighters. And they rejoiced because they had obtained a share in the Lord’s sufferings.

Chapter VI, § 3, should also be consulted, so that, besides the invincible fortitude with which the holy women offered themselves to the most savage cow, Perpetua’s modesty may also receive its due:

> For when she had been tossed by the savage beast, she fell upon her loins; and when she saw that her tunic had been torn at the side, she drew it over her thighs as a covering, more mindful of modesty than of pain.
