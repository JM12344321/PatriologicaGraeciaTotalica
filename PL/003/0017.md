VII. Secondly, it must be noted—something that follows naturally from the foregoing and confirms still more clearly what has already been said—that the Montanists, although they differed in conduct and discipline from the institutions and customs of the apostolic Churches, nevertheless did not consider themselves alienated from their communion in those matters upon which the substance of faith and the Christian religion turns. This is established from the same Tertullian and almost the same passages. For in his book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, after teaching that Catholics and Montanists agreed upon one rule of faith, and that the Paraclete had not corrupted the faith but had applied the finishing touch to discipline, he immediately adds:

> “Throughout Greece and in certain barbarian regions, a number of its Churches [Col. 0088C] conceal their virgins. Under this very sky, too, that institution is observed in some places, lest anyone attribute that custom to Greek paganism or barbarian practice. But I have set before you those Churches which the Apostles themselves, or apostolic men, founded—and, I think, before certain others. Those Churches, therefore, possess the same authority of custom: they can set their antiquity and their predecessors against these later Churches. What shall we observe? What shall we choose? We cannot reject a custom that we cannot condemn, since it is not foreign to us, because it is practiced by no foreigners, but by those with whom we share the right of peace and the name of brotherhood. We and they have one faith, one God, the same Christ, and the same sacraments of the washing. Let me say it once: we are one Church. Thus whatever belongs to our own people belongs to us.”

But nothing clearer can be adduced to prove this [Col. 0088D] than the extremely acute arguments to the same effect in the book *On Prescriptions against Heretics*, which, as I have said, he composed when he was already a Montanist. I have thought it worthwhile to adduce several passages from that book, although they are rather lengthy, because they settle the matter completely and place it in the clearest light.

He therefore says that, in order to convict heretics, this alone must be debated [Note: [Col. 0088D] Chap. 19]:

> “To whom the faith itself belongs; [Col. 0089A] whose the Scriptures are; by whom, through whom, when, and to whom the discipline by which people become Christians was handed down. For wherever the truth of Christian discipline and faith is found to be, there will also be the truth of the Scriptures, their interpretations, and all Christian traditions.”

He then adds that the Apostles, having been instructed by Christ [Note: [Col. 0089D] Chap. 20],

> “and having received the promised power of the Holy Spirit for mighty works and utterance, first bore witness throughout Judaea to faith in Jesus Christ and established Churches; then, setting out into the world, they proclaimed to the nations the same doctrine of the same faith, and accordingly founded a Church in each city. From these Churches, the other Churches thereafter borrowed—and daily continue to borrow—the shoot of faith and the seeds of doctrine, so that they themselves might become Churches. For this reason they too are regarded as apostolic [Col. 0089B], being the offspring of apostolic Churches. Every kind must necessarily be classified according to its origin. Thus the many and great Churches are that one first Church from the Apostles, from which they all derive: in this way all are first and all are apostolic, while all together demonstrate unity—while among them there is the communion of peace, the designation of brotherhood, and the mutual pledge of hospitality. These rights are governed by no principle other than the single tradition of the same sacrament.

> “We therefore lay down this prescription [Note: [Col. 0089D] Chap. 21]: if the Lord Jesus Christ sent the Apostles to preach, no preachers are to be received other than those whom Christ appointed. . . . And as to what they preached—that is, what Christ revealed to them—I prescribe here that this must be proved in no other way than through the very Churches which the Apostles themselves founded, preaching [Col. 0089C] to them personally, both by the living voice, as they say, and later through letters. If these things are so, it follows that every doctrine agreeing with those apostolic Churches, the matrices and origins of the faith, must be accounted true, as undoubtedly preserving what the Churches received from the Apostles, the Apostles from Christ, and Christ from God. Every other doctrine, however, must be prejudged false if it savors of anything contrary to the truth of the Churches, the Apostles, Christ, and God.

> “It therefore remains for us to demonstrate whether this doctrine of ours, whose rule we set forth above, is reckoned as deriving from the tradition of the Apostles, and consequently whether the others proceed from falsehood. We are in communion with the apostolic Churches because our doctrine differs from theirs in no respect: this is the testimony of truth.”

And below, against the heresies [Col. 0089D] that boasted of their antiquity and represented themselves to the unlearned as apostolic because they had existed under the Apostles, he says that it rested upon them to show either that they had some Apostle, or some apostolic man who had persevered with the Apostles, as the author of their institution, or at least that they possessed the communion of the apostolic Churches [Note: [Col. 0089D] Chap. 32]:

> “But if any of them dare to insert themselves into the apostolic age, so that they may therefore appear to have been handed down by the Apostles because they existed under the Apostles, we can say: let them then produce the origins of their Churches; let them unroll the succession of their bishops, [Col. 0090A] running down from the beginning in such a way that their first bishop had as his founder and predecessor one of the Apostles, or one of the apostolic men who nevertheless persevered with the Apostles. For this is the manner in which the apostolic Churches present their registers: thus the Church of the Smyrnaeans reports that Polycarp was installed by John; thus the Church of the Romans produces Clement, ordained by Peter; and so, of course, the others likewise exhibit those whom they possess as shoots of the apostolic seed, having been appointed to the episcopate by the Apostles.

> “Let the heretics fabricate something of this kind. For what is unlawful to them after blasphemy? Yet even if they fabricate it, they will accomplish nothing. For their very doctrine, when compared with the apostolic doctrine, will declare by its diversity and opposition that it has neither an Apostle nor an apostolic man [Col. 0090B] for its author. For just as the Apostles would not have taught doctrines differing from one another, so apostolic men would not have promulgated doctrines contrary to the Apostles, except for those who deserted the Apostles and preached otherwise.

> “To this standard, therefore, they will be summoned even by those Churches which, being much later—indeed, Churches that are established daily—can produce no Apostle or apostolic man as their founder, yet, because they agree in the same faith, are no less regarded as apostolic by reason of their kinship in doctrine. Thus let all the heresies, when summoned by our Churches to both standards, prove themselves apostolic in whatever way they think possible. But they are not apostolic, nor can they prove what they are not; nor are they received into peace and communion by Churches that are apostolic in any sense, since, because of the diversity of their sacrament, [Col. 0090C] they are in no sense apostolic.”

But nothing is clearer than what, not much further below, he says concerning the principal apostolic Churches, and especially the Roman Church, in these words [Note: [Col. 0090D] Chap. 36]:

> “Come now, you who wish to exercise your curiosity more profitably in the business of your salvation: visit the apostolic Churches, among which the very chairs of the Apostles still preside in their own places, and among which their own authentic letters are recited, sounding forth the voice and representing the face of each one. Achaia is near you: you have Corinth. If you are not far from Macedonia, you have Philippi and Thessalonica. If you can travel into Asia, you have Ephesus; but if you are adjacent to Italy, you have Rome, from which authority is also readily available to us.

> “How fortunate is that Church, upon which the Apostles poured out their whole doctrine together with their blood, [Col. 0090D] where Peter was made equal to the Lord in his manner of suffering; where Paul was crowned with an end like John’s; where the Apostle John, after being plunged into burning oil and suffering no harm, was banished to an island. Let us see what it has said, what it has taught, and what pledge of fellowship it has exchanged even with the African Churches. It knows one God, the Creator of the universe; Christ Jesus, born of the Virgin Mary, the Son of God the Creator; and the resurrection of the flesh. It combines the Law and the Prophets with the evangelical and apostolic writings, and from these it drinks in the faith; that faith it seals with water, clothes with the Holy Spirit, feeds with the Eucharist, and exhorts to martyrdom. And so, against this institution, [Col. 0091A] it receives no one.

> “This is the institution—I no longer say merely that it foretold the heresies that were to come, but that the heresies came forth from it. Yet they did not belong to it from the moment they became opposed to it. Even from the kernel of a mild, rich, and useful olive springs the harsh wild olive; even from the seed of the most pleasing and sweetest fig rises the wind-filled and empty caprifig. So too the heresies bore fruit from our stock, but were not ours: degenerate from the grain of truth, they became wild through falsehood.”

Could Baronius, Bellarmine, or others regarded as most devoted to the Roman Church have proclaimed anything about it more magnificent or more illustrious? Who would believe that these words could have escaped from a heretical man who had been cast out by the Roman Church—words by which absolutely all heresies opposed to the doctrine of that same Church [Col. 0091B] are overthrown? No one at all, unless he has attended to what we have thus far deduced from this author: namely, that it had not entered the minds of the Montanists that, on account of the new prophecy and discipline, they had fallen away from communion with the apostolic Churches in matters of faith and in those upon which the substance of religion turns.

Nevertheless, because not everyone agrees that the book *On Prescriptions* was not composed by Tertullian while he still moved among the Catholics, I shall add from Book IV *Against Marcion*—which, it is agreed, was composed by him after he had begun, as he says, to have disputes with the “Psychics” [Note: [Col. 0091D] See chap. 23]—a similar testimony concerning the apostolic Churches, and especially the Roman Church: namely, that whatever departs from their tradition in matters of faith must be regarded as adulterated and heretical, [Col. 0091C] and whatever does not share with them one and the same faith must be considered foreign [Note: [Col. 0091D] Chap. 5]:

> “In short, if it is established that what is earlier is truer, that what is from the beginning is earlier, and that what is from the Apostles is from the beginning, it will likewise certainly be established that what has been held sacred among the Churches of the Apostles was handed down by the Apostles. Let us see what milk the Corinthians drank from Paul; to what rule the Galatians were corrected; what the Philippians, Thessalonians, and Ephesians read; and what the Romans close at hand also proclaim, to whom both Peter and Paul left the Gospel sealed with their own blood.

> “We also have the Churches fostered by John. For although Marcion rejects his Apocalypse, the succession of bishops, when traced back to its origin, will rest upon John as its author. In the same way the noble descent of the other Churches [Col. 0091D] is recognized. I therefore say that among those Churches—and no longer among the apostolic Churches alone, but among all that are joined to them by fellowship in the sacrament—that Gospel of Luke which we now particularly defend has stood from the beginning of its publication.”

Certainly he would never have dared to write these and similar statements, which occur everywhere in his works, even in those books that were demonstrably published after he had fallen into the dogma of Montanus, unless he believed that he shared the same dogmas of faith as the apostolic Churches. Tertullian certainly was not unaware that the new prophecy was condemned by the Roman Church [Col. 0092A], and that the discipline introduced by Montanus was rejected. Yet everywhere he proclaims the authority of the Roman Church and the other apostolic Churches, and exalts it in a remarkable manner; and he decrees that those who do not agree in one and the same faith with the apostolic Churches are alien to Christianity, the Apostles, and Christ. This is assuredly the most certain proof that the Montanists were not persuaded that they had fallen away from the communion of the Catholic Church in matters of faith.

Finally, to pass over the innumerable statements that might be adduced here to the same effect, Tertullian’s judgment was that the true Church of Christ was not restricted to the Montanist sect alone, but that Catholics also belonged to one and the same body of the Church, [Col. 0092B] whose members, although they did not follow precisely the same institutions of conduct, were nevertheless bound together by the same faith and the same sacraments. This is clearly shown by what he argues against the pagans concerning the breadth of the Christian name and Church [Note: [Col. 0092D] *Apology*, chap. 37]:

> “We are but of yesterday, and yet we have filled everything that belongs to you: cities, islands, fortresses, towns, meeting places, the very camps, tribes, companies, the palace, the senate, and the forum. We leave you only the temples. For what war would we not have been fit and ready, even though unequal in resources—we who so willingly allow ourselves to be slaughtered—if under this discipline it were not more lawful to be killed than to kill?

> “Even unarmed and without rebelling, but merely separating from you, we could have fought against you by the hostile act of separation alone. For if so great a multitude of people had broken away from you [Col. 0092C] into some remote corner of the world, the loss of so many citizens, of whatever sort, would certainly have covered your rule with shame; indeed, it would have punished you by that very abandonment. Without doubt you would have been terrified by your desolation, by the silence of affairs, and by the stupor, as it were, of a dead city; you would have sought people over whom you might rule within it. More enemies than citizens would have remained to you. As things now stand, however, you have fewer enemies because of the multitude of Christians—almost all your citizens, almost all citizens being Christians. But you preferred to call us enemies of the human race rather than enemies of human error.”

These words certainly cannot be understood of the Montanists alone, for that sect never expanded its boundaries so widely. Nor can they be understood of Christians insofar as heretics too are included under that name. For in the same book [Note: [Col. 0092D] Ibid., chap. 46], he plainly excludes heretics from the number of Christians:

> “But someone will say that some of our own people also depart from the rule of discipline: they then cease to be regarded by us as Christians.”

This Church, therefore, whose blameless and most holy conduct he describes and defends throughout the book, and from whose bosom, he says, many—and at that time innumerable—martyrs had come forth, whose blood was the seed of Christians, included neither heretics nor Montanists alone, but especially Catholics. These, as the same Tertullian says [Note: [Col. 0092D] *On Fasts*, chap. 40], boasted of their multitude; and it is established that from among them [Col. 0093A], more than from other sects, innumerable martyrs were crowned. Nevertheless, since in Tertullian’s judgment, at the time when he published the *Apology*—namely, in the midst of intense persecution—the Montanists themselves were still counted among the Catholics, it is clear that, in his view, they and the true Catholics constituted one and the same Church of Christ. Although that Church did not everywhere employ precisely the same institutions of conduct, it was nevertheless to be regarded as one and the same body of religion because it agreed upon the same dogmas of faith and employed the same sacraments of faith.

VIII. Thirdly, it must be noted—something that likewise follows naturally from the foregoing—that Catholic martyrs were regarded by the Montanists as true martyrs, and that the Montanists sought in some manner a path to communion with them. [Col. 0093B] Asterius Urbanus recorded that Catholic martyrs abhorred communion with the Montanists, but not, conversely, that the Montanists rejected communion with Catholic martyrs [Note: [Col. 0093D]

> Ὃθεν τοι καὶ ἐπειδὰν οἱ ἐπὶ τὸ τῆς κατὰ ἀλήθειαν πίστεως μαρτύριον πληθέντες ἀπὸ τῆς Ἐκκλησίας τύχωσι μετά τινων τῶν ἀπὸ τῆς τῶν Φρυγῶν αἱρέσεως λεγομένων μαρτύρων, διαφέρονταί τε πρὸς αὐτοὺς, καὶ μὴ κοινωνήσαντες αὐτοῖς τελειοῦνται, διὰ τὸ μὴ βούλεσθαι συγκαταθέσθαι τῷ διὰ Μοντανοῦ καὶ τῶν γυναικῶν πνεύματι· καὶ ὅτι τοῦτο ἀληθὲς καὶ ἐπὶ τῶν ἡμετέρων χρόνων ἐν Ἀπαμείᾳ τῇ πρὸς Μαιάνδρῳ τυγχάνει γεγενημένον, ἐν τοῖς περὶ Γάϊον καὶ Ἀλέξανδρον ἀπὸ Εὐμενείας μαρτυρήσασι, πρόδηλον.

> “Hence, whenever members of the Church who have been brought to martyrdom for the true faith happen to encounter some of those from the Phrygian heresy who are called martyrs, they differ from them and attain their end without having entered into communion with them, because they are unwilling to assent to the spirit that speaks through Montanus and the women. That this is true is manifest from what occurred in our own times at Apamea on the Maeander in the case of Gaius and Alexander of Eumeneia, who suffered martyrdom.”

In Eusebius, Book V, chap. 16]:

> “Therefore,” he says, “whenever men of the Church called to undergo martyrdom for the true faith have happened by chance to come together with certain persons from the Phrygian heresy who are called martyrs, they always disagree with them; and, carefully avoiding communion with them, they attain the glorious end of martyrdom, since they consider it impious to assent to the spirit of Montanus and the women. That this is true is established by what was done in our own age in the city of Apamea, situated on the Maeander, by the martyrs Gaius and Alexander, natives of Eumeneia.”

Clear proofs of this matter [Col. 0093C] are also available to us from the same Tertullian, first of all from his book *To the Martyrs*. Since this was published while persecution was raging, there is no doubt that it belongs to the class of works he composed while serving in the camp of Montanus.

First, it is certain that this book was dedicated to persons who, even in Tertullian’s judgment, were true martyrs of Christ, since he heaps innumerable praises upon them and repeatedly calls them blessed [Note: [Col. 0093D] Chap. 1]:

> “Among the provisions for the flesh, O blessed martyrs-designate. First, therefore, O blessed ones, etc. [Note: [Col. 0093D] Chap. 2]. Wherefore you, O blessed ones, from prison, etc. [Note: [Col. 0093D] Chap. 3]. So now, O blessed ones. Accordingly, you blessed women, etc. [Note: [Col. 0093D] Chap. 4]. These things, O blessed ones, are not without reason, etc.”

Secondly, it is certain that those upon whom he heaps so many blessings and whom he extols with praises reaching to heaven were not Montanist [Col. 0093D] martyrs, but Catholics, or at least were for the most part drawn from the Catholics. This is established from the first chapter, where he exhorts them to mutual peace in these words [Col. 0094A]:

> “Certain persons who do not possess this peace in the Church have been accustomed to entreat it from the martyrs in prison. And for that very reason you too ought to possess and preserve it among yourselves, so that perhaps you may be able to bestow it upon others also.”

For this was a Catholic custom: those who had been cast out of communion for capital offenses implored the aid of the martyrs. Moreover, Catholics would certainly not have had recourse to Montanists, whom they knew to disapprove of such a custom and practice of the Catholic Church.

Thirdly, it is certain that Tertullian by no means believed the martyrs to whom he addressed a book of this kind to be alien from his own communion. For he asks that, among the provisions for the flesh which the Church supplied to them, they also accept from him this little gift, which would contribute to nourishing the spirit. This is assuredly an indubitable proof [Col. 0094B] of mutual communion.

Finally, it is certain that the Church which, like a devoted mother, sustained the praised martyrs with provisions for the flesh was, even in Tertullian’s judgment, the true Church of Christ:

> “Among the provisions for the flesh, O blessed martyrs-designate, which our lady mother the Church supplies to you in prison from her breasts, and which the individual brethren supply from their own resources, receive something also from us that may contribute to the nourishment of the spirit as well.”

Yet the Catholic Church would not have been concerned for any but Catholic martyrs. Furthermore, among the principal excellences of the Roman Church, on account of which, as we saw above, he commends it so highly, he also lists this: that it exhorted its own people to martyrdom. “It exhorts to martyrdom,” he says. But what praise or glory would there have been in producing for Christ not genuine but adulterous [Col. 0094C] martyrs? Or who can doubt whether Tertullian held the true martyrs of Christ in honor and desired communion with them?

Lastly, Basnage himself, I think, will not deny that the greater part of those martyrs whose faith, constancy, and multitude Tertullian so frequently proclaims both in the *Apology* and in the book *To Scapula*, as well as elsewhere, were Catholics—unless perhaps he wishes to deprive the Catholic, and therefore true, Church of Christ of this exceptional praise: that it was the most fruitful mother of holy martyrs.

IX. Once these points have been observed, I think that no difficulty remains in the arguments proposed by Basnage. For who should wonder—if he has attended to the matters we have discussed thus far—who, I ask, should wonder that a Montanist author wished to publish the acts of Catholic martyrs [Col. 0094D] and exhorted his own people to enter into communion with them? Is this more surprising than the fact that an author who was likewise a Montanist addressed to them a book filled with their praises—surely a token of communion? Or than the fact that he commended so highly the communion of the apostolic Churches, among whose principal members martyrs are numbered?

Who should also wonder that Tertullian lavished great praise upon Perpetua and adorned her with the encomium of a most courageous martyr? Is it not more remarkable that, in a book dedicated to Catholic martyrs, he should heap so many blessings upon them and say that the “director of their contest” (*agonothetes*) was [Note: [Col. 0094D] Chap. 3]:

> “the living God; their master of the training ground (*xystarches*) [Col. 0095A], the Holy Spirit; the prize, the crown of eternity; their citizenship in heaven, that of the angelic substance; their glory, for ever and ever”?

Finally, who should wonder that he wished to use Perpetua’s visions to prove that, before the day of the solemn judgment, martyrs alone are received into heaven? This is assuredly no more remarkable than the fact that, in a book published against the Church, he mentions with great praise the martyr Rutilius and uses his example to prove that one must not flee persecution, because flight is of no avail if God does not will it:

> “Rutilius, a most holy martyr, although he had so often fled persecution from place to place,” he says [Note: [Col. 0095D] *On Flight in Persecution*, chap. 5], “and had even redeemed himself from danger, as he thought, with money, was unexpectedly arrested after all the security he had provided for himself and was brought before the governor. He was torn by tortures [Col. 0095B]—I believe, as chastisement for his flight—and was then given to the flames; and he credited to the mercy of God the suffering he had avoided. What else did the Lord wish to demonstrate to us by this example except that one must not flee, because flight is of no avail if God does not will it?”

Basnage, I think, will not say that this man was a Montanist martyr, since he believed it lawful to provide for his safety by flight and to buy off danger with money—things that were regarded as unlawful under the discipline of Montanus. Nevertheless, Tertullian heaps so many and such great encomia upon him and employs his example, as that of a most holy martyr, to prove the position he was maintaining.

He also mentions with praise Mavilus, the martyr of Hadrumetum, in his book *To Scapula*, and uses his example to prove the position he was maintaining: namely, that no city [Col. 0095C] and no persecutor would shed Christian blood with impunity [Note: [Col. 0095D] Chap. 3]:

> “We also hope that in your case it was only a warning: after you had condemned Mavilus of Hadrumetum to the beasts, this affliction immediately followed; and now, from the same cause, comes this summons concerning blood.”

Will Basnage therefore dare, on so slight a conjecture—namely, that he was praised by Tertullian—to pronounce this man too a martyr of the Montanist sect?

As I have shown, the Montanists did not hold the Psychics in such contempt that they failed to distinguish them from heretics, regarded them as belonging to an alien communion in matters of faith, or treated their martyrs also with ridicule. Nor did Tertullian, although most hostile to the Catholics and a most ardent defender of Montanus, ever advance to such a point of madness that he either denied the crown of martyrdom to those who had died as martyrs in the bosom of the Catholic Church [Col. 0095D], or grudged them the praises and encomia they deserved.

X. So that we may be less surprised at this conduct among the Montanists, there is no lack of similar examples among heretics. For with what great praises do Socrates and Sozomen, although Novatians, adorn not only Catholic martyrs but also Catholic teachers? I do not mean those martyrs or teachers who flourished in the Catholic Church before Novatian created his schism, but those who arose afterward, and concerning whom the authors just praised knew perfectly well that the Novatians numbered them among the heretics.

For with what encomia do they not honor Hosius, Athanasius, Alexander of Constantinople, [Col. 0096A] Basil the Great, Gregory the Theologian, and other Catholic teachers of that kind? What authority do those same writers not grant to the Nicene Fathers? What reverence and honor do they not show to that great council, although it numbered the Cathari, or Novatians, among the heretics?

What, moreover, shall I say of those martyrs who suffered under Julian the Apostate, under the Arian emperor Valens, and in Persia under Sapor? With what praises are they celebrated by Socrates and Sozomen? Do they not number them among the true and genuine martyrs of Christ? Do they not publish their Acts, insofar as the plan followed in writing their histories permitted? Do they not ascribe to them the same authority as to the ancient martyrs, although they were well aware that these men belonged not to the Novatian but to the Catholic communion? [Col. 0096B]

And shall we then wonder if a Montanist author collected the Acts of certain Catholic martyrs and committed them to writing? If Tertullian called them most courageous martyrs and granted some authority and honor to their revelations? Shall we suppose that this constitutes the gravest presumption against their orthodoxy, and that they must consequently be removed from the number of Catholic martyrs and surrendered to the Montanists?

If that is so, then so many most holy teachers—indeed, even the Nicene Fathers themselves—so many most courageous martyrs, and so many most devout monks whose praises neither Socrates nor Sozomen passed over in silence must also be erased from the diptychs of the Catholics. Let them, I say, be erased from the diptychs of the Catholic Churches and assigned to the Novatians, merely because Novatian writers [Col. 0096C] bestowed so many and such great encomia upon them.

XI. But I believe not only that no prejudice against the holy martyrs can be created from the testimony of a Montanist author, but that from it I can fashion a most weighty argument in their favor. For a Montanist writer would not have concealed—or indicated only in ambiguous and obscure words—that the most courageous champions who became martyrs belonged to his sect; that the new prophecy had produced such fruits; and that, through the stricter discipline he had introduced, Montanus had trained even women unsuited to combat for contests of this kind.

On the contrary, he would have openly proclaimed these and other similar matters in plain words, and, whether seasonably or unseasonably, would have impressed them upon his readers on almost every page. Do we not know how heretics boast concerning such matters, how they are accustomed to take empty pride in them, and, when a similar opportunity presents itself, to rage with the greatest shamelessness against Catholics?

An example may be found in the Donatist author who committed to literary record the Acts of the holy martyrs Saturninus, Dativus, and their companions. For although the martyrs just praised did not belong to the Donatist sect—since the schism was not created under the pretext of the surrender of the Scriptures until after the persecution had subsided—nevertheless, because they suffered rather than surrender the divine Scriptures to the persecutors, and seemed by their example to have condemned the *traditores* in advance and cast them out of their communion, it is remarkable how much the writer boasts on that account, and with what shamelessness that most insolent author attacks the Catholics:

> “When the devil had been conquered and overthrown [Col. 0097A],” he says [Note: [Col. 0096D] In the preface], “all the martyrs, bearing the victorious palm in their suffering, sealed with their own blood the sentence against the *traditores* and their associates by which they had cast them out of the communion of the Church. For it was not lawful that martyrs and *traditores* should exist together in the Church.”

Does anything similar occur in the Montanist author? It is remarkable how modestly and respectfully he conducts himself. He does indeed say that he acknowledges and honors new visions and prophecies; but when committing the visions and heavenly revelations of Perpetua and Saturus to writing, he does not on that account insult the Catholics who refused to receive the new prophets. He does indeed exhort his own people to enter into communion with the martyrs; but he does not therefore deter them from communion with the Catholics who rejected the new prophets, as the Donatist author does. [Col. 0097B] Whence did a heretical man derive such modesty, unless because he knew perfectly well that he was committing to writing the deeds not of Montanist but of Catholic martyrs?

# CHAPTER III  
## A GENERAL REPLY IS GIVEN TO BASNAGE’S INTERNAL ARGUMENTS AGAINST OUR MARTYRS

I. Basnage’s internal arguments against the holy martyrs. II. The credibility of the compiler of the Acts should be rejected rather than the orthodoxy of the holy martyrs called into doubt. III. Reasons are adduced by which the genuineness (*γνησιότης*) of the Acts might be called into question. The first reason is drawn from certain foreign expressions that are by no means appropriate to Perpetua, by whom the greater part of the Acts is said to have been written. [Col. 0097C] IV. The second reason is drawn from the similarity of style, which points to one and the same writer, although the Acts are said to have been written by three different persons. V. Neither the authority of Tertullian nor that of a contemporary writer would prevent us from doubting the credibility of the Acts if they truly contained anything unbecoming Catholic martyrs.

I. In the two preceding chapters we have treated what are called the external arguments; in the following chapters we shall examine the internal ones. The learned Basnage discusses them in the following manner:

> “The inherent arguments surpass these”—that is, the external arguments. “For Perpetua conducts herself as though she had the Holy Spirit at her command, [Col. 0097D] enclosed like some jewel in a casket, ready to employ his illuminations at her own discretion whenever she wished. Let her words in chap. I, §3 be heard:

> “‘My brother said to me: “Lady sister, you are now established in great dignity—so great that you may ask for a vision, and it may be shown to you whether what awaits is martyrdom or a reprieve.” And I, knowing that I conversed with the Lord, from whom I had experienced such great benefits, confidently promised him, saying: “Tomorrow I shall report it to you.”’

> “A remarkable confidence indeed, and worthy only of the household of Montanus! Nor are the visions presented to Perpetua joined with sufficient gravity and propriety. A man dressed as a shepherd ‘gave Perpetua, from the cheese he was milking, what looked like a little morsel.’ From this she concludes that death is imminent.

> “More remarkable still is what Perpetua relates in chap. III, §2:

> “‘There came out against me an Egyptian, hideous in appearance, [Col. 0098A] who was to fight me with his assistants. Then handsome young men came to me as my assistants and supporters; and I was stripped, and became a man.’

> “Perpetua therefore goes out to fight the Egyptian with her sex changed, to receive as the prize of victory ‘a green branch upon which there were golden apples.’

> “‘And he called for silence and said: “If this Egyptian defeats her, he will kill her with the sword; and if she defeats him, she will receive this branch.” Then he withdrew. And we approached one another and began to exchange blows. He tried to seize my feet, but I struck his face with my heels.’

> “Are these things worthy of the Holy Spirit? Did he ever present anything similar to the prophets? These, of course, are the visions to which the sacred oracle of Joel is to be applied! Dreams of this kind altogether smell of the workshop of Montanus. They are closely akin to the ecstasy by which Tertullian [Col. 0098B] proves that the soul is ‘of an airy color, and in every respect of human form’ [Note: [Col. 0098D] *On the Soul*, chap. 9].”

II. To all these difficulties I first offer this general reply: if the visions attributed to Perpetua truly contained anything unworthy either of the Holy Spirit or of a martyr, it would be the authenticity of the Acts, rather than the orthodoxy of the martyrs, that ought to be called into question.

Saint Augustine was the first to employ this reply. For when Perpetua’s vision—to be examined below—in which she saw her brother Dinocrates first amid punishments and afterward in a place of refreshment, was objected to him, his first reply was [Note: [Col. 0098D] Book I *On the Soul and Its Origin*, chap. 10] that:

> “the writing itself is not canonical, nor did she write it in that manner—or whoever wrote it, etc.”

By these words he seems to doubt whether Perpetua actually committed her revelations to writing, and therefore [Col. 0098C] not to grant complete credibility to the Acts and their editor. For the editor plainly teaches that Perpetua and Saturus wrote down with their own hands the passages concerning visions and revelations. Near the beginning he says:

> “From this point she herself will relate the entire course of her martyrdom, as she left it written in her own hand and according to her own understanding. ‘While we were still,’ she says, ‘with the persecutors, and my father was trying to turn me away, etc.’”

And below:

> “‘Then my brother said to me, etc.’”

And so both she and Saturus always speak in their own persons. And in chap. III, §2:

> “‘This is what I did up to the day before the spectacle; but as for the events of the spectacle itself, let whoever wishes write them.’”

And immediately afterward, chap. IV, §1:

> “But the blessed Saturus also made known this vision of his, which he himself wrote down.”

And below, chap. V, §1:

> “These are the more notable visions of the most blessed martyrs themselves, which they themselves wrote down.”

And finally, in chap. V, §3, the editor says that he is carrying out Perpetua’s command and committing to the memory of posterity the events that occurred afterward, especially on the very day of the spectacle, just as she had desired:

> “Since, therefore, the Holy Spirit permitted—and by permitting willed—that the course of the spectacle itself should be written down, although we are unworthy to record the supplement to so great a glory, we nevertheless execute what was, as it were, the command of the most holy Perpetua—indeed, a trust committed by her, etc.”

Anyone, therefore, who calls into doubt [Col. 0099A] a matter so often attested and insistently asserted by the author plainly does not place complete confidence in his statements. Yet nowhere does Saint Augustine call into question whether Perpetua was a woman of the Church or of the Montanist sect. Wherever he mentions her—and he mentions her very frequently—he celebrates her as a most holy and most invincible martyr. Hence, in Augustine’s judgment, it would be the credibility of the Acts, rather than the orthodoxy of the martyrs, that ought to be called into question if anything occurred in the Acts that was unworthy either of the Holy Spirit or of true martyrs of Christ.
