What you destroy with your fingers, Ponticus, is a human being.

For although among the jurists an offspring not yet brought forth is said not yet to be a human being, Paul nevertheless rightly testifies, in the section *On the Status of Human Beings* [Col. 0320C], that one who is in the womb is protected just as though he were already among human affairs. How such an offspring is spoken of by the jurists before birth is evident from comparison with the words of Papinian on the *Lex Falcidia*, *Digest* XXXV, tit. 2, § 9:

> Under the Falcidian law it was settled that fruits subsequently gathered, if they were ripe at the time of death, increase the valuation of the inheritance on account of the farm, which is deemed to have been more valuable at that time. In the case of a pregnant slave woman, no distinction based on time has been admitted; and rightly so, because an offspring not yet brought forth is not properly said to have been a human being.

The discussion concerns legacies and inheritances. Tertullian, *On the Soul*, ch. 25, calls that unformed thing, not yet a human being, *pecus*; and the membrane enclosing the fetus was called by the ancients [non-Latin text omitted], as though one were to say “the skin of a lamb.” Aristotle, therefore, must not be listened to either: wishing to discourage an excessive multitude of citizens in a single state, he proposes that the seed be destroyed and made abortive before it can be called a human being. His words are in *Politics* VII, ch. 16.]

—and so too is one who will be; for every fruit is already present in the seed.

[Note: [Col. 0320C] **For every fruit is already present in the seed.** On Zeno [Col. 0320D] the Stoic, Theodoret, p. 73: [non-Latin text omitted]. For he believed that the generative seed of a human being, which is observed to be endowed with moisture and spirit, is a part of the soul and, as it were, a cutting from it. Thus Plato’s impious laws, in the same author, p. 131: [non-Latin text omitted], etc.:

> But when men and women have passed beyond the age for procreation, we shall allow them to associate as they please, warning them only not to bring any further conceived fetus into the light. But if anyone violates this ordinance and carries the fetus to birth, he must then expose it in such a way that it is entirely deprived of nourishment.

Others read: “and every fruit is already in the seed.” Rhenanus: “and the fruit of a human being.”]

Concerning nourishment by blood and tragic dishes of this sort, read whether it is reported anywhere [it is in Herodotus, I think] that certain nations established a covenant by tasting blood poured from the arms of each party.

[Note: [Col. 0320D] **Concerning nourishment by blood.** He turns back upon his adversaries the charge concerning the eating of raw flesh and drinking of blood; on this custom the commentators on Minucius have collected much.]

They also say that among certain Scythian peoples every deceased person is eaten by his own relatives.

[Note: [Col. 0322A] The Fulda manuscript reads “bandagers.” Perhaps “watchers.” Havercamp.]

Likewise, where are those who dine on wild-beast dishes from the arena, who seek meat from the boar or the stag? That boar wiped away, while struggling, the blood of the man whom it had bloodied; that stag tossed itself about in a gladiator’s blood.

[Note: [Col. 0322C] **They dine on wild-beast dishes.** Minucius, p. 34, sketched this whole passage:

> No different are those who devour from the arena beasts smeared with gore or fattened on human limbs and entrails.

How would Ovid characterize them, when in his *Metamorphoses* XV, verse 88, Pythagoras speaks against even the eating of beasts?

> Alas, what wickedness it is for entrails to be buried in entrails,  
> For a greedy body to grow fat upon an accumulated body,

[Note: [Col. 0322C] **While struggling.** The Fulda manuscript has “struggling”; Barraeus, “fighting.” But Rhenanus and manuscript Lugd. [Note: [Col. 0322A] Paris edition: “lay.” Havercamp: “That stag tossed itself in a gladiator’s blood.”]

[Note: [Col. 0322C] **Lay in a gladiator’s blood.** So manuscript L.B. and the editions of Rhenanus and Rigaltius. Barraeus, Heraldus, and Pamelius, however, have “tossed”; much the same appears in the Fulda manuscript, or Modius collation: “tossed the blood.” I think the text should be restored as: “that stag refreshed itself with a gladiator’s blood.” Caecilius, cited by Nonius under *lactare*:

> What an overflow, what pleasure, what liberality delights you?

That is, soothes, delights, affects; or: “refreshed itself in gladiatorial blood.” Cicero, in the same place: “Malice delighting in another’s misfortune”; and this is most true. For if it merely tossed or lay in it, once the bloody hide had been removed no crime remained, nor any trace of human blood; but if it drank the gore, the matter is now different.

I judge that it will not be unwelcome to insert here why the Egyptians are said by some to have abstained from pork. Aelian, *On Animals* X.16, censures the gluttony of swine, which do not abstain even from eating human flesh: [non-Latin text omitted], etc. That is:

> The swine is so devoted to filling its belly that it does not spare even its own offspring, nor does it restrain its voracity from the human body. This is why among the Egyptians an animal of this sort is held in the greatest hatred as unclean, detestable, and exceedingly voracious.

[Col. 0323B] The argument of our Septimius must be understood as follows: These animals, whose flesh you greedily devour, killed many of their opponents before they themselves were laid low in the arena by beast-fighters. Thus the boar wiped away and drank the gore which it drew from a wound with its tusks while trampling a half-dead man; the stag, wearied and thirsty, greedily gulped it down as a drink; and the bear even buried the man himself within its belly. Martial I, epigram 44:

> After such deeds let no boar be served to you;  
> Rather, let you be served to the boar, Charidemus.

Tertullian, *On the Shows*, ch. XII: “unless human bodies were also torn apart by wild beasts.” And ch. XXI: “In the same amphitheater the same spectator, with the most enduring eyes, leans over from above to view bodies gnawed and torn apart and befouled in their own blood.”]

The bellies of bears themselves are eagerly sought while they are still suffering indigestion from human entrails.

[Note: [Col. 0322A] Junius: “while slaughtering.” Havercamp: “while battering” or “thrusting.”]

> What pleasure fed on blood?

Understand this from Seneca, *Natural Questions* II.3:

> The hand, bones, sinews, and eyes are part of us; their material is the fluid of digested food, destined to pass into those parts.

You who eat these things—how far removed are you from the banquets of Christians? Do those who, with savage lust, gape after human limbs do any less because they devour them alive? Are they any less consecrated to filth through human blood because they lick blood that is yet to be?

No, they do not eat infants, certainly, but rather adults. Let your error blush before the Christians. We do not include even the blood of animals among edible banquet foods; for that reason we also abstain from strangled animals and carrion, lest we be contaminated by blood in any form, even when it is buried within the entrails.

[Note: [Col. 0323D] **Blood among edible banquet foods.** Elmenhorstius illustrated this with many observations on the passage in Minucius, explaining from it Acts XV.27, which we explained differently above. In the same place he teaches that Christians of a later period sometimes abstained from meat altogether, from this passage of Prudentius’s *Cathemerinon*:

> Far removed be that hunger, etc.,  
> That delights in tearing bloody meals  
> From the slaughtered bodies of livestock.  
> Let feasts derived from the death of quadrupeds  
> Belong to untamed nations;  
> Let the foliage of vegetables and the pod,  
> Pregnant with many kinds of pulses,  
> Feed us with harmless banquets.

Hence Blandina in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* V.1, in Rufinus’s words:

> You are greatly mistaken, gentlemen, in supposing that those who do not even eat the flesh of dumb animals feed upon the entrails of infants.

Note particularly the phrase “edible banquet foods.” Minucius says *edulium*, terms signifying the more sumptuous feasts to which Christians sometimes invited one another as circumstances permitted. If, then, even in their delicacies they did not consume the blood of beasts, how could they gulp down human blood? Domitian too won praise among the Romans for clemency on this account—but only at the beginning of his reign—when he quoted Virgil’s verse:

> With what impiety has mankind feasted upon slaughtered bullocks!

He went so far as to intend to issue an edict forbidding the sacrifice of oxen, as Suetonius relates in his *Life*, ch. IX. Evanthius the Abbot, a pious and ancient writer, composed a treatise on this subject. HAV.]

What kind of thing is it, moreover, that you believe people whom you trust to recoil from the blood of cattle to be eager for human blood—unless perhaps you have found it sweeter by experience? [Note: [Col. 0324C] **Like the sacrificial hearth and incense-box.** By these means those who held life dearer than their soul sometimes purchased their lives. *Against Marcion* I.27:

> Why, in persecutions, do you not immediately gain your life by denial when the incense-box is offered?

[Note: [Col. 0324A] Paris edition: “likewise.”]

Otherwise they would have to be killed if they tasted it, just as if they had not sacrificed.

[Note: [Col. 0324D] **They would have to be killed if they tasted it.** Heraldus, Wouwerius, and Rigaltius prefer this reading; the Fulda manuscript approaches it, reading “they would have to be killed if they did not taste it.” Yet the other reading also rests on no feeble support: “they would have to be denied as Christians if they did not taste it, just as if they had sacrificed.” Pamelius, La Cerda, and Junius accepted this, and before them Barraeus and Gelenius; manuscript Lugd. Again, who are more incestuous than those whom Jupiter himself taught?

[Note: [Col. 0325B] **Jupiter himself taught.** Seneca, in the *Apocolocyntosis*, on Claudius:

> That man a god, and from Jupiter? Jupiter, so far as it lay in Claudius’s power, was condemned by him for incest. For he killed L. Silanus, his own son-in-law. Why, I ask? He preferred to call the most charming sister of all young women—whom everyone called Venus—Juno.

I punctuate and explain this as follows: Can that man become a Jovian god, so to speak—the expression resembles Lucretius’s “Ceres from Iacchus,” that is, the mother of Iacchus, and Tertullian’s frequent “concerning prudence,” “concerning justice,” etc.—who condemned Jupiter for incest in the person of Silanus, whom he killed because Silanus was said to have treated his sister Junia Calvina as Jupiter treated Juno, that is, to have taken her as his wife? And just as our author adds the example of the Persians, so Lucian wittily says that Jupiter married several other wives, but finally his own full sister Juno, and did so according to the custom of the Persians and Assyrians. His words occur in the dialogue *On Sacrifices*: [non-Latin text omitted]. HAV.]

Ctesias reports that the Persians have intercourse with their own mothers. But the Macedonians too are suspect, because when they first heard the tragedy of Oedipus, they laughed at the anguish over incest and used to say [non-Latin text omitted], [non-Latin text omitted].

[Note: [Col. 0325A] In the manuscripts: [non-Latin text omitted] or [non-Latin text omitted], “they said,” [non-Latin text omitted]. [Note: [Col. 0325C] “[non-Latin text omitted], they used to say, [non-Latin text omitted].” Since this passage in the manuscripts of the *Apology* is variously expressed, learned commentators have extracted many ridiculous offspring from it, as can be seen in their notes. At last Rigaltius [Col. 0325D] fashioned his own [non-Latin text omitted], “they used to say,” [non-Latin text omitted], as though they had shouted to Oedipus as their leader and king, “Come, lead the attack upon your mother,” as though he were about to storm a city or fortress; and he inserted the same reading into *To the Nations* I.16 against the authority of the Agobardinus manuscript. First and foremost, you expose your sons so that they may be taken up by some passing stranger’s compassion, or emancipate them for adoption by better parents.

> Not only the person who suffocates a newborn child is deemed to kill it, but also the person who exposes it in public places to the compassion which he himself does not possess.

I cannot refrain from citing a passage from Lactantius, but in a more emended form. At VI.20 the common editions have:

> Who can doubt that he is impious who leaves no place for another’s compassion? Indeed, even if what he wished should occur, so that the child is nourished, he has certainly sentenced his own blood either to slavery or to the brothel.

At least in my copy, this says anything but what it appears to intend. Restore: “those who grant a place to another’s compassion, not their own”; the words that follow there require this. The memory of an estranged family line must at some point be dispersed; and once an error has struck, thereafter the hereditary shoot of incest will spread, as the family line creeps onward together with the crime. [Note: [Col. 0326D] **From that eventuality.** A most beautiful encomium of Christian continence. Minucius, p. 85:

## CHAPTER X

**ARGUMENT.—He now proceeds to the charge of irreligion and says that Christians refuse the worship of the gods because they are not gods, though they should be worshipped if their divinity could be demonstrated. He therefore appeals to the conscience of the Gentiles, which cannot deny that all those gods were once human beings—a point he now clearly demonstrates by the example of Saturn alone.**

[Note: [Col. 0327C] **You say, “You do not worship the gods.”** This occurs frequently in Arnobius, as in Book I:

> Are we therefore accused among you of an impious religion, and are we called ill-omened and atheists because we approach the Head and Pillar of all things with reverential homage—to use your abusive language?

Book V:

> You dare to call any of these men an atheist, irreligious, and sacrilegious because they either deny altogether that the gods exist or doubt it.

And Book VI:

> For on this point you are accustomed to stir up the most turbulent hostility against us, to call us atheists, and to say that we grant the gods very little.

See more in the commentators on Arnobius. HAV.]

You say, “You do not worship the gods, and you do not offer sacrifices on behalf of the emperors.” It follows that for the same reason we do not sacrifice on behalf of others, since we do not sacrifice even on behalf of [Col. 0328A] ourselves, once we have ceased to worship the gods. Accordingly, we are arraigned on charges of sacrilege and treason.

[Note: [Col. 0327C] **Sacrilege.** An abusive charge immediately cast at Christians; above, ch. II: “When you conduct an inquiry concerning some criminal,” etc.; and in the same place: “to order the sacrilegious man to be torn apart if he persists in confessing.” Also *To Scapula*, ch. II:

> Yet you have never caught us, whom you consider sacrilegious, even in theft, much less in sacrilege.

Indeed, the customary cry of the populace when demanding the punishment of innocent people was: [Col. 0327D] “Away with the sacrilegious!” Rufinus, from Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* IV, has the judge say to Polycarp:

> Show respect for your age and spare your extreme old age; swear by Caesar’s fortune, repent of your former conduct, and cry out yourself as well: “Away with the sacrilegious!”

In the same place, concerning Polycarp’s death:

> When the assembled multitude had been struck with amazement by so remarkable a death and had begun to marvel at the courage of the whole Christian people in despising death, they all cried out: “Away with the sacrilegious!”

From comparison with these, light may be cast upon what we read concerning the Jews’ cry against the Savior in Luke XXIII.18: [non-Latin text omitted]. The same happened to Paul in Acts XXI.36: [non-Latin text omitted], and much more so in XXII.22: [non-Latin text omitted]. HAV.]

This is the chief ground of the case—indeed, the whole of it—and certainly one worthy of investigation, provided neither prejudice nor injustice passes judgment, the one because it despairs of the truth, the other because it rejects it.

[Note: [Col. 0328B] Paris edition: “despairs.”]

[Note: [Col. 0328B] Paris edition: “rejects.”]

We have ceased to worship your gods from the moment we recognized that they are not gods.

[Note: [Col. 0327D] **We have ceased to worship.** An excellent declaration of repentance, matched by the bold freedom of Subrius Flavus, a tribune convicted of participating in the conspiracy against Nero, in Tacitus, *Annals* XV [Col. 0328B].67. When Nero asked him for what reasons he had proceeded to forget his military oath, he answered:

> I hated you; yet no soldier was more faithful to you while you deserved to be loved. I began to hate you after you became the murderer of your mother and wife, a charioteer, an actor, and an incendiary.

This, then, is what you ought to require: that we prove they are not gods and therefore must not be worshipped, since they ought to have been worshipped only if they had been gods. Christians would then also deserve punishment if it were established that those whom they did not worship, because they supposed them not to exist as gods, really were gods.

“But to us,” you say, “they are gods.” We appeal from you to yourselves, and summon your own conscience. Let it judge us; let it condemn us, if it can deny that all those gods of yours were once human beings.

[Note: [Col. 0328B] **Were human beings.** Cicero has this in *Tusculan Disputations* I.12: [Col. 0328C]

Ask whose tombs are displayed in Greece; recollect, since you have been initiated, what is handed down in the mysteries; then at last you will understand how widely this extends.

The names of certain of them were either changed so that they became similar in sound to those of the heavenly gods, or preserved without alteration according to their earlier form—namely, the names of the Sun, Saturn, Rhea, and Jupiter himself, whom some called Ammon.
