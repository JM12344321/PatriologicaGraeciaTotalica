Finally, provision was made for this form of discipline in the law concerning the leper:

> But if the discoloration breaks out upon the skin and covers the whole skin from head to foot, as far as the eye can see, and the priest, upon seeing it, pronounces him clean because it has turned white, he is clean. [Col. 1022A] But if, after its abolition, anything of that former condition comes to life again, and there appears once more in his flesh what had been reckoned dead to sin, he is to be judged unclean and may no longer be purified by the priest. Thus adultery recurring from the former condition and staining the unity of the new color from which it had been excluded is an impurity that cannot be cleansed.

Likewise concerning a house: if stains and hollows in its walls had been reported to the priest, before he entered to inspect it he commands that everything be removed from the house, so that the things belonging to the house will not thereby become unclean. Then, after entering, if the priest found greenish or reddish hollows whose appearance lay beneath the ordinary surface of the wall, [Col. 1022B] he was to go out through the doorway and quarantine that house for seven days. For when we come to Christ, the supreme priest of the Father, all hindrances must first be removed from the house of our human being during the period of the week, so that the house remaining—the flesh and the soul—may be clean. ## CHAPTER XXI

If the Apostles knew these things better, they certainly took greater care over them. But now I shall descend even to this level, separating [Note: [Col. 1023D] *Excernens*, “separating,” for *discernens*, “distinguishing.” PAM.] the Apostles’ teaching from their power.

Discipline governs the human being; power designates him; power is one thing apart, but the Spirit is God. [Note: [Col. 1023D] “Discipline governs the human being; power designates him; power is one thing apart, but the Spirit is God.” No one will deny that these words are corrupt. Both sense and wording appear to return if we read: “Discipline governs the human being; power designates him. Apart from that, what is power? The Spirit. And the Spirit? God.” RIG.—Latinius emends this passage as follows: “Power designates; but again, what is power? Spirit. But the Spirit is God” (John 4:24). EDD.—Power designates the human being, identifies him, marks him out as exceptional, χαρακτηρίζει, “characterizes him.” Thus the power given to Peter designates Peter’s person, so that the power pertained to Peter’s person alone. This, indeed, is what Tertullian says. But most, or rather virtually all, of the Fathers thought otherwise. [Col. 1024C] RIG.]

What, then, did he teach? That one must not participate in the works of darkness (Eph. 5:11). Observe what he commands. But who had the power to forgive sins? This belongs to him alone. [Note: [Col. 1024C] “This belongs to him alone”: that is, to God, or to the Spirit. RIG.] “For who forgives sins except God alone?” (Mark 2:7)—and certainly mortal sins committed against him and against his temple. For those that make a person guilty against you, you are commanded, in the person of Peter, to forgive even seventy-seven times [Col. 1024A] (Matt. 18:22).

[Note: [Col. 1024C] “For who forgives sins except God alone?” In the first place, this was not Christ’s assertion but that of the Pharisees (Mark 2:7; Luke 5:21). Next, Christ declares that this power was communicated even to himself as a human being, when he adds: “But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins,” etc. And indeed he communicated the same authority to the Apostles in Matthew 18:18, and especially in John 20:23: “Whose sins you forgive,” he says, “are forgiven them, and whose you retain are retained.” Conquered by this truth, the Author—although he deliberately omits that scriptural passage because it contradicts him—nevertheless cannot deny that the blessed Apostles themselves granted something of this sort . . . But he distorts the matter by adding that they did so not by discipline but by power, although . . . he admits that this power was granted to his own Montanist church. PAM.]

Accordingly, even if it were established that the blessed Apostles themselves granted some such indulgence [Note: [Col. 1023C] “If it had been granted even to the blessed Apostles . . .” in the Paris edition], where pardon was properly God’s to give and not a human being’s, they would have done so not by discipline but by power. For they also raised the dead, something God alone does; they restored the infirm, something no one but Christ does; indeed, they also inflicted plagues, something Christ declined to do. For it was not fitting for him to rage who had come to suffer. Ananias and Elymas were struck down—Ananias with death, Elymas with blindness (Acts 5:5; 13:11)—so that by this very fact it might be proved [Note: [Col. 1024C] “He himself might be proved” in the Paris edition] that Christ could have done these things as well. So too the prophets had forgiven murder and, along with it, adultery to those who repented, because they had also provided demonstrations of severity.

Produce for me, therefore, even now, O apostolic one, prophetic examples, [Note: [Col. 1024D] “Produce for me, therefore, even now, O apostolic one.” He calls him “apostolic” in the same form in which he called him above “the supreme pontiff,” “bishop of bishops,” “the good shepherd,” and “the blessed pope.” RIG.—Since he is undoubtedly speaking of the Roman Pontiff, the Author confirms even from this the authority of the Roman Church, because he calls its head “apostolic,” as the successor of the Apostles, just as he does more explicitly in book IV of his *Poems Against Marcion*, chapter 8. That title has moreover remained in use to the present day. PAM.] and I shall acknowledge your divine character; [Note: [Col. 1024D] “And I shall acknowledge your divine character”: that is, “I shall acknowledge that you have been designated by divine strength and power.” RIG.] claim for yourself the power to forgive sins of this kind. But if you have been allotted only the duties of discipline, not to preside by sovereign command but to serve in ministry, who or how great are you that you should grant indulgence—you who, presenting neither prophet nor Apostle, lack the power to which the granting of indulgence belongs?

“But,” you say, “the Church possesses the power to forgive sin.” I acknowledge and arrange this claim more fully than you do, for I possess the Paraclete himself speaking in the new prophets: “The Church can forgive sin, but I shall not do so, lest others also sin.”

“What if a pseudo-prophetic spirit made that declaration?” [Note: [Col. 1024D] “What if a pseudo-prophetic spirit made that declaration?” [Col. 1025C] These are the words of the catholic, whom he calls a psychic. RIG.] Yet it would have been more characteristic of a subverter both to recommend himself for his clemency and to dispose others toward sinfulness. Or if that spirit also wished to affect such conduct in accordance with the Spirit of truth, then the Spirit of truth is indeed able to grant pardon to fornicators, but is unwilling to do so at the cost of harm to the many.

I now ask, on the basis of your own position, whence you usurp this right for the Church. Is it because the Lord said to Peter, “Upon this rock I shall build my Church; I have given you the keys of the kingdom of heaven,” or, “Whatever you bind or loose on earth will be bound or loosed in heaven” (Matt. 16:18–19)? Do you therefore presume that the power of binding and loosing has been transmitted even to you—that is, to every church akin to Peter, such as yours? [Note: [Col. 1025C] “And that the power of binding and loosing has been transmitted even to you.” Greater trust should be placed in the Author when he was still a catholic than now, when he is a heretic and writes expressly against the Church, especially in this chapter. PAM.] [Note: [Col. 1025C] “To every church akin to Peter.” That is, one able to trace its origin to Peter. But other churches too, although they can produce no Apostle or apostolic man as their founder, are nevertheless regarded as no less apostolic if they agree in the same faith, by reason of the kinship of their doctrine. You overturn and alter the Lord’s manifest intention, which conferred this personally upon Peter: “Upon you,” he says, “I shall build my Church”; and, “I shall give you the keys,” not the Church; and, “Whatever you loose or bind,” not “whatever they loose or bind”? [Note: [Col. 1025D] “Altering the Lord’s manifest intention, which conferred this personally upon Peter: ‘Upon you,’ he says, ‘I shall build my Church.’” Septimius does not correctly cite Christ’s words, which occur at Matthew 16:18. Chrysostom’s observations on them, however, are exceedingly wise. RIG.]

For the outcome itself teaches this. The Church was built upon him—that is, through him. [Col. 1025B] He himself first put the key to use. [Note: [Col. 1025D] “He himself first put the key to use.” He was the first to insert the key into the lock; he began to use the key granted by God. So too, in that dispute over whether the Law was to be observed, Peter, inspired by the Spirit, was the first of all to speak in advance concerning the calling of the nations:

> And now, why do you put the Lord to the test by imposing upon the brothers a yoke that neither we nor our fathers were able to bear? But we believe that through the grace of Jesus we shall obtain salvation, just as they will. (Acts 15:10–11)

[Col. 1026A] This judgment both loosed those provisions of the Law that were set aside and bound those that were retained. Thus the power of binding and loosing bestowed upon Peter has no application to the capital sins of believers. For if the Lord had commanded him to forgive even a brother who sinned against him seventy-seven times (Matt. 18:22), he certainly would not afterward have commanded him to bind—that is, to retain—anything, except perhaps those offenses someone committed against the Lord rather than against a brother. For it is established beforehand that sins against God are not to be forgiven, since those committed against a human being are pardoned.

What, then, does this have to do with the Church—and specifically with your church, O psychic? For, in accordance with Peter’s person, this power will belong to spiritual persons [Note: [Col. 1025D] “This power will belong to spiritual persons”: to adherents of the Paraclete, whom he opposes to the psychics. RIG.], whether Apostle or prophet. For the Church itself, properly and principally, is the Spirit [Note: [Col. 1025D] “Principally, he himself is the Spirit, in whom is the Trinity.” This is the Spirit that he attributes to Montanus; and Pamelius thinks—not without amazement at such madness—that by the Trinity he means [Col. 1026C] Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla. LE PR.] in whom is the Trinity of the one divinity: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. [Note: [Col. 1026C] “The Spirit, in whom is the Trinity of the one divinity.” He appears to have added these words as a reproach against the Roman Pontiff, as though the latter agreed with Praxeas concerning the Trinity. [Col. 1026B] He gathers that Church which the Lord located in three persons. [Note: [Col. 1026C] “Not the church that is a number of bishops.” “The Church,” he says, “will forgive sins, but the Church that is Spirit”—that is, the Church that glories in the Spirit, who is God and who alone can forgive sins—not the church that prevails by the number of its bishops. ## CHAPTER XXII

But now you pour out this power even upon your martyrs. As soon as anyone, by agreement, puts on bonds that are still soft, under the novel title of imprisonment, adulterers immediately surround him and fornicators immediately approach him. [Note: [Col. 1026D] “As soon as anyone, by agreement, puts on bonds that are still soft.” He notes the devices of certain psychics who, by prior arrangement, surrendered themselves to prison and put on bonds so that, through the reputation of a martyrdom already begun, they might deceive simple believers—men or women who perhaps had been expelled from the Church for fornication—when these came repeatedly to prison out of affection to comfort the martyr; and so that they might send them back to the Church, stained with fresh marks of lust, bearing letters of peace and communion. What sort of letters of peace could come from scoundrels of this kind, who were themselves in danger of losing their own peace? Septimius says these things about Catholics under the name of psychics, but he was by then hostile to Catholic authority. RIG.—From what follows, “He gathers that Church which the Lord located in three persons, and accordingly the Church is not a number of bishops,” I think I can infer that the Author descended even to such madness as to understand by the three in whom the Church consists Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla. PAM.] [Note: [Col. 1026C] “Confession” in Latinius, Neander, and Leopold.]

[Col. 1027A] Already petitions resound about him; already the tears of every stained person form a pool around him. No one pays more eagerly for access to the prison than those who have lost the Church. [Note: [Col. 1027C] “No one pays more eagerly for access to the prison than those who have lost the Church.” Latinius preferred “access to the Church,” so that the phrase would be understood of entry into the Church. Those who have been expelled from the Church are said to have “lost the Church,” or “access to the Church”; conversely, those to whom the rights of peace or ecclesiastical communion are restored are said to be “restored to the Church.” [Col. 1027D] RIG.]

Men and women are violated in the darkness, plainly amid conditions familiar to acts of lust, and they seek peace from those whose own peace is in peril. [Note: [Col. 1027D] “Men and women are violated in the darkness.” What the pagans once hurled against Christians was no more atrocious than these accusations that Septimius levels against his psychics. RIG.]

Others take refuge in the mines and return from there as communicants, although another martyrdom is now necessary because of their new sins committed after their martyrdom. [Note: [Col. 1027D] “Others take refuge in the mines.” Others, he says, who had been expelled for adultery or fornication, bribed the pagan overseers of the mines and took refuge there, pretending that they had been dragged off to them for the name of Christ. From there they soon returned to the peace of ecclesiastical communion, as though they had tasted martyrdom. But they returned from a place where another martyrdom had already become necessary, namely because of the offenses they contracted, in a by no means trivial form of denial, while conducting their illicit bargain at the mines through fraud. What a martyr! Living in the world among pagans, a suppliant to the condemned—namely, to the enemies of God and Christ—beholden to the adulterer and fornicator, that is, to worshipers of idols or even to persons who were themselves actual adulterers and fornicators, from whom he nevertheless purchases his own peace with the Church! [Col. 1028C] RIG.]

For who upon earth and in the flesh is without fault? What martyr, while an inhabitant of the world, does not supplicate with coins and remain beholden to the physician and the moneylender? [Note: [Col. 1028C] “A suppliant with denarii, beholden to the physician and the moneylender.” I have offered this conjecture, which I value only as highly as comparison with the manuscripts will show it deserves. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1027C] “With gifts” in Latinius.]

Suppose now that he is beneath the sword, his head already poised; [Note: [Col. 1028C] “Suppose now beneath the sword.” As though he said: “Let us grant that the men discussed above are true and undoubted martyrs.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1027C] “Over the head” in Latinius.] suppose his body is already stretched upon the cross; suppose the lion has already been granted against him at the stake; [Note: [Col. 1028D] “The lion has already been granted.” That is, after the circus crowd has shouted for a lion against the Christians. Thus in *On the Spectacles*: “There daily lions are demanded against us.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1027C] “To the lion” in Latinius.] suppose he is already bound to the axle and the fire prepared. [Note: [Col. 1028C] “Bound fast” in Semler and Oberthür.] In the very security and possession of martyrdom itself, I ask: who permits a human being to forgive what must be reserved for God—acts that God has condemned without excuse, and that not even the Apostles, themselves martyrs, so far as I know, judged pardonable? For Paul had already fought with beasts at Ephesus (1 Cor. 15:32) when he decreed destruction for the incestuous man (1 Cor. 5:3 ff.).

Let it suffice for the martyr to have purged his own sins. [Note: [Col. 1028D] “Let it suffice for the martyr,” etc. Either he is speaking of martyrs who were baptized by their martyrdom, or he means the complete purification from sins that both he himself, more than once above, and blessed Cyprian, Letter 52 to Antonianus, attribute to martyrs alone, so that nothing remains to be purified after this life. PAM.] It is the act of an ungrateful or arrogant man to scatter among others as well what he obtained as a great gift. Who pays for another person’s death by his own, except the Son of God alone? For even during his Passion he delivered the robber (Luke 23:43). He had come for this very purpose: that, himself pure of sin and entirely holy, he might die for sinners. [Note: [Col. 1028C] “Obey” in Semler and Oberthür.]

Accordingly, you who imitate him by forgiving sins, if you yourself have committed no sin, then by all means suffer for me. But if you are a sinner, how will the oil of your lamp [Col. 1027C] be able to suffice for both you and me?

I have even now a means of proving Christ. [Note: [Col. 1028D] “I have now a means of proving Christ.” That is: “I now have a means of proving that Christ forgives sins, but not the martyr.” RIG.] If Christ is in the martyr for the purpose of enabling the martyr to absolve adulterers and fornicators, let him reveal the hidden things of the heart so that he may grant sins in this way—and then he is Christ. For this was how the Lord Jesus Christ demonstrated his power:

> Why do you think evil things in your hearts? For which is easier: to say to the paralytic, “Your sins are forgiven you,” or, “Rise and walk”? Therefore, that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, I say to you, paralytic: “Rise and walk.” (Matt. 9:4 ff.)

[Col. 1028A] If the Lord took such care to prove his power that he exposed men’s thoughts and then commanded healing, lest people fail to believe that he could forgive sins, I am not permitted to believe in that same power in anyone without the same proofs.

Yet when you demand pardon from a martyr for adulterers and fornicators, you yourself admit that crimes of this kind can be washed away only by the offender’s own martyrdom, though you presume upon another person’s. If that is so, martyrdom will now be another baptism. “For I have,” he says, “another baptism” (Luke 12:50). Hence water and blood flowed from the wound in the Lord’s side, providing the material for both washings (John 19:34). Therefore I ought also to deliver another person by the first washing, if I can do so by the second.

We must press the argument home against you. [Note: [Col. 1028D] “We must press it into the bosom.” Into the bosom of the psychics, to confound their obstinacy. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1028C] “I shall press it all the way” in Latinius.] Whatever authority, whatever rationale restores ecclesiastical peace to the adulterer and fornicator must likewise come to the aid of the penitent murderer and idolater—and certainly of the denier, especially one whom cruelty caused to fall after he had struggled against tortures in the battle of confession.

Otherwise it is unworthy of God and of his mercy—which prefers the sinner’s repentance to his death—that those who fell through copulation should return to the Church more readily than those who fell through combat. The indignity compels us to speak. Will you recall defiled bodies rather than bloodied ones? Which repentance is more pitiable: that which casts down flesh that was titillated, or flesh that was torn? Which pardon is more just in every case: that implored by the voluntary sinner, or that implored by the involuntary one?

No one is compelled to deny Christ willingly; no one fornicates unwillingly. There is no force driving a person to lust except lust itself; it does not know how to be compelled to do what it desires. ## ARGUMENT

[Col. 1029C]

Learned scholars agree that this little work is a defense of the cloak, sprinkled with satirical wit, and delivered before the Carthaginians. For when he was reproached by his fellow citizens because he had exchanged the toga for the cloak—that is, because he had enlisted in Christ’s army—he answered them with this satirical defense. He was unwilling to let the charge of inconstancy adhere to him, and argued that changes of roles had always occurred throughout the whole world from the time of its foundation.
