1483.

VENETIAN EDITION II. Venice, printed by Lucas Venetus, folio. *Letters and Short Works*. At the end:

> Lucas Venetus, son of Dominicus, with his customary diligence printed at Venice, in the year of salvation 1483, on the day before the Nones of December, the most carefully revised letters of the divine Caecilius Cyprianus, that most holy and eloquent man.

(Goetze, *Merkwürdigkeiten der Dresdner Bibliothek*.) A copy also existed in the Pinelli Library. Cf. Morelli, *Catalogue*, vol. I, p. 95, no. 578. Audiffredi, in the *Catalogue of the Casanatense Library*, vol. II, p. 524, indicates explicitly that several treatises and sermons were added. Yet certainly the greater part of the material furnished by Rembolt’s edition had already been published here, as we learned from the preface of C. de P. to Jacopo Grasolario, a Venetian citizen who is said to have been especially insistent in demanding this edition; that preface is reprinted in Rembolt’s edition. Its principal advantage is that each letter and treatise is preceded by a brief summary, all of which are displayed in condensed form as a table following the preface.

UNNAMED EDITION I.—Without place, year, or printer. The first of this kind. *The Letters of Saint Cyprian*, folio. At the beginning appears:

> The table of subjects in the letters of the blessed martyr Cyprian, arranged in alphabetical order [that is, a fairly copious index of noteworthy matters], begins auspiciously.

[Col. LXVII] A short table of contents with brief summaries is appended. There are seventy-three Letters.—Three books *To Quirinus*.—A sermon of Blessed Augustine on Blessed Cyprian.—A prayer of Cyprian the martyr.—Likewise a prayer of the same author, which he uttered on the day of his passion.—The *Passion of Blessed Cyprian*.—Six books *To Quirinus* conclude the series. These occupy eighteen leaves, with signatures A and B VI. Leaf XIX is almost entirely blank, except for a few words from Jerome printed at the bottom of its second page under this title:

> From the book of the blessed Jerome *On Illustrious Men*: on the blessed Cyprian.

The letters begin on fol. XX and extend from signatures a I through R inclusive, with approximately five sheets gathered in each quire. The subscription reads:

> Here end the letters of the glorious martyr Cyprian, bishop of Carthage. With how great an eloquence and how great a learning he shone among the catholic doctors is most excellently attested by his letters, splendid as they are in verbal ornament and richness of thought.

Thereafter, under a new sequence of signatures, follow quires A–D, each of four sheets, with this heading:

> Here begins the prologue to the books of the blessed martyr Cyprian written against the Jews. This prologue is in fact a certain letter written to Quirinus.

At the end of the book:

> Here ends the third book of Caecilius Cyprianus, glorious martyr, archbishop of Carthage, and most excellent orator, addressed to Quirinus. In this book, as also in the two preceding books, the catholic faith is most powerfully defended and confirmed by testimonies from Holy Scripture against the unbelief of the Jews and the errors of the gentiles. Thanks be to God.

Debure the elder, who described this edition with the greatest accuracy in the *Catalogue de la Bibliothèque du duc de La Vallière*, vol. I, p. 131 ff., states that, apart from a few capital letters, the type resembles that seen in the Mainz edition of Cicero’s *Offices* of 1465. The characters stand midway between Gothic and Roman type. I would draw particular attention to the third book of the *Testimonies against the Jews* in this edition, since Erasmus is generally believed to have been the first to publish it from a manuscript codex of the monastery of Gembloux. This edition, therefore, was known neither to Erasmus nor to any of the later editors except Pamelius. That Pamelius knew it may be learned from a comparison with his annotations on this third book. Yet many who professed to have handled a copy of it consistently mention only two books *Against the Jews*, and they have drawn other men of circumspect judgment into the same error.

Plainly, however, something else lies concealed here: there are two unnamed editions. The first, which we have just described, we shall say was employed or known by none of the editors thus far. The other, which we shall discuss immediately, was on everyone’s lips; but because no one described it even in a single word, it gave rise to a serious error. Pamelius, who indeed reported nothing more about it than that it appeared to have been printed immediately after the invention of typography, nevertheless judges it to be much more correct than Erasmus’ text.

SIXTEENTH CENTURY

1500.

PARIS EDITION I. Paris, at the press of Jodocus Badius. *Cyprian’s Letters and Short Works; Treatise on the Cardinal Works of Christ*. Concerning this edition, conclusions are possible only by conjecture. Fabricius, in *Delectus Scriptorum pro Veritate Religionis Christianae*, p. 222, mentions a book ascribed to Cyprian, *On the Cardinal Works of Christ*, etc., published at Paris in 1500, but adds no further mention either of the bookseller, the printer, or the edition itself. Erasmus, however, in an annotation on the same book included in his own edition, recalls “the Badian edition, in which this work was contained separately from the rest of the volume.” Now Badius first began to conduct a bookselling business and publish books at Paris in 1500 (Maittaire, vol. II, p. 73). I therefore infer that the Paris edition noted by Fabricius was the Badian edition and that it included not merely that single short work but several works, perhaps even Cyprian’s letters. Nevertheless, I would have it remain excluded from the catalogue of Cyprianic editions until something more certain is discovered.

CLASS II. From Rembolt’s Edition to the Erasmian Editions

1512.

REMBOLT’S EDITION. PARIS EDITION II. Paris, quarto.

> The works, gathered from various sources, of the most blessed Caecilius Cyprianus, prelate of the Carthaginians, orator, most eloquent herald of the divine Word, and most vehement proclaimer of the triune and one God, collected into one volume and printed through the labors and at the expense of Masters Berthold REMBOLT and Johann WATERLOES, most skilled and trustworthy printers [Col. LXIX]; their arrangement is indicated on the following page.

This appears on the title page, the greater part of which is occupied by Rembolt’s device, with a four-line poem to the readers beneath it. At the end (fol. CLXXX):

> The short works of the blessed Cyprian, newly printed at Paris at the Golden Sun in the Rue Saint-Jacques, at the expense of Masters Berthold Rembolt and Johann Waterloes, dwelling at the sign of Saint George. In the year of the Lord 1512, on the thirteenth day of November.

According to the table printed on the verso of the title page, this edition contains the following: *The Cardinal Works of Christ down to His Ascension, and On the Holy Spirit* (later vindicated for Arnold, abbot of Bonneval).—Four books of Letters. The first book contains twelve letters; the second, twelve; the third, twenty-five; the fourth, ten.—Six treatises by the same author. The first, *Against Demetrianus*. The second, *On the Dress of Virgins*. The third, *On the Simplicity of Prelates*, that is, *On the Unity of the Church*. The fourth, *On the Vanity of Idols*. The fifth, *On Mounts Sinai and Zion, against the Jews*. The sixth, *On the Revelation of the Head of John the Baptist*.—A book of sermons by the same blessed Cyprian, containing six sermons. The first, *On Almsgiving*. The second, *On Jealousy and Envy*. The third, *On the Benefit of Patience*. The fourth, *On Mortality*. The fifth, *On the Lapsed and the Martyrs Already Crowned*. The sixth, *On the Lord’s Prayer*.—The same author’s *Exposition of the Apostles’ Creed*.—A short work by the same author addressed to Fortunatus, *On Exhortation to Martyrdom*.—The same author’s book *On the Singularity of Clerics*.—Two books by the same author *Against the Jews, to Quirinus*; the first divided into twenty-three chapters, the second into twenty-nine.—His *Passion*, written by Pontianus, his deacon.

The items marked with a + sign are to be regarded as newly added; the rest appear to have been taken from the Venetian edition of 1483. Thus indeed says Robert Fortunatus of Saint-Malo in the letter commending the work, prefixed and addressed to Lodovicus Ruvicius (Rusicus or Ruscius) of Blois, dated at Paris, from the house of Plessis, on the Kalends of November:

> For we took care to add to those works you supplied, *The Cardinal Works of Christ down to His Ascension to the Father*, together with the same author’s *Exposition of the Apostles’ Creed* and the account of his passion by Pontianus his deacon; certain works, such as *On Gamblers* and a few others, were omitted for the present because they were insufficiently consistent with his authorship.

CLASS III. Erasmian Editions

1520.

BASEL EDITION II, ERASMIAN EDITION I. Basel, from Froben’s press, folio. *The Works of the Most Holy Martyr Caecilius Cyprianus, Bishop of Carthage . . . . . through the study and care of Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam.*

“Cyprian appears,” says Erasmus in his distinguished preface to Cardinal Lorenzo Pucci, dated at Louvain in 1519, on the day before the Kalends of August, near its conclusion:

> He appears much more correct, through the removal of errors by our labor; somewhat purer, through the exclusion of works falsely attributed to him; and fuller, through the addition from some very ancient copies of material hitherto lacking. Finally, through Froben’s labor, he appears at once more elegant and more imposing; and he will appear auspiciously, if Your Most Reverend Lordship favors our industry.

The following were added from old manuscripts:

Letters *To Jubaianus, on the Baptism of Heretics* (letter LXXIII in the editions of Pamelius and Oxford); *To Pompeius, against the Letter of Stephen* (LXXIV in the same editions); *To Quintinus, on the Baptism of Heretics and Schismatics: Opinions of the Bishops of the Council of Carthage* (LXXI in the same editions).

He rejected the following as non-Cyprianic, but nevertheless retained them from earlier editions:

*On Mounts Sinai and Zion, against the Jews*.—*On the Revelation of the Head of Saint John the Baptist*.—*An Exposition of the Creed Which They Call the Apostles’*.—*On the Singularity of Clerics*.—*To Pope Cornelius, on the Cardinal Virtues of Christ*.

In addition to these, he appended the following equally spurious works from manuscript copies:

*To Moses, Maximus, and the Others, in Praise of Martyrdom*.—*On Discipline and the Benefit of Chastity*.—*To Novatian the Heretic: That the Hope of Pardon Must Not Be Denied to the Lapsed*.—Two prayers of Blessed Cyprian.—A sermon of Blessed Augustine on Saint Cyprian the martyr.

Erasmus’ very few annotations, together with two indexes of Cyprian’s works—one arranged in the order in which the works follow one another, the other arranged alphabetically—and also an account of Cyprian’s passion by Pontius, which Erasmus nevertheless warns is not Pontius’ actual work but an excerpt made from it by some more recent author, are prefixed to the text; another index, of propositions, is appended to it.

ERASMIAN EDITION VI. Basel, from Froben’s press, folio.—*Works, now for the fourth time cleansed of errors with more exacting vigilance by Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam. There has been added an exceedingly pious book by the same author addressed to Fortunatus, On the Twofold Martyrdom, never printed before.* (*Catalogue de la Bibliothèque du Roi*, p. 365.)

Erasmus says that he found this treatise in a very ancient library. But Henricus Gravius declares that Erasmus himself was the actual craftsman of this short work and passed off a product of his own genius under another’s name; Pamelius and Ceillier appear to agree with him. Certainly, to this day no one has learned which library Erasmus took this work from, nor has the codex he says he used come into anyone’s hands. Both subject matter and language savor of Erasmus’ style; and, what increases suspicion still more, as Pamelius observes, several testimonies from Scripture are rendered in this book in precisely the same way as in Erasmus’ other compositions. Lumper, *Historia theologico-critica Patrum*, vol. XII, p. 387.

ERASMIAN EDITION XIII, commonly called GRAVIUS’ EDITION. Cologne, from the printing house of Peter Quentel, in August.

> All the works of the Divine Caecilius Cyprianus that are known to survive, now most recently, following the correction of Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam, vindicated from errors with the aid of manuscript copies, far more diligently than ever before. With the addition of a very learned and pious short work ascribed to Cyprian, *On the Twofold Martyrdom, to Fortunatus*. Erasmus’ annotations have also been added—not gathered hastily into a single place, as has been done hitherto, but now for the first time inserted, each in its proper place, in a different typeface: partly so that the reader might be spared the weariness of searching, and partly so that what Cyprian and Erasmus mean might be perceived more conveniently and keenly, as though they were set opposite one another. Also several brief annotations or corrections by Henricus Gravius, a most learned man in both languages; few and brief indeed, but nevertheless timely and learned. And a new index, far fuller and better arranged than the old one, newly added through the labor of Bartholomaeus Laurens. Those who either know the earlier editions of Cyprian well or are willing to take the trouble to compare this one with any of them will easily perceive what value is to be assigned to this edition.

1563.

MANUTIUS’ EDITION, ROMAN EDITION II. Rome, at the press of Paulus Manutius, son of Aldus, folio.

> The *Works of the Divine Caecilius Cyprianus, Bishop of Carthage and Most Glorious Martyr*, corrected with the greatest fidelity according to the true text of the most ancient copies, with the addition also of a fifth book of letters, never previously published. Other works ascribed to the same Cyprian. With an index of noteworthy subjects and words compiled with the greatest diligence.

Latino Latini revised it, using a very ancient codex brought from the library at Verona, written in capital letters a thousand years earlier, and other manuscript copies; concerning each of these, however, I would not venture to affirm whether or not it was actually at hand during this first recension. Domenico Macri writes in his *Life* of Latini:

> Through many vigils and labors, he had collected a very great body of material for elucidating certain more obscure passages. He had also prepared several brief annotations in which, after indicating the variant readings of the codices, he stated which reading he himself had especially followed. An authentic book of letters was also added by him; and, finally, many passages had been restored with the greatest fidelity. But because he observed that, contrary to the authority of the written codices, some readings had been retained, others added, and very many altered—partly also through the carelessness of the workmen—he did not permit Manutius to place his name in any way upon the edition, lest he should impair his credibility even in the slightest degree.

He then undertook a second recension in relation to Pamelius’ edition; his work on it is presented in the *Bibliotheca Sacra et Profana*, pp. 174 ff. There he complains sufficiently about Manutius and at the same time resents the praise Pamelius bestowed upon Manutius for this edition. This edition contains everything that had been published in the Erasmian editions and those of Gravius, except Firmilian’s letter to Cyprian against the letter of Pope Stephen, no. 75 in Pamelius and Oxford. Concerning this, Latino Latini writes in the *Bibliotheca*, p. 177, against Pamelius’ annotation:

> I, Latino, omitted it, not Manutius, both because I followed the examples of my predecessors and because I detested the man’s insolence.

Besides the fifth book of letters, consisting of fifteen letters, the following was newly added among the works customarily ascribed to Cyprian but regarded as of doubtful authenticity: the book *On Spectacles* [Note: [Col. LXXIV] Baluze observes that manuscript copies of this work are exceedingly rare [Col. LXXV], and takes no little pride in having obtained two copies, something granted to none of the earlier editors. SCHOENEMANN.]; then the *Life of Cyprian* composed by [Col. LXXV] Pontius, together with the *Act of the Passion* mentioned by Pontius himself, and the *Passion of Cyprian* that appeared in the earlier editions and is here attributed to Paul the Deacon.

As regards the external appearance of this edition, I do not know whether one ought to call it splendid rather than elegant. Certainly the distinction and ἀκρίβεια (*precision*) of the type employed in the dedicatory letter to Cardinal Borromeo are such that one might compare it with even the finest monuments of the art in which Italy glories today; nor, although smaller, is the type of the remaining text much inferior. In the university library there is a copy with collations from the Verona, Vatican, Benevento, and other codices; it formerly belonged to Joseph Smith, British consul at Venice. CLASS IV. Editions of Pamelius

1568.

PAMELIUS’ EDITION I. Antwerp, at the press of the widow and heirs of Johann Steelsius, folio.

> The *Works* . . . . . now once again revised with the greatest possible accuracy, by comparison of the editions of Paulus Manutius and Guillaume Morel with several very ancient manuscript copies, and now for the first time divided into three volumes in a definite order that takes chronology into account. [Col. LXXVI] The annotations of Jacques Pamelius . . . . . interspersed throughout the entire work; in these both the reasons for corrections and variant readings are noted incidentally, and everything pertaining to ecclesiastical antiquity is briefly explained. Newly added by the same editor are a *Life of the Divine Cyprian*, compiled from his own writings, and a most copious index of the cited Scriptures, hitherto lacking.

For a fairly long span of years Pamelius had devoted his labor to Cyprian. His application, interrupted first while he was more fully occupied with theological studies and then hindered by a journey to France, was rekindled when two editions of this author, those of Paulus Manutius and Guillaume Morel, appeared at almost the same time. He indeed surpassed the merits of both—though he honored them with distinguished praise, assigning first place, however, to Manutius’ edition—both through repeated attention to the text supported by new resources and through the other scholarly aids with which, as the title promises, he was the first to equip and adorn Cyprian. Foremost among these were the excellent and lucid summaries prefixed to each book and letter, and the industry with which he sought to investigate the chronological sequence of the individual works and letters.

He used very many manuscript codices gathered from almost every part of Belgium. Among the older editions, he examined especially Unnamed Edition I, discussed above, and Rembolt’s edition. Latino Latini, whose second recension we praised above, judges that Pamelius relied too often upon Manutius’ corrections. Pamelius himself affirms that through his labor Cyprian appeared not only with more than seven hundred passages restored in the marginal citation of Scripture alone, but also purified of innumerable errors, adding:

> So that I hope—if it may be said without provoking envy—that no further recension of him will hereafter be necessary.

By this work he anticipated the Englishman John Harris, formerly secretary to Thomas More, by whom in turn he wished to be anticipated in the publication of Tertullian.

Before the *Life of Cyprian* composed by Pamelius were placed the accounts transmitted under the names of Pontius and Paul the Deacon concerning Cyprian’s life and passion, together with Augustine’s oration and a passage of Agobard concerning the translation of the body of the divine Cyprian to Gaul.—Since the complete edition is divided into three volumes, the first contains eighty-three letters, in this sequence: 1. letters written immediately after the baptism of the divine Cyprian; 2. letters written during his entire two-year period of retirement; 3. letters written under the pontificates of Cornelius and Lucius; 4. miscellaneous letters written at various times during the peace of the Church; 5. letters concerning the baptism of heretics, written under the pontificate of Stephen; 6. letters composed during Cyprian’s exile and toward the end of his life. The second volume contains fifteen various books and treatises. The third contains works ascribed to Cyprian.—Pamelius admits that within the text he sometimes omitted or inserted certain things on his own judgment, at times even against the authority of the manuscript codices. By this candid admission and his frank judgment concerning the alterations of Manutius and Morel, he removed from himself any suspicion of dishonest dealing.
