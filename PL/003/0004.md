### XXXI. On Indulgences

Since the certificates of the martyrs (*libelli martyrum*) were a certain kind of indulgence—for they were letters given by the martyrs in which they asked the bishops to pardon certain penitents whom they named, or to shorten the period of their penance [Note: [Col. XXXI] Cyprian throughout his *Letters.]—it is easy to infer from them what St. Cyprian thought about indulgences.

First, the holy Doctor speaks of the martyrs’ certificates in this way both in Letter XXII, addressed to the clergy residing at Rome, concerning the many confessors, the shamelessness of Lucian, and the modesty of the confessor Celerinus (vol. IV, col. 282), and in several other letters written to the same effect: he by no means denies that the fallen can be assisted by their merits. For in Letter XII (vol. IV, col. 259 A) he decrees:

> That those who have received certificates from the martyrs [Col. XXXII] and can be assisted before God by their privilege, if they are overtaken by some affliction and danger of illness, may, without awaiting my presence, make confession (*exomologesis*) of their offense before any presbyter who is present; or, if no presbyter can be found and death begins to press upon them, even before a deacon, etc.

Cyprian therefore believed that they could be assisted in their offenses before the Lord by the privilege of the martyrs, or, as he says in Letter XIII, col. 260 C, “by their aid.” Likewise, in the book *On the Fallen* (vol. IV, cols. 481 A, 483), he admits that something may be granted in accordance with the martyrs’ instructions,

> if what is to be done by God’s priest is just, if lawful, if not contrary to the Lord himself; if the compliance of the one obeying is ready and willing; and if there is religious moderation on the part of the petitioner.

Therefore, he censures not the custom itself, which had long prevailed in the Church, but its abuse and the excessive license of certain confessors, especially Lucian, who issued certificates written in this form (vol. IV, col. 255 C): “Let that man communicate together with his own people.” Nor did they observe what Cyprian had commanded in Letter X (vol. IV, cols. 255 A–B):

> that they should carefully examine the conduct, works, and merits of each individual, as well as the kinds and qualities of the offenses themselves; and that they should designate by name in their certificates those whom they themselves knew and whose penance they perceived to be near satisfaction.

Two further points must be observed:

1. The confessors did not grant peace, but asked that it be granted.
2. They did not even venture to demand that peace be granted to the fallen before Cyprian’s return, but in this matter deferred to their bishop.

Accordingly, it was not by the authority of the confessors that the five presbyters were induced to admit the fallen to communion before the bishop’s return and entirely without consulting him, but by their own wickedness.

### XXXII. That indulgences are founded upon the practice of the Church’s earliest centuries is attested by many of St. Cyprian’s letters

Thus, in Letter XVII, St. Cyprian, seeing that he did not yet have the opportunity to return to Carthage and that summer had already begun—a season beset by persistent and serious illnesses—commands the presbyters, and in case of necessity the deacons, of his Church to exercise a certain “indulgence” toward the fallen whenever they are placed in danger of death:

> Those who have received certificates from the martyrs, says the holy Doctor [Note: [Col. XXXII] St. Cyprian, Ep. XII, vol. IV, col. 250 A.], and can be assisted before God by their privilege, if they are overtaken by some affliction and danger of illness, may, without awaiting our presence, make confession (*exomologesis*) of their offense before any presbyter who is present; or, if no presbyter can be found and death begins to press upon them, even before a deacon, so that, when a hand has been laid upon them for penance, they may come to the Lord with the peace which the martyrs, in letters addressed to us, desired to be granted.

He repeats the same thing in Letter XIII:

> Since, however, you have reported, he says in addressing his clergy [Note: [Col. XXXII] Id., Ep. XIII, vol. IV, col. 260 B.], that some are immoderate [Col. XXXIII] and urgently press to receive communion without delay, and since you have desired that I give you a rule in this matter, I believe that in my recent letter to you I wrote sufficiently fully on this matter: that those who have received a certificate from the martyrs . . . if they begin to be oppressed by some illness and danger, may, after making confession (*exomologesis*) and after a hand has been laid upon them by you for penance, be sent to the Lord with the peace promised to them by the martyrs.

## § V. Discipline Regarding Holy Orders

### XXXIII

Although it would have been necessary to begin with the Supreme Pontiff, who is the head of the ecclesiastical order and of the whole Church, since we have treated very many matters concerning this same subject in the preceding articles, and since many more must be added in the dissertations appended to the end of this volume, no one will be displeased with us if we refer the reader to those places and proceed without delay to present Cyprianic evidence concerning the other orders.

#### The Election of Bishops

As regards the other bishops, their election was conducted by the bishops of the same province in the presence of the clergy and people, who likewise cast their votes for them. A bishop who had been elected in this regular manner could not be deposed so that another might be elected unless he had committed some extremely grave crime.

This point of discipline is admirably demonstrated by the letter mentioned above, in which the divine Cyprian, speaking about Sabinus’s ordination in the place of Basilides, says that it was carried out “by the vote of the entire brotherhood and by the judgment of the bishops who had assembled in person” [Note: [Col. XXXIII] Cyprian, Ep. LXVIII, vol. III, col. 1027 B.].

The holy Doctor maintains that the election of bishops conducted in the manner just described proceeds “from divine Tradition”—that is, with “Tradition” taken in a broader sense—and “from apostolic observance.” The holy bishop says that this election is therefore to take place in the presence of the people, because the laity know most fully the life of each individual and have perceived each man’s conduct from his manner of life.

Moreover, no others are to be chosen except those who are blameless and upright, so that, by offering sacrifices to God in a holy and worthy manner, they may be heard in the prayers they offer for the safety of the Lord’s people. Cyprian presents us with the form of an episcopal election in his Letter to Antonianus [Note: [Col. XXXIII] Id., Ep. LXII, vol. III, col. 768 ff. See vol. XI, *Critical Theological History*, sect. L, on St. Cornelius, Roman Pontiff, art. 1, p. 2, where we quoted the entire passage.].

### XXXIV. There was to be only one bishop in a single Church

Two bishops could not preside over one and the same Church at the same time. We have this expressly in the letter of Pope St. Cornelius to St. Cyprian concerning the confessors who had returned to unity; they declare [Note: [Col. XXXIII] Id., Ep. XVI, vol. III, col. 722 A.] “that there ought to be one bishop in the Catholic Church,” that is, in each individual Church.

The same point is likewise established from St. Cyprian’s Letter to [Col. XXXIV] Antonianus, in which the holy Doctor, declaring that Cornelius’s election was legitimate, adds [Note: [Col. XXXIII] Id., Ep. LII, vol. III, col. 769 ff.]:

> Whoever now wishes to be made bishop at Rome must necessarily be made so outside.

And the man who is appointed after the election of the first “is no longer a second bishop, but no bishop at all.” St. Cyprian further insists upon this unity of the bishop in his Letter to Florentius Puppianus [Note: [Col. XXXIII] Id., Ep. LXIX, vol. IV, col. 400 ff.].

### XXXV. Bishops governed their own Churches with the consent and counsel of their metropolitans, clergy, and people

Bishops communicated the affairs of their Churches not only to their metropolitan, as is gathered from several of St. Cyprian’s letters, especially Letter LIII, but also to the clergy and people. Hence they governed their Churches with the consent of their metropolitans, clergy, and people; and they sought their counsel even for the ordination of inferior ministers.

St. Cyprian, notwithstanding his exceptional merit and the supreme dignity with which he was distinguished in the Church of Carthage, protests and declares in a certain letter to his clergy that from the beginning of his episcopate he had resolved to do nothing privately according to his own judgment and without the counsel and consent of the clergy and laity.

The same holy Doctor informs his clergy and people about the ordination of subdeacons and the other inferior ministers, and asks the counsel of the clergy and people on this matter. St. Cyprian, therefore, was not one who governed his Church with excessive harshness. From his profound humility we learn that what the same holy Doctor writes elsewhere—namely, that the bishop depends upon no one, that freedom has been granted to him, and that he will render an account of his conduct to the Lord alone—is not to be understood too literally and rigorously.

### XXXVI. A council of bishops was celebrated annually to regulate the affairs of the Church

In the African Church the bishops assembled together to regulate the graver affairs of the Church, as St. Cyprian teaches in a certain letter to his clergy, which is Letter XXXII in the Oxford and Pamelius editions [Note: [Col. XXXIV] Id., Ep. XXXII, vol. IV, col. 306 B.].

These meetings or councils were customarily celebrated every year around the Paschal feasts:

> This is the latest and final trial of this persecution, says Cyprian, and it too, with the Lord’s protection, will soon pass, so that I may be restored to you after Easter Day together with my colleagues. In their presence, in accordance also with your judgment and the common counsel of us all, as was once resolved, we shall be able together to arrange and carefully settle the matters that must be dealt with [Note: [Col. XXXIV] Id., Ep. XL, vol. IV, col. 338 B–C.].

And the same holy Doctor says:

> Since you have written [Note: [Col. XXXIV] Id., Ep. LIII, vol. IV, col. 347 C ff.] that I should discuss this very matter most fully with several colleagues, and since so important an affair requires the greater and more earnest counsel produced by the deliberation of many, and since nearly all are now remaining at home with the brethren during the opening solemnities of Easter, when they have fulfilled their duties in celebrating the solemnity among their own people and begin to come to me, I shall discuss the matter more fully with each of them, so that concerning the matter on which you have consulted us, a firm judgment, weighed by the counsel of many bishops, may be established [Col. XXXV] among us and reported to you in writing.

### XXXVII. In matters of greater importance bishops sent their decisions to other bishops, especially to the Bishop of Rome

In matters of greater importance, bishops, even when assembled in council, sent their decisions to other bishops, intending to await from them both their counsel and their consent, partly to manifest and partly to confirm the priestly authority and, likewise, the unity and dignity of the Catholic Church.

The Africans especially sought the counsel and consent of the Bishop of Rome. This is evident from St. Cyprian’s Letter LXXII to St. Stephen, in which the following occurs [Note: [Col. XXXV] Cyprian, Ep. LXXII, vol. III, col. 1046.]:

> For the settlement of certain matters and their careful examination by common counsel, it was necessary for us, dearest brother, when several bishops had assembled in one place, to convene and celebrate a council. In it many matters were indeed presented and transacted; but above all it was necessary to write to you and confer with your gravity and wisdom about that which especially concerns both priestly authority and the unity and dignity of the Catholic Church, proceeding from the ordering of the divine dispensation: namely, that those who have been baptized outside the Church, and stained among heretics and schismatics by the defilement of profane water, must, when they come to us and to the Church, which is one, be baptized; because it is not enough merely to lay a hand upon them so that they may receive the Holy Spirit, unless they also receive the Church’s baptism. . . . We have conveyed these things to your conscience, dearest brother, both for our common honor and out of sincere affection, believing that, in accordance with the truth of your religion and faith, you too approve those things which are alike religious and true.

Some conclude from this that already in Cyprian’s age it was customary for bishops assembled in synods to seek from the Roman Pontiff confirmation of the decrees established in their councils.

### XXXVIII. No bishop could rescind what had been done by another bishop except for the gravest reasons

Not even the metropolitan was permitted, without the gravest reasons, to rescind what had been done by another bishop. For this reason St. Cyprian rebukes Therapios, as is evident from his Letter LIX (vol. III, col. 1014 A).

Elsewhere [Note: [Col. XXXV] Id., Ep. LXVII, vol. III, col. 997 A.] St. Cyprian observes that “there cannot be” among bishops “a diversity of understanding in those in whom there is one Spirit; and therefore it is evident that the man whom we see thinking differently does not hold the truth of the Holy Spirit together with the others.”

And yet afterward Cyprian and Stephen held different views in the controversy concerning the baptism of heretics. These statements are easily reconciled if we say that the cited judgment of St. Cyprian is to be interpreted as referring to matters of faith, as the context teaches, and not to matters of discipline.

### XXXIX. Bishops sent letters to one another

Bishops customarily wrote to one another as a sign [Col. XXXVI] of mutual communion. Therefore, when a new bishop was appointed, the others took care to be informed of his faith and piety.

The first part of this discipline is gathered from Cyprian’s Letter LXVII to Stephen, in which (vol. III, col. 988 A) the following words occur:

> Inform us plainly who has been appointed at Arles in Marcian’s place, so that we may know to whom we ought to direct our brethren and to whom we ought to write.

The second part is gathered from another letter of the same holy Doctor to Pope St. Cornelius, which in this edition is numbered LV (vol. III, col. 809 A).

They sent these letters through clerics, as is evident from Letter XXIV, where (vol. IV, col. 287 B) the following occurs:

> And since it was proper for me to write through clerics, but I know that very many of ours are absent and that the few who are there are scarcely sufficient for the ministry of the daily work, it was necessary to appoint some new men who could be sent.

It is evident also from another letter to Successus, numbered LXXXII, in which he writes the following to Successus (vol. IV, col. 429 B):

> The reason why I did not write to you immediately, dearest brother, was that all the clerics, being placed under the blow of the conflict, were entirely unable to depart from here.

### XL. The clergy governed the Church in the bishop’s absence

In the bishop’s absence, the Church was governed not by some bishop from elsewhere, but by the clergy. This is clear from Cyprian’s Letter XXXIV to the presbyters and deacons of Carthage. In it, indeed, we read that the clergy of Carthage excluded Gaius of Didda from their communion [Note: [Col. XXXVI] Cyprian, Ep. XXVIII, vol. IV, col. 300 B.].

We further know that, after Fabian’s death, the Roman clergy directed that Church while the see was vacant [Note: [Col. XXXVI] “And since it falls to us, who appear to have been placed in authority”—namely, because after the death of the Roman Bishop the care of that Church fell to the clergy of the city—“to guard the flock in the shepherd’s place, etc.” Cyprian, Ep. II, vol. IV, col. 224 A. See also Ep. XXXI, vol. IV, cols. 307 ff.].

Bishops could do nothing in the dioceses of other bishops without the diocesan bishop’s permission. This is most clearly established both by the Letter to Caldonius and Herculanus, whom St. Cyprian, since they were bishops, had sent to Carthage as his vicars, and to whom he had also joined the presbyters Rogatianus and Numidicus, so that they might provide money for the needy and examine those who were to be promoted to ecclesiastical offices [Note: [Col. XXXVI] Cyprian, Ep. XXXVIII, vol. IV, cols. 329 ff.]; and by Letter LV (vol. III, col. 821 A), in which he expressly declares “that a portion of the flock has been assigned to each pastor, which each one is to rule and govern, rendering an account of his conduct to the Lord.”

Hence he also teaches elsewhere that other bishops ought to involve themselves in the government of another diocese only in a case of necessity, when its own bishop causes heresy or schism [Note: [Col. XXXVI] Id., Ep. to Pope Stephen, LXVII, vol. III, col. 995 A.]. In this matter also St. Cyprian acted with the greatest moderation and prudence, as is gathered from some of his letters [Note: [Col. XXXVI] See Ep. LXIV, to Epictetus and the people of Assuras [Col. XXXVII] concerning Fortunatianus, vol. IV, cols. 389 ff.; and LXVIII, to the clergy and people residing in Spain, vol. III, cols. 1021 ff.].

[Col. XXXVII]

### XLI. Honor shown to bishops from elsewhere

Nevertheless, the holy Doctor and bishop permits his clergy to communicate to bishops from elsewhere, should any happen to be present there, the letters he has sent to them.

> But if any, says St. Cyprian [Note: [Col. XXXVII] Cyprian, Ep. XXXII, vol. IV, col. 316 A–B.], of my colleagues among the visiting bishops . . . are present or should arrive, let them hear all these things from you. And if they wish to copy transcripts of the letters and carry them back to their own people, let them be given permission to make copies.

### XLII. Cases reserved to the bishop

Finally, certain cases were reserved to the bishop, as we readily infer from St. Cyprian’s Letter XVIII and from other passages in his works, in which he grants his presbyters the faculty of absolving certain sins in his absence, provided, however, that a case of necessity exists.

Such, at least, is the opinion of the distinguished Bernard Maréchal [Note: [Col. XXXVII] *Concordance of the Holy Fathers*, vol. II, p. 120, no. X.]. But I, thus far, have been unable to discover in the works of the divine Cyprian any trace whatsoever of the reservation of certain cases or sins; nor do I understand how that same point can be gathered from Letter XLII, vol. IV, col. 255.

### XLIII. What bishops ought to be like according to Cyprian

St. Cyprian teaches that a bishop ought to be teachable and gentle; and the teachable man is one who is mild and gentle in the patience required for learning. For a bishop, the holy Doctor continues, ought not only to teach but also to learn, because he teaches better who grows and advances every day by learning better things [Note: [Col. XXXVII] Cyprian, Ep. LXXIV, vol. III, col. 11.].

Nothing ought to hold a bishop back [Note: [Col. XXXVII] Id., Ep. LV, to Cornelius, vol. III, cols. 796 ff.]. Indeed, he ought to be sacrificed in the presence of the brethren like a victim that provides the brotherhood with an example of virtue [Note: [Col. XXXVII] Id., Ep. LVIII, to Pope Lucius, vol. III, col. 976 A.].

### XLIV. Points of discipline concerning presbyters

Certain authors following Blondel [Note: [Col. XXXVII] *Book on Bishops and Presbyters*, ed. 1646, ch. XVI, p. 41 ff.] believed that they could conclude from Cyprian that bishops did not rank above presbyters. But their gross error has been demonstrated both by very many recent scholars, among them the most learned Mamachi [Note: [Col. XXXVII] *Christian Origins and Antiquities*, vol. IV, book IV, p. 443 ff.], and by Cyprian himself in Letters XI, XXXV, LV, etc. [Note: [Col. XXXVII] Below, vol. IV, col. 247, col. 325, etc.].

Only this must be said here: a bishop was permitted to associate presbyters of another Church with his own clergy. This is proved by the example of the presbyter Numidicus, whom the divine Cyprian enrolled in his own Carthaginian clergy, perhaps so that he might succeed him in the episcopate. For at that time the prevailing discipline was that strangers and outsiders were not to be elected to the episcopate, precisely because of [Col. XXXVIII] the danger of a bad election, lest unworthy men should be chosen when their manner of life was not sufficiently known [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] “That the presbyter Numidicus be enrolled in the number of the presbyters of Carthage.” Cyprian, Ep. XXXV, vol. IV, col. 325 A.].

Moreover, from the example just cited we also properly infer the distinction that St. Cyprian made between an ordinary presbyter and a presbyter of the Carthaginian clergy. For the holy Doctor judges that he could not better acknowledge the merits of Numidicus, who was already a presbyter, than by enrolling him among the presbyters of his own Church.

Let us therefore recognize, writes the distinguished Bernard Maréchal [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] *Concordance of the Holy Fathers*, vol. II, p. 120, Venice edition.], in this passage the superior dignity of the presbyters of cathedral Churches, who are called canons. They attended the bishop in all his duties and functions, were his council, celebrated the holy mysteries with him, and governed the Church in his absence. Such indeed are canons today, and such were the presbyters among whom Numidicus was numbered.

### XLV. Origin of the diaconate

According to Cyprian [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] “Deacons ought to remember that the Lord chose the Apostles, that is, the bishops and prelates, whereas after the Lord’s ascension into heaven the Apostles appointed deacons for themselves as ministers of their episcopate and of the Church.” Cyprian, Ep. LXV, vol. IV, col. 396 B.], the institution of deacons belongs to apostolic tradition, and the order of the diaconate was instituted by the Apostles. Hence, according to St. Cyprian, the order of the diaconate is not of divine institution and therefore, strictly speaking, is by no means a sacrament.

### XLVI. Deacons as ministers of bishops and presbyters

Deacons were not only ministers of bishops but also of presbyters, as St. Cyprian confirms in a letter to his clergy with these words [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] Cyprian, Ep. XXVIII, vol. IV, col. 300 B.]:

> You acted uprightly and with discipline, dearest brethren, in judging that . . . communion ought not to be maintained with the presbyter Gaius of Didda and his deacon.

This Gaius was probably equivalent to what is today called a parish priest, who had a deacon with him to minister to him.

### XLVII. The duties of deacons

The duties of deacons were to serve at the altar and distribute the Eucharist to the faithful [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] Id., book *On the Fallen*, vol. IV, col. 485 A.]. They attended the presbyters as assistants in the celebration of the sacrifice of the Mass, as is gathered from Letter IV (vol. IV, col. 231 A), addressed to the clergy of Carthage:

> Take counsel and make provision,

he says to his presbyters and deacons,

> that the presbyters . . . who make the offering there among the confessors

—he is speaking here about prisoners—

> should each take turns with an individual deacon [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] Id., Ep. XLIX, vol. III, col. 726 B.].

They administered the Church’s revenues, participated in ecclesiastical judgments [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] Id., Ep. LV, vol. III, cols. 822 A ff.], and had a place and role in councils both at Rome and in Africa [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] Ep. to Antonianus, LII, vol. III, cols. 761 ff.]. At times they also governed Churches [Note: [Col. XXXVIII] Cyprian, Ep. LXVIII, vol. III, cols. 1021 ff.].

An example of this [Col. XXXIX] is found in Letter LXVIII, which the divine Cyprian addresses to Felix the presbyter and to the people residing at León and Astorga, and likewise to Laelius the deacon and the people of Mérida. For if even one presbyter had existed in this latter Church, he would have addressed his letter to him rather than to the deacon.

Whatever may be said, it is certain from canon 77 of the Council of Elvira that deacons formerly exercised the government of Churches, though only, as is probable, when presbyters were lacking [Note: [Col. XXXIX] “If any deacon governing a congregation without a bishop or presbyter has baptized any persons, the bishop will be obliged to bring them to completion by a blessing.” Vol. I of Labbe’s *Councils*, p. 978; and in Mansi, vol. II, col. 18.].

### XLVIII. Subdeacons and the other inferior ministers

As regards subdeacons, St. Cyprian frequently mentions them in his letters. See, for example, Letter III (vol. IV, col. 229), addressed to the Roman clergy, for in it he speaks of a certain subdeacon called Crementius [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Cyprian, Ep. II, vol. IV, col. 224 A.]; Letter XXIX, in which St. Cyprian speaks of another subdeacon named Optatus [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., Ep. XXIV, vol. IV, col. 287 B.]; and the same holy Doctor’s letter to his clergy concerning the presbyter Gaius of Didda, in which two subdeacons, Philumenus and Fortunatus, are mentioned [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., Ep. XXVIII, col. 301 B.]. A certain subdeacon is also mentioned in a letter of the Roman clergy to the divine Cyprian [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., Ep. XXX, vol. IV, col. 303 B.].

St. Cyprian more than once mentions acolytes [Note: [Col. XXXIX] The acolyte Favorinus is mentioned in Letter XXVIII, vol. IV, col. 301 B.], as well as readers [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Cyprian, Ep. XXIV, vol. IV, col. 287 B; Ep. XXXIII, col. 319; Ep. XXXIV, col. 324 A–B.] and exorcists [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., Ep. LXXVI, vol. III, col. 2. See also Firmilian’s Letter, numbered LXXV among Cyprian’s letters.]. He notes that he was accustomed “to examine whether all the qualities that ought to be present in those who were being prepared for the clergy were suitable in them” [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., Ep. XXIV, vol. IV, col. 287 B–C.].

Readers read Holy Scripture and were teachers of the “hearers,” or catechumens [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., ibid.]; they also read the Gospel from the pulpit [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., Ep. XXXIII, vol. IV, col. 319 B.].

## § VI. Discipline Regarding Marriage, Vows, and the Veneration of Relics

### XLIX

The divine Cyprian has very little to say about marriage, since he speaks about it not expressly and systematically, but only in passing. Hence, in the treatise *On the Fallen*, among the evils committed by the faithful before the persecution, he lists the custom of contracting marriages with unbelievers:

> To join, he says [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., book *On the Fallen*.], the bond of marriage with unbelievers is to prostitute the members of Christ to pagans.

In the third book of the *Testimonies*, he attempts to show from certain pronouncements of Sacred Scripture that these marriages are unlawful. Hence, he says [Note: [Col. XXXIX] Id., book III of the *Testimonies*, ch. 62, vol. IV, col. 767 B.], we read [Col. XL] in Tobit [Note: [Col. XL] Tobit IV.]:

> Take a wife from the offspring of your parents, and do not take a foreign woman who is not from the tribe of your parents.

Likewise, in Genesis [Note: [Col. XL] Genesis XIV, 3–4.]:

> Abraham sends his servant to take Rebecca from his own stock as a wife for his son Isaac.

Likewise, in Ezra [Note: [Col. XL] 1 Ezra X.]:

> It was not enough for God, when the Jews were being laid waste, unless they abandoned their foreign wives together with the children they had begotten from them.

The holy Doctor further proves his proposition by other pronouncements of Scripture, such as, for example, these words of Paul [Note: [Col. XL] 1 Corinthians VII, 39; 1 Corinthians VI; and 2 Corinthians VI.]:

> Let her marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord.

St. Augustine, however [Note: [Col. XL] Augustine, book I *On Adulterous Marriages*, ch. 25.], says that these words of the Apostle can be understood in two senses and signify nothing more than that this widow ought either to remain in the Christian religion or to be joined to a Christian man. He then adds that the marriages of Christians with unbelievers are not expressly forbidden in the New Testament.

> For I do not recall,

these are the words of the divine Augustine,

> that, in the time of the revealed New Testament, it is declared without ambiguity in the Gospel or the other apostolic writings whether the Lord prohibited the faithful from being joined to unbelievers. Nevertheless, blessed Cyprian has no doubt about it, nor does he rank among minor sins the joining of the bond of marriage with unbelievers; and he says that this is to prostitute the members of Christ to pagans.

### L. The vow of continence was in use in Cyprian’s age

As regards vows, which have not escaped the unjust censures of the innovators, St. Cyprian teaches that they were in use in his own age. In his treatise *On the Dress of Virgins*, the holy Doctor speaks of certain women “who had dedicated themselves to Christ and, withdrawing from carnal concupiscence, had vowed themselves to God in both body and mind” [Note: [Col. XL] Cyprian, book *On the Dress of Virgins*, vol. IV, col. 443 B.].

Nevertheless, we shall soon see that these vows were not absolute and that those who made vows of this kind could enter into marriage in a case of necessity. But the innovators will at least be unable to deny that, in the third century, vows distinct from the vows of Baptism existed in the Church.

Bishop Pomponius had sent a letter to St. Cyprian, asking and desiring that he write back what he thought concerning those virgins who, after they had once resolved to maintain their state continently and steadfastly, were subsequently discovered to have remained together in the same bed with men [Note: [Col. XL] Id., Ep. LXII, vol. IV, col. 364 B ff. See part XI of the *Critical Theological History*, p. 264 ff.].

St. Cyprian replies, among other things, that virgins ought not only not to sleep with men, but not even to live together with them. The holy Doctor then adds:

> But if they have dedicated themselves to Christ in good faith, let them persevere modestly and chastely, without giving occasion for any gossip. If, however, they are unwilling or unable to persevere, it is better that they marry than that they fall into the fire through their offenses.

This indeed admirably confirms the observation we proposed [Col. XLI] a little above: namely, that vows at that time were not so absolute as to bind even in a case of necessity.

Moreover, St. Cyprian teaches that it is impossible for Christ the Lord not “to be angered and indignant when he sees his virgin, dedicated to him and destined for his holiness, lying with another.” Finally, he asserts that those who fall into these crimes must be punished with excommunication and excluded from the Church’s communion until they have performed penance and disclosed their own offense to everyone by public confession.

### LI. On the relics of the saints

The innovators rise up no less bitterly against the veneration of sacred relics; but in this matter they are equally opposed to the whole of antiquity. We have already demonstrated this in the preceding volumes [Note: [Col. XLI] See *Critical Theological History*, part II, pp. 433–434; part VII, p. 349.] from the authority of certain Fathers of the Church. We now confirm the same point from the divine Cyprian.

St. Cyprian’s eighty-first letter furnishes us with very powerful arguments by which the veneration of sacred relics may be established [Note: [Col. XLI] Cyprian, Ep. LXXXI, vol. IV, col. 424 C ff. See part XI of the *Critical Theological History*, p. 268 ff.].

At the beginning of this letter, the holy bishop declares to the confessors to whom it is addressed—declares, I say—the joy with which he would be filled if ever he could embrace those limbs that had been the instruments of their confession; if he could kiss those mouths that had gloriously confessed the name of Jesus Christ; and if he could be seen by those eyes which, having despised the world, had proved worthy to behold God:

> What could be more desirable or joyful for me, he says [Note: [Col. XLI] Id., loc. cit.], than to embrace those hands which . . . preserving the faith of the Lord, rejected sacrilegious acts of homage? What could be more delightful and sublime than to kiss those mouths that confessed the Lord with a glorious voice?

We shall therefore not deserve ridicule if, from this testimony of St. Cyprian, we conclude that the relics of the saints are to be honored. For if their limbs, while they were still alive, were worthy of veneration, they must certainly be judged worthy of greater veneration after they have departed to the greater number, unless we wish to say that their precious relics become worthless through their glorious death. On the contrary, through death they acquire a new degree of glory.
