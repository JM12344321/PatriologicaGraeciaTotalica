**PATI** (“to undergo,” “to experience”), used of both favorable and adverse things, like the Greek πάσχειν. I, 376 B: “He experiences his own health.” I, 413 A: “Those who are thought to be affected by God,” that is, to be inspired or stirred; a circumlocution for τῶν ἐνθέων ἢ θεολήπτων, “the divinely inspired or God-possessed,” of whom three kinds are listed below: θεοφορούμενοι, “God-borne”; θεοπάρακτοι, “God-impelled”; and θεοπρόποι, “diviners.” II, 248 B: “The unhappy man experienced the impulse of this presumption.” II, 285 A: “But now it causes trouble,” that is, the rashness and audacity of the Marcionites—who fabricated for themselves another god distinct from the Almighty—causes trouble for God. II, 888 B: “Having now undergone the particular business of my opinion,” that is, “I who had previously been occupied in setting forth my opinion in Greek concerning the veiling of virgins”: Junius explains it thus. But Rigault thinks that, in some disputation [Col. 1345A] against the Greeks, when he had perhaps asserted rather vehemently that virgins also ought to be veiled, he was met with abuse from the Greeks and assailed as a heretic. II, 895 A: “Who has experienced a husband.” II, 904 B: “And to undergo marriage.” II, 652 B: “After the determination of the census, it undergoes the question of status.” II, 706 A: “Because a banquet undergoes funeral rites,” that is, because it is brought to the banquet, not to the tomb; expended upon the dinner, not upon the funeral.

**PATROCINIUM** (“patronage,” “support”). II, 543 A: “By the support of the compressed figure he conceals the sacrilege,” that is, in defense and confirmation of that shameful figure, made from compressed wool by felt-work; for this reason they put forward the venerable name of nature. See **CONVITIUM**. II, 668 A: “Of which I understand the mind to be the instrument, not the patronage,” that is, the governing faculty, as he called it at the beginning of the chapter.

**PATRUITUS.** II, 562 B: “And a discourse concerning paternal-uncle derivatives.” Irenaeus: πατρωνυμικός, “patronymic.” Junius prefers *de patritis*, “concerning paternal things,” as Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* I, 19: “that philosophy inherited from father and grandfather.”

**PAUPERTINUS**, the same as *pauper*, “poor.” II, 262 A: “Because of [Col. 1345B] whom, into these impoverished elements.”

**PECCATELA** (“petty sin”). II, 719 A: “For persuading or commanding a petty sin.”

**PECTEN** (“comb”). II, 545 B: “The towers of Lamia and the combs of the sun.” These were children’s tales current at Carthage in Tertullian’s time, with which nurses restrained their little children from crying.

**PECUS** (“creature,” “animal”). II, 411 B: “Not called a creature after the figure,” that is, first a living, sentient body, newly furnished with its own form. Julius Firmicus uses the same word, book VII, chapter 1: “The creature, cut limb by limb within the mother’s womb, will be brought forth by physicians.” II, 486 A: “Through the likeness of the saving blood and of Christ’s sacrificial animal.” He alludes to the blood of the paschal lamb that was sprinkled upon the doorposts in Egypt. Exodus XII.

**PEJOR** (“worse”). I, 1300 A: “Because the Christian name pleases only the worse sort.” Tertullian says that things offensive to God are introduced by evil, and that this is made evident by the fact that, among the Gentiles, those especially who are of worse and more shameful life seek marriage with Christian women. Accordingly, pagans are found who do not shrink from such women—that is, Christians—so that they may destroy them, carry them off, and shut them out from the faith.

**PELLICEUS** (“of skin,” “leathern”). II, 578 A: “Meanwhile, they say that a fleshly surface was afterward woven over the earthy man, and that this is the tunic of skin, subject to sensation.” I, 1305 B: “And do you intend to adorn yourself over your tunics of skin?” II, 803 B: “For neither, as some maintain, were those tunics of skin which Adam,” etc.

**PELLITUS**, “clothed with skin.” II, 1038 A: “Clothed in skin, he is given to the world as one is given to the mines.”

**PENDERE** (“to remain pending”). II, 450 B: “Or from the recent Augustan census records, perhaps still pending at that time.” “At that time,” namely, when the blind man said: “Jesus, Son of David.” “Pending,” namely, the census tablets. Evidently censuses seem to have been conducted more than once under Augustus. And Septimius himself, in the book *Against the Jews*, appears to doubt whether Christ was enrolled in the census immediately upon his birth. II, 819 A: “One edict of God, I think, hangs before all men: the resurrection [Col. 1345D] of the dead.” Thus he calls the creed of the Christian faith; and above he said that this matter had been posted under a heading, alluding to the praetors’ edicts, which were customarily inscribed and displayed on the *album*, that is, a tablet. I, 648 B: “Then they depend upon the sign of the year.”

**PENDULUS** (“suspended,” “uncertain”). II, 708 A: “So that the concern of faith may be tested by suspenseful expectation.”

**PENES**, “among,” “in.” I, 314 B: “Infants are sacrificed to Saturn in Africa.”

**PENSUM**, the portion of work to be completed on any given day. II, 247 A: “They accomplish their allotted task with axes.”

**PENULA** (“cloak”). II, 76 B: “There the exceedingly burdened man laid aside his cloaks, beginning to be relieved.” It is established that cloaks were disliked because of their weight. Hence Septimius, taking occasion from this, says that that soldier, glorious in God, after laying aside the exceedingly heavy burden of pagan military service, then began to be relieved, as soon as he undertook Christ’s burden—which Christ himself taught to be light.

[Col. 1346A] **PEPO** (“melon,” figuratively “blockhead”). II, 702 B: “Why not rather so insipid a melon?” From proverbial usage it denotes an insipid person. II, 461 A: “But why does he call bread his body, and not rather the melon which Marcion had in place of a heart?”

**PER.** I, 403 B: “Through him and in him,” that is, according to his teaching and the religion instituted by God.

**PERDITRIX** (“destroying,” “ruinous”). II, 929 A: “After lust, the devil discovered even a ruinous chastity.”

**PERDOCERE** (“to teach thoroughly”). II, 546 A: “But neither do they teach thoroughly all whom they instruct.”

**PERDUCTOR** (“seducer,” “procurer”). I, 495 A: “First will come pimps, procurers, and brothel-attendants.” Procurers differ from pimps in that the latter bring willing women to someone, while the former drag unwilling women to him.

**PEREGRINARI** (“to travel abroad”). I, 388 A: “We must travel through the histories and literature of the world.” He conveys the delay and time spent in investigating the origins of other nations, while we travel in mind, not in body.

[Col. 1346B] **PEREMPTORIUS** (“peremptory,” “final”). II, 980 C: “I hear that an edict has also been posted, and indeed a peremptory one.”

**PEREMPTRIX** (“destroying”). II, 721 A: “Death, the destroyer of sensation.”

**PERENNARE** (“to endure perpetually”). II, 855 A: “But teeth also endure incorrupt.” I, 1233 B: “And it endures in the leaves,” that is, lasts perpetually.

**PERGULA** (“projecting gallery”). II, 550 B: “With gallery upon gallery built above.” *Pergulae* are στενὰ οἰκήματα, “narrow dwellings,” as the Glossary explains: narrow upper rooms projecting beyond the houses and extending outward by some portion of themselves, chiefly intended to provide convenient passage from one part of a house to another. Thus Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXI, chapter 3.

**PERICLITARI**, “to come near,” “to be not far from,” like the Greek κινδυνεύειν. II, 216 B: “And it does not come near being thought to have been made from something,” that is, it is not close to being thought to have arisen from something, etc. II, 77 A: “Finally, they mutter that so good and long a peace is in danger for them,” etc. The deed of this most courageous soldier was censured even by his Christian fellow-soldiers as an example of imprudent, headlong, and untimely courage. Reports circulated about him as a rash and reckless man eager to die, who, when questioned about his attire, had brought ill repute upon the Christian name and created danger, and by his rashness had come close to overthrowing the good and long peace which Christians then enjoyed. Septimius severely rebukes these words: “They mutter,” he says, “that so good and long a peace is being endangered for them.... [Col. 1346C] Nor do I doubt that some also mutter that the sacred Scriptures are already emigrating, unpacking their baggage, and preparing for flight from city to city.” The common text reads: “Nor do I doubt that certain Scriptures are emigrating”; but the oldest manuscript has: “Nor do I doubt that certain men the Scriptures are emigrating,” and the verb must be repeated from the preceding: “Nor do I doubt that certain men mutter the Scriptures,” etc.

**PERICULUM** (“danger,” “loss”). II, 174 A: “Without danger to the light,” that is, without loss.

**PERISCELIUM** (“anklet”) is a soft and ostentatious ornament of the legs. I, 1332 A. Juvenal calls it *pariscelis*.

**PERMOVERE** (“to disturb”). I, 1317 B: “Certainly they disturb scandal even by the spirit.” Rigault very correctly reads: “They disturb the spirit by scandal,” that is, they shake and disquiet it. For the saints possess the Spirit of God. Therefore, if they admit anything from evil, they disturb God’s Spirit; for the sake of a worldly thing they shake the spirit which they received from the Lord.

**PERONES**, rustic shoes suitable against snow, rain, and cold, made of rawhide and extending very high. II, 1046 B: “The rawhide shoes are called effeminate,” because luxury had rendered them effeminate. I, 1312 A: “And they desire to plunge pearls into the mud with rawhide shoes.” Pliny, book IX, chapter 35.

**PERPES**, “perpetual,” “continuous.” I, 378 A: “Into a fire equally perpetual and unbroken.”

**PERPETRABILIS** (“accomplishable”). I, 1289 B: “But how much more practicable it is to marry in the Lord,” for *perpetrabilius*, “more practicable,” or “more accomplishable,” after Greek usage. But the word is used in a favorable sense, as at I, 1288 A: “They are accomplished with zeal”; for, according to Festus, *perpetrat* means that one carries a thing through and completes it.

[Col. 1347A] **PERSONA** (“person”). II, 391 A: “The person of the name,” that is, that person to whom this name belongs.

**PERSONALITER** (“personally”). II, 551 A: ὑποστατικῶς, “hypostatically,” in respect of an individual person.

**PERSTRINGERE** (“to graze,” “to strike lightly”). The Latins use it of a superficial wound, not one driven deep, and also of the grazing, as it were, of lightning when it does not shatter or burn. I, 489 B: “If some things strike us too.”

**PERTINAX** (“persistent”). I, 671 B: “O divine sentence, persistent all the way to earth,” that is, one that prevails everywhere with the utmost constancy and binds men even here on earth, so that from this same sentence they judge that astrologers must be expelled.

**PERTINENTIUS** (“more directly,” “more deeply”). II, 374 C: “Which he wished to be recognized more directly,” that is, more deeply, or as referring most nearly to Christ.

[Col. 1347B] **PERTRACTUS** (“protracted”). I, 1159 A: “As a certain protracted duration in the age,” that is, an enduring and perpetual continuance, a prolongation of the age, as though the Lord were drawing it out at length.

**PHILOSOPHARI** (“to philosophize,” “to interpret philosophically”). II, 861 A: “The lions of Mithras philosophize the mysteries of a dry and burning nature,” that is, they interpret by philosophical reasoning that the lions in the mystery of Mithras are signs of a dry and burning nature.

**PHILYBA** is the inner bark of the linden, cut into strips. II, 1038 B: “A *tenus philyrae* is a band or snare and bond made from linden bark.” *Tenus* and *taenia*, however, are Greek words.

**PHYSCON.** II, 1042 B: “More impure than Physcon.” A proverbial expression concerning a man who gorges himself on every kind of luxury and lust. Ptolemy Physcon was the seventh Egyptian king descended from Lagus. After ruling at Cyrene, he succeeded his deceased brother Euergetes. In addition to more than bestial lust and cruelty—the crimes are related by Justin, book XXXVIII—his fat belly and the deformity of his body and face made him more like a beast than a man. This ugliness was increased, as if deliberately—or rather through lack of restraint—by the fineness of his transparent garment. He received from the circumstance the infamous name *Physcon*, as though “pot-bellied” and “brothel-attendant,” because of his devotion to his abdomen.

[Col. 1347C] **PIGMENTUM** (“pigment,” “drug,” “spice”). I, 676 A: “Even for men, for medicinal drugs.” For pigments are also useful in medicine; hence apothecaries were called *pigmentarii*. Julius Firmicus, book VIII, chapter 17: “Whoever shall have been born under this star will be snake-charmers, or persons accustomed to procure wholesome remedies for men from prepared herbal drugs belonging to Venus.” They also called seasonings *pigmenta*. Cassian, *Conferences* XV, chapter 3: “The bodies of the dead, embalmed with fragrant spices, are laid away in higher cells.” So too, near the end of Exodus XXXVII, what the Septuagint calls ἔργον μυρεψοῦ, “the work of a perfumer,” is called “the work of a *pigmentarius*.”

**PIPIARE** (“to pipe,” “to whimper”). II, 568 A: “Achamoth whimpered forth the heavenly rains,” that is, poured them out while whimpering. *Pipum* or *pipulum* means the wailing cry and whimper of one who weeps—the cry of a weeper in the language of the Oscans, as Festus says. II, 952 B: “And whimpering infants.”

[Col. 1347D] **PLAGA** (“stroke”). II, 130 C, 131 A: “This stroke was countered.” The expression is taken from gladiators, who either evade an opponent’s thrust by swerving their body or receive it on a small shield, so that the attempt is frustrated.

**PLAGIATOR**, a kidnapper who carries off a free person or slave. II, 273 C: “Such a claimant would be condemned even in the secular court, much more a kidnapper.”

**PLAGULA** (“cloth,” “panel”). II, 342 A: “I have not sewn a new piece of cloth onto an old garment.” Turnebus, I, 25, calls the *plagula* “patches.” *Plagulae* are curtains and textiles with which litters and couches were hung or wrapped, as in Suetonius, *Titus*, chapter 10: “when the curtains of the litter had been drawn aside.” Nonius interprets *plagulae* as large linen cloths, a couch-cover, and a litter-sheet; hence at II, 382 C he calls the addition *plagulae*.

**PLANE**, “indeed,” “rather,” “correctly.” I, 405 A: “Indeed, from the good,” that is, rather, he had a demon, but one that dissuaded him from the good. *Planius*, “more easily,” II, 1044 A: “By which they are more easily given.”

[Col. 1348A] **PLANUS**, “impostor.” II, 329 B: “Whom they considered an impostor in signs and a rival in teachings.” Cicero also uses the word in *For Cluentius* 26. It also occurs twice in Pliny, XXXV, 10. II, 342 C: “Or rather some impostor.”

**PLASMATOR** (“molder,” “creator”). II, 599 A: “The molder of man.”

**PLAUSOR** (“applauder”). I, 458 A: “Accomplices or applauders of criminal factions,” that is, supporters, helpers, or at least persons wishing for the same thing.

**PLAUTIOR**, “better suited for applause.” II, 1039 A: “Whereby shells, with their mossy woolliness, grow more luxuriantly shaggy.”

**PLENITUDO** (“fullness”). I, 500 A: “From fullness of knowledge,” Greek ἐκ τῆς πληροφορίας, “from full assurance.” II, 39 B: “That the Apostles were ignorant of the fullness of preaching,” that is, did not possess the highest and most perfect knowledge of the whole of Christian doctrine. II, 202 A: “Because, by not possessing the whole, it does not enter into the fullness of the comparison.” II, 218 A: “I adore the fullness of Scripture.” I, 653 A: “And the fullness of discipline owed to it.”

**PLENIUS**, “more perfectly.” I, 377 B.

[Col. 1348B] **PLUMBATUS** (“weighted with lead”). I, 440 A: “To be in lead-weighted hands.” Lead-weighted hands are soldered to some support of lead, so that they do not easily slip or gape open. This was required especially in the bending of the arms and the extension of the hands.

**PLURIMUM**, adverbially, for “for the most part.” II, 311 B: “For the most part from recollection of the offense.” Terence, *Phormio*: “I shall proceed home, where the greater part is.”

**POENICUM**, for *Poenum*, “a Punic man.” II, 1032 A: “Lest a Punic man among Romans either blush or grieve.”

**POENITENTIA** (“penance,” “repentance”). II, 42 B: “With the condition of the penance granted to him,” namely, of penance. A bishop, or the Church, was said to “grant penance” in the sense of imposing penance and its rules. II, 200 C: “From his repentance I shall turn it back against him.” Perhaps *ex peniculo illius*, “from his brush,” should be read, for the author continues in the allegory drawn from painting. A *peniculus* is a painter’s instrument with which colors are applied. The meaning, therefore, is: “From [Col. 1348C] his brush I shall turn it back against him, and shall take colors and outlines from him.”

**ΠΟΛΙΤΕΥΜΑ** (“commonwealth,” “citizenship”). “This is ours: citizenship in heaven,” II, 355 C.

**POLLINCTOR**, νεκρόκομος, νεκρολείπτης, “an undertaker,” one who anoints and tends the bodies of the dead. I, 347 A: “From a soothsayer, an undertaker.” II, 136 C: “And an undertaker-priest,” concerning one who sacrifices human beings.

**POLUCIBILIS** (“sumptuous”). II, 978 A: “Unless more sumptuous in catering,” that is, more elegant, lavish, and opulent.

**POLLUCTUM.** Varro, book V of *The Latin Language*: “When offerings from merchandise have been presented to Hercules upon the altar, then it is *polluctum*.” A *pollucta* and *pollucibilis* dinner is opulent and splendid. *Pollucere* means to consecrate, to sacrifice with a sumptuous banquet. I, 474 A.

**POMA** (“fruits”). I, 1344 A: “For to this end I know the fruits of the sea.” Thus he calls shellfish, because they were designed not for ostentation but for flavor and food.

**PONERE** (“to stop,” “to end”). II, 721 A: “So that the subject matter may stop there,” that is, so that this discourse concerning the soul may end there.

[Col. 1348D] **PONTICUS**, that is, Marcion. II, 248 A.

**POMPATICUS** (“pompous,” “processional”). I, 1326 A: “Thus to advance in pompous procession.”

**POMPATUS** (“more pompous”). I, 638 B: “But the apparatus of the circus games is somewhat more pompous.”

**POPINA** (“cookshop,” “tavern”). II, 970 B: “It is certainly your practice to furnish cookshops in prisons for martyrs of uncertain standing.” Lucian: Συνεκάθευδον μετ’ αὐτοῦ διαφθείροντες τοὺς δεσμοφύλακας, εἶτα δεῖπνα ποικίλα εἰσεκομίζετο, “They slept there with him after bribing the prison guards; then meals of various kinds were brought in.”

**POPULARIA** was the name given in the theater to the seats of the people or commoners, as distinguished from those of the equestrian order. I, 635 A: “And the separation of the commoners’ seats along the slope.” In the same way, the *equestria* were the seats of the knights.

**POPULARITAS** (“the general public,” “public consensus”). II, 257 B: “Finally, the greater consensus of the human race,” that is, the agreement of the nations. “Public consciousness”: II, 97 A: “The duties, honors, solemnities, and popular observances of the world.” II, 700 A: “By a council for relieving the public multitude.”

**PORTA** (“gate”). II, 540 A: “Since they also institute the gates five years beforehand”—anastrophe: *portas ante*, “the gates before,” instead of *ante portas*, “before the gates.” He says that they train those who are to be initiated for five years before they enter the gates and are sealed when about to enter them. Scaliger thinks the text should read: “since they institute *epopts* five years beforehand,” that is, masters of ceremonies to train and supervise the initiands in advance. For in sacred rites the ἐπόπται ἢ ἔφοροι, “overseers or supervisors,” were those who had already spent the prescribed time in the office of μύσται, “initiates”; hence Aristophanes uses ἐποπτεύειν, “to become an epopt.” In the same passage, “so many sighs for the gates,” that is, the whole object which they had desired for so long.

[Col. 1349A] **PORTARE** (“to carry”). The Greeks also regularly use it in the sense of “to take away.” II, 373 A.

**PORTIO**, that is, “counterpart,” “like.” I, 293 A: “Nero’s counterpart in cruelty”; thus “a counterpart of the edict” means a similar edict. I, 326 A: “From some portion of seed.” Havercamp reads *sparsione*, “dispersal,” καταβολὴ σπέρματος, “depositing of seed.” Compare Hebrews XI, 11. II, 965 C: “Restricted drink is the counterpart of xerophagy”; that is, under this restricted form of drink, dry food is also signified.

**PORTIONALE** (“partial”) is opposed to *summale*, “total.” II, 226 A; 964 B: “For by the exception of certain foods, the fast is partial.” II, 894 C: “And the partial to the universal.”

**POSTERITAS** (“posteriority”), that which crept in afterward. The Schoolmen rather unusually call it *posterioritas*. II, 44 A: “Posteriority belongs to falsehood.” II, 48 A: “With that aforementioned rope of posteriority preceding,” that is, the order of succession by which, as though by a rope, one climbs up to the apostolic Churches. II, 197 B: “For brevity’s sake, we are accustomed to plead posteriority against heretics,” that is, that they introduce a doctrine later than the doctrine of truth. II, 157 A: “The very posteriority of all heretics will now prove it.”

**POSTREMUS** (“last”). I, 1316 A: “The very last of all.”

**POSTUMARE** (“to be later”). I, 387 A: “Even if they are later than Moses.” II, 858 B: “For every consummation and perfection, although later in order, anticipates in effect.”

**POSTUMATUS** (“later status”). II, 590 A. It is opposed to priority.

**POSTUMUS** (“later,” “subsequent”). II, 121 A: “Whatever is propagated from the posterior part of the body.” II, 938 A: “And it was being prepared as though a later offspring.”

[Col. 1349C] **POTESTAS** (“power,” “authority”). I, 437 A: “In view of the immediately effective authority,” that is, the authority which represents and exercises in the world the role of divine power and can exact punishment at once. II, 371 A: “Because his word was in power,” that is, it was powerful.

**POTESTATIVUS** (“possessing authority”). II, 215 A: “It takes precedence not only as ordering but also as authoritative.”

**PRAE.** I, 436 A: “At hand,” that is, readily available.

**PRAEBERE**, “to render,” “to represent.” II, 233 B: “You wish what is uncompounded to be represented by compounded things.”

**PRAEBIBERE** (“to drink beforehand”). II, 122 A: “For some will also, by making haste, drink protection beforehand; but,” etc.; that is, they fortify themselves in advance with antidotes, amulets, and φυλακτήριοις potionibus, “protective potions,” but their power is dissolved by sexual intercourse and rendered ineffective.

**PRAECERPERE**, “to taste.” II, 158 B: “If I have had a taste of both languages.”

**PRAECIPITARE**, “to strike down,” “to cast down,” used of crops and fruits that are cast down before their proper time and ripeness. I, 406 A: “Some hidden defect in the air strikes it down while in flower.” *Praecipere*, “to forestall,” “to anticipate”: I, 489 A: “He does not anticipate [Col. 1349D] the separation,” that is, God does not forestall, before the time of the eternal judgment, the separation between the good and the evil, in which judgment partly consists.

**PRAECOQUUS** (“premature,” “unripe”). II, 1031 B: “Prematurely Roman,” that is, by an early and overhasty assumption of Roman dress. Others read *Romana praecoqua*, “premature Roman things,” that is, made Roman through surrender by an early and hasty decision. II, 331 C: “Premature and, as it were, abortive Marcionites.” II, 429 A: “So premature—indeed, so bitter—a love.”

**PRAEDAMNARE**, that is, “to condemn.” I, 283 A: “The word alone condemns both the unknown thing and its author in advance.” I, 1260 B: “He always justifies the poor and condemns the rich.”

**PRAEESSE** (“to prevail”). I, 522 A: “As though every line of reasoning did not prevail,” that is, as though the entire force of the arguments by which the alternation of human souls and their transmigration into other bodies is established and defended did not bind all the more if it is affirmed that souls are recalled each to its own body.

**PRAEFARI** (“to speak beforehand”), used of prophets. I, 378 B: “Concerning the office of speaking beforehand.” Rhenanus reads “of prophesying.” But the former is more genuinely Latin among those for whom seers *fari*, “utter,” and *profari*, “speak forth,” everywhere, when they *praefari*, “speak beforehand.” I, 413 A: “They speak beforehand with panting,” concerning the preliminary utterance which they made precede their prophecy. Compare Numbers XXIV, 3, and Donatus on Terence, *Phormio* IV, scene 4, at the words: “The diviner forbade it.”

[Col. 1350A] **PRAEFORMARE**, “to train very thoroughly,” “to train beyond others.” I, 266 A–B: “For how greatly they are trained for evil.” But Heumann, with Havercamp, reads *reformantur*, “are reformed.”

**PRAEFUGERE** (“to escape beforehand”). II, 706 B: “But even if the soul should escape before the final sword,” that is, if perhaps amid the tearing of beasts the soul should depart from the body before being driven out by the final sword-stroke.

**PRAEGNATUS**, “advanced pregnancy,” pregnancy near childbirth. II, 649 A; 650 C: “The young woman’s pregnancy and childbirth, of course.” II, 754 C: “Both conception and advanced pregnancy.”

**PRAEJUDICARE** (“to prejudge,” “to establish beforehand”). I, 265 A: “They prejudge that it is so,” that is, although they wish to remain ignorant, nevertheless in that force which they inflict upon themselves so as to persist in their ignorance, a certain knowledge—a prejudgment, namely—is implicit: that those things could not be hated if they were known more fully. II, 157 A: “By which judgment has equally been given beforehand against all heresies.” *Praejudicare* often means to give or foreshadow a preliminary judgment. II, 659 A: “This condition of bodily property has already prejudged concerning the other accidents of corporeality,” that is, the condition of bodily property will now constitute a certain manifest judgment for judging the other accidents of corporeality.

[Col. 1350B] **PRAELATIO** (“preference,” “superiority”). II, 801 C: “And greater by a hand because of its superiority, lest it be compared with the whole.” I, 573 A: “Preference for one cannot exist without insult to the other.”

**PRAELATOR** (“one who prefers”). II, 984 B: “The same one who prefers mercy.”

**PRAELUDERE**, the same as *proludere*, “to skirmish beforehand,” which in military affairs is ἀκροβολίζεσθαι. II, 324 A: “I shall have made these preliminary skirmishes.”

**PRAELUMINARE** (“to illuminate beforehand”). II, 841 B: “The parable pre-illuminated by the commentator on the Gospel.”

**PRAEMEDITATORIUM**, προμελετητήριον, “a place for preliminary practice.” II, 960 C: “It becomes a rehearsal room for privies.”

**PRAEMITTERE** (“to send ahead”). II, 970 B: “Having already sent ahead the juice of his blood,” namely, sent ahead to God, toward whom he himself is already hastening in his entirety. Thus elsewhere he spoke of a wife “sent ahead” and children “sent ahead,” who had already departed from the world.

[Col. 1350C] **PRAEPARATURA** (“preparation”). II, 402 B: “In John he had performed the preparation of the Lord’s ways”: preparation, παρασκευασμός, ἑτοιμασία.

**PRAESCRIBERE** (“to plead an exception”). II, 197 B: “For brevity’s sake, we are accustomed to plead posteriority against heretics.” II, 158 B: “Yet not on that account to plead an exception against the monarchy,” etc. II, 130 B: “An exception is pleaded against me, forbidding me to call any other God.” II, 256 A: “Pleading that God could not even have been unknown,” that is, by answering the opponents’ objections and refuting them. II, 888 B: “That truth requires this, against which no one can plead an exception,” that is, from which nothing can be detracted by any argument. II, 948 A: “Plead steadfastly that things commanded to certain persons are not commanded to all.” II, 849 A: “He prescribes everything,” that is, he comprehends everything within prescribed definitions. I, 307 A: “An exception is pleaded against you on the basis of your concealment.” I, 170 C: “See, therefore, how many things they prescribe for you.”

**PRAESCRIPTIO** (“legal exception,” “preliminary plea”). In forensic usage it signifies a refutation by which the defendant overturns his adversary’s accusation or turns it back upon him. Compare *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 35. He calls it “the prescription against heretics,” because we plead against the heretics on the very ground that they are heretics. Against them we oppose this single exception: that they bring forward doctrines contrary to the rule; and thus, as persons who ought not even to be heard, we exclude them from all disputation. II, 248 A: “Concerning the prescription of novelty.” II, 60 B: “To be repelled by definite, just, and necessary prescriptions.” He calls the prescriptions necessary because, in accordance with them, a judge must repel all who wish to bring an action. II, 157 B: “But with this prescription remaining intact”; for he had said before: “Whatever is first is true; whatever is later is adulterated.” II, 248 A: “Lest the summary method of prescription, invoked everywhere, be ascribed to lack of confidence.” By the “summary method of prescription” he means that thesis: “To such a degree is heresy,” etc.

[Col. 1351A] **PRAESCRIPTIVE** (“by way of prescription”). II, 261 B: “Although it is so easy to meet it by way of prescription,” that is, by interposing a prescription through which I may exclude and destroy his entire action. What this prescription is, he shows in what follows.

**PRAESIDIUM** (“resources,” “property,” “private means”). I, 495 B: “Certainly with some resources.”

**PRAESIGNARE** (“to mark beforehand”). II, 260 A: “Which the Creator’s testimony had marked beforehand,” that is, had displayed a sign by which it might be recognized.

**PRAESPERARE** (“to hope for beforehand”). II, 513 A: “Therefore, he who was foretold was also hoped for beforehand”; Greek προελπίζειν.

**PRAESTANTIA** (“rendering,” “bestowal”). II, 302 B: “And the bestowal made on account of those who deserve it,” namely, by which he bestows rewards or punishments. II, 432 C: “Another commands them to remain secure concerning his performance.” In a bad sense: I, 1233 B: “For I recognize my own performance in sins.” II, 650 B: “But let the ambition for either superiority look to itself,” that is, φιλοπρωτεία, “love of preeminence.”

**PRAESTARE** (“to be preferable”). II, 281 C: “For whom it would be preferable to exist by cutting.” The meaning is: for which holiness it would be preferable—that is, more useful and desirable—to exist “by cutting,” that is, to be equal to marriage by cutting and excising it. “It would go better with us,” he says, “if ours were a holiness that led us to abolish marriage and made us exempt from marriage.”

[Col. 1351B] **PRAESTRUCTIM**, “by a prepared and fortified route.” II, 1038 A: “So that one might reach man by a prepared route.”

**PRAESUMERE**, “to suppose,” ὑπολαμβάνειν. I, 274 A: “Since you presume our crimes from the confession of the name.” I, 1317 B: “Wishing that it should continue in us, yet not presuming it.”

**PRAESUMPTIO** (“presumption,” “confidence”). II, 993 B: “When I have thus also overthrown the presumption of the opposing side.” I, 1237 A: “The presumption of baptism entails it,” that is, confidence that pardon will be obtained through baptism.

**PRAESUMPTORIE** (“presumptuously”). II, 462 B: “For Peter too uttered something presumptuously,” that is, with overconfident presumption.

**PRAETENDERE** (“to extend before,” “to put forward”). II, 619 B; 620 C, note 10: “And Judah extends before Israel”; *Jerusalem* should be read, from Zechariah XIV, [Col. 1351C] 14. *Praetendere* means to stretch before or opposite, or even in a circuit, as is explained in the following clause. II, 272 B: “I put forward another rule against him,” that is, I use another argument against him and unfold the rule of truth to him. II, 927 A: “We put forward the necessities of auxiliary means.” II, 80 A: “Tradition will be put before you as author.”

**PRAETER.** II, 572 A: *praeter haereticorum*, “except that of the heretics”; Greek πλήν, “except.” The heretics claimed the spiritual race for themselves. II, 169 C: “Except one God.”

**PRAETERESSE**, “to abstain,” “to renounce.” I, 466 A: “Since we also abstain from the very things concerning which they are conducted,” that is, since, moved by the conscience of our religion, we abstain from the very superstitions—or frenzy, lust, and cruelty—for the sake of which spectacles are conducted.

**PRAETRACTATUS** (“preliminary treatment”). II, 107 A: “And to determine it from this very preliminary treatment.”

**PRAEVELLERE** (“to tear away beforehand”). II, 150 A: “What does he tear away from it more effectively than martyrdoms?” Wherever God tears men away from idolatry, what does he tear away more effectively from idolatry, so as immediately to oppose what has been torn away to idolatry itself, than the martyrdoms of courageous Christians?

[Col. 1351D] **PRAEVENIRE** (“to anticipate”). II, 23 A: “He anticipates condemning.” A Hellenism. II, 312 B: “Therefore I fled beforehand to Tarshish.”

**PRAEVENTO.** II, 23 A. For this Latinius more correctly reads *praeverto*, and Rigault *praevenio*; both correspond to the Greek φθάνειν, “to anticipate.”

**PRAEVERTERE**, “to place before,” “to prefer.” II, 1045 B, as often in Plautus. II, 1028 B: “Who prefers the sinner’s repentance to his death.”

**PREMERE** (“to thresh”). II, 586 A: “He will have threshed the whole mass of his seed.”

**PRESSUS** (“deep,” “concentrated”). II, 1036 B: Purple is called *pressa* when it shines more purely and intensely, possessing the color of pressed and congealed blood and appearing blackish. II, 59 A: “More compelling testimonies.”

**PRINCIPALITAS** (“priority”), that which existed from the very beginning; the Schoolmen call it “priorness.” II, 44 A: “Toward the priority [Col. 1352A] of truth.” II, 668 A: “You have the priority of the soul.”

**PRIORATUS**, πρωτεῖον, “first place,” “primacy.” II, 546 B: “As minds are accustomed to be aroused by rivalry for first place.”

**PRISTINUS** (“former”). II, 970 B: “Which not even that Pristinus of yours, the non-Christian martyr, had attained.” Rigault thinks the text should read *pristinum vester*, “your former man,” that is, “one formerly yours,” just as at the beginning of the book *Against Praxeas* he said “a former teacher.” Many details in the story Septimius tells fit what Lucian relates concerning Peregrinus. For Lucian reports that Peregrinus too enjoyed reputation and esteem among the Christians for a time; that because of discourses about Christ which he delivered through the towns of Syria, he was arrested and cast into prison; and that there he was tended with the utmost care through the charity and devotion of the Christians, with meals of every kind brought in, and was soon afterward released from prison through the governor’s clemency. Later, however, he was expelled from Christian fellowship because of certain offenses, wandered through various cities practicing philosophy not without glory, and finally at Athens, driven by a mad craving for fame, summoned a crowd and, after delivering a lengthy discourse on contempt for death, leapt into a burning pyre and was seen no more. Septimius says that this martyr, whatever sort of man he was, was a “psychic,” that is, a Catholic, but not a Christian, because under torture he had denied Christ. Lucian writes that his Peregrinus was cast into prison because he worshiped Christ, τὸν μέγαν ἐκεῖνον ἄνθρωπον ἐν Παλαιστίνῃ ἀνασκολοπισθέντα, “that great man who was crucified in Palestine”; that the Christians immediately ran to him as to a brother and supplied every kind of consolation; and indeed that, after bribing the guards, they remained with him in prison and spent the night there.

[Col. 1352B] **PRO.** II, 1044 B: “The punishment was as for rape,” that is, the same punishment applied to so shameful a degradation as to rape.

**PROBARE** (“to demonstrate”). II, 191 A: “When he wished himself to be demonstrated.” Irenaeus: ὅτε οὖν ἠθέλησεν ἐπιδεῖξαι αὐτόν, “when, therefore, he wished to display himself.” Irenaeus’s Latin translator renders it *ostendere*, “to show.”

**PROBOLA**, προβολή, “emanation.” II, 162 D: “Προβολήν, that is, the bringing forth of one thing from another.” II, 163 B: “This will be the emanation of truth,” that is, the true generation of the Son. II, 163 A: “Valentinus distinguishes his emanations.”

**PROBUS** (“sound,” “excellent”). II, 1033 A: “Sound through calmness,” that is, sound by virtue of tranquility—tranquil, so that no one can justly complain of it. Latinius proposes *pronum*, “inclined.”

[Col. 1352C] **PROCEDERE** means to attend sacred rites, to go to a temple. I, 1294 A: “If one must attend the rites.”

**PROCLIVARE** (“to incline,” “to lean”). II, 234 B: “They inclined more toward the other.”

**PROCURARE** (“to administer,” “to serve”). II, 576 B: “For whom all heresies are administered,” ᾧ ἅπασαι αἱρέσεις οἰκονομοῦνται, “for whom all heresies are managed,” in forensic usage. II, 158 C: “Because it is administered through so many thousands of powers,” namely, the monarchy. II, 961 A: “Whom riches themselves serve.” II, 277 A: “But rivalry serves the liberation of man.” II, 799 A: “Under the pretext of administering,” that is, ministering to the emperors.

**PRODICIA** (“female betrayer,” “informer”). I, 567 A: “See what sort of informer you have suborned against us.” Here he calls her *prodicia*, whom in the *Apology* he calls an *index*, “informer.”

**PRODIGERE** (“to squander,” “to consume”). I, 525 A: “He may have squandered it into nothing,” that is, even though they may have acted extravagantly and prodigally in destroying and dissipating the body. Others read *redegerit*, “reduced.” I, 378 A: “When almost all the Creator’s ages have already been spent,” that is, carried onward. II, 483 B: “When this age has been spent,” that is, consumed. I, 1245 A: “To be restored to a salvation squandered,” that is, shamefully lavished, cast away, and lost.

[Col. 1352D] **PRODUCERE**, properly, of metals, “to extend,” “to hammer out.” I, 301 A: “And silver-bearing metals hammered out into dishes.” II, 609 A: “By which he brought them out of the land of Egypt,” for “led out.” II, 908 B: “Religion did not bring them forward,” that is, does not cause them to come into the Church of God and join themselves to it. I, 614 B: “And for this purpose brought them out onto the arena.” Athletes and gladiators are properly said to be “brought forward” into spectacles by the sponsors themselves who present the games.

**PRODUX** (“product,” “offshoot”). II, 786 B: “And the product of mutual intercourse.” He says *produx* just as above he said *tradux*, “offshoot.” Thus he calls the unity produced by mutual intercourse the “product of mutual intercourse”; or rather, that which springs apart from the joining of both when things that had been closely joined are torn from one another.

[Col. 1353A] **PROFERRE** (“to bring forth”). II, 163 A: “Was the Word of God brought forth, or not?”

**PROFESSIO**, ἀπογραφή, “declaration” or “registration,” used of merchants who declare their goods. I, 494 A: “How much is lost to taxation through the fraud and falsehood of your declarations.” II, 469 B: “But his declaration is completed by the authority of another.”

**PROFESSOR** (“declarant”). II, 469 B: “No one is both his own declarant and his own witness.”

**PROFLIGARE**, “to complete the greater part of a thing.” II, 503 B: “By how much the little work is briefly brought near completion,” that is, the more nearly this book is completed and brought to its end. Thus Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* V, 6: “The question has now been brought near completion and almost carried to its conclusion.”

**PROINDE**, the same as *perinde*, “just so,” as often in Cicero.

**PROLATIO** (“continuation,” “duration”; also “bringing forth”). II, 24 A: “Every prolongation of seeking and finding.” II, 552 A: “It received the function of its own propagation,” that is, procreation. II, 172 D: “That I introduce some προβολή, that is, a bringing forth of one thing from another”; “some extension belonging to sublimity and brightness.”

[Col. 1353B] **PROMA**, ταμεῖον, “storehouse.” II, 297 B: “You have filled your storehouses.”

**PROMULGARE** (“to make public law and bring into public use”). I, 534 B: “Who first introduced cherries from Pontus into public use in Italy.”

**PROMULSIS** (“serving vessel,” “platter”). II, 1048 B: “A five-hundred-pound platter,” that is, a dish made from five hundred pounds of silver.

**PRONUS**, construed with the dative. II, 234 C: “Inclined neither to evil nor to good.”

**PROPE.** II, 1033 A: “You come near denying that it is the same,” that is, you are not far from denying that it is the same.

**PROPERABILIS** (“rapid,” “hastening”). II, 722 A: “With rapidly advancing heat.”

**PROPERATUM**, something issued too hastily, or begun prematurely and left rough. II, 246 B. See Jerome, commentary on Obadiah.

**PROPRIE** (“properly,” “independently”). II, 664 B: “Not because it is properly spirit,” that is, apart from the soul and in itself.
