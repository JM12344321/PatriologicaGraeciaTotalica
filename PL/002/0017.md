What God has established, he himself also observes. The Father must necessarily have a Son in order to be Father, and the Son a Father in order to be Son; but to have is one thing, and to be another.

For example, in order that I may be a husband, I must have a wife; I shall not myself be my own wife. Likewise, in order that I may be a father, I have a son; I shall not myself be my own son. And in order that I may be a son, I have a father; I shall not myself be my own father. For it is what I have that makes me what I shall then be: I shall be a father if I have a son, and a son if I have a father. But if I myself am what I ought to have, then I no longer have what I myself am: neither a father, because I myself shall be the father; nor a son, because I myself shall be the son. Now, insofar as I must have one of these in order to be the other, if I am both I shall be neither, because I do not have the other. For if I myself am the Son who is also the Father, I no longer have a son, but am myself the Son. Yet by not having a son, since I myself am the Son, how shall I be Father? For I must have a son in order to be Father. Therefore I am not the Son, because I do not have the Father who makes the Son. Equally, if I myself am the Father who is also the Son, I no longer have a father, but am myself the Father. Yet by not having a father, since I myself am the Father, how shall I be Son? For I must have a father in order to be Son. Therefore I shall not be the Father, because I do not have the son who makes the father.

This will be the sum total of the devil’s ingenuity: to exclude each by means of the other, since, by enclosing both in one under the patronage of the monarchy, he causes neither to be possessed—so that he is not Father, since of course he has no Son, and he is not Son, since equally he has no Father. For insofar as he is Father, he will not be Son. Thus those who uphold the monarchy contain neither Father nor Son.

## CHAPTER XI

But you will have to prove it from the Scriptures as openly as we prove that he made his Word a Son to himself. For if he names a Son, and the Son will be none other than the one who proceeded from him, while the Word proceeded from him, then the Word will be the Son, not the one from whom he proceeded. For he did not himself proceed from himself. Moreover, when you say that the Father and the Son are the same, you make the same one, who is God, both bring forth from himself and proceed forth. Even if he could have done it, he nevertheless did not do it.

Or else produce the proof I demand, corresponding to my own; that is, show that the Scriptures present the Son and the Father as the same, just as among us Father and Son are demonstrated to be distinct—distinct, I say, not divided. Thus I bring forward what God said: “My heart has poured forth the excellent Word” (Ps. 44:1). You in turn should oppose to this some place where God said, “My heart has poured forth the excellent word,” in such a way that he himself is both the one who poured forth and what he poured forth; both the one who brought forth from himself and the one brought forth—if he himself is both the Word and God.

See, I set forth that the Father said to the Son: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you” (Ps. 2:7). If you wish me to believe that he himself is both Father and Son, show that it is stated elsewhere in this manner: “The Lord said to himself, I am my Son; today I have begotten myself”; and likewise: “Before the morning star I begot myself” (Ps. 109:3); and, “The Lord established me as the beginning of his ways for my works; before all the hills I begot myself” (Wis. 8:22); and any other texts of this sort.

Whom, however, did God, the Lord of the universe, fear, so that he would not declare it in this manner if such were the reality? Or did he fear that he would not be believed if he had straightforwardly declared himself both Father and Son? There is, however, one thing he feared: to lie. But he feared himself and his own truth. And therefore, because I believe God to be truthful, I know that he declared no otherwise than he arranged, nor arranged otherwise than he declared.

You, moreover, make him a liar, a deceiver, and one who misleads this faith, if, when he himself was his own Son, he assigned the person of the Son to another, since all the Scriptures display both a manifestation and a distinction of the Trinity. From these also our prescriptive rule is derived: that the one who speaks, the one about whom he speaks, and the one to whom he speaks cannot be regarded as one and the same, because neither perversity nor deception befits God—as would be the case if, when he himself was the one to whom he was speaking, he spoke rather to another and not to himself. [Note: [Col. 0166D] “Was speaking,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.; “would speak,” Jun.]

Receive, therefore, also other utterances of the Father concerning the Son through Isaiah (Isa. 42:1): “Behold my Son whom I have chosen, my beloved in whom I have been well pleased. I shall place my Spirit upon him, and he will announce judgment to the nations.” Receive also the words addressed to him (Isa. 49:6): “It is a great thing for you to be called my Son, to establish the tribes of Jacob and to turn back the dispersion of Israel. I have appointed you as a light of the nations, that you may be salvation to the end of the earth.”

Receive now also the utterances of the Son concerning the Father (Isa. 61:1): “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me; therefore he has anointed me to bring good news to humankind.” Again, to the Father in a Psalm concerning the same matter: “Do not forsake me until I announce your arm to every generation yet to come” (Ps. 71:18). Again, in another Psalm (Ps. 3): “Lord, why have those who oppress me multiplied?” Indeed, almost all the Psalms sustain the person of Christ: they represent the Son speaking to the Father—that is, Christ addressing God.

Observe also the Spirit speaking in the third person concerning the Father and the Son: “The Lord said to my Lord, Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies the footstool of your feet” (Ps. 110:1). Again through Isaiah: “Thus says the Lord to my lord, Christ” (45:1). Again through the same prophet, to the Father concerning the Son: “Lord, who has believed our report? And to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed? We proclaimed concerning him: he was like a little child, like a root in thirsty ground, and there was neither form nor glory in him” (Isa. 53:1–2).

These few are taken from many. For we do not seek to unroll the whole of Scripture, since, while calling to witness the full majesty and authority present in its individual chapters, we have a larger engagement with the matter in our treatises. [Note: [Col. 0167D] “We have,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] By these few passages, then, the distinction of the Trinity is nevertheless plainly set forth. For there is the one who speaks, namely the Spirit; the Father to whom he speaks; and the Son about whom he speaks. So too the other things that are declared—now to the Father about the Son, or to the Son; now to the Son about the Father, or to the Father; now to the Spirit—establish each person in his own distinctive character.

## CHAPTER XII

If the number of the Trinity still scandalizes you, as though it were not joined together in simple unity, I ask how the unique and singular God speaks in the plural: “Let us make man according to our image and likeness” (Gen. 1:26), when he ought to have said, “I shall make man according to my image and likeness,” since he is unique and singular. But also in what follows: “Behold, Adam has become like one of us.” Is he deceiving or jesting, that, although he was one, alone, and singular, he should speak numerically? Or was he perhaps speaking to angels, as the Jews interpret it, since they too do not acknowledge the Son? Or, because he himself was Father, Son, and Spirit, did he therefore, presenting himself as plural [Note: [Col. 0167D] “Displaying,” Wouw.], speak to himself in the plural?

No. Rather, because the Son, the second person, his Word, was already adhering to him, and the third, the Spirit in the Word, he therefore declared in the plural, “Let us make,” “our,” and “us.” With whom, then, did he make man, and to whom did he make him similar? With the Son, indeed, who was to clothe himself with man; and with the Spirit, who was to sanctify man. He spoke as with ministers [Note: [Col. 0167D] “Fellow ministers,” Jun.] and witnesses, from the unity of the Trinity. [Note: [Col. 0167D] “Similar? With the Son,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.]

Finally, the Scripture that follows distinguishes between the persons: “And God made man; according to the image of God he made him” (Gen. 1:27). Why did it not say “his own,” if there was one who made and no one according to whose image he made? But there was one according to whose image he made: namely, the Son. Since the Son was to be the more certain and truer man, he caused that man who was then to be formed from clay to be called his image—the image and likeness of the true one. [Note: [Col. 0167D] “Of the true,” Rhen. Pam. Seml. Oberth.]

But how is it written concerning the preceding works of the world? First, before the Son had yet appeared: “And God said, Let there be light; and it was made” (Gen. 1:3)—the Word himself being at once the true light that enlightens the human being coming into this world, and through him the worldly light also. Thereafter, with the Word, Christ, standing by and administering, God willed things to come into being, and God made them. [Note: [Col. 0167D] “May have willed,” Rig. Venet.] “And God said, Let there be a firmament; and God made the firmament” (Gen. 1:7); and, “God said, Let there be lights; and God made the greater and the lesser light” (Gen. 1:14–16). The same one who made the former things also made the rest: that is, the Word of God, through whom all things were made and without whom nothing was made. He himself is God, according to John (1:1): “The Word was God.”

You have two: one who says that it should be made, and another who makes it. But I have already declared how you should understand “another”: as a designation of person, not of substance; for distinction, not division. Otherwise, although everywhere I maintain one substance in three who cohere, nevertheless, by the necessity of the sense, I must speak of the one who commands and the one who acts as “another.” For he would not command if he himself were doing it, since he was commanding that it be done through him. [Note: [Col. 0168D] “For since he commanded, it was to be done,” Lat. Rig.; “since he commanded. Yet he commands it to be done through him,” Jun.] Nevertheless, he was commanding—whereas, if he were one, he would not have been going to command himself—or he would have acted without a command, since he would not have waited for himself to command.

## CHAPTER XIII

“Therefore,” he says, “if God spoke and God made; if one God spoke and another made, two gods are proclaimed.” If you are so obtuse, suppose it for the moment; and so that you may suppose it still more, receive the statement in the Psalm (44:7–8) that speaks of two gods: “Your throne, O God, is forever; the rod of your kingdom: you have loved righteousness and hated iniquity; therefore God, your God, has anointed you.” If it speaks to God and affirms that an anointed God was anointed by God, this passage plainly presents two gods. [Note: [Col. 0168D] “But this presents two gods” in place of “the rod of your kingdom,” Seml. Pam.; “it affirms, and this presents two Gods,” Lat.]

Hence Isaiah also says in the person of Christ (Isa. 45:14): “And the Sabaeans [*sabain*], men of lofty stature, will cross over to you, and will follow after you bound by the hands, and will worship you, because God is in you. For you are our God, and we did not know it, the God of Israel.” [Note: [Col. 0168D] “Sabaim,” Fran.; “Seboim,” Seml.] Here too, by saying both “God is in you” and “you are God,” he presents two: the one who was there, and the one in whom he was [Note: [Col. 0168D] “Who were in Christ,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.]—Christ and the Spirit. [Note: [Col. 0169D] *Spiritum* (“Spirit”), s.; *plus* (“more”), Latin.; *in Christo ex S. plus* (“in Christ from S., more”), Jun. [apparatus wording uncertain].]

More than this, you will find the same number in the Gospel: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God” (John 1:1). There is one who was, and another with whom he was. I also read the name “Lord” applied to two: “The Lord said to my Lord, Sit at my right hand” (Ps. 109:1). And Isaiah says this: “Lord, who has believed our report? And to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?” (Isa. 53:1). For he would have said “your arm” [Note: [Col. 0169D] “He would not have said ‘his arm’ if...,” Seml.], not “the Lord’s,” if he had not wished the Lord as Father and the Lord as Son to be understood.

Genesis, still more ancient, also says: “And the Lord rained sulfur and fire upon Sodom and Gomorrah from the Lord out of heaven” (Gen. 19:24). Either deny that these things are written, or who are you to think they should not be understood as they are written—especially those passages whose meaning is not contained in allegories and parables, but in definite, certain, and straightforward statements? [Note: [Col. 0169D] “And” absent from Seml.] But if you are one of those who at that time could not endure the Lord’s showing himself to be the Son of God, and would not believe him to be Lord, remember that it was written against you together with them: “I said, You are gods and sons of the Most High” (Ps. 82:6); and, “God stood in the assembly of gods.” Thus, if Scripture did not fear to call human beings—made sons of God through faith—“gods,” know that it much more justly bestowed the name “God” upon the true and only Son of God.

“Therefore,” you say, “I challenge you, on the authority of these Scriptures, steadfastly to proclaim even today two gods and two lords.” Far be it from us. For we, who by God’s grace examine both the occasions and the reasons of the Scriptures—being especially disciples of the Paraclete, not of human beings [Note: [Col. 0169D] “Especially disciples of the Paraclete, not of human beings.” Unless I am mistaken, he is referring to the Paraclete of the new prophecy. For the followers of Montanus boasted that they were not disciples of human beings, but of the Paraclete. RHEN.]—do indeed define two, Father and Son, and now three with the Holy Spirit, according to the principle of the economy [οἰκονομία, *oeconomia*], which produces number. This is so that, as your perversity infers, the Father himself may not be believed to have been born and to have suffered—a thing it is not permissible to believe, since it was not so handed down.

Nevertheless, we never bring forth from our mouth the expressions “two Gods” and “two Lords”—not as though the Father were not God, the Son God, the Holy Spirit God, and each one God; but because formerly both “two gods” and “two lords” were proclaimed, so that, when Christ had come, he might both be acknowledged as God and be called Lord, because he is the Son of God and of the Lord. For if only one person of both God and Lord were found in the Scriptures, Christ would rightly not have been admitted to the name “God” and “Lord.” [Note: [Col. 0169D] “To” added by Seml.] For no other was proclaimed besides one God and one Lord, and it would have appeared that the Father himself had descended, since “one God” and “one Lord” was read; and his entire economy, which was foreseen and dispensed as the substance of faith, would have been obscured.

But when Christ came and was known by us to be the one who had formerly produced the number [Note: [Col. 0169D] “Is” absent from Seml.]—being made second from the Father, and third with the Spirit, while the Father was now more fully manifested through him—the name of God and Lord was now reduced to unity. [Note: [Col. 0169D] “And,” Seml.] This was so that the nations might pass from the multitude of idols to the one God, and that [Note: [Col. 0170D] “So that,” Seml.] a distinction might be established between worshipers of one divinity and worshipers of a plurality. For Christians had to shine in the world as sons of light, as the light of the world, worshiping and naming one God and Lord.

Otherwise, if, from the consciousness by which we know that the name “God” and “Lord” is fitting for Father, Son, and Spirit, we called them “gods” and “lords,” we should have extinguished our torches and become more timid even before martyrdoms. For an opportunity of escape would then lie open [Note: [Col. 0170D] “Lies open,” Seml.] to us as well [Note: [Col. 0170D] “In every way,” Seml.], if we immediately swore by “gods and lords,” as do certain heretics whose gods are numerous. [Note: [Col. 0169D] Whose gods are numerous. He particularly indicates the Valentinians because of their immense medley of divine Aeons; they were, as it were, shoots and branches of the Gnostics from a corrupt root. LE PR.]

Therefore, I shall by no means say “gods” or “lords”; rather, I shall follow the Apostle, so that if Father and Son are to be named together, I shall call the Father “God” and name Jesus Christ “Lord.” But I shall be able to call Christ alone “God,” as does the same Apostle: “From whom is Christ, who is God over all, blessed forever” (Rom. 9:5). For I shall also call a ray of the sun “sun” when considered separately; yet when I name the sun to which the ray belongs, I shall not immediately call the ray “sun” as well. For although I shall not make two suns [Note: [Col. 0170D] “I shall make two,” Seml.], nevertheless I shall reckon both the sun and its ray as two things and two forms of one undivided substance, just as God and his Word, and the Father and the Son. [Note: [Col. 0170D] “As,” Seml.]

## CHAPTER XIV

[Col. 0170C]

There still supports us, in maintaining two—the Father and the Son—the rule that has defined God as invisible. [Note: [Col. 0170D] “Which,” Seml.] For when Moses in Egypt desired the sight of the Lord (Exod. 33:13, 20) [Note: [Col. 0170D] Moses had desired the sight of the Lord in Egypt. Yet Moses himself relates that he had such a conversation with God not in Egypt but on Mount Sinai in Arabia. We therefore find it remarkable that not even one interpreter or commentator, down to our own times, has noticed this error of Tertullian’s, obvious though it is. EDD.], saying, “If, therefore, I have found favor before you, manifest yourself to me, that I may see you knowingly,” he replied, “You cannot see my face, for a human being will not see my face and live”—that is, whoever sees it will die.

For we find that God was seen by many, yet none of those who had seen him died. God was indeed seen according to human capacity, not according to the fullness of divinity; for the patriarchs are reported to have seen God, such as Abraham and Jacob, and the prophets, such as Isaiah and Ezekiel, and yet they did not die. Therefore, either they ought to have died if they saw him—for “no one will see God and live”—or, if they saw God and did not die, Scripture lies when it reports that God said, “If a human being sees my face, he will not live”; or Scripture lies when it presents God now as unseen and now as seen.

There will therefore be another who was seen, because the same one who was seen cannot be defined as invisible. Consequently, we must understand the Father to be invisible on account of the fullness of his majesty, but acknowledge the Son to be visible according to the measure of his derivation. Similarly, we are not permitted to gaze upon the sun with respect to the very totality of its substance in heaven, but we can endure its ray with our eyes because of the moderated portion extended from it to the earth.

Here, in opposition, someone will wish to contend that even the Son is invisible, as Word and as Spirit; and, by claiming a single condition for Father and Son, he will insist instead that Father and Son are one and the same. [Note: [Col. 0171D] “One,” Seml.] But we have said that Scripture supports the difference through the distinction between visible and invisible. For they also add the following to their argument: “But if it was the Son who was then speaking to Moses, he himself would have declared his own face visible to no one, because he himself was of course the invisible Father under the name of the Son.” And for this reason they wish the same one to be understood as both visible and invisible, just as they understand the same one to be Father and Son: since shortly before, before he had denied his face to Moses, it was written that the Lord had spoken with Moses face to face, as one speaks to his friend. [Note: [Col. 0171D] “And” inserted by Seml.] Jacob likewise said: “I saw the Lord face to face” (Gen. 32:30). Therefore, the same one is visible and invisible. [Note: [Col. 0171D] “And” absent from Fran. Paris.] And because the same one is both, he himself is invisible insofar as he is Father, and visible insofar as he is Son. [Note: [Col. 0172D] “Because also the Son,” Seml.; “the Father was formerly visible,” Jun.]

As though our interpretation of Scripture did not apply to the Son, with the Father set apart in his invisibility! [Note: [Col. 0172D] “Visibility,” Seml.] For we say that the Son also, in his own right, is thus far invisible: insofar as he is the Word and Spirit of God, by the condition of his substance even now, and insofar as he is God, Word, and Spirit. But he was visible before the flesh in the manner in which he says to Aaron and Miriam (Num. 12:6–8): “If there is a prophet among you, I shall make myself known to him in a vision, and I shall speak to him in a dream—not as to Moses. I shall speak to him mouth to mouth in a form,” that is, in reality, “and not in a riddle,” that is, not in an image. [Note: [Col. 0172D] “Because,” Seml.; “who is God as,” Jun.; “as also,” Rig.] As the Apostle also says: “Now we see as through a mirror in a riddle, but then face to face” (1 Cor. 13:12).

Therefore, since he reserves his sight and face-to-face conversation with Moses for the future—for this was afterward fulfilled in the retirement on the mountain, as we read in the Gospel (Luke 9:30), where Moses was seen conversing with him—it is apparent that formerly God, that is, the Son of God, was always seen in a mirror, a riddle, a vision, and a dream, by the prophets and patriarchs as well as, thus far, by Moses himself.

And even if the Lord did perhaps speak face to face, nevertheless a human being did not see his face as it truly is, except perhaps in a mirror and a riddle. Finally, if the Lord spoke to Moses in such a way that Moses came to know his face close at hand, how is it that immediately, in the same place, he desires to see his face—which he would not desire if he had seen it? Likewise, how does the Lord deny that his face can be seen, if he had shown it—assuming he had shown it? Or what is the face of God whose sight is denied, if it was the one that had been seen? Jacob says, “I saw God face to face, and my soul was preserved” (Gen. 32:30). The face that kills if it is seen must be another.

Or was it perhaps the Son who was seen, and indeed his face, but this very sight occurred in a vision, dream, mirror, and riddle, because the Word and Spirit cannot be seen except in an imaginary form? [Note: [Col. 0171D] “But he calls his face,” etc. The author introduces this as an argument of the Praxeans; for he also refutes it with the words, “For what father?” etc., showing that the Son should rather be called the face of the Father, and this from Scripture, Lam. 4. PAM.] But he calls the invisible Father his face.

For what, then, is the Father? Will he not be the face of the Son, by designation of the authority that the one begotten from the Father obtains? For is it not also fitting to say of some greater person: “That man is my face,” and “He represents my face”? “The Father,” he says, “is greater than I” (John 14:28). Therefore, the Father will be the face of the Son. For what does Scripture also say? “The Spirit of his person is Christ the Lord” (Lam. 4:20). Therefore, if Christ is the Spirit of the Father’s person, he rightly declared the one whose person he was the Spirit of—that is, the Father—to be his face, namely by reason of their unity. I should indeed be surprised if the Father could be understood as the face of the Son, since he is his head: “For the head of Christ is God” (1 Cor. 11:3).

## CHAPTER XV

If I do not resolve this point by means of questions arising from the Old Scripture, I shall take confirmation of our interpretation from the New Testament, lest you likewise defend as belonging to the Father whatever I attribute to the Son. For behold, both in the Gospels and in the Apostles I find a visible and an invisible God, under a manifest and personal distinction between the condition of each.

John exclaims, as it were: “No one has ever seen God” (John 1:18). Certainly, then, no one did so in the past either. For by saying that God was never seen, he removed the question of time. The Apostle also confirms concerning God: “Whom no human being has seen, nor indeed can see” (1 Tim. 6:16)—because, of course, whoever sees him will die. Yet these same Apostles testify that they both saw Christ and touched him. [Note: [Col. 0171D] “These same Apostles,” etc. He attributes to the Apostles in the plural what John alone writes in 1 Epistle 1, and soon cites. The reason for this, apparently, is that John spoke in the plural: “What we saw, what we heard, we saw with our eyes, and our hands touched, concerning the Word of life.” Here again, a little later, he changes the order of the words, since both the Greek and the Latin now read: “what we heard, what we saw with our eyes, what we beheld, and our hands touched.” PAM.]

Moreover, if Christ himself is both Father and Son, how was he both seen and unseen? To reconcile this diversity of the seen and the unseen in one, who on the other side will not argue that both statements were rightly made: that he was visible in the flesh, but invisible before the flesh, so that the same one is the Father invisible before the flesh and the Son visible in the flesh? Yet if the same one was invisible before the flesh, how is he also found to have been seen formerly, before the flesh? Equally, if the same one whom flesh later made more fully visible was formerly seen in a riddle, then the Word, of course, who became flesh, is someone other than the one whom no one ever saw—namely the Father, to whom the Word belongs.

Finally, let us examine whom the Apostles saw. “What we saw,” says John (1 John 1:1), “what we heard, we saw with our eyes, and our hands touched concerning the Word of life.” For the Word of life was made both audible and tangible because he was flesh—the one who before the flesh was only the Word, in the beginning with God the Father, not the Father with the Word. For although the Word is God, he is with God, because he is God from God, because he is with the Father, in the Father’s presence.

“And we saw his glory, as of the only-begotten from the Father” (John 1:14)—the glory, certainly, of the Son, namely the visible one, glorified by the invisible Father. And therefore, because [Note: [Col. 0173C] “How,” Seml.] he had called the Word of God “God,” lest [Note: [Col. 0173C] “So that,” Seml.] he lend support to the presumption of his adversaries, as though he had seen the Father himself, he adds more than enough, in order to distinguish between the invisible Father and the visible Son: “No one has ever seen God” (John 1:18). Which God? The Word? Yet it was declared beforehand, “We have seen, heard, and touched concerning the Word of life.” But which God, then? The Father, of course, with whom, as God, the Word was—the only-begotten Son, namely, who himself expounded him from the Father’s bosom. [Note: [Col. 0173D] “God,” Seml.; “namely” absent from Seml.; “is in the bosom,” Seml.]

He himself was both heard and seen, and, lest he be believed a phantom, was even touched. Paul too beheld him, yet he did not see the Father. “Have I not,” he says, “seen Jesus?” (1 Cor. 9:1). He also called Christ “God”: “Whose are the fathers, and from whom is Christ according to the flesh, who is God over all, blessed forever” (Rom. 9:5). [Note: [Col. 0173D] “The Lord himself,” Seml.; “through,” Seml.] He too [Note: [Col. 0173D] “He” absent from Seml.] shows the Son of God to be visible, that is, the Word of God, because, having become flesh, he was called Christ.

But concerning the Father he says to Timothy: “Whom no human being has seen, nor indeed can see” (1 Tim. 6:16). He intensifies the statement further: “Who alone possesses immortality and dwells in inaccessible light.” Concerning him he had also said above: “But to the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God.” [Note: [Col. 0173D] “But to the King of the ages, immortal, invisible,” etc. It is worth noting here that not only the blessed Ambrose, Jerome, and Augustine, when citing this passage of the Apostle, 1 Tim. 1, but also the present author, read nothing other than “the only God.” From this it is inferred that the word “wise,” which some present-day Greek manuscripts insert, was added from the end of the Epistle to the Romans. Hence Clarius’s correction in that passage was also much too free. PAM.]

Thus we should attribute to the Son the contrary qualities: mortality [Note: [Col. 0173D] “And” inserted by Jun.] and accessibility. Scripture testifies that he died according to the Scriptures (1 Cor. 15:3), and that he was seen by Paul at the last, certainly through accessible light—although Paul himself did not experience even that light [Note: [Col. 0173D] “And” added by Seml.] without danger to his sight; nor did Peter, John, and James experience it without danger to their reason and without stupefaction. [Note: [Col. 0174D] “Nor Peter and John and James without reason and madness.” That is, neither without danger to reason nor without stupefaction. He says “danger to reason” as above “danger to sight,” that is, damage. Ursinus’s book reads, “without a testing of reason”; Pithoeus’s, “without reason and madness.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0173D] “Reasons,” Seml.; “through irrationality,” Fran.; “through wandering,” Rhen. and Jun.; “of reason from stupefaction,” Wouw.] If they had seen not the glory of the Son, which they were capable of suffering, but the Father, I believe they would have died on the spot. For “no one will see God and live.”

If these things are so, it is established that the one seen at the end is the one who was always seen from the beginning, and that the one not seen even at the end is the one who was not seen from the beginning; and thus there are two: the seen and the unseen. Therefore, it is the Son who was always seen, the Son who always held converse, and the Son who always acted, by the Father’s authority and will. For the Son can do nothing from himself unless he sees the Father acting—that is, acting in thought. [Note: [Col. 0174C] “Thinking,” Seml.] For the Father acts in thought [Note: [Col. 0174D] “In thought,” Jun.], while the Son, seeing what is in the Father’s thought, brings it to completion. [Note: [Col. 0174D] “Who,” Venet. Rhen.] Thus all things were made through the Son, and without him nothing was made.
