And so (Luke 19) salvation also comes to the house of Zacchaeus. By what merit? Had he too perhaps believed that Christ had come from Marcion? On the contrary, that cry of the blind man was still in everyone’s ears: “Have mercy on me, Jesus, son of David”; and all the people were rendering praises to God—not Marcion’s god, but David’s. Indeed, although Zacchaeus was perhaps a foreigner, he had nevertheless been inspired with some knowledge of the Scriptures through intercourse with the Jews. More than that [Note: Rhenanus inserts “if”], and though you do not know it, [Col. 0451D] he had fulfilled the commandments of Isaiah (Isa. 58:7). [Col. 0452A] “Break,” he says, “your bread for the hungry, and bring those who have no shelter into your house.” This was precisely what he was then doing, receiving the Lord into his house and feeding him. “And if you see one naked, clothe him.” This was precisely what he was promising to do, offering half his substance for every work of mercy; [Note: [Col. 0451D] “Undoing the bonds of oppressive contracts.” The word *obnexus* is compounded from *nexus*, a term very commonly used by the jurists. Hence Papinian’s expression in *Digest*, *Familiae erciscundae*, law *Si pater familias*, “the bond of a pledge”; and Ulpian’s, in law *Si non saltem*, § *Adeo*, title *De condictione indebiti*, “the bond of a sale.” Hence also Cicero, *Pro Murena*, says “to bind by a bond,” and Tacitus, book III, “released from the bonds of the law.” To the same subject belong the “law of bond” and the law concerning “money placed under obligation by bond”—that is, by a particular kind of civil obligation through which, as Budé says, a person’s body, no less than a pledge, was subjected to a bond. PAM.] undoing the bonds of oppressive contracts [Note: Semler reads “of those liable”], releasing the afflicted into freedom, and canceling every unjust written obligation, when he said: “And if I have extorted anything from anyone by false accusation, I restore it fourfold.” Accordingly the Lord says: “Today salvation has come to this house.” He thereby testified that the things commanded by the Creator’s prophet were salutary.

But when he says, “For the Son of Man came to save what was lost” [Note: Others read “what had perished”], I shall no longer press the point that he came to save what had been lost, which had belonged to the one to whom it was lost and whose lost thing [Col. 0452B] he had come to save; instead, I direct the argument to another stage of the question. There is no doubt that a human being is under discussion. Since a human being consists of two substances, body and soul, we must ask in which kind of substance he appears to have been lost. If in the body, then the body had been lost, but the soul had not. The Son of Man saves what had been lost; the flesh, therefore, also possesses salvation. If the soul too had been lost, the soul’s perdition is destined for salvation, while the flesh, which had not been lost, is already safe. If the whole human being had been lost in both substances, the whole human being must necessarily be saved; and thus the doctrine of the heretics who deny the salvation of the flesh is overthrown. Christ is now also confirmed as the Creator’s Christ, since in accordance with the Creator he promised the salvation [Note: “salvation” is absent from Rigault] of the whole human being.

The parable of the servants also, who are judged according to the accounting of the lord’s money put out at interest, [Col. 0452C] shows that God is a judge, even in respect of severity: he not only gives honor, but also takes away what someone appears to have possessed. Or if here too Marcion represents the Creator as harsh, taking up what he did not lay down and reaping what he did not sow, here too that very one instructs me whose money he teaches me to put out at interest.

## CHAPTER XXXVIII

Christ knew whence the baptism of John came (Luke 20); why, then, did he ask as though he did not know? He knew that the Pharisees would not answer him; why, then, did he ask in vain? Was it so that he might judge them from their own mouths—or from their hearts? Refer these matters, therefore, both to the vindication of the Creator and to the comparison with Christ; and now consider what would have followed if the Pharisees had given any answer to the question. Suppose they had answered that John’s baptism was of human origin: they would immediately have been crushed with stones. Some Marcion would then arise [Col. 0452D] [Note: “Some Marcion would arise”—that is, if one should emerge, originate, or rise up. For so Asconius Pedianus interprets this verb in Cicero. RHEN.] [Note: Semler reads “some Marcion”; the Venetian edition, “someone belonging to Marcion”] against Marcion, [Col. 0453A] who would say: “O most excellent God! O God so different from the Creator’s examples! Knowing that men would rush headlong, he himself placed them upon the precipice [Note: The manuscript reads *praerupium*.].” For this is also how the Creator is treated in the discussion of the law concerning the tree.

But John’s baptism was from heaven. “And why,” Christ says, “did you not believe him?” Therefore he who had wished John to be believed, and who was about to rebuke them because they had not believed, belonged to the one whose sacrament John administered. Certainly, when they refused to disclose what they thought, he himself in turn opposed them: “Neither do I tell you by what power I do these things.” He returns evil for evil [Note: Pamelius reads “he returned evil for evil”].

“Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s.” What things, then, will be God’s? Those which resemble Caesar’s denarius—namely, God’s image and likeness. He therefore commands that the human being be rendered to the Creator, in whose image and likeness, and in whose name and material, [Col. 0453B] he was stamped. Let Marcion’s god seek a coin for himself. Christ commands that the human denarius be rendered to its own Caesar, not to another’s—[Note: Except that he who has no denarius of his own must necessarily do so. Septimius means: Let Marcion’s god, who has no denarius of his own, seek a coin for himself. Let him seek another’s human being, since he has none of his own—inasmuch as he created none. RIG.] except, of course, that one who has no denarius of his own must necessarily do so.

It is a just and proper rule that in every question the meaning of the answer must pertain to the subject of the inquiry. Otherwise, to answer one thing when asked another is the act of a madman. So much the more must that be far removed from Christ which is not even becoming in a human being. The Sadducees, deniers of the resurrection, having a question about it, had put before the Lord a case from the Law concerning a woman who, after seven brothers had died in succession, had married them in accordance with the legal commandment: whose wife was she to be reckoned at the resurrection? This was the subject of the question; this was the substance of their inquiry. Christ [Col. 0453C] must necessarily have answered this. He feared no one, so that he should appear either to have evaded their questions or, on the occasion of them, to have covertly indicated something that at other times he did not teach openly.

He therefore answered: “The children of this age marry.” You see how directly this pertains to the issue. Since the question concerned the coming age, in which he was about to declare [Note: Others spell *definiturus* as *diffiniturus*.] that no one marries, he first laid down that marriage does take place here, where death also exists; but those whom God has deemed worthy of possessing that age and of the resurrection from the dead neither marry nor are given in marriage, because they will no longer die, since they are [Note: Semler reads “become”] like the angels, having become children of God and of the resurrection.

Since, therefore, the meaning of an answer must be directed to nothing other than the subject of the question, if the subject of the question is resolved by this meaning of the answer, [Col. 0454A] the Lord’s answer means nothing [Note: Others add “with respect to”] other than that by which the question is resolved. You have both the periods in which marriage is permitted and denied—not from a question concerning marriage in its own right, but from the question of the resurrection. You also have confirmation of the resurrection itself and the whole subject about which [Note: [Col. 0453D] “The Sadducees, deniers of the resurrection.” Epiphanius (*Haer.* 14) says: *ἠθετήσαν μὲν γὰρ οὗτοι νεκρῶν τὴν ἀνάστασιν, ὁμοῖα Σαμαρείταις φρονοῦντες*—“For these men denied the resurrection of the dead, holding the same opinions as the Samaritans.” Nor did they reject only the resurrection, but also the immortality of the soul, as Josephus testifies, *Jewish Antiquities*, book XVIII. Blessed Jerome, *Dialogue Against the Luciferians*, teaches that the heresies both of the Sadducees and of the Samaritans were propagated by Dositheus: “I pass over,” he says, “the heretics of Judaism, who before the coming of Christ destroyed the Law that had been handed down: that Dositheus, the leader of the Samaritans, rejected the prophets; that the Sadducees, springing from his root, denied even the resurrection of the flesh.” They were called Sadducees after Sadok, a disciple of Antigonus; Antigonus had been a disciple of Simeon the Just, as is related in *Pirke Avot*, chapter 1. LE PR.] the Sadducees were asking. They were not asking about another God, nor inquiring about the law of marriage considered by itself.

But if you make Christ answer matters about which he was not consulted, you deny that he was able to answer those about which he was questioned—having evidently been outwitted by the wisdom of the Sadducees. Now, beyond what is required, and after establishing this rule, I shall reconsider the related arguments. For, having seized upon the wording of Scripture, they ran its words together in their reading as follows: “But those whom God has deemed worthy of that age.” They join “of that age” to “God,” [Col. 0454B] so as to create another God belonging to that age. Yet it ought to be read: “But those whom God has deemed worthy”—with a division made here after “God,” so that “of that age” belongs to what follows; that is, those whom God has deemed worthy of possessing that age and the resurrection. For the inquiry concerned not God, but the condition of that age: whose wife she would be in that age after the resurrection.

Thus they also overturn the answer concerning marriage itself: “The children of this age marry and are given in marriage,” they say, refers to the Creator’s people, whose God permits marriage; but those whom the God of that age—that is, another god—has deemed worthy of the resurrection do not marry even here, because they are not children of this age. Yet when he was consulted about marriage in that age, not in this one, he denied the marriages about which he was being consulted. And so those who had grasped the very force of the wording, the utterance, and its divisions [Col. 0454C] understood nothing other than what pertained to the subject of the inquiry. Indeed, the scribes said, “Master, you have spoken well.” For he had confirmed the resurrection by setting forth its form against the opinion of the Sadducees. Finally, he did not reject the testimony of those who understood him to have answered in that way.

But if the scribes considered Christ to be the son of David, while David himself calls him Lord, what relevance has this to Christ? [Note: [Col. 0454D] “Not David, but the error of the scribes.” Lest matters be treated confusedly, it must be noted that there were two kinds of scribes. Some were *γραμματεῖς τοῦ νόμου*, “scribes of the Law,” and others *γραμματεῖς λαοῦ*, “scribes of the people.” The scribes of the people were clerks or public recorders; they were not, however, heretics, although it is certain that there were other scribes who were heretics. See Scaliger in the *Elenchus*. LE PR.] He was not refuting David, but the error of the scribes; rather, David was securing honor for Christ, whom he affirmed to be the Lord Christ [Note: “Christ” is absent from the Paris and Frankfurt editions] rather than merely the son of David—a thing that would not be fitting for one who destroyed the Creator.

But from our side, how fitting [Col. 0455A] is the interpretation! For he who had once been invoked by that blind man as the son of David—something he had then left unanswered, since the scribes were not present—now deliberately brought the matter forward of his own accord in their presence, so that he might show himself, whom the blind man in accordance with the scribes’ teaching had proclaimed only as David’s son, also to be David’s Lord. Thus he rewarded the blind man’s faith, by which the man had believed him to be the son of David, while striking at the tradition [Note: Others read “the public exposure”] of the scribes, by which they did not know him also as Lord. Whatever pertained to the glory of the Creator’s Christ [Note: Junius omits “of Christ”] would thus [Note: The Venetian edition reads “if”] be maintained by none other than the Creator’s Christ.

## CHAPTER XXXIX

It has long been established (Luke 21), concerning the ownership of the names, that it belongs to him [Note: Rigault and others insert “It has long been established concerning the ownership of the names that it belongs to him” after “him,” from the manuscript of Wouwer], who first announced his Christ among humankind and gave him the additional name Jesus. [Col. 0455B] The shamelessness of one who says that many will come in his name will therefore also be established, since it is not his name if he is not the Creator’s Christ and Jesus, to whom ownership of the names belongs. More than this: he forbids the reception of those whose equal he himself is, since he too has likewise come in another’s name. If the name was not his, it was not for him to forewarn the disciples against a false claim to the name; it was he who, through ownership of the name, possessed its truth. Those men will finally come, saying, “I am Christ.” You will receive them, since you received one just like them. For he too came in his own name.

What now of the fact that the Lord of the names himself, the Creator’s Christ and [Note: Oberthür omits “Christ and”] Jesus, will also come? Will you reject him? How inequitable, how unjust, and how unworthy of the most excellent God it would be for you not to receive the one coming in his own name, when you received another [Note: Paris reads “a stranger”] in his name!

Let us also see [Col. 0455C] what signs he assigns to the times: wars, I suppose, kingdom against kingdom and nation against nation, pestilence, famines, earthquakes, terrors, and portents from heaven—all of which are fitting for a severe and cruel God [Note: The Venetian edition reads “is fitting”]. When he also adds [Note: Others read “says”] that these things must take place, what does he show himself to be: the destroyer or the approver of the Creator? He confirms that the Creator’s arrangements must be fulfilled, whereas, if they were not his own, as the most excellent God he would have abolished such grim [Note: Junius reads “so” (*tam*)] and cruel things rather than established them.

Before these things, moreover, he foretells that persecutions and sufferings will come upon them—certainly for martyrdom and salvation. Hear what was foretold in Zechariah (Zech. 9:15): “The Lord Almighty,” he says, “will protect them; [Col. 0456A] and they will consume them and stone them with sling-stones, and they will drink their blood like wine and fill the bowls as though they were bowls of the altar [Note: Frankfurt reads “as bowls for the altar”]; and the Lord will save them on that day like sheep, his people, because [Note: Semler reads “who”] holy stones roll about.”

And lest you think that these things are foretold of the sufferings that awaited them from foreigners under the heading of so many wars, look to the particulars. No one, in a prediction of wars to be waged with regular weapons, lists stoning, which is more characteristic of popular gatherings and an unarmed riot. No one measures such rivers of blood in war by the capacity of bowls or equates them with the bloodshed of a single altar [Note: Pamelius reads *cruentatione*]. No one calls those men sheep who fall in war while armed and themselves [Note: Junius reads “and” (*et*)] contending with the same ferocity; rather, the term applies to those who, surrendering themselves in their proper character and patience, and sacrificing themselves rather than avenging themselves, [Col. 0456B] are slaughtered. Finally, [Note: [Col. 0455D] “Because holy stones roll about.” These words from Zechariah are also cited according to the Septuagint edition: “they roll about,” *κυλίονται ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς*, “they roll upon the earth.” RIG.] “because [Note: Semler reads “who”] holy stones,” he says, “roll about”—not because [Note: Paris and Frankfurt read “not but that”] soldiers fight.

For they are also stones and foundations upon which we are built: “built,” according to Paul (Eph. 2:21), “upon the foundation of the apostles,” who, as holy stones exposed to the blows of all, rolled about. Therefore here too the very one who forbids them to consider beforehand what they ought to answer before tribunals is he who suggested to Balaam [Note: Oberthür reads “Balae”] what he had not considered—indeed, the opposite of what he had considered (Num. 22)—and who, when Moses pleaded the slowness of his tongue, promised him a mouth. He also demonstrated through Isaiah (Isa. 44:5) that very wisdom which no one could resist: “This one will say, ‘I belong to God,’ and will cry out in the name of Jacob, and another will inscribe himself [Note: Others read “will inscribe”] with the name of Israel.” For what is wiser and more irrefutable than the simple and forthright confession made in [Col. 0456C] the name of a martyr who prevails with God? [Note: [Col. 0455D] This is the interpretation of “Israel.” Concerning the meaning of this word, Saint Jerome (*Questions on Genesis*) proves at length that “Israel” properly means the same as “a prince of God” or “one directed by God”: “As for the interpretation,” he says, “given in the *Book of Names*, that Israel means ‘a man seeing God’ or ‘a mind seeing God,’ though worn smooth by the speech of almost everyone, it appears to me to have been interpreted not so much truthfully as forcibly.” The question therefore remains whether “prince of God” or “one directed by God” ought rather to be accepted as the interpretation. Jerome himself, however, indirectly resolves this question when he says: “We are guided more by the authority of Scripture and of the angel, or God, who called him Israel, than by any secular eloquence.” Yet God himself, or the angel, interpreted the name Israel in neither of the ways Jerome mentioned, but—as our Tertullian most excellently puts it—as “one who prevails with God”: “Your name,” he says, “shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel; for if you have been strong against God, how much more will you prevail against men?” EDD.]—which is the interpretation of Israel.

Nor is it surprising that he restrained [Note: Others read “forbade”] forethought, since he himself received from the Father the timely supply of what he was to say: “The Lord gives me” (Isa. 50:4) “the tongue of instruction, so that I may know when I ought to bring forth a word”—unless Marcion introduces a Christ who is not subject to the Father.

Nor ought I again to demonstrate that persecutions by one’s neighbors and blasphemy arising from hatred of the name had been foretold. “But through endurance,” he says, “you will save your own selves”—the endurance, of course, of which the Psalm says (Ps. 9:19): “The endurance of the righteous will not perish [Note: Semler reads “shall perish”] forever.” For elsewhere also it says (Ps. 115:5): “Honorable is the death of the righteous”—honorable, without doubt, [Col. 0457A] because of their endurance. Zechariah also says (Zech. 6:14): “The crown, moreover, will belong to those who have endured.”

But do not venture to argue that the apostles were harassed by the Jews because they were heralds of another God. Remember that the prophets too suffered the same things at the hands of the Jews, yet were not apostles of any God other than the Creator.

After indicating the time of the destruction, when Jerusalem had begun to be surrounded by armies, he next recounts the signs of the final end: portents in the sun, moon, and stars, and distress upon the earth among nations stupefied, as though by the sound of a surging sea, in expectation of the evils threatening the world. That even the powers of the heavens are to be shaken, hear Joel (Joel 2:30): “And I will give portents in heaven and on earth: blood, fire, and vapor of smoke. The sun will be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great [Col. 0457B] and glorious day of the Lord comes” [Note: Rigault and the Venetian edition read “will come”].

You also have Habakkuk (Hab. 3:9): “The earth will be torn apart by rivers; the peoples will see you and travail; you will scatter [Note: Rhenanus and Semler read “scattering”] the waters by your stride [Note: The Venetian edition reads “strides”]. The deep gave forth its voice; the height of its fear was raised. The sun and moon stood still in their station; they will go into the light of your flashing [Note: Franequer reads “flashings”; others, “encirclements”], into the splendor; lightning is your shield. In your threatening [reading *comminatione* for the apparent OCR *communicatione*], you will diminish the earth, and in your indignation you will cast down the nations.”

The pronouncements of the Lord and those of the prophets, I suppose, agree concerning the shakings of the world and the inhabited earth, of the elements and the nations. What does the Lord say after these things? “And then [Note: [Col. 0458D] ‘They will see the Son of Man coming.’ Here he proves the second advent of Christ against Marcion from innumerable passages. LE PR.] they will see the Son of Man coming from heaven with very great power. But when these things take place, you will stand erect and lift up your heads, because your redemption will draw near” [Note: Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “will draw near”]. This will be, of course, in the time of the kingdom, to which the parable itself [Col. 0457C] will have been appended [Note: Rhenanus, Semler, and Pamelius read “will be appended”]. “So also, when you see all these things taking place, know that the kingdom of God has drawn near.”

This will be the great and glorious day of the Lord: the coming from heaven of the Son of Man according to Daniel (Dan. 7:3): “Behold, one coming with the clouds of heaven like a son of man,” and so forth. “And royal power was given to him”—the power that in the parable he had gone away to request, leaving with his servants money to be put out at interest [Note: Latinus reads the plural, “which they were to put out at interest”]. And all the nations were given to him, as the Father had promised him in the Psalm (Ps. 2:8): “Ask of me, and I shall give you the nations as your inheritance.” And all glory serves him; and his power is eternal and will not be taken away, and his kingdom will not be corrupted—because in it they will neither die nor marry, but will be like the angels.

Of the same advent of the Son of Man and its fruit [Note: Paris and Frankfurt read “fruits”], Habakkuk says (Hab. 3:13): “You went forth for the salvation of your people, to save your anointed ones”—those, of course, who, having been redeemed [Note: Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read the singular, “the redeemed one”], will stand erect and lift up [Col. 0458A] their heads in the time of the kingdom.

Therefore, since these things belonging to the promises agree in the same way as those belonging to the shakings, you will be unable, given the concord between the prophetic and the Lord’s pronouncements, to insert any distinction here, as though you might refer the shakings to the Creator, the God of cruelty, although the most excellent God ought neither to permit them, much less await them, while assigning the promises to the most excellent God—promises which the Creator would not have prophesied if he were ignorant of that god. Or, if the Creator prophesied his own promises, which do not differ from Christ’s promises, he will be equal to the most excellent God in generosity; nor will your Christ appear to promise any more than my Son of Man.

You will find that the actual course of the Gospel text, from the disciples’ question to the parable of the fig tree, so adheres throughout its connected meaning to the Son of Man [Col. 0458B] that both grim and joyful things, both shakings and promises, converge upon him, and you cannot separate either part from him. For since the advent of one Son of Man has been placed between two outcomes—the shakings and the promises—both the sufferings of the nations and the hopes of the saints must necessarily belong to one Son of Man. For he [Note: Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “who”] has been placed in the middle in such a way as to be common to both outcomes: concluding the one by his advent—that is, the sufferings of the nations—and beginning the other—that is, the hopes of the saints.

Thus, whether you concede that the coming of the Son of Man is the coming of my Christ, in order to attribute to him the impending evils that precede his advent, you will also be compelled to ascribe to him the good things that arise from his advent; or whether you prefer it to be the coming of your Christ, in order to ascribe to him the good things that arise from his advent [Col. 0458C] [Note: Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür omit “or whether you prefer it to be the coming of your Christ, in order to ascribe to him the good things that arise from his advent”], you will also be compelled to attribute to him the evils that precede his advent. For the evils cohere with the advent of the Son of Man by preceding it just as the good things do by following it.

Ask, therefore, which of the two Christs you establish in the person of the one Son of Man, to whom [Note: Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “than whom”] both arrangements are referred. Either you have granted that the Creator is most excellent, or [Note: Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür add “that”] your god is harsh by nature.

Finally, consider the illustration in the parable itself: “Look at the fig tree and all the trees. When they have produced fruit, people understand that summer is approaching. So also you, when you see these things taking place, know that the kingdom of God is near.” For if the fruiting of small trees supplies a sign of summertime by preceding it, in the same way the world’s convulsions also [Note: Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür omit “and” (*et*)] foreshadow the kingdom as a sign by preceding it. Every sign, however, belongs to the one to whom belongs [Note: The manuscript of Wouwer reads “and”] the thing of which it is a sign. And a sign is imposed upon every thing [Col. 0459A] by the one to whom that thing belongs. Thus, if the convulsions are signs of the kingdom, just as fruiting trees are signs of summer, then the kingdom also belongs to the Creator, to whom [Note: Latinus reads “whose”] are assigned the convulsions that are signs of the kingdom.

The most excellent God had declared beforehand that these things—so cruel, so dreadful, and certainly foretold by the Prophets and the Law—must take place. To that extent he was not destroying the Law and the Prophets, since he confirms that what they foretold must be fulfilled. He further insists that heaven and earth will not pass away until all things are accomplished. What “all things” are these? If they are things belonging to the Creator [Note: Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “are done”], the elements will rightly endure until their Lord’s order has been fully discharged. If they are things belonging to the most excellent God, I do not know whether heaven and earth will endure until the things appointed by their rival are fulfilled. If the Creator permits this, he is not a jealous God.

Come now, let earth and heaven pass away, for thus their Lord has appointed, [Col. 0459B] while his Word remains forever; for thus Isaiah also foretold (Isa. 40:8).

The disciples are also admonished lest at any time their hearts be weighed down with gluttony and [Note: [Col. 0459C] “Drunkenness and worldly cares.” The same two things that are forbidden here to the disciples are forbidden to clerics—both presbyters and even bishops—later in Apostolic Canons 6 and 80, canon 6 of the Council of Carthage, and law *Generaliter* 52, *Codex*, title *De clericis et episcopis*. Earlier, however, this appears in Apostolic Canons 41 and 24, by which clerics of every order are forbidden *εἰς καπηλεῖον εἰσιέναι*, “to enter a tavern.” All these provisions are found everywhere among the ancients against the intemperance and luxury of clerics. Read the letter of Blessed Bernard to Fulco, who was later made Archdeacon of Langres. LE PR.] drunkenness and worldly cares, and that sudden day come upon them like a snare—upon those, certainly, who had forgotten God through satiety and preoccupation with the world. The admonition will belong to Moses (Deut. 32:15). Thus the one who formerly introduced this warning is precisely the one who will deliver them from the snare of that day.

There were other places in Jerusalem in which he could teach, and there were places outside Jerusalem to which he could withdraw; nevertheless, during the day he taught in the temple, as one who had foretold through Hosea (Hos. 12:4): “They found me in my temple, and there the matter was disputed with them.” At night, however, he withdrew to the *elaeon* [Note: *Elaeon* means the olive grove, from the Greek word *ἔλαιον*, “olive.” EDD.]; for Zechariah had indicated it thus (Zech. 14:4): “And his feet will stand upon the Mount of Olives.”

There were [Col. 0459C] also hours appropriate for his audience. They had to assemble at daybreak because, after saying through Isaiah (Isa. 50:4), “The Lord gives me the tongue of instruction,” he added [Note: “he added” is absent from the Paris edition]: “He has applied my ear in the morning to listen.” [Col. 0460A] If this is what it means to dissolve the prophecies, what will it mean to fulfill them?
