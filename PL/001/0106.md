How greatly, moreover, the pagans despised these excellent laws and regarded them as nothing you will learn from these further words of our Author: “I see also ‘hundred-as’ dinners, which must now be named from hundreds of sesterces” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 300). “Hundred-as dinners,” as Festus observes, were those upon which, under the Licinian law, no more than one hundred asses might be spent, apart from the produce of the earth—that is, one hundred asses, which were small bronze coins [Col. 0995B] (Festus, s.v. *Centenariae*), as we noted above. Our Author therefore signifies the immoderate increase in the expense of the old frugal dinner. For a dinner which previously had to be furnished at a cost of no more than one hundred asses had in his time come to be provided for one hundred sesterces. A sestertius, moreover, was worth two and a half asses. He used “one hundred sesterces” as a round figure, employing a definite number for an indefinite one, so that by opposing one hundred asses to one hundred sesterces he might censure the excessive cost of dinners. Who, indeed, even upon the most cursory reading of the lives of the Caesars written by Suetonius and other authors, does not perceive that during their reigns dinners were so extravagant that these historians, although trustworthy, can scarcely be believed? You therefore see with what ease the Romans amended and weakened their ancient and venerable laws, [Col. 0995C] even while babbling that they were bound by the most unjust rescripts against the Christians.

Tertullian then similarly declares that those laws had been overthrown which “removed from the Senate a patrician because he possessed ten pounds of silver, regarding it as a serious mark of ostentation” (Tertullian, loc. cit.). If anyone should ask us who that patrician was, we shall answer him in the words of Aulus Gellius: “Gaius Fabricius Luscinus and Quintus Aemilius Papus were censors at Rome, and they expelled from the Senate Publius Cornelius Rufinus, who had twice been consul and had been dictator; and as the reason for this censure they recorded that he had been found to possess ten pounds of wrought silver for the purposes of dining” (Gellius, bk. XVII, *Noctes Atticae*, ch. 2, p. 1005). You hear him note there precisely the same quantity of silver possessed by Rufinus as Tertullian does. But [Col. 0995D] what the former expressed as “for the purposes of dining,” Aulus Gellius elsewhere puts thus: “Because of the stigma of luxury,” he says, “since he possessed ten pounds’ weight of wrought silver” (ibid., bk. VIII, ch. 8, p. 289). We read precisely the same thing in Livy’s *Epitome* (Livy, *Epitome*, bk. XIV). Pliny, however, testifies that the same quantity of silver was spent upon household plate (Pliny, bk. XVIII, *Historia naturalis*, ch. 6, p. 447), while Valerius Maximus says it was spent upon silver vessels (Valerius Maximus, bk. II, ch. 6, § 4). It may perhaps not be tedious to hear his words, which are highly pertinent to our subject. They are these: “Every age has related, and every age hereafter will relate, how the censorship of Fabricius Luscinus refused to retain Cornelius Rufinus in the senatorial order, as a luxurious man setting an evil example, because, after having discharged two consulships and a dictatorship with the greatest distinction, he had acquired ten pounds of silver vessels.” [Col. 0996A] He then immediately added: “By my faith, the very letters of our age seem to me astonished when they are compelled to lend their service to recounting such severity, as though fearing that they may be thought not to be recording the deeds of our city. For it is scarcely credible that within the same sacred boundary ten pounds of silver should once have been both a fortune that provoked resentment and a poverty held in the greatest contempt.”

Does not Tertullian similarly indicate that this quantity of silver was spent by Cornelius Rufinus upon making or purchasing silver vessels? He justly rebukes the still greater luxury of the Romans in these following words: “I see that for serving dishes—that is, for carrying food to diners—it is too little if the silver mines have been drawn out only for senators, and not also for freedmen or men who are still breaking the whips” [Col. 0996B] (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 301). He therefore charges the pagans with the vice that not only senators but even freedmen, still “breaking the whips”—that is, men who had cast off slavery only a few days before—had been released into such luxury. Slaves, indeed, were customarily beaten with whips as a punishment peculiar to their condition. Hence Juvenal sang these verses concerning the slave Liburnus:

> Liburnus is said to have arrived late,  
> And is forced to pay the penalty for another man’s sleep;  
> This one breaks rods, that one grows red beneath the lashes.  
> (Juvenal, *Satires* 6, v. 490.)

In Terence, moreover, Simo threatens his slave Davus with whips and blows as follows:

> I shall have you scourged, Davus, and hand you over to the mill  
> Until you die.  
> (Terence, *Andria* 1, scene 2, v. 8.)

[Col. 0996C] But we shall treat the punishments of slaves more fully in the following dissertation. Yet if the luxury of those who had only just obtained their freedom was so great, judge from this how great that of others must have been. Hence the Pliny just cited says: “That an aged triumphator was censured by the censors on account of five pounds of silver now seems fabulous” (Pliny, bk. III, *Historia naturalis*, ch. 11, p. 69). By what reasoning, then, did the pagans dare to object that they were bound by unjust laws against the Christians, when they themselves had openly and with impunity overthrown those most equitable sumptuary laws?

## ARTICLE IV. With what license the pagans, in contempt of the laws that had destroyed newly established theaters, built many more, to which they flocked wearing cloaks for the sake of greater pleasure

[Col. 0996D] Tertullian proceeds to other laws by which provision had been made for chastity, moral decency, and modesty. He openly shows that these too had been overthrown by the pagans with just as much license as the recklessness with which they alleged that they were bound by other, plainly wicked laws enacted against the Christians: “For what,” he asks, “has become of those laws intended to correct vices, which immediately destroyed theaters as they arose to corrupt morals?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 299). Yet certainly the common opinion of nearly everyone, as Servius teaches (Servius on Virgil, *Georgics* II, v. 381; and *Aeneid* II, v. 737), was that the first theatrical games were instituted in honor of Bacchus, or Father Liber, and for that reason were called the Liberalia. How greatly human morals were corrupted by these games [Col. 0997A] there is surely no one who cannot readily understand from their utterly detestable indecency and obscenity, which we have discussed elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 17, art. 1, and on Lactantius, ch. 35, art. 1). If, however, anyone contends that our Author was speaking not only of these but also of other theatrical games formerly abolished, he will certainly have Cicero as a supporter, as well as Augustine, by whom Cicero is cited. These are Augustine’s own words:

“The Romans, although they were already oppressed by a harmful superstition which made them worship gods whom they saw to have wished theatrical obscenities to be consecrated to themselves, nevertheless, mindful of their own dignity and sense of shame, did not honor the actors of such plays after the custom of the Greeks. Instead, as Scipio says in Cicero: ‘Since they regarded the dramatic art and the entire stage as disgraceful, they wished that class of men not only to be deprived of the honors enjoyed by the other citizens [Col. 0997B] but even to be removed from their tribe by censorial censure’” (Augustine, bk. II, *De civitate Dei*, ch. 13, p. 40 ff.).

Augustine praises and approves this judgment of Cicero, thus cited, in these following words: “An excellent prudence indeed, and one to be counted among the glories of Rome; but I wish that Rome had followed and imitated itself.”

Thus these excellent laws did not remain in force for long. For Tertullian attacked the pagans as follows: “I see theaters too, neither single nor bare” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 301). They were not single, because after the earlier ones had been demolished they had erected far more of them throughout the world. He added “nor bare,” because people customarily flocked there dressed not in ordinary clothing but in the *paenula*, or cloak. “For,” he says, “lest shameless pleasure should grow cold even in winter, the Lacedaemonians were the first to devise for the games [Col. 0997C] the odiousness of the cloak.” In all the manuscripts and in the editions printed before Rigault, however, we read not “the odiousness of the cloak” but simply “the cloak.” Learned men have exercised their ingenuity to no small extent in explaining these obscure and perhaps corrupt words. Heraldus thinks that they may signify that the cloak devised by the warlike Lacedaemonian nation to keep off rain was adopted by the Romans so that they might watch the games more patiently. Tertullian, however, declares clearly enough that it was invented by those same Lacedaemonians not merely for military use but also for the games. Rigault, on the other hand, suspects that Tertullian is speaking ironically of cloaks as excessively heavy and burdensome and thus capable of producing aversion rather than pleasure. But why should Tertullian speak ironically in these words rather than in the others that precede and follow them? [Col. 0997D]

Because Tertullian therefore appears to speak without irony and affirmatively, Ferrari consequently believes that his memory failed him here and that he wrote *paenula* instead of *endromis* (Ferrari, pt. II, *De re vestiaria*, bk. II, ch. 1). The *endromis* was also a thick and shaggy garment, useful in the wrestling school—or rather after exercise in the wrestling school—no less than the cloak. To confirm his opinion Ferrari adduces these verses of Martial:

> This well-padded offspring of a weaver of the Sequani,  
> Which bears the foreign name “Lacedaemonian,”  
> A coarse thing, but not to be despised in freezing December,  
> We send you as a gift, an *endromis* from abroad,  
> Whether you rub yourself with sticky wrestling oil or play the warm game of *trigon*,  
> Or snatch with your hand the dust-covered *harpastum*.  
> (Martial, bk. IV, epigram 19.)

[Col. 0998A] Yet there appears to be no need whatever to alter by conjecture the word *paenula*, which is read consistently in all the copies. No one can doubt that the Romans attended public games wearing cloaks. Suetonius is our witness that the emperor Galba ordered those attending a certain solemn spectacle to keep their hands inside their cloaks (Suetonius, *Life of Galba*, § 6). In the *Epitome* of Dio, moreover, we read that the emperor Commodus commanded the senators to come to the theater for the last gladiatorial show he gave, [non-Latin text omitted]. “He ordered them to come into the theater wearing equestrian dress and cloaks” (*Epitome of Dio*, *Life of Commodus*, p. 295). They had never previously been accustomed to enter it in such attire unless the emperor had departed this life. [Col. 0998B] But [non-Latin text omitted] signifies a particular kind of cloak. Thus Lampridius says of the same Commodus: “Contrary to custom, he ordered them to come to the show wearing cloaks rather than togas” (Lampridius, *Life of Commodus*, p. 315).

Tertullian therefore rightly reproves the Romans, since, contrary to ancient custom, they wore the cloak when attending public games and spectacles, particularly in winter. For because the theaters then lacked roofs, they put on the cloak lest cold or rain harm them and so that they might watch the shameful acts performed there without any inconvenience. Thus, contrary to former customs, they had adopted for pleasure the cloak which soldiers wore solely from necessity. For concerning the soldier who professed the Christian religion before the tribune, Tertullian says: “He laid aside the exceedingly heavy cloaks” (Tertullian, *De corona*, ch. 1). Concerning this [Col. 0998C] garment, moreover, see Octavius Ferrari and Barthold Bartholin, who published special investigations of it, as well as others who wrote books on clothing.

Who, then, does not see with what recklessness the pagans cried out that they were compelled by the emperors’ most unjust laws against the Christians to persecute them, when with such boldness and impunity they utterly despised and spurned other, most equitable laws which commanded theaters to be completely destroyed because of their monstrous corruption of morals? Nor did they value many other excellent laws any more highly, as Tertullian teaches and as we shall show after him.

## ARTICLE V. An examination of the recklessness with which the pagans trampled upon laws commanding distinctions between the insignia of rank and birth and between the garments of matrons and prostitutes, or forbidding women to adorn themselves with gold except in the betrothal ring

[Col. 0998D]

Tertullian then proceeds to another class of laws overthrown by the pagans, laws which had once made most prudent provision for modesty and sobriety, especially among women. For the very men who so insolently invoked laws injurious to the Christian name calmly and cheerfully allowed other laws to be trampled underfoot—laws which did not permit the insignia of rank and honorable birth to be assumed rashly or with impunity (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 300). Who does not know that kings, senators, knights, and plebeians possessed different insignia and different garments by which they were distinguished from one another? To pass over very many examples [Col. 0999A], these are some further words on the subject from our Author himself: “What king displays the insignia of his power upon his shoulder rather than a diadem upon his head, a scepter in his hand, or some mark upon his distinctive garment?” (ibid., *Adversus Judaeos*, ch. 10).

An edict was also issued by Caesar Augustus forbidding anyone other than a senator henceforth to wear the purple garment that was then commonly assumed (Dio, bk. XLIX, *Roman History*, p. 459). Earlier, moreover, Julius Caesar, according to Suetonius, had restricted the use of litters, garments dyed with shellfish purple, and pearls to certain persons and ages and to certain days (Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar*, § 43). The author of Eusebius’s *Chronicle* explains this regulation concerning women as follows: “Women who had neither husbands nor children and were less [Col. 0999B] than fifty-five years old were forbidden to use litters and pearls” (Eusebius, *Chronicle*, 1972).

There was also a great difference between the garments of matrons and those of prostitutes (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 301). Prostitutes, as Nonius noted, wore a more closely girded garment (Nonius, ch. 14, *De genere vestimentorum*). Hence Ulpian says: “If someone had addressed virgins improperly, yet they were dressed in the clothing of slave girls, he appears to offend less seriously, and much less so if the women were dressed in the clothing of prostitutes rather than of respectable married women” (*Digest*, bk. XLVIII, title *De injuriis*, etc., law 15).

How greatly women abused these garments, contrary to the institutions of their ancestors, learn from our Tertullian: “I see,” he says, “that no distinction in attire has been left between matrons and prostitutes” (Tertullian, loc. cit.). He says no more there, but in another book [Col. 0999C] he treats the subject more fully, continuing in this manner:

“Turn also to the women. You may behold what Caecina Severus forcefully impressed upon the Senate: matrons appearing in public without the *stola*. Finally, according to the judgments of the augur Lentulus, the punishment for one who had thus divested herself of her rank was the same as for fornication, because certain women had studiously abandoned the marks and guardians of dignity in their attire as impediments to the practice of solicitation. But now, in soliciting on their own behalf so that they may be approached more openly, they have renounced the *stola*, the *supparum*, the little sandal, the headdress, and even the litters and chairs in which, even in public, they were kept as though at home and in privacy” (ibid., *De pallio*, ch. 4).

In yet another book he attacks still more sharply the same abuse and the rejection of that distinction between the garments of respectable matrons and those of prostitutes. Hear him, I pray, as he speaks:

“How much more blasphemous it is if you who are called priestesses of chastity proceed adorned and painted after the fashion of the unchaste! Or what less do those most wretched victims of public lust possess—women whom laws once restrained from marital and matronly ornaments—but whom the wickedness of the age, continually increasing, has now placed on the same level as every most respectable woman, even to the point that they cannot be distinguished?” (ibid., bk. II, *De cultu feminarum*, ch. 12).

Then a little later:

“That Tamar who had painted and adorned herself was for that reason judged by Judah, on suspicion, to be offering herself for hire; and she was so concealed beneath her veil, the character of her attire falsely representing her as a prostitute, that he both desired her as a prostitute, addressed her as one, and made an agreement with her” (ibid.).

Jerome writes in a manner not unlike this: “Tamar deceived Judah under the guise of a prostitute” (Jerome, bk. I, *Adversus Pelagianos*). [Col. 1000A] Do you hear how ancient the laws distinguishing the garments of matrons and prostitutes were, and how freely the pagans violated them?

Nor did they pay greater heed to other laws which had provided for the modesty and sobriety of women. “In ancient times,” says Tertullian, “no woman knew gold except upon the one finger which her betrothed had pledged with the betrothal ring” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 302). No one is unaware that it was a very ancient custom for the bridegroom to send his bride a ring, called the betrothal ring, as a pledge of the future marriage. If, however, anyone desires at least one witness to this custom to be cited, let him hear Juvenal:

> Yet you arrange the meeting, the compact, and the betrothal  
> In our own day; already you are being combed by the master barber,  
> And perhaps you have given the pledge upon her finger.  
> [Col. 1000B] (Juvenal, *Satires* 6, v. 25 ff.)

Pliny testifies that in his day this ring was made of iron and had no gemstone: “Even now,” he says, “an iron ring is sent as a gift to the bride, and that without a gem” (Pliny, bk. XXXIII, *Historia naturalis*, ch. 1, p. 7).

By the age of our Tertullian, therefore, that wholly modest custom had already been changed, and a gold ring was given instead of an iron one. Later Isidore spoke in the plural of these same betrothal rings as follows: “Women did not use rings except those which a bridegroom had sent to a maiden, nor was she accustomed to have more than two gold rings upon her fingers. But now, because of gold, no part of a woman’s body is light or unburdened” (Isidore, bk. XIX, *Origines*, ch. 32). Did he not in these latter words have regard to the following words of Tertullian, by which, in the very passage [Col. 1000C] under discussion, he censures the luxury of women: “Now, because of gold, no part of a woman’s body is light” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 304)? Hence Ovid had previously sung:

> Do not come forth weighed down with gold sewn into your garments;  
> By such means you often drive away the wealth you do not seek.  
> (Ovid, bk. III, *Ars amatoria*, v. 172.)

Pliny is a further witness for us that he saw Agrippina, the wife of the emperor Claudius—that is, Nero’s wife [sic; the source reading is uncertain]—when Claudius was presenting a spectacle of a naval battle and she was sitting beside him, dressed in a military cloak woven entirely of gold, without any other material (Pliny, ibid., ch. 3, p. 39). This is confirmed both by Dio (Dio, bk. X, *Roman History*) and by Cornelius Tacitus (Tacitus, bk. XII, *Annals*, near the end). Anyone wishing to learn more about this luxury of Roman women should consult Meursius (Meursius, *De luxu Romano*, ch. 4 ff.), [Col. 1000D] Kobierzycki (Kobierzycki, bk. I, *De luxu Romano*, ch. 8 ff.), and, if he has leisure, what we have noted elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 792).

## ARTICLE VI. An examination of the severity with which women were formerly forbidden the use of wine, and of the impunity with which wives were killed by their husbands for violating that prohibition; of women’s later immoderate use of wine, on account of which they did not dare offer the customary kiss to their relatives; of the frequency of repudiating wives, a practice unknown in ancient times; and of the manner in which the pagans renounced their forefathers in speech and manner of speaking

Nothing was more worthy of the ancient integrity of the Romans [Col. 1001A] than that salutary severity by which the use of wine was so completely forbidden to women that, as our Tertullian relates, a matron’s own family starved her to death because she had unsealed the little chest containing the keys of the wine cellar. Under Romulus, moreover, a woman who had touched wine was killed with impunity by her husband Mecennius. “For this reason women were also required to offer kisses to their relatives, so that they might be judged by their breath” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 303). Pliny explains precisely the same matters, though not in the same order, more clearly:

“Women at Rome,” he says, “were not permitted to drink it”—that is, wine. “Among the recorded examples we find that the wife of Egnatius Mecennius was killed by her husband because she had drunk wine from a cask, and that Romulus acquitted him of the killing. Fabius Pictor wrote in his *Annals* that a matron was compelled by her family to die of hunger because she had unsealed a little chest in which were the keys to the wine cellar. Cato says that relatives therefore gave women [Col. 1001B] a kiss so that they might know whether they smelled of *temetum*. That was then the name for wine, whence also the word *temulentia*, drunkenness” (Pliny, bk. XIV, *Historia naturalis*, ch. 13, p. 142).

Who would not readily persuade himself that Tertullian could not have combined all these facts in precisely this way unless he drew them either from Pliny himself or from the same sources as Pliny? Valerius Maximus relates the story of the wife of Egnatius Mecennius somewhat differently, saying that because she had drunk wine she was killed by him without either an accuser or a reprover (Valerius Maximus, bk. VI, ch. 3, § 9), since everyone thought that she had suffered an exemplary punishment for violating sobriety (ibid., bk. II, ch. 1, § 5).

He had previously said that the use of wine was once unknown to Roman women because from it there is but one short step to unlawful sexual intercourse. Moreover, Cato is again cited by Aulus Gellius [Col. 1001C] as reporting that a woman who had either drunk wine or been caught in adultery was condemned in the same manner (Aulus Gellius, bk. X, *Noctes Atticae*, ch. 23, p. 557). Athenaeus, furthermore, not only mentions the Roman prohibition of wine to women, but adds that the wine cellar was not entrusted to them and that kisses were therefore offered by husbands and relatives so that they might detect by smell whether the women had actually drunk wine (Athenaeus, bk. X, *Deipnosophistae*, ch. 7, p. 429, and ch. 11, p. 440). We have already discussed this prohibition of wine (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 4, art. 1).

Yet contrary to this law, “because of wine,” Tertullian says, “no kiss is free” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 304). Various writers, and especially the satirical poets, inveigh against this vice of drunkenness. [Col. 1001D] But in so well-known a matter it will suffice to cite these verses of Juvenal, which plainly confirm what we have said in the preceding article not only about drunkenness but also about luxury:

> No crime or deed of lust has been absent since  
> Roman poverty perished. . . .  
>   
> . . . . . . . . . .  
>   
> Foul money first brought foreign morals among us,  
> And enervating riches shattered the age with shameful luxury.  
> For what does drunken Venus care?  
> She does not know the difference between groin and head,  
> She who bites into huge oysters already at midnight.  
> When perfumes, steeped in Falernian wine, foam;  
> When she drinks from a shell; when, through dizziness, the roof  
> Seems to move and the table rises beneath twin lamps.  
> Go now, and doubt what scornful sniff Tullia draws in,  
> Or what Maura says to the notorious Collacia,  
> When Maura passes the ancient altar of Chastity.  
> (Juvenal, *Satires* 6, v. 293 ff.)

[Col. 1002A] What more need be said? Our Author further reproaches the pagans because the ancient happiness of marriages had vanished—a happiness certainly sustained by morals—during which, for nearly six hundred years after the foundation of Rome, no household issued a bill of repudiation (Tertullian, ibid.). Do you wish this to be proved by certain Greek and Latin witnesses? The first shall be Dionysius of Halicarnassus, whose words are these: [non-Latin text omitted]. “It is agreed [Col. 1002B] that for 520 years no divorce occurred. Only in the seventh year of the 130th Olympiad, when Marcus Pomponius and Gaius Papirius were consuls, is Spurius Carvilius, a man of some distinction, said to have been the first to dismiss his wife. He was compelled by the censors to swear that for the sake of having children he would not retain his spouse, for she was barren; and because of this act, although compelled by necessity, he was forever hated by the people” (Dionysius of Halicarnassus, bk. II, *Roman Antiquities*, p. 96).

The Latin writers Valerius Maximus and Aulus Gellius committed the same account to writing (Valerius Maximus, bk. II, ch. 1, § 4), except that Gellius, citing Servius Sulpicius as his authority, relates that it occurred in the 523rd year after the foundation of the city, when Marcus Atilius and Publius Valerius were consuls (Aulus Gellius, bk. IV, *Noctes Atticae*, ch. 3, p. 278). But there is certainly either here or elsewhere some error in his memory or that of a copyist. [Col. 1002C] For in another book he writes that it occurred in the 519th year after the foundation of the city, in which case the consuls must accordingly have been different, perhaps those named by Dionysius of Halicarnassus (ibid., bk. XVII, ch. 21, p. 1008).

The pagans had therefore utterly and completely departed from these equitable morals and laws. Among them, Tertullian says, repudiation “is now both the object of marriage and, as it were, its fruit” (Tertullian, ibid.). Nor should you suspect that there is any exaggeration on his part here. You will not distrust Seneca, in whom we read these words concerning repudiation:

“Does any woman now blush at divorce, after certain illustrious and noble women reckon their years not by the number of consuls but by the number of their husbands, and leave marriage in order to marry and marry in order to divorce? It was feared so long as it was rare; but because no public records”—that is, records drawn up by public notaries—“were made without a divorce, they learned to do what they so often heard of” [Col. 1002D] (Seneca, bk. III, *De beneficiis*, ch. 16, p. 53).

To this may also be added Juvenal, who attacks these exceedingly frequent divorces of wives in the following verses:

> She therefore commands her husband, but soon abandons this realm,  
> Changes houses, and wears out her bridal veils; then  
> She flies back and retraces her steps to the bed she had scorned.  
> She leaves the doors only lately adorned, the curtains  
> Hanging from the house, and branches still green upon the threshold.  
> Thus the number grows; thus there are eight husbands  
> In five autumns—a fact worthy of inscription on a tomb.  
> (Juvenal, *Satires* 6, v. 223.)

You hear how plainly he said that a single wife had, within five autumns—that is, five years—[Col. 1003A] eight husbands. Martial, moreover, sharply reproves a certain Telesina who, contrary to the enacted laws, had married ten times within a single month and had done so with impunity:

> Since the Julian law, Faustinus, was renewed for the people,  
> And Chastity was commanded to enter their homes,  
> Less than—or certainly no more than—the thirtieth day has dawned,  
> And Telesina is already marrying her tenth husband.  
> A woman who marries so often does not marry: she is an adulteress by law.  
> I am less offended by a more straightforward adulteress.  
> (Martial, bk. VI, epigram 7.)

Indeed, this wicked license of divorce was so unbridled that it could scarcely ever be restrained by any laws of the emperors, many of which still survive. What of the fact that they not only trampled upon so many excellent laws in the manner we have described, but also rejected the reverence owed to their ancestors? Tertullian therefore presses them by addressing them in these words: “In habit [Col. 1003B]—that is, clothing—in diet, furnishings, property, and finally in speech itself, you have renounced your forefathers” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 305). Again, in another book, he says: “It is plainly evident how greatly you have changed from your ancestors in manner of life, clothing, equipment, and diet itself,” as is clear from what has already been said. He added “and in speech itself.” Why also in speech and manner of speaking? “Because,” he says, “you banish the old-fashioned as rancid” (ibid., bk. I, *Ad nationes*, ch. 10, p. 572)—that is, you reject it as disagreeable, putrid, and foul-smelling.

## ARTICLE VII. An examination of the ancient decree forbidding the introduction of new gods without a senatorial decree, and of Metellus’s vow to build a temple in honor of the god Alburnus, which was declared invalid; and of how that decree was violated by the pagans, who most shamelessly restored Father Liber, Serapis, and Harpocrates with his Cynocephalus after they had been rejected by decree of the Senate

[Col. 1003C]

Tertullian presses the pagans still more forcefully and utterly overwhelms them. For he clearly shows that they despised and overthrew not only the more sacred laws concerning human affairs but also those concerning divine rites. But let us, if you please, hear him argue in his book *To the Nations*:

“Yet those exceedingly wise and prudent ancestors, whose institutions you do not know how to renounce, are themselves found impious, particularly in relation to your gods. I lie if they never decreed that no commander should dedicate a shrine which he had vowed in battle before the Senate had approved it, as happened to Marcus Aemilius Probus, who had vowed one to the god Alburnus. An utterly impious, indeed an utterly insulting, offense was surely committed in placing the honor of divinity at the discretion and caprice of human judgment, so that there should be no god unless the Senate had permitted him to exist” (Tertullian, bk. I, *Ad nationes*, ch. 10, p. 573).

But he states the matter more briefly in his *Apologeticum*: “There was an ancient decree,” he says, “that no god should be consecrated by a commander unless approved by the Senate. Marcus Aemilius knows this from the case of his god Alburnus. This supports our case, because among you divinity is weighed according to human judgment. Unless a god pleases a man, he will not be a god; man must now be propitious to God” (ibid., *Apologeticum*, ch. 5, p. 290). In certain older editions, however, instead of the words “Marcus Aemilius knows,” the reading is “as Marcus Aemilius”; [Col. 1004A] and Eusebius appears to have read it thus, for he presents these words of our Author translated into Greek as follows: [non-Latin text omitted] (Eusebius, bk. II, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 2, p. 41). Yet the meaning of both readings is plainly the same. These matters must therefore first be explained, and then we must examine how Tertullian argues from them against the pagans.

There may indeed be doubt as to what edict concerning the consecration of a god this was. We know that Cicero describes one in these words: “Let no one have gods separately; nor let anyone privately worship new or foreign gods unless they have been publicly adopted” (Cicero, bk. II, *De legibus*, p. 334, line 37). Tertullian, however, appears [Col. 1004B] to designate a different decree, since he mentions an *imperator*, a military commander. The law cited by Cicero was certainly far older than the Roman emperors. What, then, if we say that our Author uses the title *imperator* for the commander of an army, or for the consul who led it? Aemilius Probus, either unaware of this law or giving it no thought amid the doubtful fortune of battle, vowed that, if he conquered the enemy, he would consecrate a shrine to a god named Alburnus. From his own experience, however, he learned that this vow, made without a decree of the Senate, had been excessively rash. For the Senate prohibited the construction of the shrine, just as it later prevented Christ, as Tertullian says in the same place, from being declared God, although Tiberius strongly desired it.

Who this god Alburnus was is undoubtedly [Col. 1004C] no less difficult to say. He is very rarely mentioned among the ancients. From Virgil, however, we learn that in Lucania, beside the river Silarus, there is a mountain which he calls Alburnus in these verses:

> There is a grove beside the Silarus and Alburnus green with holm oaks;  
> Around it an insect flies in great numbers, whose Roman name is *asilus*,  
> Which the Greeks have translated as *oestrus*.  
> (Virgil, bk. III, *Georgics*, v. 146 ff.)

That mountain, however, had been consecrated after the custom of the pagans to some god who was given the name Alburnus, and Metellus could have vowed a shrine to him. Nor should you be surprised that this god is now unknown, for he was a local god. Such gods, as Servius observes, did not pass into other regions and were consequently scarcely known to anyone except the inhabitants of their own locality (Servius on *Aeneid* VII).

Tertullian [Col. 1004D] draws two arguments against the pagans from this. First, he most clearly concludes from it that the gods of the pagans were made according to the will and judgment of men, and that consequently no pagan deity could be a god unless he pleased a man. Yet it is the height of impiety to attribute divinity to anyone at human discretion. For the true God exists from himself and is omnipotent, and is therefore subject to no one, while all things are subject to him. The pagans, therefore, could by no law whatever—unless it were a most unjust law—decree that Christians must reject the worship of the true God and adore gods made by men, and that otherwise they should be killed without even being heard.

Second, Tertullian concludes from this that the ancient decree forbidding the introduction of new gods was violated by the pagans themselves, who [Col. 1005A] with equal license rejected gods who had once been made and restored those who had been rejected. For, as he says, “the consuls, by authority of the Senate, expelled Father Liber with his mysteries not merely from the city but from all Italy” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 304). He repeats this in another book in almost the same words (bk. I, *Ad nationes*, ch. 10, p. 573). Livy accurately relates this entire history: when, on what occasion, and how the consul Postumius uncovered the abominable and horrifying crimes committed at night in these execrable rites, and how for that reason they were utterly abolished and removed from Rome and all Italy by decree of the Senate (Livy, bk. XXXIX, §§ 9–19). Augustine therefore rightly says: “These things later displeased the more sound-minded Senate, which ordered them to be removed” (Augustine, [Col. 1005B] bk. VI, *De civitate Dei*, ch. 9, p. 156).

Conversely, our Author continues:

“Serapis, Isis, and Harpocrates with his Cynocephalus were prohibited from the Capitol—that is, expelled from the assembly of the gods—and the consuls Piso and Gabinius, who were certainly not Christians, repudiated them and even overturned their altars, restraining the vices of shameful and idle superstitions” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 6, p. 305).

He treats this somewhat more fully in another book, where he also cites Varro as a witness in the following manner:

“Varro records that Serapis, Isis, Harpocrates, and Anubis were prohibited from the Capitol and that their statues were cast down by the Senate and rebuilt only through the violence of the populace. Yet when the consul Gabinius, on the Kalends of January, was scarcely able to approve the sacrificial victims before the assembly of the people because he had made no provision concerning Serapis and Isis, he valued the Senate’s censure more highly [Col. 1005C] than the violence of the crowd and prohibited the establishment of their altars” (ibid., bk. I, *Ad nationes*, ch. 10, p. 573).

But I should like you to observe how great was the pagans’ inconsistency and folly even in sacred matters. After they had with the fullest justice abolished these utterly shameful and wholly detestable mysteries of Serapis, they nevertheless restored them with the utmost impiety, as Tertullian promises later to demonstrate more fully. We have noted certain matters concerning both their destruction and their restoration in our dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius (*Dissertation on Minucius Felix’s Octavius*, ch. 22, art. 1), and shall make further observations below.
