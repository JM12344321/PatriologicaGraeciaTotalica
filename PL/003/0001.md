# Preface to the Following Two Volumes

[Col. I] I. Before we discuss Cyprian, at least, just as we previously discussed Tertullian, and before we present—manfully, in proportion to our abilities—contributions drawn more from others than from ourselves, both on his authority and use and on the Cyprianic literary annals, it is fitting to explain briefly what this third and fourth volume contribute to this *New Library of the Ancient Fathers*: which Fathers we have brought forward and with what studies by more recent interpreters or editors we have adorned them.

## Article I. Catalogue of Authors

II. I should like you to recognize, from the very names themselves, that this Library—which we have also chosen to entitle, such as it is, a *Course of Patrology*—has a twofold subject matter. On the one side are the testimonies of the ancients; on the other, the commentaries of modern scholars. Thus the ancient age produced what the hand of the present age stores away; antiquity supplied the words to which the diversity of modern teachers has added explanations. The former, therefore, belong to the *Library*; the latter to the *Course*, if there is any force in that term.

III. Some of the lesser Fathers of the third century appear in this volume. At their head proceed the holy martyrs Felicity and Perpetua, a noble pair of women, both worthy to serve as leaders of their contemporary Fathers. There follows a double triumvirate of authors: namely, three Roman pontiffs—Cornelius, Lucius, and Stephen—and three advocates of the Carthaginian bar, champions of the same Christian cause—Minucius, Octavius, and Caecilius, happily defeated, who is also believed to have won over Cyprian. After them come several authors of unknown name, whose works, humbly concealed in the Christian manner, deserve to be brought to light. Then comes Novatian, the first standard-bearer of the schismatics and an antipope; I should almost wish to count Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea, among them as well, were it not that the letter in which Stephen, a holy pontiff and martyr, is abusively called Antichrist appears to be falsely attributed to him.

Finally, in the other volume, St. Cyprian, the wholly upright bishop of Carthage, appears alone, yet by the bulk of his works scarcely unequal to all the others together.

IV. Once these teachings of the ancient Fathers have been set forth, the companies of modern interpreters follow in close array. Some gather the corrupt readings of manuscripts driven here and there by the storms of time; they untie bundles, scrape parchments, and, so to speak, disembowel palimpsests. Others scrutinize the readings they have received, comparing and elucidating ancient readings by modern ones, doubtful by certain, and like by like. [Col. II]

V. Would you like at least a foretaste of the names of the principal interpreters, those who rank above the rest and sit, as it were, as masters of the various commentators, and who therefore, above all others, are brought into this arena of the whole patristic enterprise?

When the Acts of the holy African Martyrs first present themselves for reading, a twofold kind of commentators and commentary rises to meet them, as is generally the case. One is literal: that of Dom Théodore Ruinart, Lukas Holste, and Father Poussines, who elucidate, correct, and confirm the very text of the Acts word for word. The other is discursive, as in the dispute between Basnage and the most eminent Cardinal Orsi of the Holy Roman Church over the orthodoxy of the holy Martyrs—and certainly not an inconclusive contest. It is regrettable only that the most eminent defender of the holy Martyrs, although he vindicated their faith invincibly, nevertheless conceded that the compiler of these Acts was a Montanist. This opinion, however, is deservedly and vigorously opposed in their prefaces by the already praised Ruinart and the most learned Gallandi. In light of this, I should like you to correct the somewhat harsher statements made in the first volume of this Library by Johann August Nösselt in a dissertation otherwise excellent and indeed very important, which we included among the prolegomena to Tertullian.

VI. Among the most ancient ruins of the third or perhaps even the second century, a certain headless fragment of some value concerning the Canon of Holy Scripture remained hidden for a very long time. At last, in the middle of the last century, the distinguished Muratori was the first to bring it to light. Yet he seemed to hide and bury it once again, amid innumerable monuments of a much later age and of an altogether different character, in that great mass of his *Italian Antiquities of the Middle Ages*.

We gratefully received this little work with both hands, although it is missing its beginning, born of an unknown father, published at an uncertain date, and disfigured by the repulsive grime of corruptions. We have presented Johann Kirchhofer’s cleaner and more recent recension; and we have gladly brought forward all the scholars we found discussing it, namely Dr. Freindaller, Eberhard Stosch, and above all Martin Joseph Routh, the illustrious president of Magdalen College, Oxford, to whom we owe many felicitous restorations and a continuous commentary on the same subject.

[Col. III] VII. In passing on to Minucius Felix’s *Octavius*, however, there is a wider field—or rather, a veritable battle line of extremely fierce combatants. One text preserved in a single manuscript is the sole cause of the contest. It is scarcely credible in what astonishing ways they have dared to distort this single text, how many divergent readings they have drawn from one source, how many discordant meanings they have forced upon the same words, and how thoroughly they have entangled everything, high and low, with empty inventions and foolish conjectures.

We considered it our foremost duty to present this unique text, on the basis of a new and most diligent examination of the sole manuscript, so completely pure and unadulterated that nothing would be added and nothing removed, and not even the scribes’ manifest errors would be omitted; scarcely more than a very few words have been inserted in brackets in order to produce some intelligible sense. We therefore regarded it as a most sacred obligation to provide a true and genuine likeness of the single manuscript, portrayed to the life in its original appearance.

Once the integrity of this sovereign text has been established—not to say that the truth of the first edition has thereby been restored—the *Octavius*, like the other works, is furnished with two kinds of explanation and closely flanked on both sides by two battalions of commentators. Some contend over the letter of the text like grammarians; among them we are pleased to commend Heraldus, Wowerius, Gronovius, Grüner, Elmenhorst, Rigault, Heumann, Cellarius, Mencke, Davies, Ouzel, Wopkens, Lindner, Gallandi, Meursius, Perizonius, and others.

The commentary on this little book would have grown beyond all measure had the more loquacious products of itching interpreters not been diligently rejected, had innumerable notes even by the best commentators not been omitted, and had the extravagance of superabundant annotation not been cut down. Yet those selected remnants still overflow more than enough for you, dear reader, to discover for yourself over what rough terrain the journey must be worn down if a variorum commentary is to proceed in an uninterrupted course all the way to the end—more than enough, I say, for you to recognize in particular how far the unbridled, chattering passion of scholars from beyond the Rhine can flaunt and riot.

VIII. Accordingly, lest our ears ring too loudly from this insane tumult of literal commentators, we have taken care, either in the preliminary materials or at the very end of this little book, to introduce other men of better substance, who apply themselves energetically to constructing a general criticism of the *Octavius*. Thus, at the very threshold, you have Dom Gottfried Lumper’s treatment of the life, eloquence, and writings of Marcus Minucius the apologist, arranging everything previously said on the subject, and arranging it exceedingly well. Likewise, at the outset it was fitting to append François Baudouin’s principal dissertation, in which he was the first to restore the *Octavius* to its true author.

To it we have attached the excellent letter of Johann August Ernesti, appended to Lindner’s first edition—commendable especially in a man belonging to the herd of pseudo-Reformers. Taking his occasion from the exquisite elegance of the *Octavius*, Ernesti discusses at length and with distinction the eloquence both of the ancient Fathers and of more recent writers, and especially that of St. Bernard.

Finally, this rather lengthy preliminary section is closed by Gottlieb Lindner’s introductory inquiry in the form of an analytical compendium, [Col. IV] which presents the entire argument of the Minucian Dialogue comprehended within a single synoptic and dialectical overview.

This is the road laid out to the *Octavius*. Once the entire work has been traversed in an uninterrupted course, despite the tedium of a captious commentary, we have included, by way of refreshment for the mind, Lukas Holste’s charming letter to Cardinal Francesco Barberini concerning the spits of the Ephesian Diana mentioned in the *Octavius*. We did so all the more gladly because this little dissertation is, quite undeservedly, entirely absent from the recent collection of that most learned man’s letters published at Paris by Boissonade.

The procession of commentators on this single little book, which begins with Dom Lumper, is completed by a monk of the same order, Dom Nicolas Le Nourry. With as much applause as we previously received his dissertation on Tertullian’s apologetic writings, so highly must this excellent work of an excellent man now be extolled: this most abundant harvest of ancient and modern learning poured into the *Octavius*; this anthology drawn from the best philosophers, poets, grammarians, and orators of every age and province. He has left nothing untouched, nothing undiscovered, and nothing still to be sought after his passage.

IX. Next comes the Chronicle composed by an anonymous author under Alexander Severus. It is therefore the first of all monuments of this kind, which were so frequent throughout the course of Christianity, and at least the most ancient in the Latin Church. This small ancient work enjoyed the signal distinction of being attributed to one of the greatest among the Fathers, namely St. Hippolytus, bishop of Ostia, and of being outlined and weighed by exact calculation by the most illustrious Francesco Bianchini in the dissertation we have published after Dom Lumper.

X. Thereafter, in chronological order, many letters of the confessors, whether Roman or Carthaginian, are mentioned in selections and their contents briefly indicated; the reader is referred below to the works of St. Cyprian for the letters themselves. For we adopted this principle from the outset: whenever, because of the connection of the subjects and the chronological sequence, certain smaller works had to be detached from a large collection, they should always be mentioned under their proper year and in their proper place, with their subject matter explained. Thus the complete collection from which they are taken—well known by its name, weight, and, so to speak, bearing and countenance—would neither be altered nor broken apart.

XI. Now, however, let the pontifical monuments of the century come forth into the light with the reverence, honor, and care owed to them. Let historical introductions drawn from the best sources go before them; let explicit prolegomena announce whence and amid what circumstances they arose; if some of them have been consumed by the devouring force of time and are missing, let a special notice give advance warning; let the most complete text of whatever has chanced to survive be revised according to the standard of the better edition; let variant readings be collected, compared, and examined with indefatigable labor; let the meaning be weighed by the judgments of all critics, restored or preserved by the conscientiousness of all scholars, and elucidated through the investigations of all interpreters.

Finally, let there be added whatever circulates among Catholics [Col. V] under such great names, lest, carried away excessively by the criticism of modern writers, we rashly cast the sacred gifts of antiquity into destruction, or perhaps trample pearls merely covered for a time with muddy dust.

Thus, for the years of the holy Cornelius, Lucius, and Stephen, pontiffs and martyrs, we have first interwoven a summary chronicle of their deeds and writings, drawn from ancient and modern authors: on the one side from Jerome, Anastasius, the *Synodical Book*, and Syncellus; on the other from Baronius, Binius, Fabricius, Pagi, Labbe, Tillemont, Coustant, Cellerier, Lumper, Gallandi, and the Bollandists. We then give the text of the letters according to the best recensions and, above all, adopt in its entirety the excellent edition of Dom Coustant.

Next, for a complete elucidation of the text, we have made our own the labors of all the interpreters: Pamelius, Labbe, Hardouin, Coustant, Valesius, and Baluze. Nor have we from time to time neglected selected notes from the best of the heterodox scholars, such as Fell, Reading, Routh, Goldhorn, and Schoenemann. With these Rigault is disgracefully coupled, so unworthy of the Catholic name that the English editors and other sectarians themselves rejected him and often branded him with a mark of censure.

As for the variant readings, we present all those found in the English editors, who were most diligent in collecting them, augmented by many others harvested here and there and adapted to our own version. Finally, there are added in appendices letters less certainly attributable to the same pontiffs, decrees of doubtful authenticity, and the very Decretal letters themselves, so long celebrated throughout the learned world that, for that reason alone, it would be wrong not to give them a place in this most complete collection of ours.

Lastly, because councils celebrated under the supreme pontiffs have generally been regarded as their works, written under Peter’s inspiration and preserved and authenticated by their keys—or have been adorned by certain eminent scholars—as was the case with the eight Councils of Carthage and the two Roman Councils held under Sts. Cornelius and Stephen, we therefore considered it a matter of conscience to read through them in passing. We have received and transmitted them only from Mansi’s most recent collection or from Routh’s excellent special recension among his *Sacred Remains*.

XII. Among the greatest questions concerning Christianity debated in this third century, none was more notable and none aroused greater disturbances throughout the whole Church than the most celebrated dispute concerning the baptism of heretics. In order that this controversy might be brought into full light, and that all arguments on either side might be weighed and reconsidered, we have neglected no monument of any value, whether among the writings of the ancients or among the excursuses of modern scholars, produced by that great controversy in which, amid no small disturbance, East and West, the churches of Italy and Africa, pontiffs and martyrs, became inflamed with opposing enthusiasms.

It therefore seemed proper to distribute the records of this dispute into two classes. The first comprises the principal ancient acts and writings dealing with the case. At their head, by right of pontifical preeminence, stand the decretal pronouncements of St. Stephen, which we find scattered through the letters of St. Cyprian [Col. VI] and Firmilian and which, after being reverently received in both East and West, finally brought the controversy to an end.

After these follows the Roman Council celebrated at that time with great authority by St. Stephen himself. It was indeed fitting that Peter’s voice should be heard before the dissensions of the Africans and Easterners were presented. These flow in full stream through the councils, the letters of Cyprian and Firmilian, and certain anonymous books of the ancients.

Since a new recension of the seven Councils of Carthage, adorned with selected notes by Baluze, Fell, Coustant, Valesius, and the editor, has recently been published at Oxford by Joseph Martin Routh, head of Magdalen College, in a collection rare enough to obtain, we have had the whole of it transferred intact to our own use.

We have drawn the letters written on this subject from three widely different sources: first, a fragment of only one letter by “the blessed” (*τοῦ μακαρίου*) Dionysius of Alexandria to Pope Stephen, excerpted from Eusebius; next, four letters of Cyprian—to Magnus, Quintus, Jubaianus, and Pompeianus; and finally one letter, by no means least in celebrity, from Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea, to Cyprian against St. Stephen.

I shall not deny that, in collecting these, it was our intention not only to gather matters treating the same subject into one place, but also to set apart, under a single critical eye, monuments branded with the same charge of spuriousness (*νοθείας*). Whether justly or unjustly, I shall not argue. Yet I think those documents are charged with a sufficiently serious offense, since they are removed from the genuine letters of St. Cyprian and selected for this place as the evidentiary materials of a great lawsuit still pending between theologians and philologists.

However that may be, there are appended to them, in order to confirm the true position proposed at the outset—or at least at the end—two short works without an author’s name but distinguished by excellent teaching that all should accept. With these concludes the series of ancient monuments we have assembled to shed light upon this controversy.

XIII. Many modern scholars have written much on the same dispute; and although they used the same testimonies of the ancients, some arrived at one opinion and others at another. Reviewing only the principal treatments, we have joined to the ancients those that seemed to us above all to be of the greatest value. No one will resent our regarding the most distinguished Louis Thomassin and Dom Coustant as virtually ancient authors.

Nor, unless I am mistaken, will experts deem the final two dissertations worthy of much less praise. They bring this whole array of ancient and modern witnesses to an excellent conclusion.

Yet these preliminary remarks, although prolonged in a style more expansive than I had expected, cannot truly be completed without adding much more concerning the works of St. Cyprian contained in this and the other volume. We have nevertheless thought it better to set these matters out individually in the following articles or to reserve them for the Literary Annals a little further below.

# Article II. On the Authority of St. Cyprian

I. Whatever I previously alleged in the first volume concerning the true authority and proper use of Tertullian, I should venture to ascribe likewise to St. Cyprian, with the necessary changes. Nevertheless, far be it from me in every possible respect to appear to compare a Catholic and holy teacher with a contumacious and obstinate heretic; a lawful and most devout bishop with a proud and unruly presbyter; an outstanding defender of the Church’s unity with a perverse and frenzied Montanist; a martyr with a heresiarch; a shepherd with a wolf.

Although the one is as distant from the other as the whole span of heaven, there are nevertheless certain relationships between them, not only in origin, country, and birth, but also in genius, learning, and apologetic, dogmatic, or exhortatory exposition. Let someone else take care to explain these in detail elsewhere.

Yet it has seemed astonishing to us that, although the moderns ought gladly to have enlisted Tertullian on their side and rejected Cyprian, they have in fact rejected the former the more fiercely in proportion as they have adhered more eagerly to the latter. The name of the heretic Tertullian has always been unfortunate and ill-omened among heretics, but great and commended by the highest authorities among Catholics.

Thus Georg Rosenmüller writes the following about Tertullian:

> “As this man was the foremost author in ecclesiastical Latin, so too in his books are found the first seeds of the African and Latin theology that brought so many evils upon the Church and religion. For although he defected to the Montanists, this nevertheless detracted little from his authority among Catholics. Indeed, he wrote the greatest part of his works while in the communion of Catholics; and the Montanism that he followed disturbed only ecclesiastical discipline and did not violate the faith. Furthermore, Cyprian, who flourished in the ancient Church with a great reputation for learning and orthodoxy, was a constant imitator of Tertullian and admired him so greatly that he habitually called him ‘the Master’ preeminently (*κατ’ ἐξοχήν*).”  
> [Note: [Col. VII] *Histor. Interpret. libror. SS. in Ecclesia christ.*, Hildburghausen, 1798, part II, p. 2.]

If you disregard the errors scattered here and there in these words, you will readily observe that they diminish Tertullian only to the same degree that they exalt Cyprian.

II. Whence came praise unworthy of so great a man? Whence this baleful commendation? From his conflict with the pontiff Stephen. Hence the concentrated interest of modern scholars in him and the great labors they expended; hence that splendid edition of his works published at Oxford; hence the indefatigable efforts of Fell, Pearson, Dodwell, Rigault, Routh, Marshall, Poole, and Matthies, by which all the evidence in the Cyprianic question was weighed, approved or rejected—not without inequitable partiality—and settled by definitive judgment.

This burden therefore falls upon every Catholic editor of St. Cyprian: once the grounds upon which his name is diminished among the moderns have been removed, his true authority should increase among the sons of the Church in the same measure as it seems to be lowered among outsiders. We have accordingly concluded that our whole inquiry into Cyprian’s authority must be concentrated upon explaining his own judgment regarding pontifical preeminence.

III. Everyone accepts that any writer must be judged not from one or another isolated word in his discourse, but from the continuous tenor of his teaching; not from impromptu statements suddenly elicited in correspondence by an abrupt disturbance of affairs, but from a settled opinion treated seriously; not from a position defended piecemeal and subsequently changed, but from a firm, decisive, and defined judgment.

Whoever examines Cyprian’s works with these principles in view will be so far from regarding the holy bishop as an opponent of the Roman See that he will instead discover him among the foremost defenders of Roman privileges and, I would almost say, as the standard-bearer of the Roman theologians.

IV. For these—to show that I am not speaking merely by allusion—are the very doctrines that shine everywhere like luminaries in the writings of the holy Doctor:

1. The Church, outside which no one can live, is one.
2. This one Church is catholic, that is, diffused throughout the whole world.
3. This one and catholic Church is, moreover, Roman: founded upon Peter, neither distinct from the holy Roman See nor different from the supreme pontiff.
4. This one catholic and Roman Church is, in Cyprian’s understanding, the most certain rule of faith, neither stained nor ever to be stained by any blemish of error.

V. I should like you to hear Cyprian himself speak without delay:

### 1. On the Unity of the Church

In the book bearing that title, Cyprian himself testifies that he poured into it, so far as he was able, his entire understanding of the Church:

> “Our modest ability,” he says, “has expressed the unity of the Catholic Church as fully as it could.”

“In that work,” says the deacon Pontius, the most trustworthy witness to his master’s life and mind, “he taught truth to heretics, unity to schismatics, and peace to the sons of God.”

[Note: [Col. VIII] Epistle LI, vol. IV, col. 345: This book was written on the occasion of the Novatian schism and the faction of Felicissimus. Dupin, *Bibliothèque des auteurs*, vol. I, p. 260, Latin ed.: “This book was composed to put an end to the Novatian schism.” Fell, p. 104, Bremen ed. “I am not far,” says Rollin, *Bibliotheca Sanctorum Patrum*, vol. III, p. 192, “from believing that this little book *On the Unity of the Church* is the address Cyprian delivered to the bishops and clergy assembled to end the Novatian schism. The following words from the same treatise give considerable support to this conjecture: ‘This unity we must firmly maintain and vindicate, especially we bishops who preside in the Church, so that we may also prove the episcopate itself to be one and undivided.’” Cf. vol. IV, col. 501.]

From this little book:

1. “In the *Song of Songs*, the Holy Spirit, speaking in the person of the Lord, designates the Church as one and says (Song 6:9): ‘One is my dove, my perfect one; she is the only one of her mother, the chosen one of her who bore her.’”  
   [Note: [Col. VIII] Book *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 500.]

2. “The blessed apostle Paul teaches the same thing and reveals the mystery of unity, saying (Eph. 4:4–6): ‘One body and one spirit, one hope of your calling; one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God.’”  
   [Note: [Col. VIII] Ibid., col. 501.]

3. “This unity we must firmly maintain and vindicate, especially we bishops who preside in the Church, so that we may also prove the episcopate itself to be one [Col. IX] and undivided.”  
   [Note: [Col. IX] Book *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 501.]

4. “Just as the sun has many rays but one light; and a tree many branches but one strength founded upon its tenacious root; and although many streams flow from a single spring . . . unity is nevertheless preserved in the source . . . so too the Church of the Lord. . . .”  
   [Note: [Col. IX] Ibid., col. 502.]

5. “The bride of Christ cannot be adulterated. . . . She knows one house; with chaste modesty she guards the sanctity of one bridal chamber. . . .”  
   [Note: [Col. IX] Book, cols. 502–503.]

6. “She is one, outside whom no one escapes safely, any more than outside Noah’s ark; he can no longer have God as Father who does not have the Church as mother.”  
   [Note: [Col. IX] Ibid., col. 503.]

   And elsewhere:

   > “Nor can any assurance of life or salvation remain for them if they refuse obedience to the bishops and priests. . . . For they cannot live outside, since the house of God is one and no one can have salvation except in the Church.”  
   > [Note: [Col. IX] Epistle LXII, cols. 370–371.]

7. “The Lord says (John 10:30): ‘I and the Father are one.’ And again it is written of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (1 John 5:7): ‘And these three are one.’ Does anyone believe that this unity . . . can be torn apart in the Church . . .? He who does not maintain this unity does not maintain the law of God; he does not maintain the faith of the Father and the Son; he does not maintain life and salvation.”  
   [Note: [Col. IX] Book *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 504.]

8. “This mystery of unity . . . is revealed when, in the Gospel, the tunic of the Lord Jesus Christ is not divided at all or torn. . . . It reveals the inseparable, joined, and connected concord of our people, we who have put on Christ, clinging together. . . .”  
   [Note: [Col. IX] Book *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 504.]

9. “He himself admonishes and teaches in his Gospel, saying (John 10:16): ‘And there shall be one flock and one shepherd.’”  
   [Note: [Col. IX] Ibid., col. 505.]

10. Let these words of devout prayer serve as the conclusion:

   > “I certainly desire, beloved brethren, and equally counsel and urge, that, if it can be done, none of the brethren should perish, and that the mother may joyfully enclose within her bosom the one body of a people united in agreement. . . . There is one God and one Christ, and his Church is one, and the faith one, and the people one, joined by the cement of concord into the solid [unity] of the body.”  
   > [Note: [Col. IX] Ibid., col. 317.]

VI. ### 2. On the Church as One and Catholic

Cyprian has the following statements in the same book and elsewhere; they have just been cited in excerpts:

> “The Church is one, which by the increase of fruitfulness extends ever more widely into a multitude. . . . Just as the sun has many rays but one light; and a tree many branches but one strength founded upon its tenacious root; and although many streams flow from a single spring, the number may appear widely dispersed by the abundance of the overflowing supply, yet unity is preserved in the source. . . . So too the Church of the Lord, suffused with light, extends her rays throughout the whole world; yet it is one light that is diffused everywhere, and the unity of the body is not divided. By her fruitful abundance she extends her branches over the whole earth and spreads her freely flowing streams ever more widely.”  
> [Note: [Col. IX] Ibid., cols. 501–502.]

And:

> “The universal Church, joined with us throughout the whole [Col. X] world by the bond of unity. . . . Hence you ought to know . . . that those flatter themselves in vain who, having no peace with the priests of God, steal in and secretly believe that they are in communion with certain persons, since the Church, which is catholic and one, has not been torn or divided but is certainly connected and joined together by the cement of bishops who adhere to one another.”  
> [Note: [Col. X] Epistle LXIX, vol. IV, col. 406.]

VII. These and many other statements, clearer than the noonday sun, appear to be clouded by the words that follow and that are more than once attached to the preceding passages:

> “You ought to know that the bishop is in the Church and the Church in the bishop.”  
> [Note: [Col. X] Ibid., loc. cit.]

From this, many have inferred that Cyprian was unfamiliar with the genuine conception of catholic unity. Indeed, they have attributed to him the view—which even now many Protestants would admit in good faith—that the Church’s external unity gradually grew almost imperceptibly: that the individual churches, which at first flourished and were united solely by the inward unity of faith and charity, placed the entire force and bond of catholic communion each in its own bishop; and that, stirred by the influence of this inward power, they were moved to unite other churches to themselves by the same bond.

Thus, they claim, the churches were separate at first and gradually transformed themselves into one; as the centuries passed, they assimilated themselves to one another; they converged from plurality into unity, yet without entirely losing the indefeasible rights of their original freedom and of their individual communities.
