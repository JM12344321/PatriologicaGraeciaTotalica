**CAETERUM**, otherwise. II, 13 B, “Otherwise they will have no force.” II, 546 A, “Otherwise, inhumanly.” So very frequently.

**CALCARIA**, a place where lime is either burned, stored, or sold. I, 763 A, “We have therefore passed from the limekiln . . . into the charcoal works”: a proverb meaning to pass from an unclean place into one no cleaner, and to fall from one laborious and filthy condition into another. After Tertullian had answered the heresy of Marcion as something utterly impure, he prepares to refute the equally impure doctrine of Apelles.

**CALCEATUS**. II, 254 C, “The shod will presently be beaten for their empty boasting,” that is, furnished with new shoes, in which they boast [Col. 1266C] vainly. II, 77 A, “Shod with the equipment of the Gospel.”

**CALENDARIUM** was the name of a book containing an account of receipts and expenditures. I, 1314 B, “The slender skins of their ears expend a ledger,” that is, they suspend and carry around the entire wealth of the household, as it were, upon the outermost part of the ear.

**CALERE**. II, 247 A, “Nothing there is inflamed except savagery.” II, 123 A, “Being themselves inflamed to strike”; concerning scorpions, whose venom burns fiercely when they grow hot.

**CALIENDRUM**, according to Festus, a covering for the head. II, 1044 A.

**CALIGATUS**. I, 690 A, “And whether military service, even when booted, may be admitted to the faith.” The *caliga* was a military shoe, so that *caligatus* (“wearing the military boot”) is also understood to mean a soldier. Thus Suetonius, in his *Augustus*: “He often awarded mural crowns even to common soldiers.” Baldwin, *On Footwear*, chap. 13. The *caligati* were common soldiers and men of the ranks; because of their low status, they constituted the lowest order of the military and, so to speak, the common people of the army. Nigronius, *On the Footwear of the Ancients*, chap. 3.

[Col. 1266D] **CALIX**. II, 1000 C, “The shepherd whom you depict upon the cup.” II, 991 B, “Let the very paintings upon your cups come forward.”

**CALLIBLEPHARUM**. II, 991 B, is a purple cosmetic that enhances beauty and attractiveness in women. Pliny, *Natural History* XXI, 19, teaches that it is made from burned rose leaves, date kernels, and even ox marrow; book XXVII, 4.

**CALLOSITAS**. I, 617 B, “That these things have passed into the callousness of common usage”: it signifies a custom that has, as it were, acquired a callus through long practice. Thus Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* III, chap. 22: “Prolonged reflection has covered their minds with the callus of age.”

**CANALES**. According to Festus, men of the forum were called *canales* because they stood around the channels of the forum. II, 1047 A, “I do not worship the *canales*.”

**CANCELLI**. II, 1047 A, “I do not worship the railings.” Le Prieur prefers: *non adorior*, “I do not accost them”; this is said of advocates [Col. 1267A] who undertake a case and present themselves before the judges. II, 142 B, “The powers that you arrange from behind the railings.”

**CANDIDA**. II, 147 C, “But he wished to glory in the white robe of salvation.” II, 445 A, “And let a certain white robe belonging to both judgments be discerned”—the token of those awaiting honors and of those designated for them. II, 523 B, “The token of the Pharisaic white robe,” that is, of Pharisaic honors. II, 750 B, “In a certain preliminary enjoyment and white robe of his.” *Usurpatio* is the infliction and anticipation of judicial punishments beforehand; *candida*, however, is the reception of glory yet to be completed. II, 77 A, “And better crowned with the white robe of martyrdom.”

**CANDIDATUS**. II, 315 A, “As candidates for restoration,” that is, already clothed in white through God’s grace for their own restoration. For here they are not called candidates as though they were canvassing for office, but as persons already designated for restoration. I, 1300 A, “Thus he too is God’s candidate through fear,” that is, serving his apprenticeship in the fear and reverence of God. II, 881 A, “Candidates for eternity.” I, 1156 A, “And we, candidates for the rank of angels,” that is, we who have been designated to become like angels. II, 718 A, “They acknowledge their own offspring as candidates [Col. 1267B] for the demons,” that is, as designated for the worship of demons. II, 86 B, “Because it was introduced by the devil’s candidates.”

**CANDIDARE**. II, 147 C, “And they whitened him in his own blood.” II, 147 C, “Stains, however, are whitened by martyrdom.”

**CANERE**. I, 1205 C, “That figure of bodily medicine foretold spiritual medicine.” I, 1291 B, “The Apostle foretold.” II, 610 A, “Whose coming the prophets foretold.” II, 493 B, “But this psalm too will be said to sing of Solomon.”

**CANICULA**. II, 247 B, “For that little dog Diogenes.” This is Diogenes the Cynic, so called because of the freedom he assumed in censuring both men and even the gods.

**CANINUS**. I, 1251 B, “Not a human affectation formed by the stupor of canine equanimity”; understand Cynic or Diogenic endurance. II, 1042 B, “Lest perhaps canine constancy has been commanded.”

**CANTABRUM**, a kind of Persian standard. V, 369 A.

**CANTHERIUS**. I, 365 A. Festus: “A gelding differs [Col. 1267C] from a horse in the same way as a barrow from a boar, a capon from a cock, and a wether from a ram”; for a *cantherius* is a horse whose testicles have been removed.

**CANUS**. I, 386 A, “The gray hairs of histories and records,” that is, hoary histories and records. See **MEMORIA**.

**CAPERE**, used of what is possible, like the Greek *ἐνδέχεσθαι* (“to be possible, admissible”). I, 376 A, “Since it does not admit of being evaluated.” II, 60 A, “It is possible for them to hold such opinions.” II, 207 A, “For we maintain that diminution and subjection can be admitted.” II, 213 B, “Much more is it impossible that anything should have existed without a beginning.” II, 576 A, “Or because a more sordid origin admits of it,” that is, is susceptible, namely, to disgrace. II, 162 A–B, “And thus let the second, constituted by God, receive,” that is, *δέχεται* (“receives”), as though he were saying “let him obtain.” II, 253 B, “If depreciation can be admitted in the Creator,” that is, if it is conceded or granted that the Creator may be depreciated. II, 295 A, “It is also possible to call the breath an image of the spirit,” that is, the breath also signifies an image of the spirit, or may be called an image of the spirit. II, 395 B, “Insofar, therefore, as it does not admit belief,” that is, it is neither credible nor probable. I, 687 A, “Whether a servant of God may undertake the administration of some dignity or [Col. 1267D] authority,” that is, whether he is about to undertake it or is able to do so. II, 215 A, “Primacy admits not only an ordering but also an empowering sense”; and on the same page, “*Principium* can admit nothing other than ‘beginning.’”

**CAPILLAGO**. II, 737 A, “Or a growth of hair streams forth.”

**CAPILLATURA**. I, 1224 A, “In vain do you employ all the most skilled arrangers of coiffure.” These were designated by the special term *cosmetae*. The ancient glosses: *Κοσμήτης*, “dresser, adornment-maker.” Juvenal: “The dressers lay aside their tunics.” Girls were also employed for that purpose, for in the glosses we also read: *κοσμήτριας*, “female dressers.”

**CAPTATELA**, an opportunity for catching and clothing. II, 1046 A, “Previously even for the mere opportunity of catching it.”

**CAPTATRIX**. II, 916 A, “And luxury is the ensnarer of every public rejoicing.”

**CAPULUS**, a receptacle, a little case. II, 805 A, “Should he entrust it to some most worthless receptacle”: this is what he calls the body.

[Col. 1268A] **CARO**. II, 124 B, “Through flesh the spirit grows feeble”—through flesh, that is, through a depraved love of mortal life.

**CASSIS**, a net by which the face is veiled so that no one can be recognized; it was also formerly called *cassiculum* in the diminutive, according to Festus. II, 774 B, “No one wishing to display a human being puts a helmet or mask upon him.”

**CASTRARE**. I, 426 A, “It even gave pleasure to emasculate the upper arms.” I, 1327 A, “We are trained by God as though by correcting and castrating the world.” II, 932 B, “The Apostle was therefore himself also castrated.” I, 570 B, “With emasculated throats,” that is, with the tongue cut out.

**CASTRATOR**. II, 247 B, “The castrator of the flesh.” Compare Pliny, *Natural History* VIII, 30, and XXXII, 3, concerning the Pontic mouse; likewise VIII, 37, and X, 73.

**CASTUS**, as a substantive, a sacred rite, *ἁγνεία* (“purity, sacred observance”). II, 977 A, “You make them equal to the sacred rite of Isis and Cybele.” Varro used “Greek *castus*” to mean “according to the Greek rite.”

**CATABOLICI** are inauspicious and infamous spirits that knock fanatics down and dash them to the ground. [Col. 1268B] II, 698 B.

**CATACLISTI**. II, 1036 B, “A garment from the *cataclisti*,” that is, from precious articles kept with special care. Thus Apuleius, book XI: “A chorus resplendent in snow-white and carefully preserved apparel.” Hesychius: *Κατάκλειστοι, ἐν Κορίνθῳ ἑταῖραί τινες*—“*katakleistoi*: certain courtesans in Corinth.” Others read *cataclitis*, that is, of a dark and subdued color.

**CATACLYSMUS**, a flood, the universal destruction of the first world by water. I, 481 A; 571 C. I, 701 A, “That a cataclysm had also occurred in the past because of the unbelief and iniquity of mankind.”

**CATAPHRACTE** is a coat of mail or military breastplate. II, 1043 A.

**CATECHIZARE**. II, 434 B, “And catechizes for him,” that is, instructs his servants in simple discourse toward obedience to him. I, 675 B, “Ask whether one who gives instruction about idols commits idolatry.”

**CATHARTICUM**, Greek *καθαρτικόν*, a medicine possessing purgative power. II, 1049 A, “I shall administer a purgative to the impurity of Scaurus.”

[Col. 1268C] **CATHEDRA**. II, 49 A, “Among whom the very chairs of the Apostles still remain.” Rigault does not wish this to be understood literally of the Apostles’ chairs, but of the principal churches established by the Apostles themselves, which in Tertullian’s age presided in their respective regions as mother churches of the others—such as Corinth in Achaea; Philippi and Thessalonica in Macedonia; Ephesus in Asia; and Rome in Italy.

**CATHOLICE**. II, 38 D, “When they brought them forward universally into the open,” that is, publicly among everyone, not among members of the household. II, 106 B, “That these things occur universally,” that is, in their entirety, as opposed to occurring in part.

**CATHOLICUS**. II, 304 B, “The defender of that universal and supreme goodness.”

**CAUDA**. II, 123 B, “And so at first, while still trailing the tail,” that is, not yet rising to the attack with the tail curved like scorpions, not yet fixing their stings, and not yet pouring out their venom.

**CAUPO**. II, 651 B, “After he had sampled all the wine-dealers of wisdom and eloquence there.” This is a metaphor explained [Col. 1268D] by what follows: “For accordingly”—that is, in the same manner—“the condition of the soul is also such, through the philosophized doctrines of men mixing water with wine,” etc.

**CAUTER**, Greek *καυτήρ*, an iron instrument with which wounds are burned. II, 1048 A, “I apply the cautery to ambition.”

**CAUTERIUM**, Greek *καυτήριον*. II, 198 A, “Twice a falsifier, both with cautery and with stylus.” Here *cauterium* is not the glowing iron with which either approved persons are marked or guilty persons punished, but the *καυτήριον ζωγραφικόν*, a painter’s instrument used to produce the kind of painting that painters and goldsmiths commonly call encaustic; Pliny, *Natural History* XXXV, 11; Vitruvius VII, 9; Marcianus 17. Hermogenes is therefore a falsifier with his painter’s cautery, burning differently colored waxes to fashion images of false deities. He is also a falsifier with the heretical stylus by which he corrupts the Scriptures. II, 131 C, “And medicine is plainly cruelty involving both scalpel and cautery.” I, 1246 A, “It is a wretched thing [Col. 1269A] to be cut and burned with a cautery.” I, 362 A, “Mercury testing the dead with a cautery.”

**CAUTIO**. II, 290 A, “Nor would he have assigned its guarantee,” that is, he would not have wished to provide security for it.

**CAVEA**, the gladiatorial arena and amphitheaters. I, 361 A, “Clearly, you are more religious in the arena.” II, 279 A, “And of the raging arena.”

**CAVERE**. II, 156 A, “In short, the former teacher had given security.” This is forensic language meaning that he had pledged himself to remain with his earlier teaching and abstain from innovation. I, 1265 B, “Nevertheless, he provided against the judge’s impatience,” that is, he brought the injury caused by the impatience of the judge or persecutor before a tribunal. He provided against impatience at the injury by initiating a legal action concerning it, even if he did not continue it. He took security as though the vengeance deposited with God were in danger, and by that security deprived the judge—that is, God—of his honor. I, 694 A, “Those who swear give security.” Jurists call documents or written agreements of this kind *cautiones*, and they “give security” because we thereby protect ourselves and are rendered secure when we make a loan.

[Col. 1269B] **CAVILLARI** is said of springs, II, 1033 B, when they cease to provide their water by withdrawing into the hollows of the earth; for then they deceive the neighboring inhabitants. II, 823 B, “Through which the proclamation of the ancestors is generally caviled at,” that is, it is assailed by cavils; the expression is passive. II, 844 C, “But since here too the question concerning the interpretation of the body is beset with cavils.”

**CAVOSITAS**. II, 742 B, “For us the underworld is not an empty cavity.”

**CEDERE**. II, 114 C, “The sun presented the day of our purchase,” that is, the sun represented the day of our purchase, on which Christ rose from the dead.

**CELLARIUM**, *ταμεῖον*, a storeroom or provision chamber. II, 834 C, “For what refuges of storerooms will protect us from the wrath of God?”

**CELLULA**. II, 262 A, “In this little chamber of the Creator he was also crucified,” that is, in his own body, which he assumed for himself from creation like a little chamber and a *σκηνή* (“tent”), so that he might dwell in it.

[Col. 1269C] **CENSERE**. II, 1039 B, “He registered him outside the city,” that is, expelled him from the city. Also, “to derive one’s origin”: I, 341 A, “From these your gods derive their origin,” that is, they derive their birth and beginning from them; for they are made either from some metal, stone, or marble. II, 33 B, “Whether our doctrine derives its authority from the tradition of the Apostles.” II, 895 A, “Under which virgins too are collectively registered,” that is, included under the common name of woman. I, 1160 A, “Then too his body is accounted in the bread.” Rigault interprets: “Through the sacrament of bread he commends his body.” Similarly Augustine, *Questions on the Gospels* I, chap. 43, said: “Through the sacrament of wine he commends his blood.” I, 471 A, “But also because we are designated by the appellation of brothers.” II, 920 C, “In both places we are reckoned by one marriage.” I, 363 A; 577 A, “They derive their character, of course, from contempt.”

**CENSUALES**, those who compiled daily registers, annals, and public records. Tertullian calls them by a term belonging to his own age. I, 388 A.

**CENSURA**. II, 273 A, “Why do you ensnare yourself by your own judgment?” [Col. 1269D] Heumann writes: “What of the fact that it ensnares itself by its own judgment?” that is, Trajan’s ruling overturns itself and cuts its own throat with its own sword. II, 281 B, “According to the rule of its own founder.” II, 759 B, “Your own judgment, I suppose, would prescribe for him.” I, 469 A, “Divine censure” is ecclesiastical excommunication.

**CENSUS**, origin, valuation, declaration. I, 307 A, “The origin of this discipline dates from Tiberius.” I, 329 A, “From him derives the origin of all, or at least the greater or better-known, divinity.” I, 403 A, “We have set forth the origin both of the sect and of the name, together with its founder.” II, 44 C, “The apostolic churches produce their own registers,” that is, their origins. II, 228 B, “It derives its basis from fact.” II, 575 B, “Namely, that from animate beings—by reason of their state of infirmity—spiritual things should accede.” He says that the Demiurge drew something infirm from the animate substance by reason of its infirmity; but the infirmity consisted in spiritual things acceding—that is, it was therefore infirm in regard to acceding to spiritual things, and was not strong enough [Col. 1270A] or able to attain them. II, 579 A, “The proper standing of man,” that is, that by which he should truly be accounted human, namely insofar as he is spiritual. II, 270 B, “You would find no church of apostolic origin.” II, 895 A, “And the entire census of the elements,” that is, their number as though contained in an exact enumeration or register. II, 703 A, “And a greater census of fishes.” II, 934 B, “Than the very origin of the human race,” that is, its beginning. II, 803 B, “To have strained out an origin cleared of the filth of mud”: see **EXCUSARE**. II, 646 C, “A dispute with Hermogenes concerning the origin of the soul alone.”

**CENTENARIUS**. I, 300 A, “For I see even hundred-thousand-sesterce banquets,” that is, extremely costly banquets, at which as many hundreds of thousands of sesterces were consumed as asses formerly were. II, 98 B, “And, what is more useful, even with hundred-petaled roses,” etc. He called them *centenariae* roses because they have a hundred petals. He most appropriately said that a crown made from roses, which delights both sight and breath—that is, the eyes by its color and the nostrils by its fragrance—is more useful than one made from laurel, myrtle, olive, or any other distinguished foliage, which feeds only the eyes. II, 1048 A, “Likewise, what dishes [Col. 1270B] of a hundred pounds’ weight Sulla labors over.”

**CENTONARIUS**. II, 53 A, “Original works composed from the poems of Homer in the manner of a cento,” that is, in the way centos are customarily composed and arranged.

**CERAUNIA**, a white and ruddy-gleaming gem, so named as though from thunder. Pliny, *Natural History* XXXVII, 9. I, 1305 B, “And a *ceraunia* gem should flash.” II, 660 B, “For thunder-stones too do not possess a fiery substance merely because they flash with a glowing redness.”

**CERNERE**, *σινιάζειν*, to sift. II, 165 B, “Satan asked to sift you,” from Luke’s Gospel, chap. 22.

**CERVUS**. II, 776 C, “Deer in battle.” Rhenanus: this has the flavor of a proverbial figure, apparently arising from Chabrias’s saying that an army of deer led by a lion is more formidable than an army of lions led by a deer. II, 681 B, “Without a reed, without a *cervus*.” Rigault preferred to retain the word *cervus* rather than substitute *cornus* (“cornel tree”) or *ornus* (“ash tree”). For here the subject is supports for vines, not trees friendly to vines, such as the ash and cornel. [Col. 1270C] *Cervi* are forked rods shaped like the letter V, from their resemblance to the horns of a deer, as Marcus Varro teaches in book IV.

**CHARITES**. II, 85 A, “This first chapter has been crowned by the Graces.”

**CHARISMA**. II, 547 A, “And they call natural ability a charisma.” II, 147 A, “So many charisms wrongly exercised.” II, 659 B, “For because we acknowledge spiritual charisms.” II, 155 B, “Whose charisms he also attacked.”

**CHIROGRAPHUM**. II, 156 A, “And the written bond remains among the Psychics.” II, 1019 D, “When the written bond of death had been granted.”

**CHRISTIANIZARE**. II, 270 B, “Which does not Christianize in the Creator.”

**CHRISTIANUS**. I, 281 A, “He is wrongly called *Christianus*”: one should read *Chrestianus*. Learned men believe that the name *Chrestiani* gained greater currency among the pagans than *Christiani* because among them there were many men named Chrestus but none named Christus. See Havercamp at this passage, who cites many persons of this name from the writers. I, 403 A, “By his own conscience already a Christian”: [Col. 1270D] he calls Pilate a Christian because he knew that Christ had been accused unjustly. Ibid., “So that you may believe, being Christians,” that is, so that, having now become Christians, you may believe.

**CICADA**. II, 903 A, “Or the cicadas of the Athenians.” The custom that existed among the Athenians is very well described by Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus* II, p. 199 C, ed. Sylburg:

> “The rulers of the Athenians, on the contrary, who emulated the civic mode of life, forgot their manliness and wore gold ornaments. They put on tunics reaching to the feet, and wore long garments and a *crobylus*—a form of braided hairstyle—which they bound up with gold and adorned by inserting cicadas, thereby displaying their earth-born ancestry through the truly tasteless vulgarity of their effeminacy,” etc.

This is taken from Thucydides, book I. The *crobylus* was a kind of male hair-knot extending from the middle of the forehead toward the crown and ending in a point on either side; the female style was called a *corymbus*, and the boyish style a *scorpius*. This *crobylus* was held in place by a headband, which was likewise called a *crobylus*, according to Servius—as Petronius calls the corresponding female ornament a *corymbium*—or by the proper and distinctive name *δηγγος* [probably *δήγγος*: “biter”], as in Hesychius, from the cicadas that appeared to bite and cling to it. Golden cicadas were fastened to this *δηγγος*, or *crobylus*, as though biting it, to indicate *τὸ γηγενές*, that is, that the Athenians were indigenous or aboriginal. Many authors mention this ornament. Tertullian, however, designates this whole apparatus synecdochically by the term “cicadas.”

[Col. 1271A] **CICUTA**. II, 647 A, “Having already drained the hemlock of condemnation,” that is, having already heard the sentence of his condemnation, as though Socrates had begun to drink death from the very moment when it was pronounced; for the judges are likewise considered to have deprived him of life by placing him in custody from the moment they passed sentence of death.

**CINAEDUS**. I, 348 B, note 6, “You make some catamite into a god.” This refers to Antinous, whom the emperor Hadrian kept as a favorite.

**CINCTUS**, a girdle. II, 1031 B, “With the girding left to one’s judgment,” that is, with the tunic held up by a belt according to your preference.

**CINERARIUS**, an arranger of the hair and coiffure. I, 1301 B, “And with *cinerarii* of foreign stature.” [Col. 1271B] Yet in this passage the *cinerarii* seem rather to be attendants who follow their mistress like a ceremonial procession and brush ashes and dust from her, as the little slave-attendants of courtesans customarily do. Glossary: *Cinerarius, δοῦλος ἑταίρας*—“the slave of a courtesan.” The accompanying epithet “of foreign stature” confirms this interpretation.

**CIRCULATORIUS**. II, 762 B, “But concerning some itinerant assembly,” that is, a gathering of mountebanks practicing itinerant tricks, as the author of the *Apology*, chap. 23, puts it. For in the Glossary a *circulator* is an *ἀγύρτης περιφοράριος*, “an itinerant charlatan.” I, 672 B, “Because he still entertained some thought concerning the itinerant sect.”

**CIRCUMDARE**. I, 1325 A, “To surround the hair.” The verb *circumdare* makes no sense in this passage. Rigault’s *circumradere*, “to shave all around,” is excellent.

**CIRCUMGELARE**. II, 687 A, “After receiving an immortal beginning of soul, they froze a mortal body around it.”

**CIRCUMSCRIPTIO**. I, 1257 B, “And concerning the devil’s circumvention,” that is, deception.

[Col. 1271C] **CIRCUMSCRIPTOR**, an annuller. II, 276 B, “The annuller of his own sentence,” that is, one who circumscribes it and fraudulently renders it void. *Circumscribere*, meaning to draw a line through and obliterate, is common among jurists and reputable authors. II, 293 B, “While he bars the serpent, the very deceiver, from intercourse with the woman.”

**CIRCUMSTANTIA**. II, 1156 A, “To whom that attendance of angels does not cease to say,” that is, *περίστασις*, or rather *παράστασις* (“attendance, standing beside”), stated of angels after the Greek manner. Gellius, *Attic Nights* VII, chap. 4, calls it *circumstatio*. II, 965 A, “In a time of affliction and persecution and every kind of circumstance.”

**CIRCURIANIANA**. II, 591 A, “Take these other Circurianian inventions.” Latinus thought this should be emended to: “Take these other Cercurian inventions.” A *cercurus*, he says, is a very large Asian ship. Festus does indeed report this, but it is far too insipid beside the stupidity and madness of the Valentinian whom Tertullian mocks. Rigault conjectures [Col. 1271D] that the author wrote: “Take these other inventions of Ennius’s Curruca, one of their more distinguished teachers.” Curruca was the name of a certain foolish mime-character, as we learn from the ancient commentator on this verse of Juvenal’s sixth Satire: “Now, Curruca, you are pleased with yourself, and suck the tears from her lips,” where some fool is clearly portrayed. Ennius, it appears, was the first to introduce this Curruca as a suitable comic character. Hence Tertullian here called one of the Valentinian grandees—who pronounced his ravings as though they were pontifical oracles, in pompously contrived language—“Ennius’s Curruca.” Jerome imitated Tertullian in his book *Against Rufinus*: “But we are simple men and Currucas of Ennius, and can understand neither his wisdom nor yours, who translated him.”

**CIRIA**, or *sciria*, an untanned lion skin, not worked at all but dried into bristling stiffness through being worn raw. II, 1042 A, “Lest a lion skin should chafe his feeble neck.”

**CIRRUS**. II, 903 A, “Or the hair-knots of the Germans,” that is, [Col. 1272A] twisted locks of hair bound in a knot at the crown and then spreading broadly outward. Tacitus, *On the Customs of the Germans*: “It is a mark of the people to turn their hair sideways and bind it underneath in a knot.” See also Suetonius, *Caligula* 47.

**CIVILIS**, mild. I, 276 A, “A mild, not tyrannical, dominion.”

**CLANCULARIA**, a secret mistress, with whom one habitually associates in secret. II, 1041 B, “So much was permitted to his secret Lydian mistress.”

**CLANCULUS**, secretly. II, 1041 A, “Surely by now having secretly performed the office of husband for someone.”

**CLAUSULA**, an end or conclusion. I, 447 A, “And the very conclusion of the age.” II, 109 B, “At the conclusion of his office.” II, 831 B, “Or then, at the absolute conclusion of the times.” I, 672 B, “As though it were the conclusion of the sacrifice.”

**CLIBANUS**. II, 437 A, “Because after it comes the oven or furnace of Gehenna.”

**CLIMACTERICA** are certain periods of life proceeding in ascending calculation by intervals of nine or seven years, which the Chaldeans maintained were dangerous, especially the sixty-third year. See Gellius, *Attic Nights* III, chap. 10; XV, chap. [Col. 1272B] 7. I, 675 A, “Who foretells the climacteric periods of others.”

**CLOACINAE**, goddesses who presided over sewers. II, 1043 B, “The sanctuaries of the Cloacinae are the sewers themselves.”

**CLUERE**. II, 1030 A, “Or who is renowned as Tarentum, or as Baetica?” The ancients used *cluo* and *clueo* for “to be, be named, or be celebrated.” It may be corrupted from the Greek *καλεῖν* (“to call”), like many other words, or rather from *κλύειν*, which means “to hear,” or “to be” and “to be called,” as this very expression is used in Latin. Hence *inclutus* means “celebrated.” Plautus, *Menaechmi* V, 2, 101: “Who is said to have Cygnus for his father.” Thus Prudentius: “You who are renowned for ancestral courage.” Cicero, *Brutus, or On Famous Orators*: “Then the two Caepiones, highly renowned for judgment and eloquence.” Martianus uses *praecluis* to mean noble and illustrious.

**CLUDERE**, for *claudere*, to close. II, 822 B, “If the fact that the former came first will close off belief,” that is, if this will establish, as it were, the barriers or limits of belief, beyond which a person ought to believe nothing. II, 384 B, “So that I may bring something to a close with the written truth of my Lord.” I, 337 A, “And once closed heaven.” II, 254 C, “For the present, we have enclosed it within these lines.”

[Col. 1272C] **COAETARE**. II, 858 B, “They are coeval in birth,” said of soul and body.

**COAGULUM**. II, 785 A, “For in cheese the power of the substance comes from the rennet, which coagulates it by treatment,” that is, the substance of milk.

**COCETUM** is a kind of food, as Festus teaches, made from honey and poppy. But among the ancients it was also used for a mixed drink compounded from various ingredients, such as Nestor’s drink in Homer, *Iliad* XI. I, 562 B, “Nestor’s *cocetum*.”

**COCUS**, for *coquus*, cook. I, 272 A; 282 A; and frequently.

**COELEMENTATUS**. II, 577 A; see *Against the Valentinians*, chap. 23.

**COENA**. II, 477 A, “And pure suppers,” that is, the days of Preparation, *ἐν τῇ ἕκτῃ τῶν ἡμερῶν*, “on the sixth day of the week.” Thus Irenaeus interprets it, book I, chap. 10. Glossary: *Coena pura*, *προσάββατον*, “the day before the Sabbath,” because it was observed without any apparatus or elaborate preparation, with the plainest and simplest food. II, 702 B, “And from that point his metempsychosis came to an end in him, like a summer supper after the roast.” Tertullian seems to suggest that at summer suppers, after the roast—that is, roasted meat—it was customary to serve no further courses, so that the roast marked the end of the supper. Empedocles, thoroughly roasted in burning Etna, was not changed into any other body, and so that was the end of his metempsychosis. What if Tertullian is alluding to this? The Greeks, who served eggs with thrushes, hare-meat, and honey-cakes as dessert, had the custom of serving at the end of the meal tongues roasted in the fire, to be sampled after conversation. Hence the proverb used “something is finished” to mean that nothing remained.

[Col. 1272D] **COENACULUM**. *Coenacula* are the upper stories of a house. Vitruvius, *On Architecture* II:

> “For when,” he says, “because of the extraordinary number of the Roman people, it had become necessary to live in upper stories—for previously, when the spaciousness of the city permitted it, dwellings had only a single floor—they resorted to greater building heights and a denser concentration of houses.”

**COETUS**, intercourse. [Col. 1273A] I, 326 A, “And let them not recognize those unions as involving incestuous blood.” The meaning is that by this rite the memory of parents is so obscured that those who are now blind and ignorant of their own genealogy do not recognize those marriages or unions as being of incestuous blood and forming an abominable precedent. I, 465 A, “There is no necessity for intercourse.” I, 273 A, “Gatherings before dawn for singing to Christ and God.”

**COGERE**, to enclose or collect. II, 1048 B, “In the matter of fish, it pleased him to collect wild beasts,” that is, to collect fish in ponds so that they might perform the office of wild beasts and devour men in the manner of beasts. Ibid., “With foodstuffs forcibly gathered,” that is, things placed before them as food which the animals’ nature would not in the least seek. I, 452 B, “To thicken mud with wine,” that is, to drench and intoxicate the ground with a great quantity of wine, as a sign of insolence and luxury. I, 468 B, “We are gathered for the Scriptures,” that is, we assemble.

**COGITATORIUM**. II, 814 A, “The flesh will be the soul’s thinking-place,” that is, the place or part within the body where the soul thinks. I, 632 C, “Or was the spirit implanted in the body for the purpose of becoming a thinking-place for iniquities?”

**COGITATUS**, the same as *cogitatio*, thought. II, 910 B, “Every evil thought.” II, 914 C, “It sets thought in motion.” II, 814 A, “The thought of the flesh is action.”

[Col. 1273B] **COITIO**, an assembly. II, 973 A, “And through assemblies we transgress the commands of rulers that prohibit them.”

**COLLIGERE**. II, 199 A–B, “But how shall we assemble?” Ibid., “If you cannot assemble by day, you have the night,” namely, for gatherings of the Church, which were therefore called *συλλογαί, συνάξεις*—“assemblies, meetings.” Irenaeus, book III, chap. 3: “We confound all who, in any manner, assemble through either their own evil self-pleasing or vainglory.” Optatus, book II: “For the few who, among more than forty basilicas, had no place in which to assemble ought not to have been called a flock or a people.”

**COLLINEARE**. II, 991 C, “Whether either interpretation regarding restoration applies to a Christian or a pagan sinner,” that is, whether the interpretation is suitable when restoring the sinner.

**COLLOCARE**. I, 261 A, “We therefore assign this as the first charge among you,” that is, this is the first crime charged against you. On the evidence of two manuscripts, Heumann reads *nos* for *vos*: “We assign it among ourselves,” that is, “we determine among ourselves.” II, 57 A, “Now they appoint neophytes,” namely, to the grades of ecclesiastical ordination.

[Col. 1273C] **COLLYRIS** is a kind of cake, round in shape, so that it appears to be the same as an “umbilicus” on shields. I, 1323 B, “You plainly add certain little round cakes to the weight.”

**COLOR**. II, 910 C, “Or what is the complexion of the differing opinion?” II, 548 A, “It also has the colorations of ignorance.” I, 1312 B, “For what proper honor is there in clothing, arising from the adulteration of unnatural colors?” He censures the luxury of women, who put upon their garments colors not native to the fleece or wool—namely purple, violet, and scarlet—and says that these are unjust colors and adulterations.

**COLORARE**. II, 927 A, “I know with what pretexts we color our insatiability,” etc. I, 655 A, “Disguised as a pimp.”

**COMBIBERE**. I, 647 A, “Through which they had together imbibed the defilement.”

**COMESOR**. II, 247 B, “What Pontic mouse is so great a devourer as the man who has gnawed away the Gospels?”

[Col. 1273D] **COMESTUS**. I, 533 A, “His own tongue eaten.” Pliny, who attributes this to Anaxarchus [the printed *Anaxareho* is apparently corrupt], says “gnawed beforehand by the teeth”; Valerius Maximus, “bitten off and chewed.”

**COMINUS**. II, 317 A, “Otherwise I would also have cut down Marcion’s antitheses themselves at close quarters.” II, 326 A, “But now too, intending to fight from this point on definite ground and at close quarters.” *Cominus caedere*, “to strike at close quarters,” and *cominus dimicare*, “to fight at close quarters,” mean *συστάδην μάχεσθαι καὶ ἔγγυθεν*, “to fight hand-to-hand and from nearby”; their contrary is *πόρρωθεν διὰ δοράτων ἢ βελῶν*, “from a distance by means of spears or missiles.” The expression is transferred from warfare, in which soldiers sometimes fight with swords joined and are said to join or engage hand-to-hand, while at other times they hurl weapons at the enemy from afar with engines or even throwing-straps. We use it when we wish to indicate that we intend to examine or discuss a matter more closely or from nearby, bringing arguments drawn from the nature of the thing itself, rather than from external considerations, to weaken someone’s position. II, 787 [Col. 1274A] B, “Let them come still closer to an insolently arranged contest.”

**COMITIARE**. II, 1047 A, “How greatly you have harangued in its name.” It is explained as: “How greatly you reproached me in the assembly on account of the cloak.” Plautus used *incomitiare*.

**COMMEATUS**, an extension of time. I, 447 A, “Through the reprieve of the Roman Empire.” It is used of every delay and postponement. I, 508 A, “Time for deliberating,” that is, a period in which to deliberate. II, 156 B, “During that interval,” that is, during that period of time. II, 141 A, “Nor through this interval of life.” II, 334 A, “They ought not, by being born, to have received a brief term in the flesh,” that is, the opportunity to spend a short time in the flesh. II, 441 D, “He wishes us to gain the term as profit.” By “the term of the world” he means our continued stay in the world. “He wishes us to gain the term as profit,” that is, that every day we pass innocently—or, as he says, in wise conduct—should be reckoned as profit to us. II, 297 B, “By measuring out time for his operations, he administered the plan of his goodness.” This is military language, for commanders are accustomed to measure out and delimit leave for their soldiers. I, 1237 A, “And they provide themselves with leave to sin.” *Commeatus* is leave granted to a soldier so that he may be absent from camp for a time, either for business or because of illness. The meaning is therefore: they themselves prepare and obtain the opportunity to sin rather than instruction and discipline not to sin. II, 710 A, “If all unlawful acts are not accomplished during the very first sojourn of this life.” Irenaeus: “If someone, forestalling everything in a single coming, does all things once and simultaneously—things that it is unlawful for us not only to say and hear, but even to conceive in the mind.”

[Col. 1274B] **COMMENTARI**. II, 1032 A, “Having devised the force of an engine, the feeble head of a sheep exacting vengeance”—others read *de bile*, “from bile,” that is, from the motion of an enraged ram. The meaning is: having devised a violent machine that, after the model of an enraged ram, avenges itself with its head. II, 573 B, “Achamoth having devised an image in honor of the aeons.” II, 266 C, “If this man shall devise a God.”

[Col. 1274C] **COMMENTARIUS**. II, 151 A, “And if the heretic should desire the confirmation of the record,” that is, if he wishes to be instructed on the authority even of the public archives. I, 292 A, “Consult your records.” Pamelius thinks that these words allude to the *Annals* of Cornelius Tacitus, book XV, or rather to Suetonius’s *Nero*. II, 966 B, “Further, since in the same record of Luke.”

**COMMENTATOR**. I, 370 A, “Nor any chronicler of antiquities of that kind.” II, 789 A, “First of all Matthew himself, the most faithful narrator of the Gospel.” II, 841 B, “Or illuminated beforehand by the narrator of the Gospel.”

**COMMISERO** is the Greek *συνταλαίπωρος*, “a companion in misery.” II, 374 C; 449 D.

**COMMITTERE**, to bring two or more persons together in a contest. II, 36 B, “That I may set the Apostles against one another,” a metaphor taken from gladiators. II, 570 B, “Which engaged us against Hermogenes,” that is, caused me to write against Hermogenes. I, 621 A, “That he may set you against one another,” that is, bring you into a contest among yourselves. II, 281 A, “They do battle against the gullet,” that is, they injure the very person whose gullet they have overwhelmed [Col. 1274D] with delicacies. II, 287 B, “And therefore he committed goodness to its own fullness,” that is, stored it away to be brought forth at its proper time. II, 919 B, “When he brings the anxiety of married persons into comparison,” that is, compares and contrasts it. II, 862 A, “But neither is baptism itself brought to fulfillment through regeneration,” etc. I, 439 A, “Therefore we commit an offense against the majesty of the emperors.” I, 416 A, “You commit the crime of true irreligion.”

**COMMUNICARE**. II, 33 B, “We communicate with the apostolic churches,” that is, we prove that we have communion with them because we possess no divergent doctrine, but ours is in agreement with theirs. I, 1262 A, “A person is defiled not by impurities from vessels, but by the things issued from the mouth,” that is, polluted, *κοινοῦν*. I, 650 A, “Why do the things uttered by the mouth defile a person?” etc.
