### 13. Bolder than an Amazon. Vol. III, Book I against Marcion, ch. 1, no. 14.

“The Amazons, or Amazon women, are a singular example for all ages,” says Justin, Book II. Their deeds, wars, and expeditions, full of more than masculine boldness, are commemorated. But since boldness in the female sex is generally joined with shamelessness, the proverb will apply to those who are shamelessly bold and rash. See the annotations of Pamelius.

[Col. 0213D] ### 14. A ram is poised against us, by which the flesh is battered. Vol. III, On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. 5, no. 43.

A ram is a beam stretched and suspended by ropes, its head clad in iron so that it may appear to have a very hard forehead like a ram’s; it is also drawn backward in the manner of a ram so that it may strike with greater force and undermine walls. Hence the verb *to be poised*, because skill and a precise balancing were required in preparing and counterbalancing it for its intended blows. Tertullian adds the verb *is battered*. Virgil: “They were pounding the walls with the ram”; “The door totters under the repeated ram”; “He batters at the gates.” Cicero: “The ram has struck the wall.” It may be said proverbially when a powerful argument is raised against us which is difficult to refute. We are attacked by open force. Thus rams and mines may be set against one another in arguments.

[Col. 0214A] ### 15. More just than Aristides. Vol. I, *Apology*, ch. 11, no. 173.

So celebrated among the Greeks, and especially among the Athenians, were the justice and equity of Aristides that he earned the surname τοῦ δικαίου (“the Just”). So great was this man’s observance not only of the laws but also of equity and goodness that he was banished by the wicked for this name alone, and governed the commonwealth with Themistocles despite their opposing political allegiances.

### 16. The asp borrows venom from the viper. Vol. III, Book III against Marcion, ch. 8, no. 53.

Pliny speaks thus of the asp in Book VIII, ch. 23: “The necks of asps swell up, and there is no remedy for their bite except the immediate amputation of the affected parts: so rapidly acting is their venom.” Of the viper he says in the same book, [Col. 0214B] ch. 29: “Of serpents, the viper alone is said to conceal itself in the earth; the rest hide in hollows of trees or rocks.” But Aristotle, Book V of *The Nature of Animals*, reports the contrary: that the viper conceals itself beneath rocks, while the other serpents hide in the earth; during their period of retirement they all lie dormant bereft of venom. In Book VII, ch. 2, he says that the inhabitants of Mount Athos feed upon the flesh of vipers. This is surely evidence that their venom is less effective. This proverb will therefore signify that the most pestilential person is instructed in wickedness by one who is less evil.

### 17. Attalic riches. Vol. V, On Fasting, ch. 15, no. 93.

If the reading is *Attalic*, there is no difficulty: the wealth of Attalus, king of Pergamum, is well known, and the Roman people received it by inheritance. Horace speaks of him in the *Odes*:

[Col. 0214C] Neither through wealth nor as the unknown heir  
Of Attalus have I occupied a royal palace.

And: “On Attalic terms.” Thus those magnificent and superfluous riches and luxuries are contrasted with bread, that is, with the simplest food. But if the reading is *Atellan*, it will mean those masked refinements of dress and adornment. For the very famous Atellan actors, named after Atella, a town of Campania, had been granted the right not to be compelled to remove their masks on stage. Hence masked plays were called Atellan κατ` ἔξοχην (“par excellence”). It will be said of superfluous clothing procured for luxury and display.

### 18. From the toga to the pallium. Vol. I, On the Pallium, ch. 5, no. 94, and ch. 6, no. 116.

[Col. 0214D] RHENANUS. The pallium belonged to the Greeks and the philosophers. Romans too put it on after casting aside the toga. Hence that taunt of the wondering populace concerning more powerful men who had passed over to the Christian religion: “From the toga to the pallium.” Tertullian was thereby prompted to write his book in defense of the pallium. Ammianus Marcellinus, on Julian Augustus: “For in time of peace,” he says, “his frugality was an admirable standard to those who judged rightly, as though he were shortly to return to the pallium.”

### 19. A blind man led by a blind man into the same pit. Vol. III, Book III against Marcion, ch. 7, no. 44.

A proverb used even by the Savior, Luke XVI, whenever an unlearned man attempts to teach another unlearned man, or an imprudent man to advise another imprudent man, and thus hurls him together with himself into the same errors or corrupt opinions. [Col. 0215A] Tertullian interprets it thus: “To borrow from the same man both the rationale of one’s errors and one’s guidance.”

### 20. A blind man stumbles against a rock. Vol. III, Book V against Marcion, ch. 7, no. 119.

Tertullian speaks allegorically of Marcion, who, blinded by the depravity of his errors, held a wrong opinion of Christ, the spiritual rock. Not dissimilar is: “To strike against a reef,” in *On the Trinity*, ch. 28: “Deprived of both those eyes, as though they had been gouged out, he is utterly overcome in the blindness of his doctrine.” It will be applied to one whom the perversity of his opinions drives headlong into every kind of absurdity.

### 21. To strike at close quarters. Vol. III, Book II against Marcion, final chapter, no. 205, and Book III, ch. 5, no. 14.

[Col. 0215B] “To strike at close quarters,” “to fight at close quarters,” συστάδην μάχεσθαι, καὶ ἐγγύθεν (“to fight in close combat and at close quarters”), whose contrary is πόῤῥωθεν, διὰ δορὰτων ἢ βέλων (“from afar, with spears or missiles”). It is transferred from war, in which men sometimes fight with swords in close engagement and are said to “join” or “bring together hands,” while at other times they discharge missiles against the enemy from afar by machines or even by throwing-thongs. We shall use it when we wish to indicate that we intend to examine or discuss a matter more closely or at close range, and to bring forward arguments drawn from the nature of the thing itself, rather than assumed from external considerations, in order to weaken someone’s position.

### 22. From the lime-shop to the charcoal-shop. Vol. III, On the Flesh of Christ, ch. 6, no. 52.

RHENANUS. A lime-shop is a shop in which lime is stored or sold. So too a charcoal-shop takes its name from charcoal. It will be used [Col. 0215C] when a filthy or troublesome condition is exchanged for one equally filthy or troublesome. It is an elegant proverb whenever we retreat from an unclean place to one no cleaner, or fall from one evil into another of a different kind. After Tertullian had answered Marcion’s most impure heresy, he prepared to refute the equally impure heresy of Apelles. Not dissimilar are: “From Scylla into Charybdis”; “Out of the smoke into the flame.” Yet the former proverb appears to signify a certain diversity, and almost an opposition, between the evils, just as lime and charcoal are opposed by reason of their contrary colors. It is taken from those who abhor stains and do not readily allow their clothing to be soiled, which the dust of both substances causes.

[Col. 0215D] ### 23. To spread out over its own plains. Vol. V, On the Trinity, ch. 6, no. 26.

Novatian sets these things in opposition: “To enclose the measure or shape of the divine majesty within these outlines of our body,” and “to spread it without limit over its own plains of boundless magnitude.” The open expanses and immensities of plains are familiar in the proper meaning of the word. So too Virgil’s line:

Messapus, a horseman in arms,  
And Coras with his brother—spread out over the broad plains.

Κατὰ τὴν μεταφορὰν (“By metaphor”) are these expressions: “the plains of rhetoricians”; “for an orator to range over some field”; “to force an orator from a certain vast and immeasurable field into a narrow circle”; and “these matters [Col. 0216A] are accustomed to open a very broad field of discourse to the rhetorician.” From these the use both of this proverb and of the next is evident.

### 24. A broad and spacious field is opened. Ibid., ch. 28, no. 127.

Its contrary in Cicero is “not to have a sufficiently large field in which to range.” Novatian likewise says elsewhere in the same book: “Let these things be stated briefly by us and set down concisely, without being extended through a long disputation. For they could have been extended more broadly and drawn out in a fuller discussion.”

### 25. To speak with the eyebrow of the head. Vol. III, Against Hermogenes, ch. 27, no. 91.

Pride is marked in the eyebrows, by which we either refuse or assent. Tertullian undoubtedly borrowed [Col. 0216B] this from Homer, *Iliad* α (“Book 1”). Thetis says to Jupiter:

Νημερτὲς δὴ μοι ὑπόσχεο, καὶ κατάνευσον (“Promise me truly, then, and nod your assent”).

“Promise me without ambiguity and nod your assent.” Jupiter replies:

Εἰ δ` ἄγε τοι κεφαλῇ κατανεύσομαι, ὂφρα πεποίθῃς;

Τοῦτο γὰρ ἐξ ἐμέθεν γε μετ` ἀθανάτοισι μέγιστον

Τέκμωρ` οὐ γὰρ ἐμὸν παλινὰγρετον, οὐδ` ἀπατηλὸν,

Οὐδ` ἀτελεύτητον γ` ὅ, τι κεν κεφαλῇ κατανεύσω (“Come, I shall nod to you with my head so that you may trust; for this is the greatest pledge from me among the immortals: whatever I nod to with my head is neither revocable, deceitful, nor unfulfilled”).

Ἦ, καὶ κυανέῃσιν ἐπ` ὀφρύσι νεῦσε Κρονίων (“He spoke, and the son of Cronus nodded with his dark brows”). That is:

I shall nod to you with my head, that you may rest in sure confidence;  
For this is the greatest pledge from me among the immortal gods,  
Since whatever I nod to you with my head is neither revocable,  
Nor deceitful, nor left unfulfilled.

“The son of Saturn nodded assent to her words with his dark brows.”

Hence Pliny, Book I, in his letter to Octavius Rufus: “See to what height you have raised me, since you have given me the same power and the same sovereignty that Homer gave Jupiter:

Υἱῷ δ` ἕτερον μὲν ἔδωκε πατὴρ, ἕτερον δ` ἀνένευσεν (“The father gave one thing to his son but refused another”). The father granted one request; to one he nodded assent.

For I too can answer your request with a similar nod of assent or refusal.” I should not rashly judge the expression “the censorial eyebrow” to be irrelevant here, as though one condemned or approved by the eyebrow alone—an expression others interpret as grim severity.

### 26. To give a cathartic. Vol. I, On the Pallium, ch. 5, no. 111.

Καθαρτικὸν ἢ καθαρτήριον φάρμακον, ἢ κάθαρσις (“a cathartic or purgative medicine, or a purgation”), a medicine that causes evacuation, or that empties the bowels. Hence ὑπήλατον (“a medicine administered from below; a purgative”) was said of Hippocrates. Tertullian explains himself: [Col. 0216D] “By discourse you have persuaded me with a most wise medicine,” from a Greek senarius:

Λόγῳ μ` ἕπεισας φαρμάκῳ σοφωτάτῳ (“By discourse you persuaded me with a most wise medicine”).

Καθαιροῦν (“to cleanse or purify”) means to cleanse and expiate. Horace alludes to this in Book I, *Epistles* 1, when speaking of the vices of the mind:

There are words and utterances with which you may soothe this pain  
And drive away a great part of the disease.

And:

. . . . . There are certain expiations that could  
Restore you after you had read the little book in purity three times.

“To purge with hellebore,” and similar expressions, belong here.

### 27. Wiser and graver than Cato. Vol. I, *Apology*, ch. 11, no. 173.

The Sabines called an acute man *catus*, whence the name of the Catos. An encomium of this Cato—Tertullian’s first Cato and the Censor—may be found in Pliny, Book VII, ch. 27, [Col. 0217A] and in Cicero’s *On Friendship*, where Laelius says of Cato: “Either no one was wise, or, if anyone was, he was.” It may also be found in *On Old Age*, which is inscribed with the name of this Cato.

### 28. More rugged than the Caucasus. Vol. I, Book I against Marcion, ch. 1, no. 15.

The Caucasus is an immense mountain of Scythia. As Pliny says, Book VI, ch. 5, its ridges in the Colchian region of Pontus turn toward the Riphaean mountains, descending on one side toward the Euxine and Lake Maeotis, and on the other toward the Caspian and Hyrcanian Sea, under various names. Virgil, *Aeneid* IV:

The bristling Caucasus begot you  
From its hard crags, and Hyrcanian . . .

By these words “rugged” is explained. It is applied to a coarse and hard person, intractable and inhospitable.

[Col. 0217B] ### 29. To have neither brain nor heart. Vol. III, Book IV against Marcion, ch. 10, no. 118.

In the human brain, the receptacle of the vital spirits, lies the supreme and, as it were, royal power of reason; in the heart lies the very source of all life and the seat of wisdom. Hence the senseless and foolish are said to be empty of brain and heart. Indeed, physical disorders themselves, if they affect either brain or heart, undermine the mind and conduct as well. Aesop’s familiar fable tells of the fox admiring the head of a theatrical mask: ὦ οἱα κεφαλή, καὶ ἐγκέφαλον οὐκ ἔχει· πρὸς τοῖς κατὰ τὴν ψυχὴν ἀλογίστοις (“O what a head—and it has no brain; besides those who are irrational in soul”). “O what a head! Yet it has no brain.”

### 30. Deer in battle. Vol. II, On the Soldier’s Crown, ch. 1, no. 18.

[Col. 0217C] RHENANUS. It has the flavor of a proverbial figure, arising, as it appears, from that saying of Chabrias, who said that an army of deer led by a lion was more formidable than an army of lions led by a deer. Plutarch mentions it. The deer is a simple animal of proverbial timidity, whose entire security lies in flight, since it is exceedingly swift. Hence men are called deer-like when they trust more in flight than in strength, and are fearful. To them Mars is, as Virgil says in Book II, “in their boastful tongue and fleeing feet.” And in Homer, *Iliad* α (“Book 1”), Achilles reproaches Agamemnon for cowardice in martial encounters:

Οἰνοβαρὲς, κυνὸς ὃμματ` ἔχων, κραδίηντ` ἐλάφοιο (“Wine-besotted, with the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer!”) Drunkard, with the heart of a deer and the eyes of a dog.

### 31. Chrysippus to the hellebore. Vol. III, On the Soul, ch. 6, no. 89.

[Col. 0217D] Chrysippus, the pupil of Cleanthes, was a man of exceedingly keen intellect, concerning whom there is the little verse:

Εἰ μὴ γὰρ ἦν Χρύσιππος, οὐκ ἄν ἦ Στόα (“For had Chrysippus not existed, neither would the Stoa”) . . . .

Had Chrysippus not existed, neither would the Stoa.

Valerius Maximus, Book VIII, ch. 7, reports that Carneades, when preparing to dispute with Chrysippus, used first to purge himself with hellebore so that he might express his own thought more attentively and refute Chrysippus’ more sharply. Aulus Gellius likewise, Book XVII, ch. 15: “Carneades the Academic, when about to write against the books of Zeno the Stoic, purged the upper parts of his body with white hellebore.” And in the chapter where he explains hellebore’s efficacy and its two varieties. Pliny too, Book XXV, ch. 5, attributes the same practice to Carneades and describes the nature of hellebore. Tertullian seems to attribute it to Chrysippus himself: not understanding his own propositions, Chrysippus suspects a disorder of health. Valerius writes of Chrysippus in the same chapter: “His devotion to examining the monuments of his own intellect sustained so much effort and labor that a long life is required to understand thoroughly what he wrote.” And as Cicero says of the same man: “He proposed certain things,” he says, “which even he himself could not resolve.” But Lucian tells a fable in Book II of his *True Narratives*: Chrysippus was not permitted to enter the island of the blessed until he had taken hellebore for the fourth time. This alludes to the tradition that he drank it three times during his life. Hence those who wish the defects of their mind and head to be relieved are ordered to drink or eat hellebore and to purge themselves with it.

### 32. He came down from heaven. Vol. I, *Apology*, ch. 10, no. 162.

[Col. 0218B] See the annotations of Pamelius. Tertullian applies it to unknown persons, or those who appear unexpectedly; such people were also called heaven-dwellers, persons who had descended from heaven, or who were said to have fallen from heaven, οὐρανοπετεῖς (“fallen from heaven”). The expression may appear to have been taken from tragic stages, in which there was commonly ἀπὸ τῆς μηχανῆς θεὸς (“a god from the stage machine”). For the *machine* is a place above the stage from which some god would suddenly appear.

### 33. From heaven into filth. Vol. II, On the Spectacles, ch. 25, no. 189.

Tertullian: “To proceed from the Church of God into the church of the devil.” And in *On the Soldier’s Crown*: “To transfer one’s name from one camp into the camp of darkness.” Tertullian cultivated the elegance of ἐκ τἢς παρονομασίας (“through wordplay”), so that it means to prefer darkness to light and death to life. It is used when we turn aside from the most honorable actions [Col. 0218C] and pursuits toward crimes and toward vain and shameful exercises.

### 34. A summer dinner after the roast. Vol. III, On the Soul, ch. 32, no. 387.

Tertullian appears to indicate that at summer dinners, after the roast—that is, roasted meat—it was customary for no further dishes to be brought in, so that the roast marked the end of the dinner. Similarly, Empedocles, thoroughly roasted in burning Etna, was changed into no other body, and thus this was the end of his metempsychosis. What if he is alluding to the following custom? The Greeks, who served eggs together with thrushes, hare meat, and honeyed cakes as a second course, had the custom of serving tongues roasted in the fire at the end of dinner, after the conversations, to be tasted. The proverb will therefore signify that something has been finished, [Col. 0218D] and that nothing remains.

### 35. To play with one’s own skin. Vol. I, On the Pallium, ch. 3, no. 49.

Taken from the chameleon, which throughout its whole body reproduces whatever color it next touches, except red and white. Thus the adage plainly applies to a changeable and inconstant man who alters himself with the times into every guise. Similar is: “More changeable than Proteus.”

### 36. Richer than Croesus and Crassus. Vol. I, *Apology*, ch. 11, no. 173.

The opulence of Croesus, king of the Lydians, passed into a proverb among the Greeks; he dared to make war upon Cyrus the Persian. Among the Romans, M. Crassus was given the surname “Rich.” He possessed twenty million sesterces in landed estates and, Cicero records in the *Paradoxes*, said that no one was rich unless he could maintain an army from his revenues.

[Col. 0219A] ### 37. To thrust with the elbow. Vol. III, On the Soul, ch. 55, no. 605.

If we rebuke something wrongly or rather dishonorably said, we are accustomed to thrust an elbow into the side of the person next to us whose words we disapprove, and to ward him far away from us, as though shrinking from him, as an expression of detestation. Horace, Book I, *Epistle* 6:

Let us buy a slave to prompt us with names,  
One who will dig into our left side.

He means a memory-slave, or nomenclator. Here “to dig into the side” means to poke or strike it with the hand, [Col. 0219B] which is done in order to admonish, prompt, and urge. It will therefore mean to spurn and reject persons imbued with so corrupt an opinion.

### 38. To drive out with a wedge. Vol. III, Book I against Marcion, ch. 21, no. 150.

“With this wedge of truth every heresy is driven out.” A wedge is an instrument of iron or wood with which, as Virgil says, wood capable of being split was cleft. From its shape comes the *wedge* of infantry drawn up in battle: joined to the battle-line, they advance first in a narrower and then in a broader formation, breaking the enemy’s ranks because missiles are discharged by many men into a single place. Against them is ordinarily deployed the formation called the *shears*, composed of picked soldiers massed in the shape of the letter V; it receives that [Col. 0219C] wedge and encloses it on both sides. Once this is done, the wedge cannot break through the line. From the former meaning come these expressions: “A bad knot calls for a bad wedge,” taken from those who saw timber, meaning “to repel evil with evil” and “to drive out one nail with another.” Tertullian seems to allude to these here. In what follows, he seems to play upon the second meaning.

### 39. To meet a wedge. Vol. III, On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. 2, no. 18.

Taken from warfare. Virgil:

Let the ranks be massed in wedges . . . .

And:

All form a wedge and are borne toward the walls  
In a dense mass.

And:

Where that most densely packed mass of men presses on.

It will mean to oppose someone’s reasoning with powerful and densely marshaled arguments.

[Col. 0219D] ### 40. He arms the first wedge of the encounter. Vol. III, Against the Valentinians, ch. 3, no. 24.

Tertullian adds: “We inaugurate this first victory as the revealer of their conscience.” Thus advance scouts—horsemen sent ahead—are accustomed to uncover the enemy’s forces, ambushes, and numbers. And a first victory is, as it were, an omen for the battle. All these expressions are transferred from the martial conflict of battle to disputation, which is a literary contest.

### 41. More eloquent than Demosthenes. Vol. I, *Apology*, ch. 2, no. 173.

“Nothing is more renowned than the oratorical power of Demosthenes,” says Valerius Maximus, Book IV, ch. 5, and Book VIII, ch. 7. “Demosthenes, at the mention of whose [Col. 0220A] name the consummation of supreme eloquence arises in the hearer’s mind.” Quintilian, Book IX, records the same encomium.

### 42. To speak with a nod of the finger. Vol. III, Against Hermogenes, ch. 27, no. 91.

In the *Apology*: “with the calculating gestures of the fingers.” The ancients commonly used a method of calculation performed by bending the fingers. The Venerable Bede calls it “the speech of the fingers,” or speech by gestures of the fingers. Alexander ab Alexandro, Book I, ch. 14: “The ancients often counted years by gestures of the hand, now the right and now the left, and by lowering or curving the fingers,” etc. Tertullian alludes to this custom when he adds: “with an appropriate gesture, raising it higher and casting it backward, as it were.” It is applied to a person who assumes far too much importance and determines whatever he pleases [Col. 0220B] according to his own judgment.

### 43. To point out with the finger. Vol. I, On the Pallium, ch. 5, no. 84.

Tertullian in *On the Veiling of Virgins*: “While she is titillated by the fingers of those pointing her out.” It is the mark of a powerful person to be indicated and pointed out with fingers. Horace, *Odes* IV.3:

Because the fingers of passersby point me out  
As the minstrel of the Roman lyre.

And in the *Epodes*, Ode 4, he explains our proverb in accordance with Tertullian’s sense:

Do you see, as you measure the Sacred Way  
In a toga twice three cubits broad,  
How the wholly uninhibited indignation  
Of those passing this way and that turns their faces toward you?

[Col. 0220C] ### 44. To fight against a definite target. Vol. III, Book III against Marcion, ch. 5, no. 14.

Gladiators and boxers preparing to engage one another are accustomed, before coming to grips and blows, to give a kind of preliminary display and, as the blessed Paul says, to beat the air. Virgil, Book V:

Thus Dares first raises his head high for battle,  
Displays his broad shoulders, and successively throws  
His arms forward, beating the air with blows.

Then afterward:

They mingle hand with hand and provoke the fight.

And Book XII:

He assails the winds  
With blows, and rehearses the battle in the scattered sand.

It may also appear to have been taken from javelin throwers, who set up a target [Col. 0220D] at which to aim. Here belongs that passage of the blessed Paul, 1 Cor. IX: “I therefore run in such a way, not as though toward an uncertainty; I fight in such a way, not as though beating the air.”

### 45. To fight at close quarters. Ibid. See “To strike at close quarters,” above.

### 46. An *epikitharisma* after the play. Vol. III, Against the Valentinians, ch. 33, no. 367.

RHENANUS. When plays were formerly performed publicly, some cithara-player would come forward at the end, before the spectators were dismissed, to soothe the minds of those already wearied by the tedium of the long narrative. Both before and after the regular play, certain irrelevant pieces were customarily sung in the music hall solely to dispel the audience’s weariness. [Col. 0221A] Hence the *proem*, as though a prelude, πρὸς τῆς οἴμου (“before the song,” contextually; the recovered Greek is textually uncertain) before the regular song; rhetoricians metaphorically called the opening of their speech by this name. In poetry there is the prologue and, conversely, the epilogue; in a performance on pipes, the *proaulion*. The expression may be used for the appendix to some vain undertaking, or an added fiction.

### 47. To taste honeycombs after gall. Vol. II, On the Soldier’s Crown, ch. 14, no. 196.

RHENANUS. It has the appearance of a proverb commonly spoken of one who, after enduring adversity, finally attains happier circumstances. The following are pertinent: “From the storm into port”; “After the contest, the crown.” Common sayings are: “From black bread to white”; “The roots of virtue are bitter, its fruits exceedingly sweet.”

[Col. 0221B] ### 48. To loosen or release the clasp. Vol. II, On the Soldier’s Crown, ch. 11, no. 144.

RHENANUS. Said proverbially for “to open a window” and “to provide a handle for transgressions,” expressions which themselves also have a proverbial form. It is opposed to what Tertullian says in *On Monogamy*: “to impose a clasp,” that is, to restrain and curb. It appears to have arisen from the clasp of a belt, by which a loose garment is tightened or a tight one released. Virgil:

A golden clasp fastens his purple garment.

But since Tertullian generally refers it to the slippery pleasures of the flesh, it may be related to the constriction of the clasp with which the genitals of youths who were lustful and prone to sexual indulgence were formerly pierced, a practice they called *infibulation*. An account of this practice occurs in Cornelius Celsus, and mention of it in Martial and Juvenal. Thus it may be said of those who, having broken through the barriers of modesty, rush headlong into lust and are unrestrained in every kind of license.

### 49. To venture upon the thinnest thread with one’s footing suspended—perhaps the reading is “to walk upon.” Vol. V, On Modesty, ch. 10, no. 83.

Tertullian explains himself and indicates that this manner of speech is transferred from tightrope walkers when he first says: “of tightrope-walking chastity.” Tightrope walkers, νευροβάται (“cord-walkers”) and σχοινοβάται (“rope-walkers”), walked along a stretched rope, and for the unskilled this kind of walking was not free from the danger of falling. Hence he contrasts “with suspended footing”—for they appear to hang in the air—with “to be wholly in one’s position,” “to be on solid ground.” [Col. 0221D] It will therefore apply to those who undertake a matter full of danger. Ovid, on the vicissitudes of human affairs:

All human things hang by a slender thread.

### 50. To mark out a trench as a boundary. Vol. III, On the Prescription against Heretics, ch. 10, no. 68.

RHENANUS. A metaphorical expression for a boundary beyond which one is not permitted to leap. A military rampart was ordinarily fortified either with earth heaped up from turf or by the digging of a vast trench, which no one could cross together with its fortifications; within it the soldiers were contained. Or the expression may relate to what was said above about the *scamma*. It will therefore mean that certain laws have been prescribed for a person, from which it is forbidden to depart. Not dissimilar is the Greek ὑπερ τὰ ἐσκαμμένα πηδᾷν (“to leap beyond the trench”), “to leap beyond the trench.”

[Col. 0222A] ### 51. The waves engulf you on both sides. Vol. III, Book I against Marcion, ch. 7, no. 50.

Taken from a storm at sea and from mountainous waves; it is transferred to arguments that catch an opponent on both sides, for Tertullian also uses a dilemma. It is as though he were to say: “There is no place of escape; you are held fast, constrained and ensnared from either side, or from each side of the argument.” It may also be applied to one who is surrounded by twin and twofold evils. Virgil:

He tosses on the shifting tide of cares.

### 52. To bridle. Vol. V, Book I, On Modesty, ch. 16, no. 136.

Tertullian: “It will be bound to bridling marriages by continence.” [Col. 0222B] A very common metaphor from horses. Virgil:

And Justice granted him to bridle proud nations.

Horace:

This mind—restrain it with bridles, restrain it with chains.

That is, check and suppress the disturbances of the mind. One may oppose to this Tertullian’s expression “to loosen or release the clasp for the flesh.” Cicero: “to put raging bridles upon someone” and “to bridle someone’s fury.”

### 53. To release or loosen those who have been bridled; and to tighten the reins. Vol. V, On Modesty, ch. 2, no. 22.

They are contraries. Virgil, *Aeneid* I:

Who, under a fixed covenant, knew how  
Both to restrain and, when ordered, to give loose reins.

Cicero: “One needs the reins, the other the spurs.” Tertullian [Col. 0222C] transferred this to discipline, so that it signifies the use either of excessive indulgence or of immoderate severity in the observance of discipline.

### 54. To be set forth with one’s own face. Vol. V, On Monogamy, ch. 12, no. 100.

RHENANUS. It means that something ought to be set forth freely and openly, not under compulsion but straightforwardly. In Cicero’s speech *For C. Rabirius*, “with a true face” means to do or say something sincerely and without disguise; conversely, “with a lying face and expression” means through pretense. Hence the saying: “No trust is to be placed in the face.” For the face and eyes most clearly reveal the disposition of the mind. And in Terence, “to fashion one’s expression” is to compose it for the purpose of pretending or concealing something. Yet I think it more probable that the authors of the proverb had the following in view. Since the forehead is the seat of shame—whence “a man with a hardened forehead”—when we do or say something that does not depart from honor and will not readily turn to our shame, we may use the expression that we do or say it “with our own face.” But if something shameful or indecorous must be done, we do and say it “with another’s face,” or through agents whom we have suborned, and spare our own sense of shame.

### 55. He who fled will fight again. Vol. V, On Flight in Persecution, ch. 10, no. 72.

RHENANUS. When Demosthenes was fleeing from the battle of Chaeronea, in which the Athenians were defeated while fighting against Philip, king of the Macedonians, people reproached him for his shameful flight and for casting away his shield. He was accustomed to answer them with this verse: Ἀνὴρ ὁ φεύγων (“The man who flees”) [Col. 0223A] πάλιν μαχήσεται (“will fight again”), that is: “The man who flees will enter battle again.” Shortly before this, Tertullian sets the following in opposition: “He surely commands them to stand immovable and not to flee; to be ready to move and girded for flight—or for hastening to the Gospel?” “To cast away the spear,” and the Greek ρίψασπις (“shield-thrower”) or ἀσπιδαποβλὴς, ἀπὸ τοῦ ῥίπτειν καὶ ἀποβἁλλειν τὴν ἀσπίδα (“shield-caster, from throwing and casting away the shield”), are proverbial expressions meaning to lose confidence in one’s cause or to abandon its defense. By Tertullian’s proverb, which originated among the Greeks, we may be warned not immediately to lose heart if something turns out unhappily and, when one way has not succeeded, “to attempt another way,” as the comic poet says.

### 56. To pull the rope of contention. Vol. III, Book IV against Marcion, ch. 4, no. 28.

The expression “to pull the rope” is frequent in good authors. [Col. 0223B] It is taken μεταφορικῶς (“metaphorically”) from a comic dance which they call κόρδακα (“the cordax”) and κορδακισμὸν (“cordax-dancing”). Terence, *The Brothers*: “You will dance about among them, continually pulling the rope”; and in Aristophanes: νεφέλαις οὐδὲ κόρδακι ἕλκυσην (approximately, “in the Clouds, not even in the cordax … pull”; the recovered wording is corrupt and its syntax is uncertain). In these choral dances the leader and director pulls the rope, while the others grasp it and follow him as he goes before them. Hence in Cicero “to lead the household” means to be the chief and founder of some school; and Horace says of Money, *Epistles* I:

Worthy rather of following the entire rope than of pulling it.

But what have these things to do with contention and conflict, or with a stubborn defense of opinions? He therefore appears to allude to another kind of game, in which each side, perhaps, pulled in opposite directions upon the middle of a rope and attempted to drag it toward itself and wrench it away from the other side. [Col. 0223C] In this contest a prize for victory was offered. This is still observed in many places today by children. Tertullian calls it the “rope of contention” or “contentious rope” from the struggle between the parties—as the Flemish call *Kyfbal* in the game of court-tennis when the ball is played back and forth—and adds: “surging to and fro with equal effort”; perhaps the reading is “surging beneath the rope.”

### 57. To draw the day toward evening with a contentious rope. Vol. I, Against the Jews, ch. 1, no. 1, and Vol. III, On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. 34, no. 254.

Or “to drag it into the evening.” Tertullian adds “by alternating turns,” as above, “with equal effort on either side.” Cicero, *Letters to Friends*, Book I, letter 2: “This controversy was prolonged until night”; and *Letters to Atticus*, Book IV, letter 2: “When the discussion reached Clodius, he wished to consume the day.” Pliny: “Night broke off my pleading as it breaks off battles”; and: “He spoke on into the night, [Col. 0223D] even after lamps had been brought in.” Among the Romans, when the intercessors wished something to be postponed or adjourned to another day, they prolonged the day by speaking, so that the Senate might reach no decision that very day. In Sallust they are said “to draw out the day,” “to prolong the time,” and “to protract deliberations.” Thus the expression will signify the obstinacy of a protracted contention. In *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, “to lead something along by a contentious rope” means to discuss a matter with lengthy and intricate arguments.

### 58. To stretch a contentious rope in opposite directions by alternating pulls. Vol. V, On Modesty, ch. 2, no. 22.

Besides what has been adduced above, this proverb reminds me that a *hauling rope* is the rope passed through the small wheels of a pulley—a hauling machine with a bronze [Col. 0224A] or wooden wheel that turns around a small axle, or a grooved wheel which the Belgians call *Caterol* and *Moufle*. We use this machine to lift burdens, bundles, and sacks of grain aloft and bring them in through windows. The rope is said, conversely, to be let down. Virgil:

Having slid down by the lowered rope.

“By an alternating pull,” as above, “by alternating turns,” “with equal effort on either side.” It will signify the same thing: to debate a matter from both sides with great persistence.

### 59. To draw a long rope. Vol. II, On the Veiling of Virgins, ch. 14, no. 115.

That statement of the blessed Gregory in the *Morals* is familiar: “From suggestion arises thought; from thought, affection; from affection, pleasure; from pleasure, consent,” etc., as far as certain damnation. This is that long and dreadful chain of sins: the ropes and fetters with which Satan hurls the bound man not only into every kind of evil but even into the abyss of Tartarus. In secular authors: “a chain of affairs”; “chained labors.” Pindar: δυσφόρων χοινίον μεριμνῶν (“a cord of grievous cares”; the recovered χοινίον may be corrupt).

### 60. More fragile than ice. Vol. III, Book I against Marcion, ch. 1, no. 14.

Others read: “More fragile than frost.” It is said of an inconstant and faithless man, for “fragile” is opposed to “firm” and “stable.” The cause of fragility in ice is the looseness of its parts after heat—the bond that joins them together—has been expelled.

[Col. 0224C] ### 61. To yield one’s position, to yield from one’s position, or to withdraw briefly. Vol. III, On the Prescription against Heretics, ch. 9, no. 64; Book V against Marcion, ch. 16, no. 256; and Vol. V, On Modesty, ch. 7, no. 53.

All these metaphors are taken from athletes already engaging one another, or joining position, foot, and hand. In *On the Prescription against Heretics*: “I now yield this position voluntarily.” That is: “Not driven back or overcome by arguments, I surrender my right; I grant my opponent what it would be easy for me to defend and have recourse to firmer reasons.”

### 62. To meet at the same position. Vol. III, Book IV against Marcion, ch. 29, no. 492.

Taken from gladiators, who, when about to contend with an opponent, plant their feet; retreating in order to evade blows, they advance again, take up the same position, and resist as they meet him. It will mean to employ the same argument and weapon.

### 63. To exclude from one’s position, to drive out, to drive from one’s position. Vol. III, On the Soul, ch. 6, no. 82; Book I against Marcion, ch. 13, no. 71; and Book IV against Marcion, ch. 9, no. 104.

In *On the Soul*: “I shall have excluded the position of this definition”; that is: “I shall have shown that the definition cannot stand, nor that the thing and its definition can travel back and forth together,” which is a fundamental defect in the method of defining a thing. “We drive God from this position”; that is, when we take away from God this power, prerogative, and capacity [Col. 0225A] to create. “I shall drive you from your position”; that is, “I shall convict you of falsehood and weaken your arguments.” All these expressions, taken from wrestlers, signify refuting one’s opponent and overcoming him in the contest of disputation.

### 64. To be cast down or moved from one’s position. Vol. III, On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. 2, no. 18.

In Cicero, *On Duties*, Book I, “to be cast down from one’s position” means to become dismayed in spirit, to be displaced, as it were, from one’s state of mind, and not to use the counsel of a mind present to itself. Pertinent here are “to be moved from one’s place” and “to be driven from one’s position.” Their contraries are “to be restored to one’s position” and “to be returned to one’s place.” Tertullian: “Cast down or moved from the position of that hope,” that is, one who has now abandoned the hope upon which he rested, having been induced to do so by the persuasion of others.

[Col. 0225B] ### 65. To enter upon another position. Vol. III, Book IV against Marcion, ch. 6, no. 52.

It means to attempt another course and enter upon a new contest, or at least to do so in a new manner; or, after one matter has already been disproved and undermined, to prepare oneself to overthrow another.

### 66. To bring one’s position to bear. Vol. I, Against the Jews, ch. 2, no. 6, and ch. 7, no. 27; and Book III against Marcion, ch. 2, no. 4.

A military phrase, like “to fight foot to foot”; “to bring hand or foot to bear”; “to bring one’s position to bear against the enemy.” Κατἀρχεσθαι τῆς μάχης (“To begin the battle”). It means to inaugurate the contest of disputation and approach the matter more closely. Here ἐγγὺς ἴοντες (“approaching closely”), “having engaged at close quarters.”
