First, I shall not concede that the soul is capable of forgetfulness, because Plato has granted it such divinity that it is made equal to God. He makes it unbegotten—a point which alone I could have marshaled as evidence of complete divinity. He adds that it is immortal, incorruptible, incorporeal—because he believed God also to be such—invisible, incapable of being represented, uniform, sovereign, rational, and intellectual. What more could he have asserted of the soul if he had called it God?

We, however, who attach nothing to God as an appendage, by this very fact assess the soul as far beneath God: we acknowledge that it was born and is therefore of a more diluted divinity and a more meager felicity; that it is breath, not spirit; and that, even if it is immortal, as belonging to divinity, it is [Col. 0687C] nevertheless capable of suffering, as belonging to birth. Hence it was capable of deviation from the beginning, and consequently also liable to forgetfulness. Enough has been said about this in the controversy with [Col. 0688A] Hermogenes.

[Note: [Col. 0688C] **Enough has been said about this in the controversy with Hermogenes.** He alludes to the book *On the Origin of the Soul against Hermogenes*, fragments of which survive. PAM.]

Moreover, a soul that might deservedly be regarded as God through the complete equality of all its attributes will be subject to no passion, and thus not to forgetfulness either, since forgetfulness is as great an injury as the glory of that to which it does injury—namely, memory. Plato himself called memory the preservation of sense-perceptions and intellectual conceptions, and Cicero proclaimed it the treasury of every branch of learning.

[Note: [Col. 0687C] “God” added here by Fran.]

[Note: [Col. 0688C] “Of memory”: Jun.]

[Note: [Col. 0688D] **Cicero proclaimed it the treasury of every branch of learning.** In Book I of *On the Orator* he calls memory “the treasury of all things.” See also Cassiodorus, *Variae* V, epistle 22, and *On the Soul*, chapter 12. LAC.]

The question will no longer be whether so divine a soul could have lost its memory, but whether, after losing it, it can recover it again. For if it has forgotten what it ought not to have forgotten, I do not know whether it is capable of recollecting it. Thus both capacities will suit my soul, but not Plato’s.

At the second stage I shall object: you make the soul naturally possessed of those Ideas, do you not? “Indeed, naturally,” you say. No one, therefore, will concede that a natural knowledge of natural arts can perish. [Col. 0688B] Knowledge of studies may perish; knowledge of teachings and disciplines may perish; perhaps even talents and dispositions may perish. These appear to belong to nature, but they do not, because, as we stated beforehand, they are constituted by accidents according to places, forms of education, bodily constitutions and states of health, ruling powers, and freedoms of choice. But knowledge of natural things does not fail even in beasts.

Certainly a lion will forget its ferocity once forestalled by training in gentleness, and, with the full display of its mane, will become the pet of some Queen Berenice, licking the blemishes from her cheeks with its tongue: its habits will forsake the beast, but its knowledge of natural things will remain.

[Note: [Col. 0688D] **With the full display of its mane.** That is, with all its ornament. Perhaps he is alluding to the celestial sign of Leo, at whose tail are seven stars which astrologers call Berenice’s locks or hair. LAC.—**With the full display of its mane.** The pet of Berenice. We have noted more than once that the Author uses *suggestus* for ornament. Aelian, *On Animals* V.39, relates that a lion trained to gentleness became the pet of a certain Queen Berenice. He says “a certain” both in accordance with his usual manner of speaking and because there were several queens of that name. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0688C] “Trumpets” (*tubarum*): Fran.]

It will not forget its natural food, its natural remedies, or its natural fears. Even if the queen offers it fish and cakes, it will desire meat; [Col. 0688C] even if she compounds an antidote for it when it is sick, the lion will seek an ape; and even if no hunting spear withstands it, it will nevertheless fear a cock.

[Note: [Col. 0688D] **If she compounds an antidote for it when it is sick.** He alludes to the words of Pliny, Book VII, chapter 40: “It experiences only the sickness of loss of appetite, in which it is treated by affront, through the wantonness of apes tied to it driving it into a rage.” PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0688D] **It will nevertheless fear a cock.** This too is from Pliny in the same place: “And yet,” he says, “this animal, of such a kind and so savage, is terrified by wheels spun round, by empty chariots, by the crests of barnyard fowl, and still more by their crowing.” PAM.]

[Col. 0689A] Likewise, in the human being—perhaps the most forgetful of all creatures—the knowledge of natural things alone will persist unerased, precisely because it alone is natural: one always remembers that in hunger one must eat, in thirst one must drink, that one must see with the eyes, hear with the ears, smell with the nostrils, taste with the mouth, and touch with the hand. These, surely, are the senses which philosophy depreciates by giving preference to intellectual things. Therefore, if the natural knowledge of sensible things remains, how does the knowledge of intellectual things, which is regarded as superior, perish?

Whence, then, comes that power of forgetfulness which precedes recollection? “From the great length of time,” he says.

[Note: [Col. 0689D] **From the great length of time, he says.** He alludes to those words of Plato in the *Phaedo*: “Is it not recollection, especially when this happens to someone concerning things which, because of the passage of time and because he has not seen them for a long while…?” The Author rendered this “from the great length of time.” But the Author beautifully refutes even this on the ground that Plato called the soul unbegotten and therefore eternal, without any beginning in time. PAM.]

That is most improvidently said. For a quantity of time will have no bearing upon a thing said to be unbegotten and therefore believed, above all, to be eternal. For what is eternal, precisely because it is also unbegotten, admits neither a beginning nor an end of time and suffers no measure of time. That for which there is no measure of time is neither subject to time through any change nor exposed to that force arising from a great length of time. [Col. 0689B]

[Note: [Col. 0689C] “Of time” (*temporis*): Pam., Rhen.]

[Note: [Col. 0689C] “Nor is the force the same through a great length of time”: Pam., Rhen.; “in the multitude”: Lat.]

If time is the cause of forgetfulness, why does memory fail from the moment when the soul is introduced into the body, as though only from that point onward did the soul endure time, although it unquestionably existed before the body and certainly did not exist without time? Once it has entered the body, does it forget immediately or some time later? If immediately, what great length of time can there be when time has not yet begun to be reckoned? Infancy, presumably. If some time later, then during that interval before the time of forgetfulness the soul will still act with memory. And what sort of thing is it that it should later forget and later still recollect once again?

At whatever time forgetfulness assails it, how great a measure of time will even this be regarded as? The whole course of life, I think, will not be enough [Col. 0689C] to overthrow the memory of so great an age before the body.

[Note: [Col. 0689D] “To turn” rather than “to overturn”: Pam., Rhen., Seml., Oberth.]

But Plato shifts the cause once more to the body—as though it were credible that a created substance could extinguish the power of an uncreated one. Yet bodies have great and numerous differences according to nation, size, condition, age, and health. Are differences in forgetfulness therefore also to be posited?

[Note: [Col. 0690C] “Forgetfulness” in the singular: Venet., incorrectly.]

But forgetfulness is uniform; therefore multiform corporeality will not be the cause of a uniform result.

Again, many proofs, as Plato himself bears witness, have established the soul’s power of divination—proofs which we have already set before Hermogenes. [Col. 0690A] Indeed, there is no human being who does not sometimes feel that his own soul is prescient, foretelling either an omen, a danger, or a joy. If the body does not obstruct divination, I think it will not hinder memory either. Certainly, in the very same body souls both forget and recollect.

If some property of the body induces forgetfulness, how will it admit forgetfulness’s contrary, recollection? For recollection after forgetfulness is itself a revived memory. Why does that which opposes the first memory not also resist the second?

[Note: [Col. 0690C] “Resist” (*refragetur*): Rhen., Pam.]

Finally, who ought to recollect more readily than little children, since their souls are more recent, not yet submerged in domestic and public cares, and devoted solely to those studies whose learning is supposed to constitute recollection? Indeed, why do we not all recollect equally, since we all forget equally, but philosophers alone do so? [Col. 0690B] Not even all of them do.

[Note: [Col. 0690C] “Not even they” (*nec ii*): Lat.]

Plato alone, it seems, in so great a forest of nations and so broad a meadow of sages, both forgot and recollected the Ideas.

[Note: [Col. 0689D] **Both forgot and recollected the Ideas.** The passage concerns Plato, who first brought dreams about the Ideas into the light, writing on that subject the dialogue entitled *Parmenides* and introducing extremely frequent mention of them elsewhere, as we have also noted above. PAM.]

Therefore, if this principal argument is in no way sound, the whole theory to which it was applied is overthrown along with it: namely, that souls should be believed unbegotten, to have lived in the heavens, to have been conscious there of divine things, to have been brought down from there, and to have recollected them here—plainly furnishing opportunities for the heretics.

## CHAPTER XXV

Now I shall return to the subject from which this digression arose, so that I may explain how souls issue in abundance from a single soul, when and where they are received, and by what principle. With respect to this question, it makes no difference whether it comes from a philosopher, a heretic, or the common people. For professors of the truth, [Col. 0690C] there is no difference among its adversaries, especially those as audacious as, first, the men who presume that the soul is not conceived in the womb, nor compacted and produced together with the molding of the flesh, but is impressed upon the infant from outside only after the child has been delivered and is not yet alive.

[Note: [Col. 0690D] **The soul is not conceived in the womb, etc.** Tertullian attributes this opinion to the Stoics; but Aëtius adds Diogenes and Herophilus, and even Empedocles, in his book *On the History of Philosophy*. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0690D] “And” added by Pam., Rhen.]

[Note: [Col. 0690D] **Is impressed upon it from outside.** Lucretius, Book III, presents the opinion of the Stoics, whom Tertullian presently adduces in support of this tenet:

> But if perhaps you think that, introduced from without,  
> The soul is accustomed to permeate our limbs.

They used the term “is impressed” or “is introduced” so as not to say that the soul is born; Lactantius, Book III, chapter 18. LAC.]

They hold, moreover, that the seed separated through intercourse in the female organs, and quickened by natural movement, thickens into the substance of the flesh alone; and that, once it has been brought forth, still smoking from the furnace of the womb and relaxed by heat, it is struck by the chill of the air just as glowing iron is when plunged into cold water, and so seizes the vital force and produces vocal sound.

[Note: [Col. 0690D] **And produces vocal sound.** Some thought that the soul, cast down from its celestial dwelling, then began to inhabit the earthly body, and that the infant was consequently forced to suffer pain and cry, as Alexander of Aphrodisias reports in *Problems* 64 while answering the question, “Why do infants cry as soon as they are born?” His solution is this: “Because their sensation and nature are, as it were, in a foreign land. For they pass from the warm and soft body of the womb itself into the cold air. Perhaps, however, they are also frightened by the light—not, as some say, because the soul, exiled from the celestial regions, inhabits an earthly body and infants are therefore forced to cry.” Soranus of Ephesus, *Introduction* 17, assigns a similar cause to an infant’s crying. LAC.]

[Col. 0691A] This is the view of the Stoics together with Aenesidemus, and sometimes of Plato himself, when he likewise says that the soul, otherwise alien and exiled from the womb, is brought in with the newborn infant’s first inhalation, just as it is led out with its final exhalation.

[Note: [Col. 0691C] “Is led” (*duci*): Fran.]

We shall see whether he fabricated this opinion.

[Note: [Col. 0691C] Rig. proposes that the text means “whether the opinion has fixed it.”]

Not even among physicians was Hicesius lacking—a traitor both to nature and to his own art.

[Note: [Col. 0691D] **Hicesius, a traitor both to nature and to his own art.** The physician Hicesius denied that the soul was conceived in the womb and said that it was impressed from outside upon the infant, not yet alive, only after the child had been delivered—namely, with its first inhalation. Septimius calls him a traitor to nature and to his own art: to nature, because pregnant women affirm that the fetus lives in the womb already endowed with a soul; and to his art, because the physicians themselves, whenever it is necessary to extract a child from the womb by a violent delivery, do not ordinarily dismember its limbs [Col. 0692C] before they have killed it—since it is already living and endowed with a soul. RIG.]

They were ashamed, I suppose, to establish something which women would recognize. Yet how much more shameful [Col. 0692D] the outcome—to be refuted by women rather than approved by them!

[Note: [Col. 0692D] **A more shameful outcome.** He calls an outcome “more shameful” when it occasions greater embarrassment and shame. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0691C] “More strongly confirmed” (*roboratior*): Pam., Rhen.]

For in this matter no teacher, judge, or witness is so competent as the sex itself. Answer, mothers, pregnant women, and women who have given birth; let barren women and men be silent. The truth of your nature is being investigated; the credibility of your experience is being summoned as witness. Do you perceive in the fetus some vitality distinct from your own, causing your groin to throb, your sides to quiver, the entire circuit of the belly to be struck, and the position of the burden to shift everywhere? [Col. 0691B]

[Note: [Col. 0692D] **The entire circuit of the belly.** Jerome uses the same meaning in his epistle to Principia: “The interior members of the Church are wrapped up, and the entire circuit of the womb is covered”—that is, the whole womb, the whole belly. RIG.]

Are these movements your joys and your firm assurance that you may thus trust the infant both to be alive and to be playing? If its restlessness ceases, do you not first fear for it? Does it not already hear within you when it is startled by a new sound? Do you not desire various foods for its sake and likewise reject them for its sake? Do you not share illnesses with it in turn?

[Note: [Col. 0691C] “And” omitted by others.]

[Note: [Col. 0691C] “Vanities” rather than “varieties”: Rhen., Pam., Rig.]

Indeed it does so to such an extent that, from your bruises, by which it too is marked inwardly upon the corresponding limbs, it takes to itself the injuries of its mother.

[Note: [Col. 0691C] “To such an extent” omitted by Lat.; “therefore” omitted by Rhen., Pam.]

If bruising and redness are a condition of the blood, there will be no blood without a soul. If health is an accession of the soul, there will be no health without a soul. If nourishment, hunger, growth, decline, fear, movement, and handling are activities of the soul, whoever performs these activities is alive.

[Note: [Col. 0691C] “Every” (*omnis*): Fran.]

[Col. 0691C] Finally, whoever ceases to perform them ceases to live. And finally, children are also born dead; how could they be, unless they had also been alive? And how could they be dead unless they had first been alive?

Indeed, while still in the womb the infant is sometimes slain by a necessary cruelty, when, lying crosswise at the outlet, it prevents delivery—destined to kill its mother unless it dies. [Col. 0692A] Accordingly, among the physicians’ instruments there is one by which the hidden passages are first forced open, with a twisting mechanism; then there is a ring-shaped knife by which the limbs within are severed under anxious judgment; then a blunt hook by which the whole victim is dragged out in a violent delivery.

[Note: [Col. 0692C] “Instruments” (*organa*): Fran.]

[Note: [Col. 0692C] “A knife-bearing ring” (*anulo cultrato*): Rhen., Pam.]

[Note: [Col. 0692C] “Animal” (*pecus*): Fran., Pam.; likewise, “is drawn out.”]

There is also a bronze spike by which the actual killing is performed in a blind act of violence. They call it the *embryospháktēs* (ἐμβρυοσφάκτην), “embryo-slayer,” from its function in infanticide—an instrument, surely, for destroying a living infant.

[Note: [Col. 0692C] Ἐμβρυονέκτην: Lat.; ἐμβρυορέκτην: Fran.; ἐμβρυοπακτην: others; ἐμβρυοσέκτην: Codex Agobardinus.]

Hippocrates possessed such an instrument, as did Asclepiades, Erasistratus, Herophilus—who dissected even adults—and even Soranus himself, though gentler than the others. They were certain that the conceived fetus was a living creature, and so pitied this most unhappy infancy in such a way that it should first be killed, lest it be torn apart alive.

Hicesius too, I believe, did not doubt the necessity of this crime, although he superimposed the soul upon children only after birth, as a result of the cold air’s impact, because the Greek word for soul was itself supposed to correspond to cooling. [Col. 0692B]

[Note: [Col. 0692C] “Corresponding” (*respondens*): Rhen., Pam.]

Are barbarian and Roman peoples, then, endowed with souls in some different way because they have given the soul a name other than *psychē* (ψυχή)? And how many nations live beneath the most burning part of the heavens, their very complexion baked by it? Whence do they receive a soul, since they experience no chill of air?

I pass over the heat of bedrooms and all the arrangements for warmth required there by women in labor, for whom exposure to a draft is especially dangerous. A fetus slips forth almost in the baths themselves, and its wailing is heard immediately. But if the chill of the air is the treasury of the soul, no one ought to be born outside Germany, the Alps, and Mount Argaeus.

[Note: [Col. 0692D] **The Alps and Mount Argaeus.** He joins Argaeus, or the Argaean mountains in Cappadocia, with the Alps because this mountain, like the Alps, is covered with perpetual snow. LE PR.]

And yet populations are more numerous in the eastern and southern climates, and their intellects more acute than those of all the Sarmatians, who are benumbed even in mind. [Col. 0692C] For minds also would emerge more intelligent from cold regions if souls originated from little chills: with the substance would come its power.

With these foundations thus laid, we may also call to mind those who, after their mother’s womb had been cut open, drew breath alive—certain Libers and Scipios. [Col. 0693A]

[Note: [Col. 0692D] **Certain Libers and Scipios.** The elder Scipio Africanus was born after the womb of his mother Pomponia had been cut open. Liber too is said in the fable to have been cut out after being conceived from Jupiter’s seed, although some explain the statement as referring to a Roman clan or family named Liber. Everyone thinks the Caesars were likewise named from the cutting open of the mother’s womb, although Pliny says that before the family of the Caesars there was a Claudius Caesar prior to the Samnite War. Those born after the mother’s womb had been cut open, like Scipio, were also called *Caesones*. LE PR.]

But if someone, like Plato, likewise does not think that two souls can come together in one being, just as neither can two bodies, I would have shown him not only two souls gathered together, just as bodies are in a multiple pregnancy, but many other beings joined to a soul as well: demons, for example—not merely one, as in Socrates himself, but seven spirits, as in Magdalene, and a legionary number, as in the Gerasene. Thus one soul may all the more readily be joined to another soul because of their community of substance, when evil spirits can be joined to it despite the difference of their nature.

[Note: [Col. 0693D] **As Plato likewise does not think, etc.** Plato affirmed that each body contains one single, simple soul and did not admit two souls simultaneously in the same human being. Tertullian attributed the latter position above to Chrysippus. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0693C] “Should be reckoned” (*censeretur*): Seml., Rhen., Oberth., Codex Ursini.]

Yet in the sixth book of the *Laws*, when warning that one must take care lest a corruption of the seed arising from some baseness in intercourse attach a stain to the body and soul, I do not know whether Plato forgot his former opinion or this present one.

[Note: [Col. 0693C] “Former” in the singular: Rhen., Pam.]

For he shows that the soul is conveyed through the seed, which he warns must be cared for, rather than through the newborn’s first inhalation. [Col. 0693B]

Why, I ask you, do we also resemble our parents in soul with respect to our dispositions, according to the testimony of Cleanthes, if we are not produced from the soul’s seed? And why did the ancient astrologers arrange a person’s nativity from the beginning of conception, if the soul also does not exist from that point—the soul to which equally belongs whatever may pertain to its condition?

[Note: [Col. 0693C] “Condition” (*status*): Codex Wouw.]

## CHAPTER XXVI

But all the inconsistency of human opinion extends only as far as the boundaries established by God. I shall now gather my step within our own lines, so that what I answered to philosophers and physicians I may prove to a Christian.

[Note: [Col. 0693D] **But all the inconsistency of human opinion extends only as far as the boundaries established by God.** As though he were saying: “But let human opinions concerning the soul have remained inconsistent, uncertain, and inconstant only until the divine testimonies.” RIG.]

Build your faith, brother, upon your own foundation.

[Note: [Col. 0693D] **Build your faith, brother, upon your own foundation.** He now addresses a Christian, whose foundation he calls Sacred Scripture; he supplies him with the testimony of Sacred Scripture as a foundation. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0693C] “Let me build” (*aedificem*): Pam., Rhen., Gagnae.]

Look upon the living wombs of the most holy women: not merely were infants already breathing within them, but they were also prophesying.

[Note: [Col. 0693D] **Look upon the living wombs.** Galen too draws an argument from the same proof, though within his own boundaries, confirming from pagan examples that the fetus is alive in the womb, in the book he wrote entitled *Whether What Is in the Womb Is a Living Creature*. LAC.]

[Col. 0693C] Behold, the womb of Rebecca is disturbed (Gen. 25), though birth is still far off and there has been no impact of air.

[Note: [Col. 0693D] **Behold the womb of Rebecca.** He treats at length the history in Genesis 25 concerning Rebecca and her two sons, both while they were in the womb and during birth; unless they had already been endowed with souls, they could not have begun those struggles. PAM.]

Behold, twin fetuses riot in their mother’s recesses, though the two peoples are nowhere yet to be seen. Perhaps this was the monstrous impudence of infancy, contending before it lived, high-spirited before it possessed a soul—if it had merely disturbed its mother by leaping. [Col. 0694A] But when the birth passage is opened, their number is observed, and the prophecy is recognized, I think that not merely the infants’ souls but their very struggles are now established.

The one who had been first to be born was held back by the one whom he had forestalled, though that second child had not yet fully emerged and only his hand had been born. And if the first drank in his soul with his first inhalation, in the Platonic manner, or seized it from the chill of the air in Stoic fashion, what of the other, who was still awaited, was still being held within, and yet was already holding the one outside?

[Note: [Col. 0694D] **Drank in his soul with his first inhalation, in the Platonic manner.** Others read “carried.” He alludes to the statements in the preceding chapter, where he charged Plato with holding that the soul is first drawn in through breathing. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0694C] “Breathing” (*spiratione*): Pam., Rhen.]

[Note: [Col. 0694D] **Or seized it from the chill of the air in Stoic fashion.** Here too he recalls what he attributed to the Stoics in the preceding chapter: that after birth a child seizes the soul when struck by the chill of the air. LAC.]

Not yet breathing, I suppose, he had seized his brother’s heel; still warm with his mother’s heat, he desired to emerge first. O infant, at once rivalrous, strong, and already contentious! I believe it was because he was alive.

Look also at the single conceptions in Luke 1—indeed, ones still more portentous: that of a barren woman and that of a virgin. By this very fact they might have produced imperfect offspring because of nature’s overthrow, since the one was insensible to seed and the other untouched. [Col. 0694B]

[Note: [Col. 0694D] **Look also at the singular conceptions.** Here likewise are the conceptions of John the Baptist and Christ, and the mutual movements of the infants while still in the womb, concerning whom Luke speaks in chapter 1. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0694C] “Seed” in the genitive: Pam., Fran.]

If ever there were reason for it, those improperly conceived ought to have been born without souls; but they too live, each in his own womb. Elizabeth exults: John had stirred her from within. Mary glorifies the Lord: Christ had impelled her from within. The mothers recognize one another’s fetuses, having themselves been mutually recognized by those who were surely alive—who were not only souls but also spirits.

Thus you also read the voice of God to Jeremiah (Jer. 1): “Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you.”

[Note: [Col. 0694D] **Thus also to Jeremiah, etc.** To prove the same matter he adduces the testimony of the prophet Jeremiah from chapter 1, comparing it with the frequently cited passage in Genesis 1. PAM.]

If God forms a person in the womb, he also breathes into him according to the pattern of the beginning (Gen. 1): “And God formed the human being and breathed into him the breath of life.” God, moreover, would not have known the human being in the womb unless he were whole. “And before you came forth from the womb, I sanctified you.” Did he sanctify a body still dead? Certainly not, for God is the God of the living, not of the dead (Matt. 22). [Col. 0694C]

[Note: [Col. 0694C] The editions suggest that “perhaps” (*forte*) should be read as “if” (*si*).]

## CHAPTER XXVII

How, then, is a living creature conceived? Are the two substances, body and soul, formed together, or does one precede the other? We say, rather, that both are conceived, formed, and perfected simultaneously, just as they are also brought forth, and that no intervening moment occurs in conception by which an order of position might be assigned. [Col. 0695A]

[Note: [Col. 0694D] **Both are conceived, formed, and perfected simultaneously, etc.** Here, and more fully in chapter 36, Tertullian unfolds his paradox, which he had suggested above in chapters 9 and 22, concerning the propagation of souls together with the seed by transmission from Adam. Rufinus forces Lactantius into the same opinion, though Rufinus himself remains doubtful. His position is summarized from Jerome, *Apology against Rufinus* II. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0695C] **And no intervening moment occurs in conception, etc.** That is, in conception no order of existence can be established between soul and body, nor can it be affirmed whether the soul existed first or not, whether it takes the first or second place, whether it precedes the body or follows it. Tertullian himself says in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*: “Flesh and soul come into being simultaneously, without a calculation of time; they are blended in the fetus and are born at the same age.” RIG.]

Reconsider first things from last things. If death is determined to be nothing other than the separation of body and soul, life, the opposite of death, will be defined as nothing other than the conjunction of body and soul.

[Note: [Col. 0695D] **If death is determined to be nothing else.** This argument does not establish that souls are sown together with the body, but only that they are conjoined with it—something that can occur through creation. Tertullian treats this argument again in chapter 42. LAC.]

If separation happens simultaneously to both substances through death, the corresponding form of conjunction must have prescribed that life likewise comes simultaneously to both substances.

Furthermore, we acknowledge life from conception because we claim the soul from conception.

[Note: [Col. 0695D] **We acknowledge life from conception because we claim the soul, etc.** Pamelius comments that this is utterly false, because according to the opinion of physicists and physicians a male is not endowed with a soul until the fortieth day, and a female not until the eightieth. Yet Tertullian is not so far wrong that many have not believed his view to be true, since they did not believe nature so slow and sluggish in bestowing the benefit of life. LAC.—The opinion of Tertullian has been embraced, after St. Basil and Congiamila, by most recent writers, among them P. J. C. D. Breyne, in the work entitled *Essay on Moral Theology Considered in Its Relations with Physiology and Medicine*. EDD.]

For life begins at the point at which the soul does. Those things, therefore, which are separated together in death are compacted together into life.

[Note: [Col. 0695C] “Then” is absent from Par.]

If, then, we grant primacy to one and second place to the other, the times of their seeds must also be distinguished according to their ordered status: when will the seed of the body be implanted, [Col. 0695B] and when that of the soul? Indeed, if the times of the seeds are divided, their materials too will be regarded as different because of that distance in time.

For although we shall acknowledge two kinds of seed, bodily and psychic, we nevertheless maintain that they are undivided, and in this sense contemporaneous and belonging to the same moment.

Let us not, therefore, be ashamed of an interpretation demanded by necessity. Nature is to be revered, not blushed at. Lust, not the institution itself, has defiled sexual intercourse.

[Note: [Col. 0695D] **Lust, not the institution itself, has defiled sexual intercourse.** Septimius does not always remain faithful to this position, although it is entirely true. Accordingly, in his hatred of marriage he sometimes seems to betray it. Theano, the wife of Pythagoras, used to say that a woman who had intercourse with a man, provided he was her own husband, was pure immediately afterward; but if he belonged to another, she would never be pure. RIG.—**Lust, not the institution itself, has defiled sexual intercourse.** A most beautiful passage concerning the state of marriage blessed before God, which is treated more fully near the end of Book I *Against Marcion*. PAM.]

Excess, not the state itself, is immodest. For the state is blessed before God (Gen. 1): “Increase and multiply.” But excess is accursed: adulteries, debaucheries, and brothels.

Accordingly, in this solemn function of the sexes which joins male and female—in ordinary intercourse, I mean—we know that both soul and flesh act together: the soul through desire, the flesh through performance; the soul through impulse, the flesh through action. Therefore, by the single impulse of both, when the whole human being has been shaken, the seed of the whole human being foams forth, having moisture from the bodily substance and heat from the psychic substance. [Col. 0695C–0696A]

[Note: [Col. 0696C] **The seed of the whole human being foams forth.** This metaphor, derived from foam, is not uncommon among philosophers and physicians. Aristotle, *Generation of Animals* II.2: οἷον δὲ καὶ ὁ ἀφρὸς γίνεται παχύτερος καὶ λευκός, “just as foam also becomes thicker and white.” According to Plutarch, *Opinions of the Philosophers* V.3, Pythagoras said: ἀφρὸν χρηστοτάτου τοῦ αἵματος, “that it is the foam of the most serviceable blood.” I omit Galen, *On the Usefulness of the Parts* XIV.9. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0695C] “Foams forth into seed”: Fran., Pam.]

And if the Greek name for soul is a word denoting cold, why does the body grow cold when the soul has been removed?

[Note: [Col. 0696D] **The Greek name for soul is a word denoting cold.** Tertullian evidently derives the Greek word ψυχή, “soul,” from ψῦχος, which means “cold” in Latin. EDD.]

Finally—though I risk modesty here more than proof—in that final heat of pleasure in which the generative fluid is expelled, do we not feel something from the soul also departing, so that we languish and lose vigor, with a diminution of our light?

[Note: [Col. 0696D] **In that final heat of pleasure.** Augustine, *City of God* XIV.16: “At the very moment when its culmination is reached, nearly all acuteness and, as it were, wakefulness of thought is overwhelmed.” RIG.]

This, accordingly, will be the psychic seed, arising from the soul’s distillation, just as that bodily fluid is seed arising from the purification of the flesh.

[Note: [Col. 0696D] **From the soul’s distillation.** The same appears to be the opinion of Epicurus, which we transcribed a little above. Tertullian seems to agree with those who thought that seed is drawn from every part of a living being. St. Macarius likewise holds this regarding bodily components, as did some of the ancient philosophers. LAC.]

The examples furnished by the beginning are entirely trustworthy. In Adam the flesh came from clay: what else is clay but a rich liquid? From this will come the generative fluid. The soul came from God’s breath: what else is God’s breath but the vapor of spirit? From this will come that which we breathe out through that fluid.

Thus, when at the beginning two diverse and separate things, clay and breath, had combined to form one human being, both substances, now mingled in one, also mixed their respective seeds and thereafter transmitted the pattern for propagating the race. Consequently, the two, although diverse, now flow out united and together; and, being implanted together in their own furrow and field, they together cause a human being to sprout from both substances. In that human being, in turn, his own seed is present according to kind, just as has been prescribed for every condition capable of generation. [Col. 0696B]

Thus this whole abundance of souls proceeds from one human being, while nature observes God’s decree: “Increase and multiply.”

[Note: [Col. 0696C] “Proceeds” is absent from Par.]

For even in the very preface to this single work of creation (Gen. 1), “Let us make the human being,” all posterity was announced in the plural: “And let them have dominion over the fish of the sea.” No wonder: the promise of the harvest is contained within the seed. [Col. 0696C]
