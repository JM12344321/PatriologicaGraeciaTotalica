## CHAPTER XXII

**ARGUMENT.—**He now explains the nature of demons, knowledge of whom, he teaches, was imparted even by philosophers and poets. He next expounds their spiritual substance and thence deduces their manner of acting upon men so as to harm them. It is shown that by its deceits this activity aims solely to make credulous mortals abandon the true God and become enslaved to false religion.

Accordingly, we say that there are certain spiritual substances; [Col. 0405A] nor is the name new. Philosophers know demons, since Socrates himself waited upon the judgment of his daemon. Why not? For a daemon is said to have attached itself to him from childhood—plainly one that discouraged him from the good. [Note: [Col. 0405B] “Discouraging. Plainly from the good.” Previously the passage was everywhere read and punctuated thus: “is said. All poets know that Orpheus discourages from the good.” But Rigault rightly emended it and placed a comma after “is said”; we also place a full stop after “discouraging.” For that genius or daemon of Socrates was celebrated as discouraging him if some evil threatened or confronted him; indeed, Tertullian says mockingly, “from the good,” namely as turning him away from it. I therefore punctuate thus: “is said to have been discouraging. Plainly—from the good”; that is, he certainly had a daemon, but one that discouraged him from the good. Thus “[non-Latin text omitted]” frequently means “plainly,” and in *To the Nations* I.10: “Plainly, since heaven lies open to them,” that is, “indeed, rightly.” Thus Lactantius aptly says, Book I, ch. 14: “But although they are themselves destroyers of men, they nevertheless wish to appear their guardians, so that they themselves may be worshiped and God may not be worshiped.” The passage might also be punctuated: “is said to have been discouraging. Plainly from the good.” But however the punctuation is arranged, no other meaning is possible than the one we have given. Caspar Barth, certainly misled from the truth by bad punctuation, goes astray; after considering these words in his *Adversaria*, Book V, ch. 5, he wishes the reading to be, “The poets know that Orpheus discourages from the good,” so that those poems and hymns, which survive even today under his name and are quite ancient, might thus be indicated. Spiritual powers are capable of much, so that, being invisible and imperceptible, they appear in their effect rather than in their own action. It is just as when some hidden corruption in the air brings fruit or crops prematurely to the ground in blossom, kills them in the bud, or wounds them in their maturity; or as when the air, attacked by some unseen cause, pours out its pestilential breaths. By the same obscurity of contagion, therefore, the breath of demons and angels also produces corruptions of the mind through frenzies and foul madnesses, or savage lusts accompanied by manifold errors.
