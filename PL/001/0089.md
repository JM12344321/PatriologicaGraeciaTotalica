Although the Gentile philosophers, either by natural reason or from the sacred Scriptures, as Tertullian says, recognized—or could without much difficulty have recognized—that there is only one God (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47), they nevertheless never ceased fighting among themselves, whether through the vice of vainglory or a passion for disputation, over the very nature, character, and dwelling-place of God. “For what certain answer,” [Col. 0816C] in the words of our author, “did Thales, that chief of the natural philosophers, give to Croesus when he questioned him about the Divinity? None at all; rather, though repeatedly granted time for deliberation, he disappointed him” (*Ibid.*, ch. 46; and bk. II *Ad Nationes*, ch. 2). But Cicero, and Minucius Felix, who, as we have seen elsewhere, follows him, attributed this to Simonides and the tyrant Hiero (*Dissertat. in Minut.*, ch. 3, art. 2). Nor should you reply that some corruption underlies Tertullian’s text. For he calls Thales the chief of the natural philosophers (*Ibid.*, ch. 4, art. 1, vol. I; *Apparat.*, p. 1119 ff.), that is, the first of those who, as we have also shown, instituted disputations on natural subjects. To determine, however, whether Tertullian’s opinion is truer than that of Cicero and Minucius, the respective ages of Thales, Simonides, and Croesus must be investigated. [Col. 0816D] According to Eusebius’ *Chronicle*, in the year 1266 Thales of Miletus is recognized as a natural philosopher. Under the year 1465, however, “the events related of Croesus—how he tested the oracles—occurred at this time.” Two years afterward, Thales dies. Thus, as Scaliger observes, two men named Thales are recorded: the earlier, a natural philosopher in the age of Lycurgus and Orpheus; the later, said to have flourished almost two hundred years afterward, in the time of Croesus (Scaliger, notes on Eusebius’ *Chronicle*, p. 74). Yet Tertullian speaks of Thales, the chief of the natural philosophers, who, being two centuries older than Croesus, could not have lived at the same time as he did and consequently could not have answered him.

Simonides, however, is said in the same Eusebian *Chronicle* to have lived in the year 1531. For there we read: “Pindar [Col. 0817A] and Simonides are regarded as distinguished lyric poets.” Later, under the year 1542, Hiero, whom Minucius Felix mentions, exercised the tyranny at Syracuse after Gelon. Thus, by as much as the earlier Thales is separated in time from Croesus, Simonides is said to have been close in time to Hiero. The cited question and answer concerning the Divinity therefore fit Simonides better than Thales, the chief of the natural philosophers. Moreover, Minucius’ opinion seems still more probable because it is supported by Cicero’s authority.

Nor should you say that Eusebius’ *Chronicle* contains many errors in assigning the dates of illustrious men; for whatever those errors may be, no one will deny that the times of Thales the natural philosopher and Croesus are farther separated from one another than would permit them to be said to have lived together. Nor is there any reason [Col. 0817B] for you to insist that the recorded answer properly suits Thales, a philosopher and chief of the natural philosophers, but by no means Simonides, who, as we have seen elsewhere, devoted himself to poetry (*Dissertat. in Minuc. Octav.*, ch. 3, art. 2; and vol. I, *Apparat.*, p. 1284). For among the Gentiles poets were counted among theologians.

Finally, relying upon Cicero’s testimony, Minucius could correct Tertullian’s opinion concerning the first Thales and Croesus, even though, as we have observed, he excerpted many things from Tertullian’s books. And indeed, how could this Thales, when questioned about the Divinity, have dared give no answer, when—as we have noted elsewhere, following the same Cicero—he taught, as confidently as falsely, that water was the beginning of all things, and that God was the mind that formed all things from water (Cicero, bk. I *De Natura* [Col. 0817C] *Deorum*, p. 199; *Dissertat. in Minut.*, ch. 4, art. 1; and *in Lactant.*, ch. 12, art. 1)? No one has shown us that he ever doubted this opinion or ever changed it. Furthermore, if anyone proves that this answer about God should be attributed to the second Thales, he will show that these words must be deleted from Tertullian’s text: “chief of the natural philosophers.” But whoever the philosopher was who gave that answer, who will call into doubt that he asserted it? He therefore openly confessed that the Divinity was unknown to him.

In the second book *Ad Nationes*, however, Tertullian placed Diogenes before Thales. When asked what happens in heaven—these are Tertullian’s own words—he said: “I have never ascended into heaven” (Tertullian, bk. II *Ad Nationes*, ch. 2). Likewise, when asked whether gods existed, he answered: “I do not know, [Col. 0817D] except that it is expedient that they should”; that is: I do not know, but it is useful and expedient for everyone that gods should exist. This is not very far removed from the former answer, which Laertius reports was given by Diogenes the Cynic to a man discoursing on celestial matters: Ποσταῖος πάρει ἀπὸ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ “When did you come down from heaven?” (Laertius, bk. VI, *Life of Diogenes*, §39). But consider, please, whether the following answer of the same Diogenes to Lysias the druggist, who asked whether he believed that gods existed, is farther removed from the second: Πῶς οὐ νομίζω, ὅπου καὶ σε θεοῖς ἐχθρὸν υπολαμβάνω “How could I not believe it, when I regard you as an enemy of the gods?” (*Ibid.*, §42). Laertius nevertheless warns us in the same place that this saying is attributed to Theodorus, who was undoubtedly surnamed “the atheist,” as we have observed more than once. Yet if you contend that the statements which our Tertullian [Col. 0818A] ascribes to Diogenes were written by another man of the same name, but an atheist, we do indeed have Aelian, who, as some say, numbered Diogenes the Phrygian among this band of wicked men (Aelian, bk. II *Varia Historia*, §31). But besides the fact that our edition of Aelian’s books reads “Dionysius the Phrygian,” no one has indicated to us where this Diogenes, or Dionysius, uttered the words cited by our author.

There was, however, the greatest variety and disagreement among the other philosophers’ opinions about God. “Some,” says Tertullian—namely, the Platonists—“assert that God is incorporeal” (*Apologeticus*, ch. 47, p. 515). Would you like a witness to be produced? Here is Cicero, who introduces Velleius speaking thus about Plato: [Col. 0818B] “But as for his wish that God should be without any body, what the Greeks call ἀσώματον, it is impossible to understand what such a thing could be” (Cicero, bk. I *De Natura Deorum*, p. 200, l. 13). Laertius relates the same concerning this chief of the philosophers: “He holds,” he says, “that the soul, like God, is incorporeal ἀσώματον εἶναι” (Laertius, bk. III, *Life of Plato*, §77). Plutarch, Eusebius, Apuleius, and others agree with Laertius (Plutarch, bk. I *De Placitis Philosophorum*, ch. 7, p. 881; Eusebius, *Oration of Constantine*, p. 200; Plato, vol. III, pp. 27–28; and elsewhere Apuleius, *On God*).

However true this opinion may be, it was nevertheless rejected by other Gentile philosophers. For Velleius in Cicero, as we were just saying, maintained that it was impossible to understand what or of what kind that which is called incorporeal [Col. 0818C] might be (Socrates; Cicero, loc. cit.). Nor could the other philosophers explain what the true idea and notion of an incorporeal thing is.

“But the Stoics,” our author continues, “proclaimed that God is corporeal” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47 [the printed “515” appears corrupt]; vol. I, *Apparat.*, bk. III, diss. 1, ch. 11, art. 2; *Dissertat. in Minut.*, ch. 4, art. 10). We have discussed elsewhere this opinion of the Stoics, which, because of their contradictions, is not free of difficulties. We have also cited several authors who testify that they really did fall into this error.

Tertullian continues: “Some—namely Epicurus and his followers—chattered that God consists of atoms; others, such as Pythagoras, of numbers; others, like Heraclitus, of fire” (Tertullian, loc. cit., [Col. 0818D] vol. I, *Apparat.*, p. 435; *Dissertat. in Minut.*, loc. cit.; *Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 12, art. 1). All these men believed that these were the first principles of all things and that the gods were nothing other than these, as we have observed elsewhere more than once.

We have no less clearly proved that the philosophers held various and mutually opposed opinions concerning divine providence, as Tertullian calls it. “For the Platonists,” he says, “proclaimed that he takes care of things; the Epicureans, by contrast, that he is idle and inactive and, so to speak, a nonentity in relation to human affairs” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47; and bk. II *Ad Nationes*, ch. 2). If you desire more concerning Plato, go to Laertius, and you will hear him say: οἴεται καὶ θεοὺς ἐφορᾷν τὰ ἀνθρώπινα. [Col. 0819A] “He holds that the gods both observe and care for human affairs” (Laertius, bk. III, *Life of Plato*, §78). There is no need to cite many others, since Plato himself asserted this repeatedly (Plato, *Phaedo*, vol. I, p. 62, after the middle). For in one place he says that God cares for us no differently than for his own property and possession: Τὸ θεόν τε εῖναι τὸν ἐπιμελουμένον ἡμῶν, καὶ ἡμᾶς ἐκείνου κτήματα εἶναι (the same, bk. X *Laws*, p. 285). Elsewhere, however, he confidently asserts that the first madness is to deny the gods, and the second to assert that they have no care for men: Τὸ δεύτερον ὄντας οὐ φροντίζειν ἀνθρώπων (*Ibid.*, p. 899 ff.). But afterward he pursues this at greater length and refutes and overthrows that mad error (*Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 14, art. 1). Yet the error of Epicurus and his followers is utterly absurd, as we [Col. 0819B] have demonstrated.

For this reason our author added shortly afterward that the Platonists’ opinion concerning God’s dwelling-place was that he resides within the world, remaining, after the manner of a governor, within that which he governs (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47; and bk. II *Ad Nationes*, ch. 2). And indeed Plato determines and proves that this world is constantly governed according to a fixed and established order by a certain marvelous mind and wisdom, which is God himself (Plato, *Philebus*, vol. II, p. 28 ff.). In another of his books, however, we read that God, the governor of the universe, after the world and all things had been created, let go the reins by which it was governed and returned, as it were, to his watchtower (the same, *Politicus*, p. 272 ff.). But afterward, lest the world, buffeted by various storms and evils, [Col. 0819C] should be utterly dissolved, he resumed its helm and, taking anxious charge of it, arranged and governs the parts of that same world.

Against this opinion of the Platonists, however, the Stoics proclaimed, as Tertullian says, that God is situated outside the world and, like a potter, turns this mass from without (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47; and bk. II *Ad Nationes*, ch. 2). But there is considerable room for doubt as to whether this was in fact the Stoics’ position. For Zeno, their leader, and his followers, as may be learned from Laertius and Plutarch, believed God to be a mind that reaches and penetrates all things (Laertius, bk. VII, *Life of Zeno*, §§138, 147; Plutarch, bk. I *De Placitis Philosophorum*, ch. 7, p. 882). We have also observed elsewhere that this [Col. 0819D] really was their opinion. By what reasoning, then, could our author so confidently affirm that the Stoics taught that God, like a potter, is situated outside the entire mass of the world fashioned by him (vol. I, *Apparat.*, p. 780; Lipsius, bk. I *Psychologia Stoica*, diss. 31, p. 565, vol. IV)?

Lipsius cuts rather than unties the knot of this difficulty. For without hesitation he charges Tertullian here with the error of attributing to the Stoics something they had neither said nor even thought. Did Tertullian truly err, then? Was he ignorant of, or had it slipped his mind, what the Stoics taught concerning the divine mind permeating the whole world? Certainly not. For in another book he committed these words to writing: [Col. 0820A] “The Stoics hold that God coursed through matter as honey does through the combs” (Tertullian, *Adversus Hermogenem*, ch. 44). He should therefore have been charged with contradiction rather than forgetfulness or ignorance. But the contradictions of the Stoics were undoubtedly greater than Tertullian’s; Plutarch exposed them in entire books, and we have noted them elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 21, art. 2). Could this not therefore have been attributed to their contradictions rather than his?

But what if Seneca, himself a Stoic, suggests to us a way of removing this contradiction? For he speaks about God in this manner: “What is God? The mind of the universe. What is God? The whole of what you see and the whole of what you do not see. Only thus is his own greatness, than which nothing greater can be conceived, restored to him, if [Col. 0820B] he alone is. He embraces all his work, both within and without” (Seneca, preface to bk. I *Naturales Quaestiones*, p. 793). Does he not signify by these words that God is both within and outside the world, because he is greater than all his work? But if this was the Stoics’ opinion, it had two parts. What, then, if Tertullian mentioned only one part in order to oppose the Stoics to the Platonists?

It can nevertheless be objected that this same opinion is attributed by Seneca himself to the Epicureans, whom for that reason he sharply rebukes and assails: “You, Epicurus,” he said, “make God unarmed. . . . You have cast him beyond fear” (others prefer to read, “beyond the boundary”). “Thus you have him enclosed by some vast and inexplicable wall, and separated from contact with and sight of mortals; you have no reason to fear him. He has no means either of bestowing benefits [Col. 0820C] or of doing harm. Abandoned in the interval between this heaven and another, without living creature, without man, without anything, he escapes the wreckage of worlds falling above and around him, neither hearing prayers nor taking thought for us” (the same, bk. IV *De Beneficiis*, ch. 19, p. 79). In one of his letters too he rejects and condemns the same error of theirs in this way: “I am not speaking of that philosophy which placed the citizen outside his country, the gods outside the world, and surrendered virtue to pleasure” (the same, *Epistle* 79, p. 71), namely, Epicurean philosophy. He therefore testifies that this false opinion belonged not to the Stoics but to the Epicureans.

“Can it not, then,” someone will say, “be concluded from this testimony of Seneca that Tertullian’s text should be emended, and that ‘Epicureans’ should be written there in place of [Col. 0820D] ‘Stoics’?” Certainly—if that is proved by some sufficiently valid argument. But the Stoics did not have a single position concerning God; rather, as we have said, there was a very great variety of opinions. If some of them, such as the aforementioned Seneca, imagined God in some manner situated beyond the limits of the world, who will ever deny that this was sufficient for Tertullian? For he could omit the Epicureans, whom the Gentiles despised, and mention the Stoics. Nevertheless, whatever the genuine reading of Tertullian may be, the weight and force of his argument will remain the same. For from it, and from all that has been discussed thus far, it will be clearly apparent to anyone how deservedly he asserts that it was as difficult for all the philosophers to define the nature, character, and dwelling-place of God [Col. 0821A] as it was ready and easy for even a Christian of the lowest station to do so.

This is further confirmed by the authority of Plato, easily the chief of them all, who, as Tertullian himself recalls and as we have more than once observed, openly professed that “the maker of the universe is not easy to discover, and, once discovered, is difficult to describe to everyone” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46; Plato, *Timaeus*, p. 28). Consequently his followers, as our author says in another book, “could neither know nor fear him whom they had not fully accepted, nor therefore become wise,” since they strayed from the beginning of wisdom, that is, fear of God (vol. I, *Apparat.*, p. 948; *Dissertat. in Minut. Octav.*, ch. 4, art. 2). But any Christian discovers him and, in actual fact, also ascribes to him that which is sought in God (Tertullian, bk. II *Ad Nationes*, ch. 2): [Col. 0821B] namely, that he is one, omnipotent, infinite, the creator of the world, and such that nothing greater or better can be conceived. Therefore the Christian fears him and is far wiser than those who were wiser than the other philosophers.

Finally, no slight difficulty still attaches to those words of Tertullian by which he noted another disagreement among the philosophers concerning God as creator of the world: “This entire body of the world,” he says, “whether unbegotten and unmade according to Pythagoras, or begotten and made according to Plato” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 11, p. 332). For just as certain as it is that this was Plato’s opinion concerning the created world, as we have shown elsewhere, so false does it seem that Pythagoras taught that it was unbegotten and unmade. [Col. 0821C] For in that same place we observed that Lactantius emphatically declares that all philosophers down to Epicurus, and the Pythagoreans by name, consistently asserted that the world was made and created (*Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 21, art. 2). Indeed, Plutarch declares most explicitly that Pythagoras and Plato held the same opinion on this subject. Here are his Greek words: Πυθαγόρας καὶ Πλάτων γενητὸν ὑπὸ Θεοῦ τὸν κόσμον. “Pythagoras and Plato held that the world was made by God” (Plutarch, bk. II *De Placitis Philosophorum*, ch. 4, p. 886). What then? Did Tertullian err here too, and must it be said that either he or a copyist wrote the name Pythagoras in place of another? Or rather, because this celebrated philosopher is reported by the same Plutarch, Laertius, Theodoret, and others to have taught that unity is the [Col. 0821D] principle of all things and that all things come from numbers, did our author infer from this that the world, like unity and numbers, was neither begotten nor made (the same, *ibid.*, bk. II, ch. 3; Laertius, bk. VIII, §25; Theodoret, discourse 2 *On the Principle*, p. 489)? But if the matter stands thus, the authors cited by us will undoubtedly be convicted of error. Can it therefore be said more probably that Pythagoras, in the manner of men in error, did not remain of the same opinion? I would have you weigh these matters and pronounce judgment.

Far truer and more certain, therefore, is what our author taught: that the world was created by God from nothing, “by the word with which he commanded, by the reason with which he arranged it, by the power with which he was able” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 17, [Col. 0822A] and ch. 21). This was indeed repeated by him and transcribed by Minucius Felix, as we have observed elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Minut. Octav.*, ch. 2, art. 5). But let us now, if you please, examine carefully what Tertullian teaches concerning this Word of God.

## CHAPTER VIII. On the Divinity of Christ

### ARTICLE I. On the Various Names of the Son of God, and How Clearly Tertullian Teaches That the Attributes of the True and Supreme God Belong to Him, and That He Is One Eternal God with His Father

To make plain to everyone what the Word, Reason, Speech, and Son of God are, Tertullian [Col. 0822B] teaches that God and the Word, the Son of God, can each be considered in two ways. Both may be considered before God himself founded the world or any creature, or when the world had been created by him. Concerning both before the creation of the world, however, he establishes the following as entirely certain:

“Before all things God was alone, being himself his own world, place, and everything. He was alone, however, because there was nothing else external besides him. Yet not even then was he alone, for he had with him that which he had within himself, namely his own Reason. For God is rational, and Reason was first in him, and thus all things were from him. This Reason is his consciousness. The Greeks call it λόγον, a word by which we also designate Speech. And therefore, through the simplicity of translation, it is now customary among us to say that Speech was in the beginning with God, [Col. 0822C] although it is more fitting that Reason be regarded as prior; because God was not ‘speech-bearing’ from the beginning, but rational even before the beginning, and because Speech itself, since it consists in Reason, shows Reason to be prior as its substance. Nevertheless, even so, it makes no difference. For although God had not yet sent forth his Speech, he nonetheless possessed it within himself together with and in Reason itself, silently thinking and arranging with himself the things which he was soon to utter through Speech. For in thinking and arranging with his Reason, he made it into Speech, which he treated by means of Speech” (Tertullian, *Adversus Praxean*, ch. 5).

We have quoted this passage entire, though somewhat lengthy, because every part of it deserves singular attention and examination.

First, then, he declares that before the beginning, [Col. 0822D] that is, from all eternity and consequently before the world was created, God was both alone and not alone. Before the creation of the world he was alone, being himself his own world, place, and everything, because he had nothing external besides himself. Yet even then he was not alone, because he had his own Reason. For before the beginning, or from all eternity, he was rational, since God cannot be God unless he is endowed with consciousness and reason.

Second, that divine and eternal Reason of God, he says, the Greeks call λόγον, undoubtedly because John wrote at the beginning of his Gospel: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ Λόγος (John 1:1). For Tertullian himself cited these words in Latin thus: “In the beginning was the Speech . . . and the Speech was with God, and the Speech was God” (Tertullian, *Adversus Hermogenem*, [Col. 0823A] ch. 20). In his time, however, a custom had become established among Latin speakers of calling the Son of God *Sermo* (“Speech”), although it would have been better for them to render that Greek term λόγος as *Ratio* (“Reason”). We have discussed this translation where we showed that Lactantius transcribed this passage of Tertullian (*Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 16, art. 2).

Third, he advises us why the Greek term λόγος ought to be translated into Latin as “Reason” rather than “Speech.” God, he says, was rational before the beginning, but not yet “speech-bearing,” because he possessed Reason within himself but had not yet sent forth Speech outside himself. Yet because from eternity he possessed Speech within himself just as he possessed Reason, Tertullian granted everyone freedom to use either name, Reason or Speech, as one pleased. [Col. 0823B] The Son of God, however, is called by him not only Speech, Reason, and Word, but also *Sophia*, or Wisdom (Tertullian, *De Carne Christi*, ch. 18; *Adversus Hermogenem*, ch. 18).

Moreover, in his *Apologeticus*, and afterward Lactantius, as we have seen in its proper place, informed us that this Logos (λόγον) was recognized by Zeno, a Gentile philosopher, who proclaimed it to be the maker of the universe (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 21; *Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 16, art. 1). Our author adds: “Cleanthes gathers it into the Spirit, which he affirms to be the permeator of the universe” (Tertullian, loc. cit.; vol. I, *Apparat.*, p. 782; *Dissertat. in Minuc. Oct.*, ch. 4, art. 1; and *Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 12, art. 1). This Cleanthes was a disciple of Zeno, [Col. 0823C] as we have noted elsewhere, where we also explained what his opinion concerning God was, from which the account given by Tertullian appears to have been derived.

Now, from Tertullian’s statements concerning the Word, Speech, and Reason of God, explained in this manner, it can certainly be inferred that he was entirely persuaded that this interior Word of God is as eternal as his Father himself, who indeed brought him forth from eternity, though only within himself. The eternity of the divine Word must, however, be treated more fully below (below, art. 8 ff.). For first we must most carefully elucidate and expound our author’s true and genuine opinion concerning his supreme divinity.

First, then, he teaches that all the names and attributes of his eternal Father [Col. 0823D] belong to this divine Word and Son of God:

“The names of the Father—God Almighty, the Most High, Lord of Hosts, King of Israel, He Who Is—insofar as the Scriptures teach thus, we say also belonged to the Son, and that the Son came under these names, always acted under them, and thus manifested them in himself to men. ‘All things of the Father,’ he says, ‘are mine.’ Why not the names also? Therefore, when you read ‘God Almighty,’ ‘the Most High,’ ‘God of Hosts,’ ‘King of Israel,’ and ‘He Who Is,’ see whether the Son also is not indicated by these: Almighty in his own right, as the Speech of Almighty God and as he who received authority over all things; the Most High, as exalted at the right hand of God, as Peter proclaims in Acts; Lord of Hosts, because all things were subjected to him by the Father; King of Israel, because the lot of that nation fell peculiarly to him; likewise He Who Is, since [Col. 0824A] many are called sons who are not” (Tertullian, *Adversus Praxean*, ch. 17).

By what words, I ask you, could the supreme divinity of Christ, the Son of God, be declared and asserted more clearly? Who can possess all these attributes of the true and supreme God except the supreme God, one with the eternal Father? What of the fact that Tertullian not only affirms that each and every one of these belongs to the Speech and Son of God, but also proves it and endeavors to confirm it by various arguments? He was therefore persuaded that the Son of God is one and the same God with his Father.

Nor let some Arian or Socinian, whoever he may be, rise up and object that Tertullian does not correctly prove how those divine attributes belong to the Son of God. For we are not now investigating how strong and firm his arguments are—although this is very easy to prove—[Col. 0824B] but what opinion he wished to prove. Yet from his own words it is most evident that he and all the other orthodox Christians of his age held it as entirely certain that the Word and Son of God is true God, and that those who thought otherwise—such as Praxeas, against whom he disputes there—were altogether in error.

Accordingly, elsewhere he professes most explicitly that this Speech and Son of God is equal to his Father, and elsewhere that he is not less than him: “For,” he says, “making that Father equal to himself, from whom by proceeding he became the Son” (*Ibid.*, ch. 7). Later: “The same Son of God, by saying, ‘I and the Father are one,’ shows that there are two whom he makes equal and joins together” (*Ibid.*, ch. 22). Again, [Col. 0824C] in another book: “And the Speech, God, who, being established in the form of God, did not regard equality with God as robbery” (the same, *De Resurrectione Carnis*, ch. 6). You have heard how clearly he asserts that the Son of God is equal to his Father; now hear how he proclaims that he is not inferior: “He says that all things were delivered to him by the Father. You may believe this if Christ belongs to the Creator, to whom all things belong, because the Creator delivered all things to a Son not inferior to himself—the things he created through him, namely through his own Speech” (the same, *Adversus Marcionem*, ch. 25). Yet if the Son of God is not inferior but equal to his Father, he is undoubtedly true God with him. For he is also called by the same Tertullian, as we have just seen, the Most High and Almighty. Yet there cannot be two Most High and Almighty beings, just as there cannot be two gods. For [Col. 0824D] elsewhere, as we have noted, he had said that God is one precisely because he is supreme, and therefore, he says, “If God is not one, he is not God” (preceding chapter, art. 1).

But if anyone desires more, let him consult Tertullian’s *Apologeticus*, with which we are dealing. There, after a brief exposition of Christian doctrine concerning the Son of God, Christ the Lord, he addresses the Gentiles in these words:

“Investigate, therefore, whether this divinity of Christ is true. If it is such that, once it is known, a person is reformed toward the good, it follows that every other divinity discovered is false—above all that which” (our manuscript codices and the printed editions add “by every means,” which Rigault removed) “lurking beneath the names and images of dead men, establishes belief in its divinity by certain signs, miracles, and oracles” (the same, [Col. 0825A] *Apologeticus*, ch. 21).

In what way could the true and supreme divinity of Christ ever have been asserted and proclaimed more openly and confidently? For Tertullian openly asserts that it is as true as every other divinity recognized and introduced by the Gentiles is false. Indeed, he cries out that the divinity of Christ is so true that it follows from this that the divinity of all others is false, including that of the demons, who, lurking beneath the names and images of the dead, established belief in their divinity by signs, miracles, and oracles; that is, they tried to persuade everyone that these signs, miracles, and oracles were performed by their own divine power and that they themselves were true gods. Who, then, does not see that through this opposition between true and fictitious divinity, Tertullian declares the true and supreme divinity of Christ?

[Col. 0825B] Nor did he think it enough to say this there; shortly afterward he further addresses the same Gentiles with these words: “You cannot say that Christ is a man of the common condition” (*Ibid.*, ch. 23). For he will come at the end of the world “as the power of God, and the Spirit of God, and the Speech, and the Wisdom, and the Reason, and the Son of God.” Thus, openly and boldly, in the hearing and before the eyes of the fiercest enemies of the Christian name, he pronounces and testifies that Christ is the Speech, Wisdom, and Reason of his eternal Father, by which he is his Son from eternity. Accordingly, he explicitly affirms that in Christ there are two natures, of God and of man, and that he is as truly God as he is truly man.

### ARTICLE II. How Explicitly Tertullian Asserts That the Eternal Father and His Son Have One and the Same Substance and Nature, Although the Person of Each Is Distinct

[Col. 0825C]

Just as explicitly and consistently as Tertullian taught that the Son of God is one and the same God with his Father, so too he asserted that both have one and the same substance and nature, but distinct persons. For he said this not once but repeatedly, especially in his *Apologeticus*, where he discusses the generation of the Word, or Son of God, as follows:

“We have learned,” he says, “that this Logos (λόγον) was brought forth from God, and by being brought forth was begotten, and therefore was called the Son of God, and God, from the unity of substance. For God too is Spirit; and when a ray is extended from the sun, it is a portion from the whole, but the sun will be in the ray, because it is a ray of the sun, and the substance is not separated but extended. [Col. 0825D] So too, Spirit from Spirit, and God from God, as light kindled from light. The matrix of the substance remains whole and undiminished, even though you derive from it several offshoots of its qualities. Thus, what proceeded from God is God and the Son of God, and the two are one. Thus, as Spirit from Spirit and God from God, he was made second in number by mode, in degree but not in condition, and went forth from the matrix without withdrawing from it” (*Ibid.*, ch. 21).

Thus he declares simply and sincerely to the Gentiles what Christians believed concerning the nature and generation of the divine Word, and consequently of the Son of God. How sincere this explanation is becomes clear from the fact that, if he could have concealed or dissembled anything, he certainly would not have said it in a public book which he offered to all the Gentiles and even to the rulers of the Roman Empire. [Col. 0826A] But the truer and more certain this exposition is, the more carefully it must be examined by us.

First, then, he says: “We have learned that this was brought forth from God.” But whence, I ask, had he and the other Christians learned this? Surely from no other source than the sacred Scriptures and a sure and constant tradition from the Apostles down to his own time. Elsewhere he cites many passages of the same sacred Scripture on this matter, especially in his book against Praxeas, a supporter of the Patripassian heresy. There also he excellently teaches that this is “the rule of our faith, which has come down from the beginning of the Gospel and is older than any heretics.” “It is established,” he says, “that whatever is first is true, and whatever is later is false” (Tertullian, *Adversus Praxean*, ch. 2). Therefore certain [Col. 0826B] most foolish men wrongly advertised the claim that the doctrine concerning the Logos (λόγου) and the Son of God was unknown to the Apostles and their first disciples, and that only in the second century of the Church was it drawn from the opinions of the philosophers and received by Christians. For if any alteration had been made in a principal article of the Christian faith, it ought to have struck the mind of everyone, and especially that of Tertullian. That highly learned man flourished at the end of the second century and the beginning of the third, and therefore could not have been ignorant of how certainly this common doctrine of all had been transmitted from the Apostles down to his own time.

Second, he teaches that the divine Word is Spirit from Spirit, because it is God’s Word, Reason, and Speech (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 21). For, as he says elsewhere: “We understand Spirit under the name of Speech; so too we recognize Speech under the name of Spirit. For Spirit [Col. 0826C] is the substance of Speech, and Speech the operation of Spirit, and the two are one” (the same, *Adversus Praxean*, ch. 26). He therefore held that the Speech, or Word, and Son of God is spirit and a wholly spiritual reality, distinct from every kind of matter and from everything corporeal. This opinion of his is confirmed by the fact that in the same place he declares him to be Spirit from Spirit, that is, brought forth and begotten by the spirit and thought of his eternal Father (the same, *Apologeticus*, ch. 21). For the bringing-forth of divine thought not only admits nothing corporeal; he who is brought forth is also one God with his Father, by whom he is brought forth. Yet why need this be proved by us when Tertullian asserts it most explicitly in these very words: [Col. 0826D] “What proceeded from God is God and the Son of God, and the two are one, and God from God” (*Ibid.*)?

Third, Tertullian affirms that he is one God with God the Father by unity of substance, and consequently that the substance of both is one and the same (*Ibid.*). For the substance, he says, is not separated from his Father but extended, since “we have learned,” as he further expresses it, “that this was brought forth from God, and by being brought forth was begotten” (*Ibid.*). But because he thus proceeded from him and was begotten, he is distinguished from him in person, just as his substance is not separated from that of his Father. For the person of the Father and of the Son of God cannot be the same. Yet the substance of both is the same, since he is one God with the Father by unity of substance, from which [Col. 0827A] he is not separated but extended (*Ibid.*). For he did not withdraw from this substance, as from a matrix, but went forth from it; and thus the two are one and the same God.
