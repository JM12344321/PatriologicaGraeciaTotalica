### XXVIII

Furthermore, he attacks Plato’s rash falsehood, derived from Pythagoras, concerning **metempsychosis** (μετεμψύχωσις), that is, the recurrence of souls in a revolving cycle through the alternating replacement of the dead by the living.

### XXIX

For even if the dead are made from the living, it does not therefore follow that the living are made from the dead.

### XXX

Next, if the living always come from the dead, the number of human beings must always have been one and the same; whereas, on the contrary, we find that the human race has gradually increased beyond measure.

[Col. 0644C]

### XXXI

Even if this were so, individual souls would have to return into individual bodies—with the same age, the same training, dispositions, and skills, and from every nation, sex, and rank; yet Pythagoras himself did not admit this.

### XXXII

Empedocles, however, raved still more wildly, for he introduced **metensomatosis** (μετενσωμάτωσις), making beasts return from human beings and human beings from beasts.

### XXXIII

Nor, indeed, can this doctrine be defended under the name of judgment; for judgment is nullified if crimes are punished in no other way, or if no other reward is given to the good, than restoration into animal bodies.

### XXXIV

The similar opinion of Simon the Samaritan heretic concerning his Helena is struck down by the same rebuttal. He claimed that she had passed successively through one female form after another, [Col. 0644D] and that she had even been the woman most ruinous to Priam.

### XXXV

Much more does this apply to Carpocrates’ reincorporation of souls, by which he maintained that a soul is recalled into bodies as many times as it has failed to make full satisfaction for its offenses. He also interpreted Elijah’s return, though vainly, in support of his own position.

### XXXVI

Returning from this point to his former subject, he teaches that the sex of the soul and that of the body arise together.

### XXXVII

Moreover, the entire formation of a human being is regulated by some power ministering to the divine will—namely, by some angel—and soul and body grow and advance together: the soul in intelligence and perception, the body in size and physical condition.

### XXXVIII

Puberty likewise arises simultaneously in the soul and in the body, through the prompting of the senses. The craving for food, too, is in the soul, but because of the body’s necessity, not as a property of the soul itself—lest anyone should therefore believe it mortal.

[Col. 0645A]

### XXXIX

Likewise, scarcely any soul is without a demon—at least among the gentiles—until they are born in Christ.

### XL

For so long as the soul remains in association with the flesh in Adam, it is regarded as unclean; and both soul and flesh are said to be sinful.

### XLI

Thus evil is not merely superimposed upon the soul by the arrival of an evil spirit, but is also present from its origin. Its prior good is thereby obscured until the soul is re-formed through its second birth from water and through the heavenly power of the Holy Spirit. At that birth it is received by the Holy Spirit, just as at its former birth it was received by the profane spirit.

### XLII

Yet the consideration of death also concerns us—contrary to Epicurus’ opinion—for in death the soul reaches its consummation.

[Col. 0645B]

### XLIII

Accordingly, sleep is also the mirror of death. He proves, both by other arguments and by the sacred Scriptures, that sleep is not supernatural but natural.

### XLIV

For the story written about Hermotimus—that he was without his soul during sleep—is a fabrication, devised so that sleep might be believed to be, not the soul’s inactivity, but its withdrawal.

### XLV

Dreams and the incidents of sleep must also be discussed, insofar as they differ from ecstasy. Dreams show that the soul is perpetually active, which is a ground for its immortality. Yet in dreams we shall no more be condemned for a vision of sexual violation than we shall be crowned for martyrdom.

### XLVI

Many examples prove that dreams are not altogether empty, as Epicurus judged; and there have been various interpreters and advocates of this position.

[Col. 0645C]

### XLVII

The Christian view, however, is that dreams are very often cast into us by demons, even when they are sometimes true; but that any dreams which are honorable, holy, prophetic, revelatory, edifying, or vocational must be assigned to God. The soul also fashions some dreams for itself from its concentration upon surrounding circumstances. Finally, the remaining phenomena must be distinguished from ecstasy.

### XLVIII

Thus Plato evaluates dreams more according to conjecture than according to any consistent principle, supposing them to be more reliable at the close of night, at certain seasons of the year, according to one’s posture in sleep, according to distinctions among foods, or through abstinence from them.

### XLIX

Indeed, infants and nursing children also dream. And since dreams also come from God, no nation is now foreign to God, while the Gospel flashes forth to all the boundaries of the world.

### L

Now, as regards death, the end appointed by nature is a debt owed by the whole human race, however much Epicurus denies it, and however much the heretic Menander denies that it applies to those initiated by his baptism. Among his followers, there is not even a law requiring one to die for God; for even Enoch and Elijah are destined to die, and John has already died.

[Col. 0645D; Col. 0646A]

### LI

The work of death is the separation of body and soul, however much certain philosophers believed that, after death, some souls remain attached to their bodies.

### LII

Every death, whether ordinary or extraordinary, derives not from nature but from fault—and not even the fault itself is natural.

### LIII

Furthermore, the soul departs; it does not fail as the body’s instruments gradually dissolve. As death approaches, its vision is already clearer, as though it were emerging from sleep. It then exults or trembles according as it perceives, from the face of the angel who summons souls, the preparation of its own lodging-place.

[Col. 0646B]

### LIV

There are various philosophical opinions concerning the place to which souls are conducted. Among these philosophers, the Stoics place the souls of the wise in heavenly dwellings and all others in the underworld. Christians locate this underworld, not in the bosom of the earth, as Plato does, but in its innermost depths.

### LV

He states as his own opinion—though it is not accepted by the Church—that only the souls of martyrs migrate to Abraham’s bosom, or paradise, concerning which he published a book, while all other souls go to the underworld.

### LVI

Yet those opinions are empty according to which souls were thought to be detained on earth until they had received their proper burial rites, or according to which those overtaken by an untimely death wander here until the remainder of their allotted lifetime has been completed.

### LVII

Nor, indeed, do souls return here from the underworld either at the command of demons through magic—several forms of which he enumerates—or at their own discretion.

[Col. 0646C]

### LVIII

Yet there, while they await the body, the souls of the innocent rejoice, while those of the guilty suffer pain and punishment—even for thoughts alone, which divine judgment pursues. This is especially so because the Gospel teaches that even slight offenses must be paid for there, down to the last farthing.

**Note [Col. 0645D]:** In Agobard’s manuscript this book is repeatedly entitled *On the Origin of the Soul* (*De censu Animae*), but incorrectly. For, as is evident from the beginning of the present work, that was the title of another book composed by our Septimius against Hermogenes, in order to show what the soul ought to be considered as deriving from. In that work he had argued against the heretic only this point: that the soul consists of God’s breath, not, as Hermogenes said, of a substratum of matter. That little book is now lost. Here he treats the soul’s condition and the remaining questions concerning it, in opposition to the philosophers. We have therefore retained the general title *On the Soul*, now universally accepted in the printed editions. **RIG.**

**Note [Col. 0652C]:** Junius reads “severity.”

Aristotle’s tenor, Epicurus’ insensibility, Heraclitus’ gloom, or Empedocles’ madness. [Col. 0651C]

Divine doctrine was at fault, I suppose, for arising from Judaea rather than Greece. Christ too was mistaken in sending fishermen to preach before he sent sophists.

If, therefore, anything of this kind darkens the bright and pure [Col. 0652A] air of truth with the fumes of philosophy, Christians must clear it of clouds, both by striking down the original—that is, philosophical—arguments and by opposing to them heavenly—that is, the Lord’s—definitions. Thus both those teachings by which gentiles are captivated by philosophy may be destroyed, and those by which the faithful are shaken by heresy may be blunted.

One controversy has already been decided against Hermogenes, as we said in the preface: we maintain that the soul comes from the breath of God, not from matter. There, too, we were fortified by the undarkenable rule of the divine determination:

> “And God,” it says, “breathed the breath of life into the face of the man, and the man became a living soul”—

assuredly from the breath of God. Nothing further concerning this point must be reconsidered. It has its own title and its own heretic. From this point I shall introduce the beginning of the remaining questions.

# CHAPTER IV

[Col. 0652B] After the definition of the soul’s origin, the question of its condition follows. For since we have professed that the soul comes from the breath of God, it follows that we must assign it a beginning.

**Note [Col. 0652C]:** Pamelius reads “we should assign.”

**Note [Col. 0651D]:** “Plato excludes this.” That is, the soul’s beginning. For in the *Phaedrus*, while discussing the immortality of the soul, he says—according to Cicero’s translation in *Tusculan Disputations* I and *Republic* VI: “If among all things there is one soul which moves itself, and is thus the beginning of motion for itself, it certainly was not born and is eternal. For that which arose from elsewhere would not be a beginning.” **PAM.**

Plato excludes this, since he holds that the soul is unborn and unmade. We, however, teach that it is both born and made, on the basis that it has an appointed beginning. We have not immediately fallen into error by saying both, because “born” is plainly one thing and “made” another, since the former properly applies to living creatures. Yet distinctions, while possessing their own places and times, sometimes also share one another’s modes of being acted upon.

**Note [Col. 0652C]:** “It is therefore permissible to say that a created thing is generated, in the sense of being brought into existence.” *Capit* means “it is permitted,” “it is lawful.” By the soul’s *factura*, “making,” he means what elsewhere he would call “creation.” **RIG.**

It is therefore permissible to say that a created thing is generated, in the sense of being brought into existence, since everything that in any manner receives existence is generated. For even the maker himself can be called the parent of what he has made; Plato too uses the word in this way.

Therefore, so far as concerns our faith that the soul was made or born, the philosopher’s opinion has been driven away by the authority of prophecy as well. [Col. 0652C]

Let him summon some Eubulus,

> Εὐφράνορος δὲ διήκουσεν Εὔβουλος Ἀλεξανδρεύς.

> Eubulus of Alexandria was a pupil of Euphranor.

But the *Suda* writes otherwise:

> Εὔβουλος Κήττιος Ἀθηναῖος, υἱὸς Εὐφράνορος.

> Eubulus, an Athenian of Cettus, son of Euphranor.

The Author here gathers, by gradation, the classes of philosophers who held the soul to be incorporeal. **LAC.**

and Critolaus,

**Note [Col. 0652D]:** “Critolaus.” Critolaus of Phaselis was a pupil of Lyco and a Peripatetic, as will be stated in this same chapter. I do not think that Tertullian is referring here to two different men named Critolaus, as we shall soon see, but to only one, who thought the soul incorporeal like a point, as Marsilio Ficino confirms. Cicero mentions Critolaus and Xenocrates in book V of the *Tusculan Disputations*, where he cites their testimonies concerning virtue. **LAC.**

and Xenocrates,

**Note [Col. 0652D]:** “Xenocrates.” He was Plato’s disciple, according to Laertius. Cicero gives his opinion concerning the soul in book I of the *Tusculan Disputations*: “Xenocrates denied that the mind had a shape or anything like a body, but said that it was number, whose power, as Pythagoras had previously believed, was supreme in nature”; and in book IV of the *Academica*: “Or, as Xenocrates says, is the mind without any body?” Aristotle, *On the Soul* I.2 and I.4, mentions this doctrine. [Col. 0653D] **LAC.**

and, in this matter, Plato’s friend Aristotle. [Col. 0653A] Perhaps their case for removing corporeality from the soul would be strengthened if they had not looked at others who, on the contrary—and in greater numbers—maintain that the soul is a body.

I do not mean only those who fashion it from manifest bodily substances: Hipparchus and Heraclitus from fire; Hippo and Thales from water; Empedocles and Critias from blood; Epicurus from atoms, if even atoms produce corporeal masses from their conjunction;

**Note [Col. 0653D]:** “If even atoms produce corporeal masses from their conjunction.” Quintilian, *Institutes* X, called atoms “figures.” **RIG.**

or Critolaus and his Peripatetics from some fifth substance or other—if that too is a body, since it contains bodies. I also invoke the Stoics. By declaring that the soul is spirit, they come close to us, given the near relation between breath and spirit; nevertheless, they will readily establish that the soul is a body.

Thus Zeno, defining the soul as an implanted spirit, argues in this manner:

> That whose departure causes the living creature to die is a body. But when the implanted spirit departs, the living creature dies. Therefore the implanted spirit is a body. But the implanted spirit is the soul. Therefore the soul is a body.

[Col. 0653B]

Cleanthes also maintains that not only the bodily features but also the soul’s characteristics correspond by resemblance from parents to children, as is evident in the reflection of their characters, dispositions, and emotions. The soul, moreover, receives the body’s likeness and unlikeness. Therefore the soul is subject to likeness or unlikeness, which is bodily.

Likewise, the experiences of corporeal and incorporeal things cannot be shared with one another. Yet the soul suffers together with the body: when the body is injured by blows, wounds, or ulcers, the soul shares its pain. The body also suffers with the soul: when the soul is afflicted by anxiety, anguish, or love, the body becomes ill together with it,

**Note [Col. 0653C]:** The Latin edition reads *coagrescit*.

namely, through loss of vigor; and it bears witness to the soul’s shame and fear by blushing and growing pale. Therefore the soul is a body, because it shares in bodily experiences. [Col. 0653C]

Chrysippus also extends his hand in support, establishing that corporeal things cannot possibly be abandoned by incorporeal things, because neither can corporeal things be touched by them.

**Note [Col. 0653D]:** “Hence Lucretius.” Lucretius, the follower of Epicurus, in book I of *On the Nature of Things*, following his master’s example, proves that those things which reach the senses—such as odors, heat, cold, and voices—are corporeal. Lactantius, chapter VI of *On the Workmanship of God*, says that he raved, and argues against him in book VII of the *Divine Institutes*. **LE PR.**

Hence Lucretius says:

> For nothing except body can touch or be touched.

But when the soul abandons the body, the body is affected by death. Therefore the soul is a body, since, were it not corporeal, it would not abandon the body.
