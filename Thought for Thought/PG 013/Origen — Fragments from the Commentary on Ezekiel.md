## Ezekiel 30.25

Pharaoh’s “arms” signify wickedness expressed in sinful action and, in impious doctrines, the fleshly disposition. Because he is good, God breaks these arms. Pharaoh symbolizes the devil, the ruler of sin among people. The Lord breaks sinners’ power so that they cannot seize a sword.

<!-- source-locus: 32.6 -->
## Ezekiel 32.6

This especially displays Scripture’s allegorical sense. Pharaoh does not literally irrigate the earth with his excretions; instead, the foul discharge of the Adversary nourishes what is earthly.

## Ezekiel 32.23

The Hebrew wording reads “in the length of a pit.” The Hebrew word *iarēch*, when aspirated, can mean both “thigh” and “length.” In its application to the synagogue it carries the extended sense of “length”: “pit” denotes the grave’s depth, while “length” denotes its extent.

## Ezekiel 44.1

Ezekiel, “son of man,” describes many gates and says something about each. He now addresses those with ears to hear about the saints’ east-facing gate, of which Scripture says that it will remain shut. It is shut not only because the Lord God of Israel passes through it, but also because the ruler sits there to eat before the Lord by the way of Ailam, interpreted as “the vestibule of a gate.” The closed gate signifies the mysteries of God, which cannot be shut up. Knowledge has a key, as the Lord tells the legal experts; the gate is therefore closed to human beings, since what lies inside is “what eye has not seen and ear has not heard.” Isaiah’s sealed book conveys the same idea, and the same book and its interpretation appear more clearly in John’s Apocalypse: the one from Judah’s tribe, and he alone, opened these things. Until Jesus Christ came, the law and the prophetic word remained shut, veiled by the stone of hardening that Jacob removed from the well. Scripture opens this gate’s meaning in many places.
