Thus God’s goodness is prior, according to nature; his severity is subsequent, according to its cause. The former is innate, the latter accidental; the former proper to him, the latter adapted to circumstances; the former brought forth from within, the latter called into use. For his nature ought not to have kept goodness inactive, nor ought the cause to have escaped severity through its being passed over. God furnished the one for himself, the other for the situation.

Now begin to accuse even the condition of a judge as akin to evil—you who dreamed of another God who is solely good because you cannot tolerate a judge, although we have shown that your god too is a judge. Or, if he is not a judge, he is certainly perverse [Col. 0298C] and ineffectual: the founder of a discipline that is not to be enforced, that is, not to be judged. Yet do you not condemn God as judge when you approve God as no judge? You will undoubtedly have to accuse justice itself, which produces the judge, or else even reckon it among the forms of wickedness—that is, enroll injustice among the titles of goodness. For justice is now evil if injustice is good. But since you are compelled to pronounce injustice one of the worst things, by the same constraint you are forced to rank justice among the best. For nothing opposed to evil is anything but good, just as nothing opposed to good is anything but evil. Therefore, to the same degree that injustice is evil, justice is good. And it must be regarded not merely as a form of goodness but as its safeguard, because unless goodness is governed by justice, so that it is just, it will not be goodness if it is unjust. For nothing unjust is good, while [Col. 0298D] everything just is good.

## CHAPTER XIV

## CHAPTER XV

First, then, examine [Note: [Col. 0302C] Rhen. reads “despise.”] the justice of the judge. If its rationale stands firm, his severity will stand firm as well, and the means through which that severity runs its course will be reckoned to reason and justice. And, lest we linger over numerous examples, assert the other causes too before you condemn the sentences; excuse the sins before you reject the judgments. Do not censure the judge, but prove him to be an evil judge.

For even if [Note: [Col. 0302C] Seml. omits “if.”] he exacted the sins of the fathers from [Note: [Col. 0302C] Seml. reads “from” differently: *de*.] their sons, the people’s hardness had compelled remedies of this kind, so that at least, by taking thought for their descendants, they might obey the divine law. For who does not care more for [Col. 0302B] the salvation of his sons than for his own? But if the fathers’ blessing was also destined for their offspring without any merit of theirs as yet, why should the fathers’ guilt not likewise overflow to their sons? As with favor, so also with offense, so that both favor and offense might run through the entire race.

This is subject to what was afterwards to be decreed: that people would no longer say that the fathers had eaten a sour grape and the children’s teeth had been set on edge (Jer. 31:29); that is, the father would not bear the son’s sin, nor the son the father’s sin, but each would become answerable for his own sin. Thus, after the people’s hardness had been subdued by the hardness of the law, justice would now judge not a lineage but individual persons. Yet if you accept the Gospel of truth, you will recognize to whom the sentence that repays the fathers’ sins upon the sons applies: namely, to those who were of their own accord to pronounce this sentence upon themselves: “His blood be upon our heads and upon the heads of our children” (Matt. 27:25). [Col. 0302C] All this, therefore, God’s providence adjudged because it had already heard it.

## CHAPTER XVI

What if you were to say that there ought indeed to be a physician, but then accuse his instruments because they cut, cauterize, amputate, and constrict, although without the instruments of his art he could not be a physician? ## CHAPTER XVIII

But which benefits of the Law should I defend in preference to those that the heresy has coveted as grounds for attack—for example, the rule of retaliation, which requires eye [Col. 0305C] for eye, tooth for tooth, and bruise for bruise (Exod. 21:24)? It does not convey permission for injuries to be inflicted in return; rather, it provides for the complete restraint of violence. For to a people exceedingly hard and faithless toward God, it seemed too long a delay, or even incredible, to await from God the vengeance [Note: [Col. 0305D] *Defensam*, that is, vengeance or retribution. EDD.] that was afterwards to be proclaimed through the prophet: “Vengeance is mine, and I will avenge, says the Lord” (Deut. 32:35; Rom. 12:19). Meanwhile, the commission of an injury was to be rooted out through fear of an immediate equivalent response, and permission to retaliate was to serve as a prohibition against provocation. Thus cunning wickedness [Note: [Col. 0305D] Formerly *aestuata*, “inflamed,” was read.] would cease: by permitting the second injury, the first is deterred; and when the first is deterred, the second is not committed either. For [Note: [Col. 0305D] Jun. reads “because.”] in other respects too, fear of retaliation is more effective because the suffering has the same taste. Nothing is more bitter than to suffer precisely what you have done to others.

## CHAPTER XX

But indeed these cuttlefish [Note: [Col. 0308D] “But indeed these cuttlefish.” He elegantly and aptly compares the Marcionite heretics to cuttlefish. For as Pliny relates in book 9, chapter 29, cuttlefish are a kind of fish which, when they perceive that they are being caught, discharge ink—which serves them in place of blood—and hide themselves in the darkened water. In the same way, when the Marcionite heretics perceive that they are being exposed—that is, when they are accused and convicted of error—they spew the darkness of this blasphemy between themselves and their accuser like the ink of a cuttlefish. They thereby hinder and divert the one accusing them by alleging things that darken and obscure the Creator’s goodness. RHEN.] [Note: [Col. 0308C] Formerly “often” was read.]—a figure of that little sea creature which the Law likewise rejected as food—when they perceive themselves being exposed, spew forth from here a darkness of blasphemy and thereby scatter each person’s attention just when it has drawn close to the point, loudly alleging [Note: [Col. 0308C] Seml. reads “casting.”] and affirming things that may darken the Creator’s shining goodness. Yet we shall pursue their wickedness even through those mists and drag the devices of darkness into the light, especially those that charge the Creator with that fraud and theft of gold and silver which he commanded [Note: [Col. 0308C] Pamel. and Seml. read “commanded” in the neuter plural; Jun., feminine plural.] the Hebrews to perpetrate against the Egyptians.

Come now, most wretched heretic: I demand that you yourself act as arbiter. First investigate the case between the two nations, and then you will judge the author of the command. The Egyptians demand their gold and silver vessels back from the Hebrews; [Note: [Col. 0308D] “The Hebrews institute counterclaims.” Thus the emperor Alexander, law 1, chapter *Things Removed*: “For it is not fair that what you are established to owe should be paid before a response has been given to the counterclaim.” RIG.] the Hebrews institute counterclaims against them, alleging that, in the name of those same fathers and on the evidence of the same written record, wages for that laborious slavery ought also to be restored to them: for the bricks they had made and for the cities and villages they had built. [Col. 0308C] What judgment will you give, elector of the supremely good God? That the Hebrews ought to acknowledge fraud, or that the Egyptians ought to acknowledge a setoff? [Col. 0309A]

[Note: [Col. 0309D] “For they also say that the case was argued through envoys.” But Moses says so. RIG.] For they also say that the case was argued through envoys on both sides: the Egyptians demanding the vessels back and the Jews demanding their labor back; and at last the Egyptians renounced their claim to those vessels [Note: [Col. 0309D] “The Egyptians renounced their claim.” This seems to be ironic: namely, that the Egyptians justly renounced the Hebrews’ labor—that is, denied the wages owed for the labor. RHEN.] [Note: [Col. 0309D] Seml. and Pamel. read “and yet justice regarded these …”].

Today the Hebrews argue still more fully against the Marcionites, denying that any quantity of gold and silver, however great, was sufficient for the setoff if the labor of six hundred thousand people over so many years were valued at even a single coin per day for each. Which party’s claim, then, is greater: that of those demanding their vessels, or that of those inhabiting the villages and cities they had built? Was it therefore the grievance of the Egyptians, or [Note: [Col. 0309D] “Or the due of the Hebrews”—that which was owed to them and which they had earned, namely their wages. RHEN.] the due of the Hebrews?

Even if the Hebrews had merely retaliated against the Egyptians on the basis of the injuries done to them—free persons driven into a work prison—so that their shoulders alone, lacerated by the insulting savagery of the lash, were displayed before the Egyptians’ scribes at their judgment seats, would you not have pronounced that the Hebrews must receive satisfaction, not with a few [Col. 0309B] plates and cups, belonging of course everywhere to only a few rich men, but with the Egyptians’ entire wealth and the contributions of the whole population [Note: [Col. 0309D] Paris and Rhen. read “the Hebrew would have pronounced”; the Wouwer manuscript, “you would have pronounced the Hebrews”; Seml., “he would have pronounced for the Hebrews.”]?

Therefore, if the Hebrews’ case was good, good too was its cause—that is, the Creator’s command. He both made the Egyptians render compensation without knowing it and, in the short time available for their departure, settled his people’s account with some consolation afforded by a tacit settlement. Clearly, he ordered less to be exacted than was due: for the Egyptians ought also to have restored the Hebrews’ sons [Note: [Col. 0309D] Seml. omits “for.”].
