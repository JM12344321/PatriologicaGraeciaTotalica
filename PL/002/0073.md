What, then, is that ancient saying remembered by Plato concerning the reciprocal passage of souls: that those departing from here go there, and again return here and live, and thereafter depart from life, and that the living are again produced from the dead?

[Note: [Col. 0697C] “What is that ancient saying now remembered by Plato?” Thus Socrates speaks in Plato’s *Phaedo*: “There is, then, an ancient saying which we remember, that souls arriving there come from here, and that they return here again and are born from the dead,” etc. RIG.]

Was it Pythagorean, as they maintain—or even divine, as Albinus thinks—or perhaps derived from the Egyptian Mercury?

[Note: [Col. 0697C] Some read “a certain one.” PAM. RHEN.]

[Note: [Col. 0697C] “As Albinus thinks.” A short introductory work by Albinus the Platonist on Plato’s dialogues is extant in the Royal Library. Cassiodorus Senator also records that, before the pagan invasion, he possessed Albinus’s book on music in his library at Rome. RIG.]

But no saying is divine except that of the one God—the saying with which the prophets, the apostles, and Christ himself thundered. Moses is far more ancient even than Saturn, by about nine hundred years, to say nothing of Saturn’s great-grandsons; and certainly he is far more divine, for he set out the course of the human race from the beginning of the world, birth by birth, with names and dates, sufficiently proving the divinity of his work by the prophetic character of his utterance.

[Note: [Col. 0697D] “Moses is far more ancient even than Saturn, by about nine hundred years.” Anyone who considers what Tertullian says in *Apologeticum*, chapter 19, will not be surprised that here he calls Moses about nine hundred years older than Saturn; for there he says that Moses preceded the fall of Priam by about a thousand years. As I long ago replied to a letter on this subject from François Baudouin, jurisconsult, on the testimony of Eusebius, Bede, and Hermannus Contractus, we read that Saturn reigned only 150 years before Aeneas’s arrival in Italy. If, therefore, Moses preceded the fall of Priam by a thousand years, he was certainly about nine hundred years older than Saturn. I know that some others hold a different opinion; but we have noted in the *Apologeticum* that our author took this from Josephus. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0697C] Some read “all the way through.” JUN.]

But if the Samian [Col. 0697B] sophist was Plato’s authority for the doctrine that souls repeatedly return in a revolving cycle, through the alternating replacement of the dead by the living and the living by the dead, then that Pythagoras—though otherwise a good man—nevertheless, in order to establish this doctrine, resorted not merely to a shameful but also to a reckless lie.

[Note: [Col. 0697D] “But if the Samian sophist.” “Samian” is an epithet of Pythagoras, from the island of Samos, where he became renowned; he carried the Italic discipline into the other Greece. Perhaps the author calls him a sophist not out of contempt, since immediately afterward he speaks of him honorably and calls him good. For the name “sophist” was not always used in a bad sense, but sometimes to signify a man of outstanding intellect and consummate wisdom. Accordingly, Isocrates writes that Solon was the first of all men to be called a sophist. LAC.]

Learn the facts, if you do not know them, and believe with us. He feigns death and hides underground, condemning himself to remain there patiently for seven years. Meanwhile, from his mother—the sole accomplice and agent in the deception—he learns the facts concerning those who died afterward, which he will report in order to gain credibility. When he thinks that he has sufficiently altered his corpulence into all the ghastliness of a long-dead man, he emerges from the sanctuary of his deception as though restored from the underworld. Who would not believe that a man had returned to life whom he had believed dead—especially upon hearing from him facts concerning persons who had subsequently died, which he would not appear capable of learning except in the underworld? This, then, [Col. 0697C] is how the saying that the living are produced from the dead became ancient.

But what if it were also recent? Truth has no need of antiquity, nor does falsehood avoid novelty. [Col. 0698A] I plainly possess a falsehood ennobled by age. Why should it not be false, when even its testimony proceeds from a falsehood? How am I to believe that Pythagoras is not lying, when he lies in order to make me believe? How will he persuade me that before he was Pythagoras he had successively been Aethalides, Euphorbus, Pyrrhus the fisherman, and Hermotimus, so as to persuade me that the living are produced from the dead, when he perjured himself yet again in claiming to be Pythagoras? For the more credible it would have been that he himself returned to life from himself only once rather than becoming one different person after another so many times, the more culpably did he deceive in the harder claims, since he had lied in the easier ones.

[Note: [Col. 0697C] “Ephaclides” in the Paris manuscript and others.]

[Note: [Col. 0698C] “Hermippus.” PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0698C] PAM. omits “only once.”]

But he recognized the shield of Euphorbus, which had long ago been dedicated at Delphi; he declared that it was his and proved it by markings generally unknown.

[Note: [Col. 0698D] “But the shield of Euphorbus.” Besides Diogenes and Lactantius, Iamblichus and Philostratus also mention this, as does our author frequently: chapter 31, where he refutes it; chapter 34; and chapter 1 of *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*. Maximus of Tyre gave a particularly full account of this fable in Discourse 28. LAC.]

Look back at his underground chamber—and believe, if you can.

[Note: [Col. 0698D] “And believe, if you can.” That is: “If you can bring yourself to believe it, believe.” By an elegant omission he has suppressed the second verb. RIG.]

For a man who devised such a trick, injuring his good health and defrauding his life—[Col. 0698B] tortured underground for seven years by hunger, inactivity, and darkness—a man to whom the open sky was worth so much aversion: what recklessness would he not have embraced? What inquiry would he not have attempted in order to discover the identifying mark on that shield? What if he found it in some comparatively obscure historical work? What if he caught some lingering breath of a tradition whose transmission had already failed? What if he learned it from the temple attendant by secretly purchasing an inspection? We know that magic can also investigate hidden things through *catabolic* spirits, attendant spirits, and Pythonic spirits.

[Note: [Col. 0698D] “Through catabolic spirits.” The very learned Turnebus taught me in his *Adversaria* that “catabolic” spirits are sinister and infamous demons that overthrow fanatics and dash them to the ground. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0698D] “And attendant spirits.” Demons that accompany certain persons continually. Simon Magus had such a one. LE PR.—“And Pythonic spirits.” Pythons, or ventriloquists (*engastrimythoi*, ἐγγαστριμύθους), possessed by a demon. LE PR.]

Did not Pherecydes, Pythagoras’s teacher, perhaps practice divination by these arts—not to say that he dreamed? What if the same demon was in him that also performed the deeds of bloodshed in Euphorbus? Finally, since he had proved himself to be Euphorbus from the evidence of the shield, why did he not likewise recognize any one of his Trojan comrades-in-arms? [Col. 0698C] For they too would already have returned to life, if the living were produced from the dead.

## CHAPTER XXIX

It is established that the dead are produced from the living; it does not therefore [Col. 0699A] follow that the living are also produced from the dead.

[Note: [Col. 0698D] “It is established that the dead are produced from the living.” In this chapter he argues against Plato in the *Phaedo*, whose inference Tertullian here rejects. Plato argued that the dead are produced from the living, and therefore that the living are also produced from the dead, according to the philosophers’ rule that one contrary arises from another. Our author refutes this rule, when taken universally, by evident examples. LAC.]

For in the beginning the living came first; consequently, from the beginning the dead came afterward, and from no other source than the living. The former had a source from which they might more properly arise, provided it was not from the dead; the latter had no other source from which they could more properly be derived except the living. Therefore, if at the beginning the living did not arise from the dead, why should they do so afterward? Had that original source, whatever it may be, failed? Or had it repented of its method? And how is that method preserved among the dead? Because at the beginning the dead arose from the living, does it not follow rather that they must always arise from the living? For the original pattern should either have continued on both sides or have changed on both sides. If afterward the living had to arise from the dead, then likewise the dead would have had to cease arising from the living. If fidelity to the original institution need not be maintained equally, then it is not invariably true that contraries are restored from contraries in alternation.

For we too shall set against them [Col. 0699B] the contrary pairs of born and unborn, sight and blindness, youth and old age, wisdom and folly. Yet the unborn does not therefore proceed from the born merely because one contrary arises from another; nor does sight return from blindness merely because blindness supervenes upon sight; nor does youth revive again from old age merely because old age withers out of youth; nor is folly blunted anew out of wisdom merely because wisdom is sharpened out of folly. Albinus too, fearing these objections for his Plato’s sake, subtly seeks to distinguish different kinds of contraries—as though these examples were not placed among contraries just as absolutely as those which he interprets in support of his master’s doctrine, namely life and death. Yet life is not restored from death merely because death is brought forth from life.

## CHAPTER XXX

[Col. 0699C] But what shall we answer concerning the remaining points? First, if the living arose from the dead just as the dead arise from the living, the total number of all people would always have remained one and the same: namely, that number which first entered life. For the living preceded the dead; afterward came the dead from the living, and again the living from the dead. And since this would always occur from the same persons, the same number would always arise from the same persons: no more and no fewer would have departed than returned.

[Note: [Col. 0699C] Others read “would have returned.”]

Yet we find, even in the records of human antiquity, that the human race gradually increased, while [Col. 0700A] indigenous peoples, wanderers, exiles, or all manner of celebrated peoples occupied new lands: the Scythians, Parthian territory; the Amyclaeans, the Peloponnese; the Athenians, Asia; the Phrygians, Italy; and the Phoenicians, Africa; and while formal migrations, which they call colonies (*apoikiai*, ἀποικίαι), disgorged swarms of a people into other territories for the deliberate purpose of relieving overpopulation.

[Note: [Col. 0699D] “While indigenous peoples, wanderers, exiles, or all manner of celebrated peoples occupied lands.” We know that indigenous peoples are called *autochthones* (αὐτόχθονας) and *enchōrioi* (ἐγχωρίους). We also know that in ancient books *ab origine* is not infrequently written where *aborigines* certainly ought to have been written; and some accordingly read *aborigines* here from Ursinus’s manuscript, nor do we deny that this can be read. But since the reading found in that most ancient Agobardine manuscript, *ab origine*—that is, “from primeval generation” (*apo archaiogonias*, ἀπὸ ἀρχαιογονίας)—can be accepted and readily explained, we do not think that anything more recondite should be sought. RIG.—“While indigenous peoples.” He contrasts indigenous peoples with wanderers; he therefore does not mean the ancient peoples of Italy called Latins, but those whom the Greeks call *autochōroi* (αὐτόχωροι), *proterogeneis* (προτερογενεῖς), or *enchōrioi* (ἐγχώριοι), as Ambrose uses the word in his *Hexameron*: namely, the earliest ancestors, inhabitants of cities, and natives, who were called *aborigines* as though they had no foreign origin. On these see Thucydides, book I, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, book I. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0699C] Wouwer reads “Parthian territory”; Ursinus’s manuscript, “Parthia.”]

[Note: [Col. 0699C] Wouwer reads “Amadae”; Junius, “Semenidae.”]

[Note: [Col. 0700D] “Formal migrations.” The dispatching of colonies, but formal ones—that is, those undertaken in accordance with a solemn procedure, either by a public law or at an emperor’s command—not colonies formed fortuitously, by chance or through the desire of the victors, without other authority or the requisite rite. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0699D] PAM. and RHEN. read *metoikiai* (μετοικίαι), “migrations.”]

For the indigenous peoples still remain in their own territories, while elsewhere they have lent out their nationality at interest and increased it. Certainly the world itself lies open before us, more cultivated and better equipped every day than formerly. Everything is now accessible, everything known, everything engaged in commerce. Most delightful estates have erased the wildernesses once notorious; cultivated fields have subdued the forests; flocks have put wild beasts to flight; sandy wastes are sown, rocks are planted, marshes are drained. There are now cities as numerous as once there were not even [Col. 0700B] huts.

[Note: [Col. 0700C] PAM. and RHEN. omit “now.”]

No longer do islands inspire dread or cliffs terror. Everywhere are houses, everywhere people, everywhere civil communities, everywhere life. The supreme testimony to humanity’s abundance is this: we are a burden to the world; the elements scarcely suffice for us; our necessities grow more constricting, and there are complaints among all, because nature can no longer sustain us. In truth, plagues, famines, wars, and the engulfing of cities must be regarded as remedies, like the pruning of an overgrown human race. And yet, even when axes of this kind cut down at one stroke a very great multitude of mortals, never has the world once stood aghast at their restoration bringing the living back from the dead after a thousand years.

[Note: [Col. 0700D] “Bringing the living back from the dead after a thousand years.” Manilius had this in view in *Astronomica* I when he said:

> Or whether nature, dissolved into indivisible  
> First principles, remains destined to return to the same state  
> After a thousand years.  
>
> RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0700D] “After a thousand years.” He alludes to the thousand-year journey that Plato describes after this life in dialogue X of the *Republic* and in the *Phaedrus*. Since our author here refutes these accounts as fables, it is remarkable that he afterward fell into the paradox of the Millenarians. PAM.]

For this too—the equal magnitude of loss and restoration—would have produced a perceptible effect if the living arose from the dead. Why, moreover, should the living arise from the dead after a thousand years, and not immediately? [Col. 0700C] For unless what is expended were immediately replaced, it would be in danger of being wholly consumed, depletion overtaking restoration; because this provision of life could not have equaled a millennium in duration, being of course far shorter, and therefore more liable to be extinguished before it could be rekindled. Accordingly, something that would have perished in this way if the living arose from the dead—since it has not perished—does not permit us to believe that the living arise from the dead.

[Note: [Col. 0700D] PAM. and RHEN. read “what has been expended.”]

## CHAPTER XXXI

Now if the living arise from the dead, each individual must arise from an individual. The souls of individual bodies would therefore have had to return individually [Col. 0701A] into individual bodies. But if two, three, and as many as five are taken up together in a single womb, the living will not arise from the dead, because they do not arise one by one from individuals. Yet in this very manner the pattern of the beginning is preserved in its singularity, since even now several souls are brought forth from one.

[Note: [Col. 0701C] “The pattern of the beginning is preserved in its singularity.” Thus reads the Agobardine manuscript: “Indeed,” he says, “the true pattern of humanity’s beginning is preserved in its singularity, since even now several are generated from one individual human being.” In this way the singular character of humanity’s beginning is preserved, since even now several souls are brought forth from one. RIG.]

Again, since souls depart at various ages, why do they all return at the same age? For all begin their formation in infancy.

[Note: [Col. 0701C] PAM. and RHEN. add, “At what age does the infant return?” Junius prefers the Latin reading, “it returns.”]

What sort of arrangement is it that one who died an old man should return as an infant? If the soul grows younger outside the body by a retrograde course of age, how much more fitting would it be for it to return more advanced in age after a thousand years! It should at least be of the same age as at its death, so that it might take up again the span of life that it had left behind.

But even if the same souls were always returning, though they did not also bring back the same bodily forms, they ought at least to bring back with them [Col. 0701B] the former characteristics of their dispositions, pursuits, and affections. For they would groundlessly be regarded as the same if they lacked the features by which their identity could be proved.

“How do you know,” you ask, “whether this does not in fact occur secretly, but the interval of a thousand years destroys the ability to recognize them, because those who return are unknown to you?”

[Note: [Col. 0701C] PAM. and RHEN. read, “Because both here and there they are unknown.”]

But I know that it does not occur, since you set Pythagoras and Euphorbus before me. For Euphorbus plainly possessed a military and warlike soul, as is sufficiently established even by the glory of the dedicated shields; Pythagoras, by contrast, was so sedentary and unwarlike that, avoiding the wars then raging in Greece, he preferred the peace of Italy. He was devoted to geometry, astrology, and music, and was alien to the pursuits and disposition of Euphorbus.

Moreover, that Pyrrhus was occupied in deceiving fish; Pythagoras, by contrast, would not even eat them, [Col. 0701C] since he abstained from animals.

[Note: [Col. 0701C] “But that Pyrrhus.” Pyrrhus, whom Pythagoras falsely claimed that he had formerly been, was a Delian fisherman. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0701C] “Pythagoras, by contrast, would not even eat them.” Elsewhere our author gives the reason why Pythagoras abstained from animals, *Apologeticum*, chapter 48: “Lest someone should happen to serve up beef taken from one of his own great-grandfathers.” Relevant here also is the statement of the blessed Jerome, *Against Jovinianus*, book II: “And I shall prove that we do not follow the doctrine of Empedocles and Pythagoras, who, because of metempsychosis (*metempsychōsis*, μετεμψύχωσιν), think that nothing which moves and lives should be eaten.” Lactantius, book III, chapter 19, interprets this more mildly. PAM.]

Aethalides and [Col. 0702A] Hermotimus, however, had even included beans among ordinary foods; but Pythagoras taught his disciples that they must not even pass through a bean field.

[Note: [Col. 0701C] “Ephafides” in the Paris manuscript.]

[Note: [Col. 0701D] “Even included beans among ordinary foods.” What is better known than Pythagoras’s precept, *kyamōn apechou* (κυάμων ἀπέχου), “abstain from beans”? Iamblichus writes that this was forbidden for many reasons—some religious, some natural, and some concerning the soul—on which the authorities are full. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0701D] “That they must not even pass through a bean field.” Iamblichus, chapter 31, writes that Pythagoras’s precept, *kyamōn mē thinganein* (κυάμων μὴ θιγγάνειν), “beans must not be touched,” was observed even at the risk of life. He tells how certain disciples of Pythagoras, while fleeing the soldiers of the tyrant Dionysius, happened upon a bean field. Remembering the decree and refusing to violate it, they stopped, not daring to cross the field, and chose instead to be slaughtered and buried. Myllias alone was captured and brought before the tyrant, where he gave no lesser demonstration of courage. When Dionysius asked why his companions had preferred death to trampling beans, he replied: “They stopped so that they would not trample the beans; I, for my part, shall choose death rather than explain to you why we refuse to trample beans.” Suidas likewise writes that when Pythagoras’s enemies pursued him after setting fire to his house, he stopped upon reaching a field planted with beans and was killed. LAC.]

How, then, are the same souls recovered, when they will not be proved the same in dispositions, practices, or even diet?

And now, out of so large a census of Greece, only four souls are identified. But why, in any event, only from the census of Greece? Why do transmigrations of souls (*metempsychōseis*, μετεμψυχώσεις) and reincorporations (*metensōmatōseis*, μετενσωματώσεις) not occur daily from every nation, every age and rank, and indeed every sex? Why does Pythagoras alone recognize that he has been one person after another, and not I too? Or if this is a privilege of philosophers—and specifically Greek philosophers, as though Scythians and Indians did not also philosophize—why does Epicurus not remember that he formerly was someone else, nor Chrysippus, nor Zeno, nor even Plato himself, whom perhaps [Col. 0702B] we might have believed to be Nestor because of the honeyed sweetness of his eloquence?

[Note: [Col. 0701C] “And” added by Semler.]

## CHAPTER XXXII

But Empedocles, because he had raved that he was a god, therefore, I suppose, disdained to remember that he had been one of the heroes.

[Note: [Col. 0702C] “Empedocles, because he had raved that he was a god.” Together with the historians I marvel at the man’s madness and stupidity. For the following verse of Empedocles is cited here:

> Χαίρετ᾽· ἐγὼ δὲ ὑμῖν θεὸς ἄμβροτος, οὐκέτι θνητός·  
> Greetings! For to you I am an immortal god, no longer a mortal man.  
>
> LE PR.]

“I have been a shrub and a fish,” he says. Why not rather a melon, since he was so tasteless, and a chameleon, since he was so puffed up?

[Note: [Col. 0702D] “I have been a shrub and a fish.” Empedocles himself accordingly says, in Athenaeus, book VIII, and in Laertius:

> Ἤδη γάρ ποτ᾽ ἐγὼ γενόμην κούρη τε κόρος τε,  
> Θάμνος τ᾽ οἰωνός τε, καὶ εἰν ἁλὶ ἔλλοπος ἰχθύς.  
>
> For once already I became both girl and boy,  
> A shrub, a bird, and in the sea a darting fish.
>
> We think that the hitherto badly corrupted translation of Calcidius should be restored as follows:
>
> > For long ago I lived as boy and solid tree,  
> > A bird, a creature from the waves, and then a milk-white maiden.
>
> In the latter verse, however, Cyril, book VIII *Against Julian*, seems to have read:
>
> > καὶ εἰν ἁλὶ φαίδιμος ἰχθύς  
> > and in the sea a shining fish.
>
> LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0702D] “And a chameleon, since he was so puffed up.” Many of the ancients noted Empedocles’s arrogance and “tragic vanity” (*tragikon typhon*, τραγικὸν τῦφον). LE PR.]

Clearly, like a fish, lest he putrefy through some more respectable kind of burial, he preferred to be roasted by hurling himself into Etna. And from that point let reincorporation have ended in his case, like a summer dinner after the roast.

[Note: [Col. 0702C] Wouwer reads “be exposed”; Pamel and Rhenanus, “consider the condition.”]

Accordingly, we must likewise contend here against the still more monstrous presumption that rotates beasts out of human beings and human beings out of beasts. Let the shrubs look to themselves. It will be permissible to deal with the matter in passing, [Col. 0702C] lest we be compelled to provoke more laughter than instruction.

[Note: [Col. 0702D] “It will be permissible to deal with the matter in passing.” That is: it will suffice to touch upon it incidentally. For these claims are so ridiculous that merely pointing them out in passing is enough to refute them. We have therefore emended the common reading, “It will be permissible—and sorrel too.” The Agobardine manuscript reads, “It will be permissible and seized.” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0702C] Variant readings: “sorrels,” PAM. RHEN.; “seized,” Paris manuscript; “twice exalted,” Latin manuscript; “and burrs,” JUN.]

We say that the [Col. 0703A] human soul can in no way be transferred into beasts, even if, according to the philosophers, it were reckoned as deriving from elemental substances. For whether the soul is fire, water, blood, breath, air, or light, we must consider the animals that are contrary to each individual kind. Contrary to fire are those that are cold: snakes, spotted lizards, and salamanders; and indeed all creatures produced from the element opposed to fire, namely water. Correspondingly contrary to water, of course, are those creatures that are dry and without moisture: locusts, papiunculi [a kind of small creature; reading uncertain], and chameleons, which in fact delight in arid conditions.

[Note: [Col. 0703D] Junius reads “of water.”]

Likewise contrary to blood are creatures that lack its purple color: snails, little worms, and the greater part of the fishes. Contrary to breath are those that do not appear to breathe, lacking lungs and arteries: gnats, ants, moths, and tiny creatures of this kind. Likewise contrary to air [Col. 0703B] are creatures that always live underground or underwater and do without inhaling it—you will know the realities better than their names.

[Note: [Col. 0703D] The Paris manuscript, Gelenius, and Junius read “I should know.”]

Likewise contrary to light are creatures that are entirely blind or have eyes only for darkness: moles, bats, and owls.

[Note: [Col. 0703D] “Bats.” Latin *vesperugines*, that is, bats. They received their name from Lucifer, or the evening star (*vesperugo*), the planet Venus, because they are commonly believed not to leave their caves before that star appears. LE PR.]

I offer these examples by way of substances that are visible and manifest. But if I could grasp Epicurus’s atoms, see Pythagoras’s numbers, encounter Plato’s Ideas, and apprehend Aristotle’s entelechies, perhaps I would find animals to set in opposition to these kinds as well under the category of contrariety.

For I maintain that, from whichever of the aforesaid substances the human soul were constituted, it could not be reconstituted in animals so contrary to each particular substance and confer upon them their origin by its transference. It ought rather to be excluded and rejected by them than admitted and received; [Col. 0703C] by reason of this first contrariety, which produces the diversity of substantial condition, and then by reason of all the remaining contrarieties that follow in due order from each creature’s nature.

For the human soul has also been allotted different habitations, food, equipment, senses, affections, acts of intercourse, offspring, and likewise dispositions; then works, joys, distresses, faults, desires, pleasures, states of health, and remedies; and, finally, its own modes of life and manners of death.

[Note: [Col. 0703D] “How, then, will that soul,” etc. From the diversity of foods he demonstrates that transformations and transmigrations of souls cannot occur. LE PR.]

How, then, will that soul which clung to the earth, fearless neither of heights nor of depths, liable to fatigue even from climbing stairs and to strangulation even from being submerged in swimming pools—how will it afterward assault the air [Col. 0704A] in an eagle or plunge into the sea in an eel?

Likewise, how will a soul reared on generous, delicate, and carefully prepared foods chew—not merely straw, but thorns, the rustic bitterness of leaves, and the vermin of dung heaps, or even the poisons of worms, if it has passed into a goat or a quail? Indeed, how will it chew carrion—even human flesh—in a bear or lion, though surely mindful of itself?

[Note: [Col. 0703D] François reads “of water.”]

[Note: [Col. 0703D] “It will chew even poisons.” Perhaps this is because Pliny, book X, chapter 23, writes: “Quails are nourished on poison.” LE PR.]

In the same way you may reduce all the other cases to incongruity, so that we need not delay over arguing each individually.

Whatever the size or dimensions of the human soul, what will it do in animals far larger or far smaller? For every body must be completely filled by its soul, and every soul must be completely enclosed by its body. How, then, will a human soul fill an elephant? Or how, in turn, will it be enclosed within [Col. 0704B] a gnat?

[Note: [Col. 0704D] “How, then, will a human soul fill an elephant?” He argues against reincorporation from the inequality and incompatibility of bodies. For how can what was an elephant become a gnat? LE PR.]

If it is stretched or contracted to such a degree, it will certainly be endangered.

[Note: [Col. 0704D] PAM. and RHEN. read “were stretched or contracted.”]

And therefore I add: if the soul is by no means capable of a transference of this kind into animals—animals corresponding neither to the dimensions of its body nor to the other laws of its nature—will it then perhaps be transformed in accordance with the characteristics of those species and their manner of life, which is contrary to human life, itself becoming contrary to the human soul through transformation?

Yet if it admits transformation and loses what it was, it will not be what it was; and if it is not what it was, reincorporation is dissolved, since it plainly cannot be attributed to a soul which, if it is transformed, will no longer exist as that soul. For reincorporation will be said to belong to whatever undergoes it while remaining in its own condition. Therefore, if the soul can neither be changed, lest it cease to be itself, nor remain in its condition, because that condition cannot admit contraries, I still seek some [Col. 0704C] credible cause for a transference of this kind.

For although certain human beings are compared to beasts because of the qualities of their morals, dispositions, and affections—for God too says, “Man has been made like irrational cattle”—rapacious persons will not therefore become kites, nor filthy persons dogs, nor savage persons panthers, nor good persons sheep, nor talkative persons swallows, nor chaste persons doves.

[Note: [Col. 0704D] “Man has been made like irrational cattle.” The Greek is *hōmoiōthē* (ὡμοιώθη), “was made like.” Our translator rendered *tois anoētois* (τοῖς ἀνοήτοις) more literally as “foolish,” whereas the author has “irrational.” PAM.]

It is not as though the same substance of soul everywhere repeats its own nature in the characteristic traits of animals.

[Note: [Col. 0704D] “As though the same substance of soul.” Whether Plato maintained substantial reincorporation or only a moral reincorporation is disputed. Most Platonists defend him as having meant only a moral one; but Crispus, Division III, book I—who should certainly be consulted—attempts to establish the contrary with many arguments. Yet although he condemns Plato, he defends Pythagoras. LAC.]

Substance is one thing, and the nature of a substance another. For the substance of each thing is peculiar to it, whereas a nature can be shared. Take an example. [Col. 0705A] Stone and iron are substances; the hardness of stone and iron is a nature of their substance. Hardness is shared, but the substances differ. The softness of wool and the softness of a feather make their natural properties equal; they do not make their substances equal.

Thus, too, if a man is called a savage or a good beast, he does not therefore possess the same soul as a beast. For it is precisely when a likeness of nature is noted that a dissimilarity of substance is perceived. By the very fact that you judge a human being similar to a beast, you acknowledge that the soul is not the same: in calling it similar, you deny that it is the very same thing.

[Note: [Col. 0705C] PAM. adds “similar again.”]

This is also the meaning of the divine declaration, which equates man with cattle in nature, not in substance. Otherwise, God would not have characterized man in this way if he knew him to be a beast in substance.
