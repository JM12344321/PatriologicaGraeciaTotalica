## CHAPTER XXVII

### That they also established one rule of faith, both for Churches that were rebuked and subsequently corrected and for those that they commended. [Col. 0039B]

[Note: [Col. 0040C] “If, therefore, it is incredible.” He touches upon the mark of true and apostolic doctrine, which is the most ancient mark of the Church. He therefore says that it is incredible that the apostles were ignorant, as though to say: That doctrine which the apostles knew is the most ancient; all the rest are novel and adulterated, since the apostles did not know them. Hence below, ch. 3: “Yet agreeing in the same faith,” etc. For those who agree in the same faith that the apostles maintained plainly retain their doctrine, as though they were blood relatives of the apostles. Hence Sozomen, book VII, relates that Theodosius, who commanded the leaders of the sects to assemble, then asked whether they thought that the ancient Fathers who had governed the Church before that dissension over religion arose had agreed rightly and had been truly holy and apostolic. When they conceded this, he added the reasoning by which he would restrain the heretics, namely: “Let us therefore examine your doctrine against their writings; if it agrees with them, let it be retained; if not, let it be rejected.” LAC.]

If, therefore, it is incredible either that the apostles were ignorant of the fullness of the proclamation or that they did not disclose the entire order of the rule to everyone, let us see whether perhaps [Note: [Col. 0039C] “Perhaps” deleted by Semler.] the apostles did indeed proclaim it simply and fully [Note: [Col. 0039C] “Plainly,” others.], but the Churches, through their own fault, received it otherwise than the apostles presented it. You will find all these provocations to scrupulous doubt advanced by the heretics.

They seize upon the Churches rebuked by the apostle: “O foolish Galatians, who has bewitched you?”; and: “You were running so well; who hindered you?”; and the very opening: “I marvel that you are so quickly turning away from him who called you [Note: [Col. 0040B] “His own,” Venice, Rigault.] in grace, to [Col. 0040A] another gospel.” Likewise, it was written to the Corinthians that they were still carnal, being reared upon milk and not yet fit for solid food; and that they thought themselves to know something when they did not yet know as they ought to know. When they bring forward the rebuked Churches, let them also believe that they were corrected. But let them also acknowledge those Churches [Note: [Col. 0040D] “Concerning whose faith, knowledge, and conduct.” At the beginnings of his Epistles, the apostle commends the Romans, Ephesians, and Philippians for their faith and love, which he here calls “conduct.” He commends the Romans particularly for knowledge, saying to them in chapter 15: “For you yourselves are full of love, filled with all knowledge.” PAM.] over whose faith, knowledge, and conduct the apostle rejoices and gives thanks to God. Yet today these share the rights of a single institution with those that were rebuked.

## CHAPTER XXVIII

### That what is found as one thing among many must be judged not an error, but a tradition.

Come now: let them all have erred. Let the apostle have been deceived in giving testimony to certain Churches [Note: [Col. 0040B] “To certain Churches” deleted by Rigault, Venice.]. Let the Holy Spirit have regarded none of them, so as to lead it into the truth (John 14:26), although he was sent by Christ and requested from the Father for this very purpose, that he might be the teacher of truth (John 15:26). Let the steward of God, the vicar of Christ, have neglected his duty by allowing the Churches meanwhile to understand one thing and believe another than what [Note: [Col. 0040C] “Which,” Venice, Junius.] he himself was preaching through the apostles. Is it at all credible that so many and such great Churches should all have gone astray into one faith? [Note: [Col. 0040D] “Among many events there is no single outcome.” An accident dispersed among many is not received by everyone in one and the same way. RIG.] Among many events there is no single outcome: error in the doctrine of the Churches ought to have produced variations. But what is found as one thing among many is not an error, but a tradition [Note: [Col. 0040C] “But a tradition” suppressed by Rhenanus, Semler.]. Let someone dare, then, to say that those who handed it down were in error!

## CHAPTER XXIX

[Col. 0041A]

### That it is rather the heresies which have erred, since they are later than the true doctrine.

[Note: [Col. 0041C] “However the error may have occurred.” With the strongest reason I can answer this very thing to the supreme critics of our time, who do not fear to say that the Church has hitherto erred: “If the truth awaited certain Lutherans, Calvinists, or Anabaptists to set it free,” etc. PAM.]

However the error may have occurred, error undoubtedly reigned [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Erred,” Junius, Semler.] for as long as the heresies did not exist [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Are in error,” Junius.]. The truth awaited certain Marcionites and Valentinians to set it free. Meanwhile, the Gospel was preached wrongly, believed wrongly [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Was believed wrongly” omitted by Semler.]; so many thousands upon thousands were baptized wrongly; so many works of faith administered wrongly [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Ministered,” Semler alone against all the others.]; so many mighty works, so many spiritual gifts [Note: [Col. 0041C] “So many mighty works, so many spiritual gifts.” There is no mention here of virtue or charity, but of the power to perform miracles and of the gifts of the Holy Spirit. ALBASP.] wrongly exercised; so many priestly offices, so many ministries [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Mysteries,” Rigault, from Agobard’s book.] wrongly discharged; and, finally, so many martyrdoms crowned in vain [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Wrongly and in vain,” with “crowned” omitted, Semler; “wrongly crowned,” Pamelius, Rigault.].

Or, if these things occurred neither wrongly nor in vain [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Or, if neither wrongly nor in vain” omitted by Semler; “not wrongly,” Venice, de Gourcy.], what sense does it make that the things of God should have run their course [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Should care,” Semler.] before it was known to which God they belonged; that there should have been Christians before Christ was found, and heresy before [Col. 0041B] true doctrine? Yet in everything truth precedes its image; the likeness follows the reality afterward. Moreover, it is absurd enough that heresy should be considered prior in doctrine [Note: [Col. 0041B] “Prior to that doctrine,” Junius; “the prior doctrine,” Wouwer, from [Col. 0041C] an old manuscript.], merely because it is the very doctrine that foretold future heresies and warned that they must be avoided [Note: [Col. 0042B] “Was foretelling,” Venice.]. It was written to the Church belonging to that doctrine—indeed, the doctrine itself writes to its own [Note: [Col. 0042B] “Its own” absent from Rigault, Venice.] Church: “Even if an angel from heaven should preach a gospel other than what we preached to you, let him be anathema” (Gal. 1:8).

## CHAPTER XXX

### That Marcion and Valentinus at first believed the catholic doctrine in the Roman Church under the episcopate of Eleutherus; until, having been expelled once and again because of their restless curiosity, they were finally banished into perpetual separation and disseminated the poisons of their doctrines. And that the same judgment applies to others who cannot prove by mighty works and miracles that they are new apostles.

Where was Marcion then, the Pontic shipmaster and student of Stoicism? Where was Valentinus, the follower of the Platonic sect? ## CHAPTER XXXI

[Col. 0044A]

### That what was handed down earlier is therefore true and from the Lord, while what is later is false.

But I shall return from this digression to discussing the priority of truth [Note: [Col. 0044D] “I shall return to the priority of truth and the posteriority of falsehood.” By “priority” he means what existed immediately from the beginning; by “posteriority,” what crept in afterward. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0044C] “To truth,” Franeker.] and the posteriority of falsehood [Note: [Col. 0044C] “To be assigned to falsehood,” Semler.]. Support is also supplied by that parable which places the good seed of wheat, sown by the Lord, at the beginning [Note: [Col. 0044C] “Places first,” Paris, François; “restores in the first place,” Semler.], but afterward introduces the adulteration of barren grass—the tares—by the enemy, the devil (Matt. 13:37, 39) [Note: [Col. 0044C] “Adulterous,” Venice.]. For it specifically symbolizes the distinction between doctrines [Note: [Col. 0044C] “For its part,” Semler.], since elsewhere too the word of God is likened to seed.

Thus the very order makes it manifest that what was handed down first is from the Lord and true, while what was introduced later is foreign and false. This judgment will stand against every later heresy, [Col. 0044B] since no steadfast assurance arising from conscience belongs to them [Note: [Col. 0044C] “And conscience grants,” Semler; “the conscience of steadfastness grants,” Junius.] for defending the truth as their own.

## CHAPTER XXXII

### If, meanwhile, certain heresies have existed from the apostolic age itself, they must be met with the prescription that they disclose the succession of their bishops, running down through successive appointments from the beginning, so that their first bishop has some apostle or apostolic man as predecessor, as in the Churches of Smyrna and Rome. Since they cannot prove this, they are not to be received into communion.

Moreover, if any heresies dare to insert themselves into the apostolic age, so that they may therefore appear to have been handed down by the apostles because they existed under the apostles, we can say: Let them publish [Col. 0044C] the origins of their Churches. Let them unroll the succession of their bishops, running down through successive appointments [Note: [Col. 0044D] “Through successive appointments.” Nothing in the whole doctrine of the ancients is clearer than the succession of the Roman pontiffs; you may learn the rationale of this from St Epiphanius, *Heresies* 27, the question of the Carpocratians. LE PR.] from the beginning [Note: [Col. 0044C] “Running down,” ablative, Semler.], so that their first bishop had as his author and predecessor one of the apostles, or one of the apostolic men who nevertheless persevered with the apostles [Note: [Col. 0044C] “Who persevered,” plural, François.].

[Note: [Col. 0044D] “For in this manner the apostolic Churches present their registers.” “Their registers” means their origins. Thus in book I *Against Marcion*, he called the Church “of apostolic register”; and in *On Monogamy*: “Our register is transferred to Christ.” RIG.] For this is the manner in which the apostolic Churches present their registers [Note: [Col. 0044D] “They present their registers.” Interpreters here think that “registers” must be understood as authority and dignity; I, however, believe that it signifies origin, as may be gathered from various passages and from these words, “every kind is registered according to its origin,” [Col. 0045D] and from *Against Marcion*, book IV, ch. 5: “If you inquire into their register.” ALBASP.]. Thus the Church of the Smyrnaeans [Col. 0045A] [Note: [Col. 0045D] “Thus that of the Smyrnaeans,” etc. Irenaeus, book III, relates that Polycarp was appointed by the apostles in the Church at Smyrna. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0045C] “Having” added by François, Paris.] reports that Polycarp was installed by John. Thus the Church of the Romans [Note: [Col. 0045D] “Thus that of the Romans, Clement.” Jerome, *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*: “Clement was the fourth bishop of Rome after Peter, for Linus was second and Cletus third, although most Latin writers think Clement was second after the apostle Peter.” Irenaeus had related that the blessed apostles entrusted to Linus the episcopate of administering the Church; that Anacletus succeeded Linus; and that after him, in the third place from the apostles, the episcopate was conferred upon Clement. RIG.] declares [Note: [Col. 0045C] “Likewise,” Venice; “the same,” Semler.] that Clement was ordained by Peter [Note: [Col. 0045D] “Clement ordained by Peter.” As though Clement exercised the episcopate at Rome while Peter was still alive, and apostolic men were everywhere assigned to various episcopates while the apostles were still performing their apostolic office. Yet we read that immediately after the Lord’s Passion, James the apostle, the Lord’s brother, was appointed bishop of Jerusalem by the apostles themselves, while Peter was already exercising the apostolate in a preeminent manner. Chrysostom says that this occurred because Christ had appointed Peter διδάσκαλον τῆς οἰκουμένης (*didaskalon tēs oikoumenēs*, “teacher of the inhabited world”) (homily on John, ch. 21). This passage of Tertullian plainly shows that the lines from which some think the succession of Roman bishops can be established with certainty are not Tertullian’s. RIG.]. In the same way, of course, the other Churches exhibit those whom they have as shoots of the apostolic seed, men appointed to the episcopate by the apostles [Note: [Col. 0045C] “The apostles,” nominative, Semler.] [Note: [Col. 0045C] “Have,” Latinus, Junius.].

Let the heretics fabricate something of this kind. For what is unlawful to them after blasphemy? Yet even if they fabricate it, they will accomplish nothing. For their doctrine itself, when compared with the apostolic doctrine, will pronounce by its own diversity and contrariety that it has neither an apostle nor an apostolic man as its author. For just as the apostles would not have taught doctrines differing from one another, so apostolic men would not have published doctrines contrary to the apostles—unless those who learned from the apostles afterward preached differently [Note: [Col. 0045C] “Defected and preached,” Semler, de Gourcy.].

They will therefore be tested [Col. 0045B] according to this standard [Note: [Col. 0045C] “Challenged,” Semler.] by those Churches which, although they can present neither an apostle nor an apostolic man as their founder—being much later Churches that are, indeed, established every day—are nevertheless reckoned no less apostolic because they agree in the same faith, [Note: [Col. 0046D] “Because of the consanguinity of doctrine.” Doctrines handed down by one and the same author are consanguineous, as though born of one parent. Thus he himself, in book I of *On the Dress of Women*, spoke of “consanguineous materials.” So too Calcidius speaks of “domestic and consanguineous reasons.” RIG.] because of their consanguinity of doctrine.

Accordingly, when all heresies are challenged by our Churches according to both standards, let them prove themselves apostolic in whatever way they think possible. But they are not apostolic at all; they cannot prove what they are not; nor are they received into peace and communion by Churches that are apostolic in any sense, precisely because, [Note: [Col. 0046D] “Because of the diversity of the mystery.” Here too I shall depart from the interpreters’ judgment when they say that by “mystery” (*sacramentum*) one must understand the oath that the faithful pronounced at the threshold of the Church shortly before they were washed. ## CHAPTER XXXIII

### Moreover, that they were already both identified and repudiated by the apostles at that time, and that all the remaining heresies took their seeds from them. [Col. 0045C]

Beyond these arguments, I apply an examination of the doctrines themselves that existed under the apostles and were both identified and repudiated [Note: [Col. 0045C] “Repudiated,” François.] by those same apostles. For in this way they will be exposed more easily, when they are found either to have existed already at that time or to have taken their seeds [Col. 0046A] from those which already existed then.

Paul, in the First Epistle to the Corinthians (15:12), censures those who deny or doubt the resurrection. This opinion is peculiar [Note: [Col. 0045D] “First,” Semler, Pamelius.] to the Sadducees; Marcion, Apelles, Valentinus, and any others who undermine the resurrection of the flesh appropriate part of it.

Writing to the Galatians (5:2), he inveighs against the observers and defenders of circumcision and the Law: this is the heresy of Ebion [Note: [Col. 0046C] Semler adds “thus.”].

In instructing Timothy (1 Tim. 4:3), he also brands those who prohibit marriage: Marcion and his follower Apelles teach accordingly.

He likewise touches upon those who said that the resurrection had already taken place (2 Tim. 2:18): the Valentinians affirm this of themselves [Note: [Col. 0046C] “Thus of themselves,” Semler.].

And when he mentions endless genealogies (1 Tim. 1:4), Valentinus is recognizable. In his system, some Aeon or other, of a new and compound name, begets Mind and Truth from his Grace [Note: [Col. 0046C] “And from his love,” Semler.]; these in turn procreate two others, Word and Life; then these generate Man and Church. This is the first Ogdoad [Col. 0046B] of Aeons [Note: [Col. 0046C] “This first [group] is therefore a Decad,” Semler; “in the first Ogdoad,” Paris, François; “of which this is the first Ogdoad, thus far,” Semler.]. From these arise ten other and twelve remaining Aeons with extraordinary names, making a sheer [Note: [Col. 0046C] “Extraordinary,” others.] fable of thirty Aeons.

When the same apostle condemns those who serve the elements, he exposes something of Hermogenes [Note: [Col. 0046C] “A certain Hermogenes,” Semler.], who introduces unbegotten matter and compares it with the unbegotten God. Thus, by making the mother of the elements a goddess, he can serve that which he compares with God.

In the Apocalypse, John commands that those who eat food sacrificed to idols and commit sexual immorality be chastised [Note: [Col. 0046C] “Is commanded,” Paris, Venice, François, Rigault.]. There are others of this kind even now—the Nicolaitans; it is called the Cainite [Note: [Col. 0046D] “Gaian,” Semler.] heresy.

But in his Epistle he particularly calls those antichrists who denied that Christ had come in the flesh and those who did not think Jesus was the Son of God. Marcion claimed the former; [Col. 0046C] Ebion the latter.

The teaching of Simonian magic, moreover, since it served angels, was itself of course reckoned among the idolatries and was condemned by the apostle Peter in Simon himself.

## CHAPTER XXXIV

### And consequently, since these heresies share fellowship with those others in their preaching, they also share fellowship in their condemnation—especially since they were foretold by the apostles. [Col. 0047A]

These, as I judge, are the classes of adulterated doctrines which we learn from the apostles themselves existed under the apostles. Yet among so many varieties [Note: [Col. 0047C] “As varieties,” Semler.] of perversity we find no system that raised a controversy about God, the Creator of the universe.

[Note: [Col. 0047D] “No one dared [suppose] another God,” etc. Here, says Quintinus, you see the advance or progression of the heretics, concerning whom the maxim from the philosophical school is most truly spoken: “A small error at the beginning is greatest at the end.” Twice at the Synod of Gangra we attempted to declare that these foxes are tied together by their tails: namely, that someone guilty of one heresy gradually becomes guilty of the others and is finally burned altogether; for they are linked in a chain. PAM.]

No one dared even to imagine another God. There was doubt about the Son more readily than about the Father, until Marcion introduced, besides the Creator, another God of goodness alone; Apelles made some glorious being of the higher God, the creator of the angels [Note: [Col. 0047C] “An angel,” Semler.], into the God of the Law and of Israel, affirming that he was fiery; and Valentinus scattered abroad his Aeons and traced the origin [Col. 0047B] of God the Creator to the fault of one Aeon.

To these alone—and to these first—was the truth of the Divinity revealed! They had evidently obtained greater favor and fuller grace from the devil, who wished to rival God even in this respect: from doctrines of poison, he himself made disciples superior to their master, something the Lord denied.

Let all the heresies therefore choose for themselves their respective periods—whenever each may have existed, provided that [Note: [Col. 0047C] “Provided not,” Rhenanus; “then not,” Junius; “not,” Wouwer.] it makes no difference which existed when, since [Note: [Col. 0047C] “Since” absent from Rhenanus.] they do not belong to the truth. Those which were not named by the apostles [Note: [Col. 0047C] “Were not under the apostles,” Rhenanus.] certainly cannot have existed under the apostles [Note: [Col. 0047C] François adds “to say that they”; “under the apostles” absent from Rhenanus.]; for if they had existed, they too would have been named, so that they too might be restrained. Those which did exist under the apostles are condemned under their own names [Note: [Col. 0047C] “Are deemed worthy,” Rhenanus.].

Therefore, whether the present heresies are those same heresies in a somewhat more polished form [Note: [Col. 0047D] “Are in some degree more polished.” You hear, says Quintinus, that heresies are crude at first and afterward become more polished. For forty years heresies likewise began to be renovated in such a way that the more skilled a heretic appeared in some sort of Greek and Latin learning, the more applauded and accepted he became: “He speaks purely, elegantly, and neatly.” Hence they gather followers of that eloquence. Heresy, then, is pure, polished, and neat! It is highly reprehensible [Col. 0048C] that even serious theologians are carried away by those expressions and prettinesses of human eloquence. They ask whether the diction is good. Yet we ought to remember that there is no good diction in any Christian which is not at the same time a benediction in Christ Jesus. Let us leave those fine dictiones and rhetorical flourishes to grammarians such as Cresconius, as Augustine did. They do not befit those who handle sacred things sincerely, any more than white lead befits a matron or wrestling oil an ox. PAM.] which were crude under the apostles, they possess their condemnation from that time onward. Or whether the former heresies were one thing and others afterward arose [Col. 0047C] [Note: [Col. 0047C] “Arose” added by François, Paris.], but appropriated certain things from the former [Note: [Col. 0047C] “Certain ones among them,” Rhenanus.], then, because they share fellowship with them in preaching, [Note: [Col. 0048D] “By sharing fellowship with them.” Gelasius I, says the same Quintinus, an African, writes something similar to Tertullian: “Whoever falls into a heresy once condemned involves himself in its condemnation,” 24, q. 1, c. 1. The definitions of the Council of Chalcedon and others have the same bearing, where it is expressly stipulated that it is no longer permissible to doubt matters once defined, but that those who follow their heresy must be condemned with them and must share—as the author puts it—with “condemned heresies,” that is, as Quintinus interprets it, heresies already condemned. Compare *fictitium*, “fabricated,” from *fictum*, “a fabrication”; *donatitium*, “given”; *dedititium*, “surrendered”; and *conductitium*, “hired.” PAM.] they must also share [Col. 0048A] in their condemnation.

This is in addition to the foregoing boundary-line [Note: [Col. 0048C] “Cord,” Junius.] of posteriority, by which they would be prejudged on grounds of age alone even if they shared nothing with those already condemned [Note: [Col. 0048C] “Were shared,” Rhenanus.]. They are all the more adulterated because they were not even named by [Note: [Col. 0048C] “By” absent from Paris.] the apostles. From this it is established all the more firmly that these are the heresies which were at that time still being announced as future.
