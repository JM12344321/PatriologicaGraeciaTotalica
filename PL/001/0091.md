Although, as has been explained, Tertullian’s teaching on the supreme divinity and eternal generation of the Word and Son of God was entirely orthodox, certain persons—even defenders of this Catholic dogma—nevertheless boldly accuse [Col. 0837D] him of no slight error, but of the gravest. For if Petavius is to be believed, he surpasses the Arian heresy in impiety and absurdity (Petavius, vol. 2, *Theologia dogmatica*, book 2, chap. 5, p. 24). But that learned man was ultimately compelled to correct himself. Elsewhere, indeed, he acknowledges that not a few passages occur in the same Tertullian in which the force of the common teaching and dogma is apparent—a tradition that had permeated the minds of Christians (Idem, Preface to the cited volume, chap. 5). Nevertheless, he added there that orthodox faith in the Trinity is repeatedly bespattered in Tertullian’s writings with certain stains, which may have been daubed upon them through contamination by the foul Montanist heresy into which he defected. Yet although there he seems to mitigate rather than amend the severity of his judgment, [Col. 0838A] he is nevertheless not sufficiently consistent with himself. For he contends that the dogma of the Trinity was undermined not only in books written by Tertullian as a heretic, but also in others composed while he was still a Catholic. We, however, have sufficiently shown that this dogma was rightly asserted by Tertullian, whether Catholic or Montanist, and admirably defended against others who denied it.

Bull, however, branded this same Tertullian of ours, even where he argues against Praxeas, with another mark of censorious severity (Bull, *Defensio fidei Nicaenae*, sect. 3, chap. 10, nos. 5 and 11, pp. 403 and 416). For although he concedes that Tertullian thought rightly concerning the supreme divinity, eternity, and consubstantiality of the Son and Word of God—that is, the identity of his substance with the Father’s—he nevertheless appears to him to have mingled some dross into the golden veins, so to speak, of Catholic tradition, to have spoken quite absurdly, and to have trifled in the most august mystery of the Trinity and pursued foolish conceits. But who will readily persuade himself that Tertullian, a man of such gravity, trifles and talks nonsense about this mystery of the Trinity in the very book in which, by Bull’s own admission, he asserts and defends with all the powers of his mind that principal and greatest article of the Christian faith against the errors of Praxeas and other heretics? (Idem, ibid., § 2, chap. 7, no. 1, p. 150). And certainly we have already proved that the orthodox teaching is clearly taught and explained by him not only against Praxeas and other heretics, but also against the pagans, whom—as we have seen—he could easily have conciliated by concealing it.

[Col. 0838C] But lest we leave anything untouched, all those arguments brought against us by Petavius, Bull, and others must be examined. First, Petavius does not think it necessary to press the statement we read in our Author, that the Son, the Word of God, was brought forth by his Father and, as it were, embodied. For why should he press this? Have we not shown clearly enough that by the word “body” Tertullian understands nothing other than a substance of its own kind, a person, and a hypostasis? Nor have we shown any less clearly—and it will become still more evident from what remains to be said—that he most expressly asserted the Son of God to be pure spirit, proceeding from the understanding and thought of his eternal Father, and therefore incapable of having anything corporeal, or even the least corporeal admixture.

Petavius and others therefore think those statements [Col. 0838D] of far greater weight by which our Author appears to them to have denied that the substance of the eternal Father and of his Son is the same. For, they say, he openly teaches that the whole substance of God is in the Father, and only a certain portion of it in the Son. What, they say, could be clearer or more explicit than these words? “The Father is the whole substance, while the Son is a derivation and portion of the whole, as he himself professes: ‘BECAUSE THE FATHER IS GREATER THAN I’” (John 14:28). Tertullian therefore believed, they say, that the whole substance of the Godhead is in the Father, as the greater, and that only a part and portion is in the Son, as the lesser (Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 9).

They press the point further from his assertion in the passage where he refutes the absurd error of Marcion, who babbled that the Father was born of the Virgin and had suffered: [Col. 0839A] “While not denying,” he says, “that his Christ, the Son and Spirit, is also the substance of the Creator, you must concede that those who did not know the Father could not have recognized the Son either, by reason of his being of the same substance, the fullness of which was not understood—much less its portion, certainly insofar as it shares in the fullness” (Tertullian, book 3, *Against Marcion*, chap. 6).

Finally, where he compares the generation of the Son of God with the sun and its ray, he speaks thus as well: “For God too is spirit; and when a ray is extended from the sun, it is a portion from the whole; but the sun will be in the ray, because it is the sun’s ray, and the substance is not separated but extended” (Idem, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 399). Petavius therefore concludes from all this that, in Tertullian’s view, the Son of God possesses not the whole divine substance but only a [Col. 0839B] portion of it, and consequently that the substance and nature of the eternal Father and of his Son are not the same.

But if that is how matters stand, Tertullian certainly contradicted himself in the most foolish fashion. For we saw in the preceding articles how often, how consistently, and how expressly he affirms that the substance in the Father and the Son is wholly one, undivided, and the same. But if the Son has only a portion or part of it, it is surely not the same in him as in the Father, but is divided from the whole as a part is (above, this chapter, art. 1). On the contrary, however, Tertullian, as we saw, committed these words to writing about him in another book against Marcion: “The Creator delivered all things to one who was not lesser than himself” (Idem, book 4, *Against Marcion*, chap. 25). But if the Son received only one portion of the divine substance from the Father, he is undoubtedly lesser through being deprived of the remainder. [Col. 0839C] Did Tertullian, then, both assert and deny that the Son of God is lesser than his Father? Who would believe this?

And what of the fact that in the passage against Marcion brought against us, the Son is indeed called a portion of the divine substance, but there is immediately added, “certainly insofar as it shares in the fullness”? But if he is a sharer, associate, and participant in the divine substance—which is most simple and, as we have observed that Tertullian himself says repeatedly, is not separated in him and the Father—he surely possesses it in its entirety.

Furthermore, if only a portion or some part of the divine substance is in the Son, the Father himself does not possess it entire. For a part of it will be in the Son and detached from the Father. In these words, therefore—“The Father is the whole substance, [Col. 0839D] while the Son is a portion”—an entirely evident contradiction will be found. For the whole of one and the same thing, especially a most simple thing, cannot be in one, and a part of it in another. Since no one, therefore, can convict Tertullian of so absurd a contradiction, what remains except to say that by the word “portion” he did not understand some part of the divine substance, which he most consistently affirmed can neither be divided nor separated?

In what sense, then, someone will ask, does he take the word “portion”? We shall readily allow it to be proved that it is taken in the same signification as partition, or participation and communication of the divine substance, which the Father derived into the Son. For in the very passage against Praxeas brought against us, the Son is called [Col. 0840A] “a derivation and portion of the whole,” and those words appear there to be synonymous. That derivation, however, is called a “portion,” or “partition,” because the Father communicated his whole substance to the Son in such a way that he nevertheless remained distinct from him in person. For since the persons of the Son and Father are distinguished from one another, and the Son received from the Father his own person together with the whole of that same divine substance, he is therefore called its “portion,” or “derivation.”

That this, moreover, is Tertullian’s true meaning may be gathered from another argument of his against the same Praxeas. For that heretic and others, who foolishly supposed the Father and the Son to be the same, contended that these words of Luke the Evangelist—“The Spirit of God will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you”—signified precisely the same thing as if he had said: “The Son of God [Col. 0840B] is God, and the power of the Most High is the Most High” (Luke 1:35 [the source reads “25”]). Tertullian attacks this heretical interpretation as follows: “If this,” he says, “had been the Evangelist’s meaning, he would surely have written: ‘God will come upon you, and the Most High will overshadow you’” (Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 26). But he did not write so; rather, by saying “the power of God”—not naming God directly, that is, in the nominative case—he intended a portion of the whole to be understood, which was to pass into the name of the Son. He therefore said that by these words, “the Spirit and power of God,” a portion is understood, because the name of God, taken not in the nominative but in the genitive case, signifies the portion—that is, the generation and derivation—of the Son from his Father. For by that derivation or generation, although he received the whole divine substance, his person is nevertheless not the same as the Father’s. Hence [Col. 0840C] our Author continues the discourse he had begun as follows: “Just as, therefore, the Word of God is not the very one whose Word he is, namely the Father, so neither is the Spirit, although he has been called God, nevertheless the very one whose Spirit he is called. . . . And therefore the Spirit of God is God, and the Word of God is God, because he is from God, yet he is not the very one from whom he is. But if he is God from God, as a substantial entity, he will not be the very God, but is God thus far: because he is from his substance, by which he is both a substantial entity and, as it were, some portion of the whole.”

Do you hear how the Son and Word of God is there openly denied to be “the God of God,” but is said, like his Father, to be true God? Yet although he is true God and his substance is the same as the Father’s, because he came forth and was begotten from the Father’s thought and intellection, he is distinguished from him in person. By this generation, however, he is called [Col. 0840D] “a derivation and portion of the whole”—that is, a partition and communication—but one by which, as Tertullian says, “he shares in the fullness”; that is, he possesses the whole and same substance and divinity of the Father from whom he arose.

Nor do we think those persons should be heard who will insist: if this is Tertullian’s meaning, then he takes the word “portion” in a wholly improper and entirely unusual expression. For Tertullian sprinkled his books, as no one fails to know and as we have often observed, with barbarous, improper, and unusual words and terms. For—to omit now the very many examples encountered everywhere in all his books—should he not, in this very *Apologeticum* about which we are disputing, which he addressed to pagan magistrates, have spoken more Latinate, elegantly, and correctly than in his other works? [Col. 0841A] Yet we have already noted, and shall still note, several barbarisms and improper and unfamiliar modes of expression in it. He employed the word “portion,” however, in the sense we have noted in order to refute more effectively Praxeas, who blathered that the Son was in no way distinguished from the Father in person.

## ARTICLE VII. Other arguments are answered by which it is objected that Tertullian denied the divinity of the Son of God, because he said that the Son was lesser than the Father, and that the Father was visible and the Son invisible.

They think another argument for Tertullian’s denial or undermining of the supreme divinity of the Son of God can be drawn from these words of his: “The Father is the whole substance, [Col. 0841B] while the Son is a derivation and portion of the whole, as he himself professes: ‘The Father is greater than I.’ By him he is also sung in the psalm as having been made a little lower than the angels. Thus the Father is other than the Son, insofar as he is greater than the Son” (Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 9). Does he not therefore, they say, openly signify there that the whole substance of the Godhead is in the Father, who is greater than the Son, and only some portion of it in the Son, who is for that reason lesser than he? Did he really say, however, that the Son was lesser than the Father, when, as we showed above, he asserted that the Son is equal to the Father, is made equal and equated with him, is joined to him, and is not lesser than him, because he himself had said: “I and the Father are one”? (above, this chapter, art. 1). Have we not discussed the statement he made before the words brought against us: “And never separated from the Father, or other than the Father, because: ‘I and the Father are one.’ This will be the projection [Col. 0841C] of truth, the guardian of truth, by which we say that the Son was brought forth from the Father, but not separated from him”? (cited book, chap. 8). Must he therefore be condemned for a nauseating contradiction such as could never be forgiven anyone? Was his mind ever so estranged from reason that he said the Son was lesser than his Father and not lesser, equal and not equal, one with him and not one—unless under different aspects? And certainly we have sufficiently proved how expressly he taught that there is in the Father and the Son one most simple nature and substance, which can neither be separated nor divided. But because Praxeas endeavored to infer from this that their person also was the same, our Author therefore shows that they are distinguished not in nature, but in person, because the Son was brought forth and begotten by his Father. Hence [Col. 0841D] immediately after the very words brought against us he added: “Thus too the Father is other than the Son, insofar as he is greater than the Son; insofar as the one who begets is other than the one who is begotten, the one who sends other than the one who is sent, and the one who makes other than the one through whom something is made. It is well that the Lord also used this word with respect to the person of the Paraclete, signifying not division, but arrangement: ‘For I will ask the Father,’ he says, ‘and he will send you another Advocate, the Spirit of truth.’ Thus he called the Paraclete other than himself, just as we call the Son other than the Father, so that he might show a third degree in the Paraclete, just as we show a second in the Son, for the preservation of the economy” (ibid., chap. 9).

He gives two reasons there why the Father is called greater and [Col. 0842A] the Son lesser. The first is that the Father sent the Son and the Son is sent by the Father. But this appears to concern the Son’s human nature, by which no one denies that he is lesser than his Father. The second is that the Son is begotten by his Father and received his divine nature from him. But by this generation he has the second degree and order after the Father, by which he can indeed be called lesser than him, but only, as Tertullian says, “for the preservation of the economy,” so that not division but arrangement is signified. Thus, on both those grounds, the Son could be called by him lesser than the Father and other than him, as one begotten by him.

Nor, I ask, should you reply that this explanation is not orthodox. For the ancient Fathers of the Church, even after the Council of Nicaea, interpreted in this very sense those words of Christ: [Col. 0842B] “The Father is greater than I.” Hilary, indeed—as vigorous a defender of Catholic teaching as ever there was—wrote without the least hesitation: “The Father is greater than the Son, but as Father than Son, by generation, not by kind. For he is the Son and came forth from him” (Hilary, *Tractate on Psalm 118*, § 17). We know, indeed, that the same words of John are elsewhere explained by him (Idem, book 9, *On the Trinity*, § 51), and by others also, of the human nature assumed by the Son of God. But it is enough for us that Hilary and several others later than the Nicene Synod, as we have noted elsewhere, also understood them of the divine generation of the Son of God himself, by which he is second in order after his Father (*Notes on Ambrose, On the Faith*, book 2). For it is established that nothing can be inferred from this against our Tertullian, who, since he did not contradict himself, is not without reason thought to have understood [Col. 0842C] the Evangelist’s words in this Catholic sense. And certainly he could interpret them thus all the more readily because he was less able to foresee the tasteless cavils of the Arians and was thereby more effectively refuting Praxeas’s tasteless error.

Nevertheless, they again object against us that Tertullian, in his books against Marcion, denied that the substance of the eternal Father and of the Son is the same (Petavius, vol. 2, *Theologia dogmatica*, book 1, chap. 5). For there he teaches that the Son was made and is visible, whereas the Father was neither visible nor made. He therefore thought their substance was different. To prove this, they bring against us these words of his: “We profess that Christ always acted in the name of God the Father; that it was he who from the beginning held converse and met with the Patriarchs and [Col. 0842D] Prophets—the Son of the Creator, his Word, whom by bringing him forth from himself he made Son, and thereafter set over his every arrangement and will, making him a little lower than the angels, as it is written in David; by which abasement he was also fitted by the Father for those very things that you censure as human, learning already from the beginning, already from that time, to be the man whom he was to become in the end. It is he who descends, he who questions, he who swears. As for the Father, the common testimony of the Gospel will attest that he was seen by no one . . . determining that the Father is invisible, in whose authority and name the one who was seen, the Son of God, was himself God” (Tertullian, book 2, *Against Marcion*, chap. 27).

But it must be observed that Tertullian is there arguing against Marcion, who, as he says in the same place, wrongly [Col. 0843A] proclaimed that the Creator was not the good God, since many things paltry, weak, and unworthy of God were proclaimed of him (ibid.). But Tertullian replies to this heretic that these lowly characteristics are indeed in Christ, though not insofar as he is God, but insofar as he is man. He proves, however, that Christ truly is God because he always acted in the name of the Father, because it was he who spoke with the Patriarchs and Prophets, because he is the Word of God and the Son of the Creator. Yet this same Tertullian most consistently asserted that the Son and Word of God is true God and the Reason of his Father, by which the Father is rational from eternity. He therefore believed the Son’s eternity to be the same as the Father’s, and likewise his substance.

But, you will say, in the same place he wrote with sufficient clarity: “whom by bringing him forth from himself he made Son, and thereafter [Col. 0843B] set over his arrangement and will” (ibid.). Quite so. But is the Son there said to have been made at some point in time, or is his eternal generation denied? Certainly not. For Tertullian would then have confessed that there are two Gods: an eternal Father and a non-eternal Son, and thus would have fallen into the very error of Marcion whom he was attacking. When, therefore, he says that the Son of God was then made by his Father when he brought him forth from himself, his meaning plainly seems to be this: the Father made his Son when he brought him forth from himself—that is, from the matrix, as he calls it, and womb of his heart and intellect, or from his eternal reason, understanding, and thought; afterward, however, when he willed to create all things, he set him over his arrangement and will. But if anyone contends that by the words “from himself” is signified the same as “by sending him outside himself,” [Col. 0843C] his meaning will be: at the beginning of the world the Father brought the Son forth outside himself and made him, in that he made him manifest to creatures. For until the first creation of the world his divine generation, as we explained shortly before, lay hidden in the eternal bosom of the Father; at the beginning of the world it began to be recognized by rational creatures. Then, as our Author speaks and as we shall explain more fully below, his nativity was perfected and received a certain external completion.

Nor should you press the fact that the Father is called by him invisible, but the Son visible, and that the latter is therefore lesser than the Father and not properly called God. For nothing is more trivial or feeble than this argument. Indeed, in his book against Praxeas the same Tertullian openly declares: “The Father invisible because of the fullness of his majesty, [Col. 0843D] but the Son visible because of the measure of his derivation” (ibid., chap. 14). A little later, however: “We say that the Son too, in his own right, is invisible insofar as he is the Word and Spirit of God—by the condition of his substance even now—and insofar as he is God, Word, and Spirit; but visible before the flesh” (ibid.). He explains there in the following manner how the Son made himself visible before assuming flesh: “In former times God—that is, the Son of God—always appeared and was seen in a mirror and an enigma, in a vision and a dream, both by the Patriarchs and Prophets and even by Moses himself. And even if the Lord perhaps spoke face to face, nevertheless man would not see his face as it is, except perhaps in a mirror and an enigma. . . . . . . . Or was it indeed the Son who was seen, and was it thus his face? Yet this very thing was in a vision and dream, in a mirror and enigma, because the Word and Spirit cannot be seen except in an imaginary form” (ibid.).

[Col. 0844A] Who, after hearing these words of Tertullian, would not openly confess that he is so far from denying the Son’s divinity on the ground that the Father is called invisible and the Son visible, that he instead clearly proves from this against Praxeas that the same Son of God is true God, distinguished from his Father only by the measure of derivation—that is, by his person? Indeed, he even declares affirmatively that the same Son of God, insofar as he is Word and Spirit, was invisible in precisely the same manner as the Father. He nevertheless does not deny that before assuming flesh the Son presented himself to be seen by the Patriarchs; but in what manner, I ask? Only in a vision, he says, in a dream, in a mirror, in an enigma, and in what he calls an imaginary form.

[Col. 0844B] Perhaps there will be some who wonder that these objections are brought against the true teaching of our Tertullian concerning the divinity of the Son of God by Petavius, undoubtedly a most learned man (Petavius, vol. 2, *Theologia*, book 1, chap. 4). For he himself not only freely admits, but demonstrates from numerous testimonies of the Fathers of the Church, both before and after the Nicene Synod, that they most openly declared that the Son of God, although his divine nature, like the Father’s, cannot be seen, was nevertheless seen and beheld by the Patriarchs and Prophets, before he became man, under another, visible appearance (Idem, ibid., book 3, chap. 2, § 10 ff.). And what of the fact that Petavius himself considers it certain that not only the Son, but also the Father and [Col. 0844C] the Holy Spirit, appeared to human beings under an externally assumed appearance? From these points, duly proved, he concludes that the arguments the Arians drew from them against the Son’s divinity are utterly trivial and will be scorned by everyone. In our preceding dissertations, however, we showed that these visions in no way obstruct the supreme divinity of the Son of God, and why he rather than his Father should have presented himself to be seen by human beings (vol. 1, *Apparatus*, pp. 404, 593, and 689 ff.).

## ARTICLE VIII. It is shown once again how clearly Tertullian asserts the eternity of the Son of God; and the contrary arguments are dispelled by which some think it can be established that Tertullian taught that before the world was created the Son of God had no substance, and that his nativity was perfected when God undertook to create the world.

[Col. 0844D]

Having set forth and weighed in an equitable balance the force of those arguments by which Tertullian is wrongly accused of not acknowledging that the Father and the Son have the same substance, let us now come to those by which he is accused still more vehemently of having denied the eternity of the same Son of God. For if any credit is to be given to the most learned Petavius, in this respect Tertullian surpasses the Arian heresy in impiety and absurdity (Petavius, book 1, *Theologia dogmatica: De Trinitate*, chap. 5, p. 24). But if Tertullian himself rightly asserted, as we have seen, that the nature of the Son and the Father is the same, he undoubtedly believed the Son truly coeternal with his Father. [Col. 0845A] Anyone could readily perceive, from the passages we cited above and especially in the first article of this chapter, that this tenet of the Catholic faith is asserted and proclaimed by him in the clearest possible terms.

But let us now consider attentively, if you please, what he specifically determined concerning the Son’s eternity, and first how he defines the eternity of God: “What other origin,” he says, “does God have than eternity? What other origin does eternity have than always to have been and always to be, by the prerogative of having neither beginning nor end?” (Tertullian, *Against Hermogenes*, chap. 4). There, as you see, he not only defines what eternity is, but makes it so proper to God alone that it can belong to no other. [Col. 0845B] Yet, as we showed above, he no less clearly and distinctly teaches and proves that the Son of God is true God, one and the same God with his Father. He was therefore persuaded that the Son’s eternity is the same as the Father’s.

Indeed, this is not only our argument but Tertullian’s own, by which he demonstrates the eternity of the Son of God most luminously. Hear, I ask, his words, and you will confess this with us: “God,” he says, “must be believed immutable and incapable of change of form, as being eternal. Transformation, however, is the destruction of the former state. For everything whatsoever that is transformed into another thing ceases to be what it had been and begins to be what it was not. But God neither ceases to be nor can be anything else; the Word, however, is God, and the Word is Lord, and abides forever by persevering in his own form” (Idem, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 27). This Word, moreover, is the Word and Son of God, who, as Tertullian immediately added, [Col. 0845C] was made flesh. Who, then, ever asserted his eternity in clearer words or by a clearer argument? Did Tertullian therefore build up there with one hand what he had elsewhere destroyed with the other? Yet some do not shrink from charging our Author with so great a paradox.

Petavius, indeed, objects against us that Tertullian taught no less certainly and evidently that the Son of God had no substance before the beginnings of the world, but received it from his Father when he undertook to create the world. Petavius was not alone in thinking this; some others too believed it could be established from the following words of Tertullian: “As soon as God willed to bring forth into their own substances and forms those things which he had arranged within himself together with the reason and discourse of Wisdom, he first brought forth the Word himself, having in himself the inseparable forms, Reason [Col. 0845D] and Wisdom, so that through him all things might be made, through whom they had been conceived and arranged—indeed, already made insofar as they were in the mind of God. For this alone was lacking: that they should also be known and apprehended outwardly, in their own forms and substances. Then, therefore, the Word himself also assumes his own form and adornment, sound and voice, when God said: ‘Let there be light’” (ibid., chap. 6). From this Petavius thinks it can be gathered that such was Tertullian’s opinion concerning the generation of the Son of God: that it was not eternal, but temporal. He adds further that shortly before Tertullian had said that God, who by his nature is rational, possessed Reason within himself from eternity, [non-Latin text omitted], and the Word. But he contained this Word within himself, silently thinking and arranging with himself the things that he was [Col. 0846A] going to utter through the Word (ibid., chap. 5). At that time, therefore, this Word was only interior and did not yet subsist in himself. But when God began to fashion the world, he brought forth this Word and, by animating him, made him a substance and person. Tertullian therefore thought, they say, that the Son of God was not made before then.

But I truly do not know whether Petavius and the others who argue in this fashion paid sufficient attention to those words of Tertullian which immediately follow the very passages they bring against us: “This,” he says (ibid., chap. 6), “is the perfect nativity of the Word, when he proceeds from God, first established by him for thought under the name of Wisdom: ‘The Lord established me as the beginning of his ways.’ Thereafter begotten for effect: ‘When he prepared heaven, I was present with him’” (Proverbs 8:22 ff.). [Col. 0846B] “Thereafter, making him equal to himself, from whom by proceeding he was made Son; firstborn, as begotten before all things; and only-begotten, as alone properly begotten from God, from the womb of his very heart, in accordance with what the Father himself also testifies: ‘My heart has uttered an excellent word’” (Psalm 44:1).

For there he distinguished a twofold kind of nativity of the Son of God. The first, or first and properly so-called generation, is that by which the eternal Father brought forth Reason and the Word within himself before all creatures. The second, less properly so called, was his generation when the Father brought him forth at the beginning of the world—or, as our Author himself says, “begot him for effect,” that is, so that through him he might create all things. But the first generation was undoubtedly not ineffectual, and by it the Father begot a true Son. For as Tertullian says: “I may, without rashness, have established beforehand that even then, before the constitution of the universe, God was not alone, since he had in himself Reason, and in Reason the Word, whom he made second to himself” (ibid., chap. 5). [Col. 0846C] Who does not see, moreover, that Tertullian is there treating of the Word or Discourse of God before the creation of the world, while he was still within God his Father? Yet he clearly declares there that even then the Father was not alone, but had within himself this Word, distinct from his own person, whom he made second to himself and his Son—that is, whom he begot. Therefore before the constitution of the world, that is, from eternity, the Word or Son of God, as we observed above, had received his nature and substance from God the Father and was distinguished from the Father only in person.

[Col. 0846D] But the Father, Petavius argues, is said by Tertullian to have been, “before all things, God alone, because there was nothing external besides him” (Tertullian, cited passage). But we can scarcely, if at all, bring ourselves to believe that this is seriously objected by so learned and perspicacious a man. For Tertullian immediately added: “Yet even then he was not alone; for he had with him that which he had within himself, namely his Reason” (ibid.). Tertullian was therefore persuaded, as we showed above, that the eternal Father, before the world was created, or from eternity, both was alone and was not alone. For he was alone because he had nothing outside himself, but was not alone because he had within himself the Word, who is a person distinct from him and is his Son. We saw besides that the same Tertullian observed that the Greek term [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0847A] ought to have been rendered in Latin as “reason” rather than “word”: “Because,” he says, “God was not discursive from the beginning, but rational even before the beginning; and because the Word himself also, subsisting in Reason, shows Reason to be prior as his substance” (ibid.). By these words he plainly signifies that the term [non-Latin text omitted] can therefore be better rendered in Latin as “reason,” because God the Father was not “discursive” from eternity—that is, he had not brought the Word forth outside himself. Nevertheless, because he is rational by his very nature, he had Reason within himself from eternity, which is of the same substance as he, but is a second and distinct person, since the Father was not alone from eternity.

That this, indeed, is Tertullian’s true sense and meaning is established clearly from the fact that he is arguing there [Col. 0847B] against Praxeas, who babbled that the Son, the Word of God, was entirely the same as the Father and was not distinct from him. What, I ask, would he have gained against Praxeas if he admitted that the Son and Word of God did not subsist before the beginning of the world and was not a person distinct from the Father? By voluntarily abandoning the principal part of his case, he would have offered his throat to his adversary. But so far is he from conceding this that, on the contrary, he most consistently asserts that the Word of God was distinct from the Father before the creation of the world and from eternity, and was second to him.

But Petavius relents not at all and presses the point that Tertullian openly taught that the nativity of the Son of God was perfected when God created all things from nothing. Before then, therefore, he possessed neither origin, person, nor substance, [Col. 0847C] and consequently was not the Son of God (Petavius, vol. 2, *Theologia dogmatica*, book 1, chap. 5, p. 25). For these are his words in the passage cited, which we have already quoted (Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 7): “This is the perfect nativity of the Word, when he proceeds from God, first established by him for thought under the name of Wisdom: ‘The Lord established me as the beginning of his ways.’ Thereafter begotten for effect: ‘When he prepared heaven, I was present with him’” (Proverbs 8:22, 27). Therefore, Petavius says, the nativity of the Son of God is said by Tertullian to have been perfected only when God created through him, who is his Word, both the world and all things.

But that most learned man could have observed that our Author distinguishes a twofold Word of God the Father. For the first is inward to him, [Col. 0847D] which the Greeks call [non-Latin text omitted]; the other is external and brought forth, which they call [non-Latin text omitted] (Tertullian, cited passage). God the Father, Tertullian says, had arranged and conceived within himself, by that former inward Word, that he would create all things and bring them forth into their substances and forms. Then afterward, by sending this Word of his outside himself and outwardly, he created the universe. But we have already seen Tertullian assert, repeat, and inculcate that this inward Word was begotten by his Father from all eternity and, being therefore distinct from him in degree and order, is the second person of the most holy Trinity.
