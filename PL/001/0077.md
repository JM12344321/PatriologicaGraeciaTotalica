## ARGUMENT AND SYNOPSIS

[Col. 0697A] Scholars disagree concerning the date of the book *To Scapula*. Pamelius and Baronius taught that it was published by Tertullian in the year 203; others place it under Emperor Heliogabalus, in 217. It seems more probable, however, that this occurred in the intervening period, around the year 212, when Caracalla was ruling alone after his father’s death. Hence Tertullian mentions only one emperor in that book:

> We therefore honor the emperor in the manner permitted to us and advantageous to him, as a man second after God;

and again:

> Thus we also offer sacrifice for the emperor’s welfare—but to our God, who is also his God.

That Tertullian wrote this after the prolonged persecution that occurred under Severus’ reign is evident from his enumeration of five proconsuls who had persecuted the Christians and had themselves fallen into various calamities. By their example he exhorts Scapula to take care for himself, particularly since certain portents were seen at Carthage as soon as Scapula entered the province and condemned the Christian Mavilius of Hadrumetum to the beasts:

> In your case too we hope it was only an admonition, that after you had condemned Mavilius of Hadrumetum to the beasts, this affliction immediately followed.

[Col. 0697B] He relates what that affliction was in these words:

> Moreover, from last year’s rainfall it became evident what the human race had deserved—namely, that the flood of old also occurred because of human unbelief and iniquity. Those who saw them know what was threatened by the fires that very recently hung above the walls of Carthage throughout the night; those who have hardened themselves know what the recent thunderclaps proclaimed. All these are signs of God’s imminent wrath, which we must, by every means we can, both announce, proclaim, and pray may for the present remain local; for in its own time those who interpret its examples otherwise will experience it as universal and final. That sun too, at the assembly in Utica, when its light was wholly extinguished, was so truly a portent that it could not have suffered this from an ordinary eclipse, since it stood in its own altitude and station. [Col. 0697C] You have astrologers.

Then, to deter Scapula from shedding Christian blood, he recounts what other governors suffered:

> We must grieve that no city will escape unpunished for the shedding of our blood. Thus, under the governor Hilarianus, when people had cried out concerning the grounds of our cemeteries, “Let there be no burial grounds,” their own threshing-floors ceased to exist, for they did not gather their harvests.

After some matters related elsewhere, he adds:

> We can likewise set before you the ends of certain governors who at the close of their lives remembered that they had sinned by persecuting Christians. Vigellius Saturninus, who was the first here to wield the sword against us, lost his sight. Claudius Herminianus in Cappadocia, indignant that his wife had passed over to this sect, treated the Christians cruelly; afterward, when he alone in his governor’s residence was ravaged by plague [Col. 0697D] and seethed alive with worms, he would say, “Let no one know, lest the Christians rejoice in hope.” Later, recognizing his error in having caused some by torture to fall away from their resolution, he died almost a Christian. Caecilius Capella, at that catastrophe in Byzantium, cried out, “Christians, rejoice!” But even those who seem to themselves to have escaped unpunished will come to the day of divine judgment.

[Col. 0698A] He also adds examples of governors who dealt more mildly with Christians:

> How many governors, both firmer and more cruel, have turned a blind eye to cases of this kind! Cincius Severus, for example, himself provided Christians at Thysdrus with a way to answer so that they might be released. Vespronius Candidus released a Christian, as though he were merely a turbulent man, to satisfy his fellow citizens. Asper, after subjecting a man to mild harassment and immediately causing him to give way, did not compel him to perform the sacrifice; beforehand, in the presence of the advocates and assessors, he declared that he regretted having become involved in this case. Pudens too, when a Christian was sent to him, perceived from the written charge that the man had been subjected to extortion and released him; he tore up the charge and refused, in accordance with the imperial mandate, to hear a man’s case without an accuser. All these matters can be suggested to you both in an official capacity and by those same advocates who have themselves received benefits [Col. 0698B] from Christians, although they shout whatever they please. For one man’s clerk was delivered when a demon was casting him headlong; so too were the relative and the young child of certain others. And how many respectable men—to say nothing of common people—have been cured either of demons or diseases!

Next he relates the example of Emperor Severus, who had been cured by a Christian man through anointing with oil:

> Severus himself too, the father of Antoninus, remembered the Christians. For he sought out the Christian Proculus, surnamed Toparcion, the steward of Evodias, who had once cured him with oil, and kept him in his palace until the man’s death. Antoninus too knew him very well, having been reared on Christian milk.

Finally he warns Scapula to cease his investigation of Christians, lest all Carthage be filled with Christian blood:

> What will Carthage itself suffer when you decimate it, when each person recognizes among those there his own relatives and household companions; when perhaps he sees there men even of your own order, [Col. 0698C] matrons, and all the most prominent persons, as well as relatives or friends of your own friends? Spare Carthage, if not yourself; spare the province, which, upon witnessing your severity, has been made liable to extortion by both soldiers and each person’s enemies.

He says this because, once it became known that a governor was hostile to Christians, pagans were accustomed to attack them in bands with great fury, plunder them, and put them to death. He further observes in these words that persecution does not extinguish the Christian religion, but rather propagates it:

> Yet this sect will not fail; you should know that it is built up all the more whenever it seems to be cut down. For everyone who beholds such endurance is struck by some uneasiness and kindled to investigate what the cause may be; and when he has learned the truth, he himself immediately follows it.

Thus far Tertullian [Col. 0698D] *To Scapula*. LUMP.

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 0697D]

We neither shudder nor tremble with fear at the things we suffer from the ignorant, since we came to this sect having assuredly accepted its condition and entered into a covenant that we would approach these contests after pledging even our lives, desiring to obtain what God promises in return and fearing to suffer what he threatens against a contrary way of life. Finally, we contend against all your savagery, even breaking forth to meet it voluntarily, [Col. 0698D] and we rejoice more when condemned than when acquitted.

Therefore, we have sent this little book not because we fear for ourselves, but because we fear for you and for all our enemies, much more our friends. [Note: [Col. 0697D] “Not yet,” Rhen.] For by our discipline we are commanded to love even our enemies and to pray for those who persecute us, so that this goodness of ours may be perfect and peculiar to us, not common to all. To love friends belongs to everyone; to love enemies, however, belongs only to Christians.

Since, therefore, we grieve at your ignorance, pity human error, foresee [Note: [Col. 0698D] “And,” absent from Rig.] future events, and see their signs daily being held out before us, [Note: [Col. 0699C] “See them being displayed,” conjecture of Jun.] we must break forth, at least in this manner, to set before you those things that you are unwilling to hear openly. [Note: [Col. 0699C] **Those things that you are unwilling to hear openly.** These words plainly attest that this book of petition was not delivered into Scapula’s hands, but merely sent. LE PR.]
