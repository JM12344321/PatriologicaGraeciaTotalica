But there are very many of Tertullian’s books whose date eludes the acumen of investigators and which bear upon themselves nothing but certain traces of Montanist errors; yet from these it can scarcely be determined which books he wrote before, and which after, his defection to [Col. 0099D] the Montanists. Hence it has come about that those who investigate these matters more subtly have said, not inaptly, that Tertullian as a Montanist must be distinguished from Tertullian as Montanizing. But John William Hoffmann, formerly a most learned professor of history at the Academy of Wittenberg, thought otherwise. Since he held the opinion that all the surviving works of Tertullian had been written during his Montanism, he undertook to demonstrate this in a separate dissertation (Wittenberg, 1739). He, indeed, clearly perceived that, if this subtle distinction were admitted, his demonstration would suffer some loss, and therefore denied that the distinction could be admitted. “For it lacks definite boundaries,” he says (§ IV, p. 17), “and the appearance of one who is becoming a Montanist and of one who is a Montanist is no less ambiguous than that of a person beginning to fall ill and one who is ill.”

[Col. 0100A] For my part, however, I very much doubt whether Hoffmann understood—or wished to understand—what those who speak in this way mean. “The books of Tertullian as Montanizing,” he adds (p. 18), “are perhaps those which are indeed infected with the doctrines of Montanus but do not assail those who hold the correct view; while those of Tertullian as a Montanist are those which not only contain the heresy but also defend it against the ‘Catholics.’” But among us and, so far as I know, among all those who speak in this way, μοντανίζειν (“to Montanize”) is the term applied to one who indeed shares either all or some errors with the Montanists, yet has not, after rejecting communion in the same rites with his own Church, already gone over to the rites of that community with which he agrees in certain doctrines; whereas a Montanist is one who has done both. Hoffmann, however, says that these distinctions [Col. 0100B] lack definite boundaries. Let it be so. Let the appearance of Tertullian as a Montanist and as μοντανίζοντος (“one who Montanizes”) be exceedingly ambiguous. What follows, then? Evidently, it cannot be judged from Tertullian’s appearance whether he had already been confined to bed by the disease or had merely been lightly touched by one of its attacks. And this is precisely what we maintained: that one cannot safely infer from certain slight indications of errors shared by Tertullian with the Montanists that he had already embraced the Montanists’ rites themselves.

Yet we do not wish this to be understood as though every argument drawn from Montanist doctrines encountered in this or that book of Tertullian, in order to assign those books to his Montanism, were spurious or suspect. For in this matter there are boundaries and, as it were, landmarks which I think must be employed, [Col. 0100C] lest we appear to reason either too little or too much about recognizing Tertullian’s Montanism from his books.

We therefore wish the first rule for judging this matter to be the following: a book of this kind should be numbered among those which he composed after he had already been enrolled in the Montanist family if the name “Psychics” occurs in it. For this was, as it were, the watchword by which he distinguished the members of the orthodox Church from the members of his own sect, whom he calls “spiritual” (see Tertullian, *On Monogamy*, chap. I, ed. Nicolas Rigault, II, p. 673 R.); and it seems to have been coined at approximately the time when he began to attack those who did not accept the austere discipline of Montanus. Nor does it occur in any other book which considerations either of chronology or of doctrine itself show to have been written before his defection. Accordingly, there is no reason to doubt that all those books which contain this name were written [Col. 0100D] after he had embraced Montanism.

Let the second canon be this: every book of Tertullian which treats doctrines not indeed peculiar to the Montanists, but nevertheless confirmed by the authority of a certain Paraclete, should be regarded as having come from the workshop of Montanus. For this was the Montanists’ principal error: they boasted that in their own age a Paraclete had come who would both guide them into all truth and imbue his hearers with a new knowledge of divine things, so that they might know what had been revealed to no person of an earlier age and might now aspire to the highest perfection (see the same author in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chap. I, and book IV *Against Marcion*, chap. 22, p. 537 C). He says that it was his acknowledgment of this Paraclete that separated him from the Church (*Against* [Col. 0101A] *Praxeas*, chap. I, p. 634 D). Nor did members of the orthodox Church ever allow themselves to be persuaded of this error, because it was the principal doctrine of those who followed Montanus as their teacher, although all the other errors of the Montanists were accepted in some measure by certain members of the ancient Church, either during the controversy itself or shortly afterward.

But since we have said that Tertullian never abandoned the errors which he had once learned in the school of the Montanists, it follows of itself from what we have just stated that those books of Tertullian in which he advanced other [errors], undoubtedly tainted by Montanist tenets, belong to the same class. Other rules could be added to these, identifying books as Montanist with the utmost certainty, such as Tertullian’s own explicit declaration; [Col. 0101B] but rules of that sort are generally so constituted that they either require no discussion or are not of the most certain reliability. If, however, something is found in a particular book of Tertullian that can be referred to one of these rules, it may be judged without danger of error that the book was published at a time when Tertullian had already entered into hostility with the orthodox Church. But let us now come to the books themselves.

§ 5.—The First Class of Books, Written During the Earlier Harassment of the Christians under the Emperor Severus

Of these, we promised first to examine those whose date and doctrine were not especially doubtful. First among them must be placed those which are known [Col. 0101C] to have been written at the time when, during the reign of the Emperor Severus, Christians were afflicted with many calamities by the worshippers of the gods because of their doctrine. Since their date cannot properly be determined unless we fix, as it were, the precise point in time at which the savagery against the Christians began, we must make it clear to everyone when this occurred.

But because this harassment arose twice—first before, and then after, Severus’s laws hostile to the Christians—we have learned from observation that the two-hundredth year after Christ’s birth divides Tertullian’s books into, as it were, two classes: one comprising those completed by him while orthodox, the other those completed by him as a Montanist. Since, therefore, our purpose is first [Col. 0101D] to consider that class of Tertullian’s books whose date is sufficiently clear, we shall conduct this treatment by demonstrating first that Christians were already being harassed because of their doctrine before Severus’s edict; then, after offering a few observations on the character of this earlier storm itself, we shall inquire precisely into the year in which this affliction began.

§ 6.—This Harassment Itself Is Discussed, with a Demonstration That It Existed before Severus’s Edict; To Mosheim’s Arguments on This Point New Ones Are Added, and It Is Shown to Have Begun in A.D. 197

The reason many have erred in determining the date of those books of Tertullian which were written during the Severan harassment is undoubtedly that [Col. 0102A] they failed to see that, even before Severus’s law forbidding the adoption of the Christian religion, the worshippers of the gods had harassed Christians. Thus it happened that, since everyone agreed that this law had not been issued by Severus until A.D. 202, all these books were absurdly placed after that year. From time to time there were some who perceived that this conflicted with the books themselves; yet even they did not arrive at the truth, because they had not followed the ancient records of these events candidly and prudently as their guides.

John Laurence Mosheim, a man of immortal memory, ὁ μακαρίτης (“the man of blessed memory”), therefore deserves the highest praise. After investigating the matter more carefully with his characteristic acumen and the greatest candor of mind, he demolished that error with most carefully devised arguments.

> **Note [Col. 0102D].** In the *Dissertation on the Date of Tertullian’s Apologeticum and the Beginning of the Persecution of the Christians under Severus*, which is included in vol. I of his *Dissertations Pertaining to Ecclesiastical History*, pp. 1 ff. He had also promised to provide a life of Tertullian and a chronology of his books more accurate than that offered by Allix (see *Thesaurus Epistolicus Lacrozianus*, vol. I, p. 284); but more serious concerns belonging to his sacred office later deprived him of the opportunity to carry out that plan, as he admits in the additions to vol. I of the *Dissertations on Ecclesiastical History*, p. 752.

[Col. 0102B] By his diligence this great man ensured that we need not labor extensively in demonstrating the matter. Nevertheless, so that those who have not been able to read that most elegant dissertation may not be deprived of the weightiest arguments by which he assigned the Christians’ calamities to the period preceding Severus’s edict, we shall recite them in a kind of summary. We assent to them entirely, and shall add two others which appear to possess some force in confirming them.

His first argument was drawn from the fact that Tertullian himself, in the *Apologeticum*, and especially throughout that entire discussion of the laws, produced so many indications that Severus had not yet fought against the Christians by legislation that no room for doubt whatsoever remains.

His second came from the book *To the Martyrs*, chap. 4, in which Tertullian recalls [Col. 0102C] the punishments Severus inflicted upon the friends of Albinus—an event which Mosheim most correctly observed to have occurred in A.D. 196, or, as I should prefer, 197.

The third was supplied to him by the words of Clement of Alexandria (*Stromata*, book II, Cologne ed., p. 414 B), in which Clement acknowledges that Christians were already being subjected to torture when he was composing the books of the *Stromata*, which he did at approximately the same time we have mentioned.

The fourth was supplied by the date at which the most severe punishments suffered by the Scillitan Martyrs occurred, in the second consulship of Claudius, who held the fasces together with Gaius Aufidius Victorinus in A.D. 200. Finally, he thinks that some additional authority is lent to these arguments by the testimony of Orosius and Freculphus themselves.

We are certainly not among those who think that these arguments [Col. 0102D] are insufficient to demonstrate the point, for we believe that, taken together, they place the matter beyond every doubt. Nevertheless, lest we appear to have omitted anything that could contribute in some way to its confirmation, we have resolved to add two other arguments. Although we readily concede that they are weaker than Mosheim’s, they nevertheless do not appear deserving of complete rejection.

One is what Aelius Spartianus says about [Col. 0103A] Caracalla (*Life of Antoninus Caracalla*, chap. I): when he was a boy of seven and heard that a boy who was his playmate had been severely beaten on account of the Jewish religion, for a long time he would look neither at his own father nor at the boy’s father, nor at those who had ordered the beating. From this passage it appears that the boy’s father had not himself punished him because of the Jewish religion, but that Severus himself had ordered others to punish the boy. For how could Caracalla have been angry with his father Severus unless Severus himself had wished this to be done? There is accordingly some ground for suspecting that violence was already being employed against Christians when Caracalla was seven years old, although Severus did not issue laws against the religion of the Christians and Jews until A.D. 202.

Caracalla passed through his seventh year in A.D. 195, which is easy to demonstrate. For everyone knows that Severus died in A.D. 211 and that Caracalla, succeeding him, was killed after he had reigned for more than six years (Cassius Dio, *Roman History*, book LXXVII, ed. Reimar, vol. II, p. 1314), in A.D. 217. But when he died he had reached his twenty-ninth year. When that number is subtracted from the year of his death, [Col. 0103B] the year 188 emerges, in which he must have been born; whence it follows that Caracalla passed through his seventh year in A.D. 195, long before Severus’s edict.

Nor do I think anyone will object that the situations of the Christian and Jewish religions were altogether different, and that it cannot be demonstrated that Christians were harassed by the worshippers of the gods merely because those who had attached themselves to the Jewish religion were punished. For those who think thus [Col. 0103C] must imagine that even the common people already distinguished Christians sufficiently from Jews—for we say that this disturbance was instigated by the common people. Yet since, before the time when Severus took possession of the empire, this distinction was not observed even by those who were best able to know both religions, and since I do not know how anyone could prove that people in Severus’s time were more perceptive than before, shall we suppose that an excited mob under Severus’s rule took care over this point and distinguished them sufficiently? But even if that inference may be very doubtful, it is nevertheless evident that there were examples of pagan hatred against those who were estranged from the worship of the gods, and that Severus himself was not altogether averse to that hatred.

If Severus acted in this way toward [Col. 0103D] children, to whom we very often forgive things that are by no means conceded to adults, I scarcely doubt that governors, driven by greed and popular unrest, were induced to proceed more severely against Christians.

I shall take the second argument from the book Tertullian wrote *To Scapula* (chap. 4, p. 87 D): “Severus, knowing that most illustrious women and most illustrious men belonged to this sect”—that is, the Christians—“not only did not injure them, but even honored them with his testimony and openly resisted the people when they raged against us.” Severus cannot be said to have openly resisted the people when they raged against the Christians after he had already enacted an edict against Christians. The harsh and almost iron character of Severus prevents us from believing this; certainly no one could have prevailed upon him to rescind a law once enacted. And does not [Col. 0104A] the very fact that Tertullian says that the common people harassed the Christians, indeed against Severus’s wishes, demonstrate that this did not occur under Severus’s laws? Here, therefore, we have another by no means obscure testimony that, before his edict, the Christians were afflicted with the greatest calamities.

§ 7.—What Were the Causes of This Earlier Harassment?

But now we must examine that other question concerning the time at which this earlier storm occurred, after I have first stated what appear to have been the causes of this outrage by the common people against the Christians. It is established concerning every popular disturbance against the Christians that the people attributed to them, as public enemies of the human race, every kind of calamity sent by the gods. [Col. 0104B] It is, moreover, clear from Tertullian’s writings that this was the reason Christians were especially persecuted at this time (cf. *Apologeticum*, chap. 40; *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 9, etc.).

Indeed, the rivalry over the greatest matters which existed between Severus and Niger appears not only to have displeased certain people who had demanded Niger, rather than Severus, as emperor against Didius Julianus (see Herodian, book II, chap. 8; III, chap. 5; Spartianus, *Life of Niger*, chap. 2), but the war he undertook against Albinus also greatly agitated everyone’s minds (see Dio in Xiphilinus, book LXXV, chap. 4, p. 1258 B). Accordingly, after both had been overthrown and Severus was preparing to destroy all those whom he knew to have supported Niger and Albinus, [Col. 0104C] an excellent opportunity presented itself to the common people to attack the Christians as enemies of the commonwealth and of the emperor. These people imagined that they could do this all the more justly because they believed that the Christians were hostile even to the gods themselves, toward whom they thought they could render good service after the gods had created this period of comparative peace for them.

The Christians themselves further persuaded the common people of this very opinion by their complete unwillingness either to participate in the games and celebrations instituted on account of Severus’s victory over his enemies (see Tertullian, *On the Spectacles*, chap. 1 and elsewhere), or to swear by the genius of the prince (the same author, *Apologeticum*, chap. 32, and book I *To the Nations*, chap. 17), or to make supplication to the gods for his safety (idem, *Apologeticum*, chaps. 10, 29 ff.).

Through this very disturbance the common people could easily drive the governors to the same course, since the governors themselves were not especially averse [Col. 0104D] to this kind of cruelty. For they prescribed to themselves a rule handed down, as it were, from hand to hand: Christians who had once been summoned before the court were by no means to be released unless they abandoned their own rites (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chap. 21, ed. Reading, p. 240). As a witness to this we have Tertullian himself, who for this reason undertook a formal disputation against them concerning the correct interpretation of these laws (*Apologeticum*, chaps. 4 ff.).

Profit-seeking governors seized this opportunity to harass the Christians all the more readily because they clearly saw that it would bring no small advantage to their treasury. I know that I have not invented this from the example of the martyr Rutilius (in Tertullian, *On Flight in Persecution*, chap. 5, p. 693 A), whom I suspect to have been dragged before the court [Col. 0105A] while judgment was still being rendered not under Severus’s law but under the ancient laws.

> **Note [Col. 0105D].** This book was evidently written at a time when Christians had to expect more serious calamities from the worshippers of the gods (compare chap. 1), that is, as will be stated later, when Severus’s edict had been published and the Christians feared for their safety. But when Tertullian composed that little work, Rutilius had already been slaughtered. At first he had several times sought safety in flight; later he had purchased peace for himself with money; but, captured again, he returned to punishment. This must therefore have happened before Severus’s unjust law, and it supplies a new argument for what we have said: that under Severus Christians were condemned to death before that law.

For many Christians who feared for their wealth and their lives were thereby induced to ward off with an offer of money a danger which they could not avoid by another means. If we comprehend in our minds these causes of the cruelty of the governors and the common people, we shall indeed be able to determine the time at which this earlier storm arose once we have demonstrated when, and in what year, this specious opportunity to injure the Christians presented itself.

§ 8.—The Beginning of the Harassment of the Christians Is Determined from the Year in Which Albinus Perished; That He Was Killed in A.D. 197 Is Proved, First, from the Sequence of the War Which Severus Waged against Pescennius Niger. Along the Way, Certain Marks on Severus’s Coins Are Explained and Mosheim’s Arguments Are Corrected

[Col. 0105B] In investigating this date, let us first employ the very causes of the calamities which we have enumerated. For what provided the common people, as it were, with handles for attacking the Christian community was Severus’s eagerness to seek out all those whom he had learned to have sided with Niger or Albinus against him. Since he did this when, after Albinus had been destroyed, he returned victorious to Rome—for it was then that he decreed the most severe punishments against them (see Dio, book LXXV, chap. 7, p. 1262 D; Herodian, book III, chap. 8; and Spartianus, *Severus*, chaps. 12–13)—it is worthwhile to see in what year Albinus lost both his life and his rule.

We see that Mosheim has already devoted so much labor and care to determining this point (*Dissertation* cited above, § XX ff.) that we might almost dispense with the labor, were there not certain readily available considerations which appear capable of being urged against him. [Col. 0105C] Accordingly, since an opportunity here presents itself to call a certain popular opinion into question, and since the subject itself requires the calculations to be made somewhat differently, we shall retain the same class of arguments employed by that eminent man and candidly discuss what accords with the truth itself. Where we differ in opinion, we shall note below the reason for our disagreement, while preserving the reverence due to so great a man, whose name remains venerable to us even after his death.

When Severus came to power and saw Pescennius Niger preparing forces in Syria with which to oppose him, he first, lest he be pressed from both sides, made Albinus commander of the army in Britain, and decreed both coins and other tokens of honor for him. Afterward, having levied troops [Col. 0105D] throughout Italy and equipped a fleet, he set out against Niger in A.D. 193, when approximately the month of June had elapsed.

> **Note [Col. 0105D].** For Severus came to Rome at the beginning of June A.D. 193. This becomes clear when we count the sixty-six days which Dio assigns to the rule of Didius Julianus (book LXXIII, final chapter, p. 1240), or the two months and five days assigned to him by Spartianus (*Julianus*, chap. 9), beginning from the fifth day before the Kalends of April—that is, March 28—the day on which Julius Capitolinus reports that Pertinax was killed (*Life of Pertinax*, chap. 15). It is no less apparent [Col. 0106D] from this that Severus assumed control of the supreme power approximately on June 2. I shall not demonstrate at greater length that the terms of his tribunician power must likewise be reckoned from that day, since I recall that C. G. Schwarz, a most accomplished man, has done so in his dissertation *On Reckoning the Tribunician Power of the Roman Emperors and Caesars*, Observation IV. This being so, the time at which Severus departed from Rome is by no means obscure, provided Spartianus correctly records (*Life of Severus*, chap. 8) that he left Rome within thirty days of arriving there.

[Col. 0106A] When Severus’s generals engaged Niger’s soldiers in A.D. 194, they prevailed at Cyzicus. They enjoyed the same good fortune in the following year, when battle was joined in the narrows of Nicaea and Cius. Finally, in approximately A.D. 196, after the Severan forces had by chance occupied the fortress in the Taurus and broken into Cilicia, Niger’s army was defeated at the Cilician Gates, and not long afterward Pescennius himself was killed.

But here I suspect that some will wonder what moved me to set down calculations altogether different from those of others, since those who think that this last battle was fought in A.D. 194 are virtually unanimous. I therefore consider it incumbent upon me to produce sound arguments by which it can be proved that our calculations have been made entirely correctly.

First, then, I should like [Col. 0106B] the appellations *Imperator* III, IV, V, VI, VII, and VIII, which Severus is shown by the coins to have received, to be observed. He must have been honored with these during the interval which elapsed from the beginning of his reign to the time when he defeated Albinus, because we read that on assuming power he was styled *Imperator* II on the coins (see Vaillant, *Coins of the Roman Emperors*, vol. II, 3rd ed., Paris, p. 238), whereas, after Albinus was defeated, he was styled *Imperator* IX (in Mediobarbus, *Coins of the Roman Emperors*, under the year 197, ed. Milan, 1730, p. 271).

Now, since the Roman emperors were honored with the name *Imperator*, distinguished by numbers on coins, whenever they had accomplished something in war worthy of that title (on this subject see Cassius Dio, *Roman History*, book XL, chap. 44, p. 372), come, let us see [Col. 0106C] to what events this title of *Imperator*, repeated six times within that interval, must be referred. Within this period, of course, we read that Severus won three victories over Niger’s soldiers and subjected three peoples—the Osroenians, Adiabenians, and Arabs—to Roman power. Since he subdued the latter after Pescennius had already been defeated, let us refer the appellations *Imperator* VI, VII, and VIII to the defeat of these peoples.

> **Note [Col. 0106D].** On the coins cited by Mediobarbus under the year 196, p. 270, the appellations *Imp.* VI and VII are combined with *Tr. Pot.* III and the names *Parth. Arab.*, *Parth. Adiab.*, and other indications of victory. Likewise, *Imp.* VIII, p. 271, is combined with the Parthian victory and *Tr. P.* IV; and it appears with the same designations and the titles *Arabicus* and *Adiabenicus* in an inscription in Muratori, *New Treasury of Ancient Inscriptions*, vol. I, p. 244.

Let us say that the title *Imperator* III was granted to Severus because of the battle of Cyzicus, IV because of the battle in the narrows of Nicaea and Cius, and finally V because Niger was overthrown at the Cilician Gates. Let us now determine with what number of the tribunician power the individual marks of the imperial title are combined; for once we know this, the year to which [Col. 0106D] each battle must be assigned will present itself of its own accord.

> **Note [Col. 0106D].** But perhaps someone will say that these coins were not struck at the time when Severus returned victorious from the battles with Niger, but only afterward, and that Spartianus himself, in the *Life of Severus*, chap. 9, indicates that Severus did not write to the Senate about the matter until after the second battle in the narrows of Cius and Nicaea. But apart from the fact that Spartianus differs somewhat in his account from the other and more important historians, and in that passage speaks only of the first and second battles, it can scarcely be denied—since the appellations *Imp.* III, IV, and V are combined with entirely different numbers of *Trib. Pot.*—that this designation of tribunician power indicates the years in which he three times won victory over the enemy.

Yet the appellation *Imp.* [Col. 0107A] III, which we have said was a token of the victory at Cyzicus, is found combined with *Trib. Pot.* II (in Laurentius Berger’s continuation of the *Brandenburg Treasury*, p. 690; Vaillant, *Coins of the Roman Emperors*, vol. I, pp. 221–22; and Mediobarbus under the year 194, p. 269). This tribunician year must be reckoned from June 2, A.D. 194, to the same day of the following year (compare note y of this section and Observation II of Schwarz’s dissertation cited above). It follows, therefore, that this first battle must be assigned either to the latter part of A.D. 194 or to the earlier part of A.D. 195.

Moreover, a coin in Vaillant presents Severus bearing the title *Imp.* IV and holding tribunician power III, and there are several coins of this kind in Mediobarbus. Therefore, the second battle in the narrows of Nicaea and Cius fell either in the latter part of A.D. 195 or the earlier part of the following year. [Col. 0107B] Consequently, the appellation *Imp.* V and the final victory won over Niger can scarcely, it seems, be claimed for any year other than the one we have stated, A.D. 196.

Since Severus had therefore overthrown Pescennius approximately at the beginning of that year, he proceeded into Mesopotamia and from there led his army against the Osroenians, Adiabenians, and Arabs (Dio, book LXXV, opening). All these events must have been accomplished in approximately six months—unless one prefers to suppose that the last part of the preceding year brought death to Niger—since chronological considerations themselves suggest that, as will appear in its proper place, Severus had already set out against Albinus before this year ended.

But how could it have happened that in so few months he subdued so many great and highly warlike peoples? This can, in fact, readily be conceived. Dio himself, chap. 2 of the book cited, says that the cities of these peoples were captured by three of Severus’s [Col. 0107C] generals, not by Severus himself. This perhaps occurred when Severus had already gone to meet Albinus; for after his departure Laetus opposed these adversaries, as appears from Dio himself, chap. 9. Or it may have happened somewhat earlier, while Severus was pursuing Niger, for at that time he accomplished a great many things through his generals. Alternatively, Severus may have been content with some limited suppression of the barbarians.

Certainly Severus appears not to have wished to subject these peoples entirely to himself, nor to have done so at that time, since, after sending his generals against the enemy, he was occupied with other matters at Nisibis, and only after Albinus had been defeated did he at last assail these provinces with the utmost force. This could be confirmed by Herodian’s silence, for he has nothing whatsoever about the defeat of these peoples at this time.

Let this first rule therefore be established: Severus did not [Col. 0107D] set out against Albinus before the end of A.D. 196.

> **Note [Col. 0107D].** Here we do not venture to infer anything further from the sequence of events which we have related, but shall do so later. From what has been said, however, one may judge Mosheim’s view τῷ μακαρὶτῇ (“of blessed memory”) (*Dissertation* cited above, § 21): that Niger perished in A.D. 194; that in A.D. 195 Severus sent envoys into Gaul to kill Albinus; and that near the end of that year their plot was detected, whereupon Severus came to Gaul with the greatest speed in A.D. 196. I could scarcely believe—for I have already stated what must be thought about the dates of the battles—that those envoys remained inactive for almost an entire year before delivering the letters to Albinus; or that, when the plot was finally detected near the end of A.D. 195, Severus, whom Mosheim correctly describes as resembling one running rather than one merely setting out, either passed almost the whole of the year 196 in idleness or allowed Albinus enough time to gather his forces. It is much closer to the truth to say that Severus set out against Albinus near the close of A.D. 196 and allowed little time to pass before he could contend with Albinus in war.

§ 9.—That Albinus Was Killed in A.D. 197 Is Proved, Secondly, from the Date of the Capture of Byzantium

[Col. 0108A]

The date at which Byzantium was captured must be regarded as another and by no means insignificant weapon for accomplishing this demonstration. Dio says, book LXXIV, chap. 14, p. 1254, that it was occupied by Severus’s soldiers while Severus was staying in Mesopotamia—that is, after he had defeated Niger and before he proceeded against Albinus. The same Dio records, in the book cited, chap. 11, p. 1252, that the Byzantines were finally reduced by famine to surrender after they had been under siege for three complete years. We shall therefore know the year in which Severus was in Mesopotamia once we determine when that siege began.

I think its beginning coincided [Col. 0108B] with the beginning of Severus’s own expedition against Pescennius in A.D. 193. This can be understood from the fact that it is scarcely credible that Severus, when going to meet Niger, would have left in his rear a very strongly fortified city which Niger’s own soldiers held and to which they would have withdrawn if events turned against them. This also agrees very well with the events themselves. Suppose, then, that Severus began the assault through his soldiers in approximately September A.D. 193—for he had left Rome when June was almost over (§ VIII, note y). If the actual capture took place after three complete years, it will indeed fall in September A.D. 196, at which time it appears from what was said above that Severus was in Mesopotamia.

Consequently, there is no need for us to depart from the natural force of the words Ἐπὶ ὅλον, τριετῆ χρόνον (“for a full period of three years”), as Mosheim ὁ μακαρίτης (“of blessed memory”) allowed himself to be induced to do by Tillemont, whose interpretation, unless I am mistaken, does not adequately satisfy the force of the words. Accordingly, since Severus was in Mesopotamia in approximately September, he could not have arrived in Gaul until A.D. 196 was already drawing toward its close.

Nor do we read anywhere that, after Severus had been in Mesopotamia, he set out against the Osroenians, Adiabenians, and Arabs; for, according to Dio, book LXXV, chap. 3, p. 1257, these peoples were subdued by Lateranus, Candidus, and Laetus, Severus’s generals, while Severus was resting at Nisibis, a city of Mesopotamia. It follows from this that news of Albinus’s revolt was brought to Severus in Mesopotamia. Indeed, it is not improbable that Severus, who was especially accustomed to act with speed in his counsels, immediately after Niger’s defeat sent men to lay a trap for Albinus under the pretext of delivering letters, and that this plot was suddenly detected. It is therefore all the more apparent that Severus went into Gaul approximately as the year 196 was ending.

> **Note [Col. 0108D].** Mosheim entered upon another line of argument in § 22. He says that the city of the Byzantines was captured in A.D. 196; but that, according to Herodian, book III, chap. 6, it had not yet been brought under the emperor’s power when he went to meet Albinus; therefore Severus set out against Albinus in the year 196. I do not deny that this argument would have greater force than mine if Herodian’s words did not conflict with what was reported by Cassius Dio, who says that Severus received news of the capture of Byzantium in Mesopotamia. Dio is deservedly to be heeded, and Herodian’s authority rejected, as the most distinguished H. S. Reimar has already excellently observed in his notes on Cassius Dio, p. 1252 § 70 and p. 1256 § 1.
