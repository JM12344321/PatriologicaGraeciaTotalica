[Col. 1037D] They also venerated complete crosses in the images of their gods that represented the human form. For these could not be fashioned by modelers and craftsmen unless clay or some other material were first actually laid upon supports arranged in the form of a complete cross. Tertullian therefore rightly says: “We have said that the origin of your gods is derived by the modelers from the cross” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 366). Earlier he had said: “First the clay begins to shape the image” (*Ibid.*, ch. 12, p. 340), that is, begins to form it, being built upon the cross and stake. Again, in another book:

> Upon this beginning and, as it were, framework, clay is woven over it; little by little it completes the limbs, constructs the body, and imposes upon the cross within it whatever form was desired for the clay. . . . From [Col. 1038A] the cross it passes, in a manner, to clay, and from clay to a god: the cross passes through the clay into a god. You therefore consecrate the cross, from which the process of consecration begins.

(Book I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 12, p. 578.)

We have already discussed these matters in our dissertation on the book of Minucius Felix (*Dissertat. in Minut. Octav.*, ch. 4, art. 12), as well as trophies, *cantabra*, and standards. For the same reason our Author adds all these words: “Those *siphara* of the standards and *cantabra*” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 369). Now *sipharum*, or *sypharium*, *supparus*, and *supparum* were names given to various kinds of garment and covering—for example, the coverings of ships and of the theater. But there, as is sufficiently clear, Tertullian uses the word for a covering of military standards placed upon a cross, whether wooden or made of some other material. For immediately [Col. 1038B] afterward he says: “These *siphara* are the robes of the cross” (*Ibid.*); that is, they are the garments and coverings by which the cross was veiled or covered. By the name *sipharum*, therefore, he denotes the cloth hanging from the transverse part of the cross, with which standards and *cantabra* were adorned. He consequently mocks the pagans in these words: “I commend your diligence. You were unwilling to consecrate naked and unadorned crosses” (*Ibid.*). Likewise, in another book: “Those *siphara* are the garments of crosses. You are ashamed, I suppose, to worship unadorned and naked crosses” (Idem, Book I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 12, p. 579).

He presses the point further: “You worship Victories, although in trophies crosses are the inward structure of the trophies” (Idem, *Apologeticum*, loc. cit.); that is, they underlie the trophies. In another book, however, he puts it somewhat differently: “You venerate Victories as divine powers, and indeed as more august the more joyful they are; with . . . [Col. 1038C] what better might they raise aloft? Crosses will be, in a manner, the inward structure of the trophies” (Idem, *Ad Nationes*, loc. cit.; the text is defective at “with . . .”). It is certainly established that Victory was placed by them among the number of goddesses. Since this fact is scarcely unknown to anyone, it will suffice to prove it by these words of Cicero:

> What of the temple of Concord, of Liberty, and of Victory? Because the power of all these things was so great that it could not be governed without a god, the thing itself acquired the name of a god.

(Cicero, Book II, *De Natura Deorum*, p. 223, line 5.)

Concerning Victory and her image, Claudian composed these verses:

> Victory herself would open the sacred halls to the commander.  
> O maiden rejoicing in the green palm and clad in trophies,  
> guardian of the Empire, who alone can heal  
> wounds and teach men to feel no toil.

(Claudian, Book III, *De Laudibus Stilichonis*, v. 204 ff.)

Her image therefore shone upon trophies, that is, upon the captured [Col. 1038D] spoils of enemies, which were placed upon the top of a stake erected in the form of a cross and customarily carried in solemn procession. Thus, by worshiping Victory not only in her temple but also upon these trophies, the pagans worshiped the cross as well. For, as Minucius Felix says, “Victorious trophies imitate not only the form of a simple cross but also that of a man affixed to it” (Minucius, p. 287).

But let us follow Tertullian as he presses the argument still more sharply:

> The religion of the Romans is wholly a religion of the camp: it venerates the standards, swears by the standards, and places the standards before all the gods. All those distinguished structures supporting images are necklaces of crosses.

(Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 367.)

So indeed the text stands in our handwritten codices and in the older editions. But Rigault removed the word “Romans” [Col. 1039A] there without giving any reason. We nevertheless suspect that his reason was that, in the book *Ad Nationes*, Tertullian repeats the same statement while omitting the word “Romans”:

> In its victories the religion of the camp also worships crosses; it adores the standards, swears solemnly by the standards, and places the standards before Jupiter himself. But that structure supporting images, and the entire adornment of gold, are necklaces of crosses.

(Idem, Book I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 12, p. 579.)

Rigault, however, does not appear sufficiently to have noticed that the word “Romans” could be omitted in this book, which was addressed generally to all nations, but not in the *Apologeticum*, which was sent to all governors and especially to the Roman ones. Among the Romans, moreover, the custom of worshiping military standards had become so firmly established that, according to Suetonius, Artabanus, king of the Parthians, when he came to Caligula’s legate, “worshiped the eagles and Roman standards and the images [Col. 1039B] of the Caesars” (Suetonius, Book IV, *Life of Caligula*, §14).

Why it is called the religion of the camp, and why it swore by its standards, may be understood from these words of Seneca:

> The first bond of military service is religious obligation, love of the standards, and the sacrilege of deserting them; afterward all other things are easily demanded and commanded of those who have been bound by oath.

(Seneca, Epistle 95, p. 305.)

The first of these, therefore, was the military oath by which soldiers pledged themselves to service. Schelius discusses it more fully in his notes on Polybius (Schelius, ch. 1, *De Sacramento*), as do Brissonius (Brisson., beginning of Book IV, *De Formulis*), Justus Lipsius (Lipsius, Book I, *De Militia Romana*, dialogue 6, p. 26), and others. But in addition to this oath, the soldiers also swore by their standards. Thus Lucan represents a centurion swearing to Caesar by them:

> [Col. 1039C] . . . By the ten standards fortunate for the camp,  
> and by all your triumphs over every enemy, I swear.

(Lucan, Book II, v. 278.)

Nor is this surprising, since, as we learn from Cornelius Tacitus, they regarded their standards and eagles as “the peculiar divinities of the legions” (Tacitus, Book II, *Annals*, near the beginning, p. 20; cf. Lucan, Book II, v. 370). Elsewhere, speaking of Antonius suppressing a mutiny, Tacitus says:

> Then, turning toward the standards and the gods of war, he prayed that they would cast that madness and discord upon the armies of the enemy instead, until the mutiny died away.

(Idem, Book III, *Histories*, near the beginning, p. 180.)

Dionysius of Halicarnassus speaks still more clearly: Σημεῖα τιμιώτατα Ῥωμαίοις ταῦτα ἐπὶ στρατείας καὶ ὥσπερ ἰδρύματα θεῶν ἱερὰ νομίζονται (“These standards are held in the highest honor by the Romans on campaign and are regarded as sacred, like images of the gods”). “Nothing in the Roman army is held in greater veneration than the standards, nor are they judged less sacred than the images of the gods” (Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Book VI, [Col. 1039D] *Roman Antiquities*, p. 375).

Tertullian therefore added that the religion of the camp “places the standards before all the gods” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 16, p. 368), because soldiers venerated them, like their banners, with greater devotion than all their other gods. Perhaps there will be someone who thinks that by these words Tertullian meant that, in the army and on the battlefield, military standards were carried by the standard-bearers before the statues and images of the gods. But the former meaning appears more consistent with his words and intention. For in another book he said: “It places the standards before Jupiter himself, even though he is the greatest of their gods” (Idem, Book I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 12, p. 579).

[Col. 1040A] “But all those distinguished structures supporting images,” he says, “are necklaces of crosses.” Some have wished to advise us that certain manuscripts there read *in signis* (“upon the standards”) rather than *insignes* (“distinguished”), and this is by no means entirely absurd, since military standards are the subject under discussion. Yet because *insignes* is found in far more numerous manuscripts of better quality, we do not dare reject this reading. Nothing, however, can be drawn in confirmation of this reading rather than the other from the book *Ad Nationes*, where Tertullian omitted the word and added: “That structure supporting images and the adornment of all the gold are necklaces of crosses” (*Ibid.*).

Some indeed think that the word *suggestus* is used there for clothing and ornamentation, but they should have proved this. For what if it is understood as the very act of raising up or carrying beneath [Col. 1040B] these images, which hung from the upper portion of a spear or elongated piece of wood and were the necklaces and ornaments of the crosses, since the crosses were veiled and adorned by the images placed above them? In any event, we have already discussed all these matters in our dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius Felix (*Dissert. in Minut.*, ch. 12, art. 4), where we also showed that a man with his hands outstretched, as our Author finally says, displays in his own person the image of a cross (Tertullian, Book I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 12, p. 578).

But we seem to hear someone object that this argument of Tertullian is altogether weak. For the pagans, he will say, when worshiping the images of their gods, their standards, trophies, Victories, and any other things of this kind, gave no thought at all to the crosses that lay beneath or covered them. Yet [Col. 1040C] he should observe that the argument of Tertullian and the other defenders of the Christian religion rests upon that pagan belief according to which they thought the images and standards of their gods to be true gods. They therefore worshiped those objects in their entirety and all their parts, among which the crosses were principal elements. For they thought that a god was made not only from the image’s form, appearance, and character, but also from its material; and they customarily venerated the whole object as composed of all its parts. We must therefore say that the crosses lying beneath the cloths and necklaces of the military standards, no less than the other parts of those same standards—parts of which perhaps they were not then thinking explicitly—were honored by them with religious worship.

## CHAPTER XXI  
### On the Crimes of Treason Against Human Majesty of Which the Pagans Wrongly Endeavored to Accuse the Christians

[Col. 1040D]

### ARTICLE I  
#### How clearly Tertullian shows that Christians were undeservedly accused of treason against human majesty, since with their whole devotion they prayed—not to false gods, but to the true and almighty God—for the emperor’s safety and prosperity and for the continuance of the Roman Empire, which Tertullian and many others thought would come to an end together with the whole world

We have thus far set forth and explained all the arguments by which Tertullian demonstrates with perfect clarity [Col. 1041A] that the Christians were falsely and most shamelessly accused by the pagans of treason against the divine majesty, although those very accusers were themselves manifestly guilty of it on many counts. We must now examine the considerations by which, in a similar manner, he shows that the pagans were actually conscious of treason against human majesty, although with the greatest rashness they still did not hesitate to accuse the Christians of this crime. For, to use his words: “We have come to the second charge, that of injuring the more august majesty” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 28, p. 436), namely, the majesty of the emperors. Their majesty is called “more august” because the pagans, as we shall shortly explain, were far more afraid to injure the majesty of a living emperor than that of Jupiter and the other dead gods.

But the Christians, while honoring the emperor with due [Col. 1041B] reverence, prayed for his safety not to dead and fictitious gods, but to the true and living God, from whom those emperors had actually received supreme power, second only to His, the enjoyment of life, and everything they themselves were (*Ibid.*, ch. 30, p. 441).

For from this sole and almighty God, in their daily prayers, the Christians implored for those same emperors, as Tertullian says, “a long life, a secure empire, a safe household, brave armies, a faithful senate, an upright and peaceful people, and whatever a man and a Caesar may desire” (*Ibid.*); that is, whatever the emperors could desire for their private lives and imperial dignity in accordance with the laws of equity and justice. Other eminent defenders of the Christian religion proclaimed this no less emphatically. Θεὸν μὲν μόνον (“God alone”), Justin Martyr used to say, [Col. 1041C] προσκυνοῦμεν, ὑμῖν δὲ πρὸς τὰ ἄλλα χαίροντες ὑπηρετοῦμεν, βασιλεῖς καὶ ἄρχοντας ἀνθρώπων ὁμολογοῦντες, καὶ εὐχομένοι μετὰ τῆς βασιλικῆς δυνάμεως, καὶ σώφρονα τὸν λογισμὸν ἔχοντας εὐρεθῆναι (“we worship; but in other matters we gladly serve you, acknowledging you as emperors and rulers of men and praying that, together with imperial power, you may be found to possess sound judgment”). “We worship God alone, and in other matters we serve you, acknowledging that you are emperors and rulers of men, while praying at the same time that you may be found to possess sound judgment together with imperial power” (Justin, *Apology to Antoninus*, p. 64). At the end of his embassy on behalf of the Christians, Athenagoras openly declares that those same Christians prayed to God for the imperial rule of Augustus, that it might increase more and more, that everything might befall him according to his desire, and that his son and heir might succeed him in his place (Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, p. 40). But there is no need to pursue more fully matters that are sufficiently known to everyone. [Col. 1041D]

We have also made clear to all (above, ch. 14, art. 3) with what sincere devotion of mind, what innocent soul, and what humble and abject posture of body they endeavored to obtain these things from their almighty God by both private and public prayers. We shall therefore add nothing further except what Tertullian wrote to Scapula, a pagan governor:

> We also honor the emperor, in the manner permitted to us and beneficial to him: as a man who comes from God, second . . . . Accordingly, we offer sacrifice for the emperor’s safety, but to our God and his, and in the manner God has commanded—with pure prayer. For God, the Creator of the universe, has no need of any odor or blood. These things are food for demons.

(Tertullian, *Ad Scapulam*, ch. 2.)

[Col. 1042A] Nor could the pagans reply that these Christian wishes for the emperor’s safety were false and deceitful, or that their prayers were fraudulent and uttered only with the lips so that they might not undergo the punishments they deserved. For by this futile response the pagans, as Tertullian says, conceded that the Christians really did offer prayers to their God for the safety of the emperors (Idem, *Apologeticum*, ch. 31, p. 446). But who has ever proved that those prayers proceeded only from the lips and not from the innermost disposition of the heart?

Tertullian plainly proves that they were entirely sincere from the fact that our sacred Scriptures—which, as we have shown, were not unknown even to the pagans—command each and every Christian “as an overflow of kindness, even to pray to God for his enemies and to ask blessings for his persecutors” (*Ibid.*). Who does not see [Col. 1042B] that by these words he designates this commandment of Christ:

> You have heard that it was said: You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy. But I say to you: Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who persecute and slander you.

(Matthew 5:43 ff.)

By these words Christ commands Christians no longer to hate their enemies, as the Jews did, but, in the overflowing abundance of Christian kindness, to love them, do good to them, and pray to God for their persecutors and slanderers with the intimate and sincere affection of the heart.

“But who,” Tertullian continues, “are more the enemies and persecutors of Christians than the emperors, against whose injured majesty we are brought under accusation”—that is, accused (Tertullian, loc. cit.)? Elsewhere he uses this verb more than once (*Ibid.*, ch. 35, [Col. 1042C] p. 454). Above, for example, he had said: “We are arraigned as guilty of sacrilege and treason.” Below he says: “We are arraigned for a second sacrilege against the majesty that is second, namely, after God.” This is a jurists’ term and means the same as being summoned before a court. Under the law laid down by Christ, therefore, Christians were bound to supplicate God for the emperors and their safety, however hostile those emperors might be and however much they might persecute them.

What is more, under another law given by the Apostle, Christians were required to petition God expressly “for kings, rulers, and powers, that all things might be peaceful” (*Ibid.*, ch. 31, p. 446). In those few words, while leaving Paul’s name unspoken, Tertullian encompasses what Paul wrote to Timothy and what we have already cited (above, ch. 12, art. 1):

> I therefore beseech, [Col. 1042D] first of all, that supplications and thanksgivings be made for all men, for kings and all who are in high position, that we may lead a quiet and peaceful life in all piety and chastity.

(Paul, First Epistle to Timothy 2:1 ff.)

Tertullian does not prove this solely by the authority of sacred Scripture and the law imposed upon Christians, but also by an argument against which no one could so much as mutter:

> When the Empire is shaken, and its other members are shaken with it, we too, although we are regarded as strangers to the disorders, are nevertheless found somewhere within the disaster.

(Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, loc. cit.)

Yet in Rigault’s edition the word *aestimemur* (“we are regarded”) was removed there, contrary to the evidence of our codices and editions. [Col. 1043A] Whether this was due to the printers’ carelessness or to Rigault’s own authority, we do not know; but we certainly have no doubt that our Author correctly included it. For earlier, as we have seen, he asserted that Christians were everywhere intermingled with pagans in every aspect of public life. Nevertheless, they were regarded as strangers to the pagans’ disorders because they were entirely alien to their mysteries and superstitious worship. In other respects they lived together with the pagans, and therefore, when the Empire was shaken, they too necessarily suffered the shock. Consequently they prayed to God with calm and wholehearted sincerity for the peace of the Empire and the safety of the emperors, not only privately but also publicly in their assemblies, as we observed above (above, ch. 15, art. 1).

What is more, if we believe Tertullian, a still greater necessity [Col. 1043B] demanded that Christians offer these prayers sincerely (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 32, p. 447). Their minds had been pervaded by the belief that various portents then foretold the imminent end of the world, but that, as we have already observed (above, ch. 15, art. 1), the world would not be destroyed before the Roman Empire was overthrown. They therefore prayed for the Empire’s continuance and that the end of the world, which they did not wish to experience, might be delayed. Yet although that Christian belief was not true, as we saw above (*Dissertat. in Lactant.*, ch. 29, art. 1 ff.), it nonetheless proves with equal certainty that in sincere prayers they petitioned God both for the safety of the emperors and for the peaceful continuance of their empire.

### ARTICLE II  
#### Christians were undeservedly accused of treason against human majesty because they refused either to sacrifice to the gods of the pagans for the emperor’s safety or to pray to gods who could protect neither their own temples nor themselves, and could neither bestow nor preserve the emperor’s safety—which the almighty God alone could do, and from whom the Christians could obtain it by their prayers

[Col. 1043C]

By so many weighty arguments it has been sufficiently demonstrated that Christians, who prayed daily to God for the emperors’ safety with their whole hearts, were not guilty of injuring their majesty. What, then, could the pagans oppose to this except wholly feeble arguments and absurd quibbles, which collapse of themselves and fall utterly to pieces? Nevertheless, so that we, like our Tertullian, may leave nothing untouched, they must be examined, so that it may become still more evident [Col. 1043D] to everyone with what great injustice those same Christians were harassed and oppressed because of their religion.

The pagans therefore objected that Christians were proved guilty of treason against human majesty because they stubbornly refused to sacrifice to the pagans’ false gods for the safety of the emperors or to pray to them. But to whom, Tertullian asks, should such sacrifice be offered (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 29, p. 438)? Surely to those who can bestow safety upon any person, including emperors. Yet the pagans’ gods could bestow it neither upon emperors nor upon any human being. For those gods were either demons and, as was said above, the vilest spirits, lost and condemned; or rather they were dead men, as [Col. 1044A] the pagans knew from their own consciousness and as we shall demonstrate below. Christians therefore could neither hope for the emperors’ safety from either kind nor ought they to seek it from them.

For how could those false gods bestow safety when they had never been able to protect either themselves or their own temples and images from thieves or from the injuries of time? To preserve them, therefore, they required the sentries of soldiers, as our Author says—that is, their guardianship by day and night. The author of the Epistle to Diognetus fully supports Tertullian when he justly and deservedly reproaches the pagans as follows: Τοὺς μὲν λιθινοὺς καὶ ὀστρακίνους σέβοντες ἀφυλάκτους, τοὺς δὲ ἀργυρέους καὶ χρυσοὺς ἐγκλείοντες ταῖς νυξὶ, καὶ ταῖς ἡμέραις φύλακας παρακαθίσαντες ἳνα μὴ κλαπῶσιν (“You venerate those of stone and pottery though they are unguarded, but those of silver and gold you lock away at night and station guards beside them during the day, lest they be stolen”). “You venerate gods of stone and [Col. 1044B] pottery that have no guardians; but those of silver and gold you lock away at night and appoint guards over during the day, lest they be stolen” (Epistle to Diognetus, near the beginning, p. 495).

Tertullian therefore rightly declares that such guard duty is forbidden to a Christian soldier:

> Will he stand guard over temples that he has renounced, and dine where the Apostle does not approve? Will he defend at night, leaning and resting upon his javelin, those whom during the day he has driven away by exorcisms—namely, the demons and gods of the pagans?

(Tertullian, *De Corona*, ch. 11.)

Other editions read “upon the *lunchus*,” or lance, with which Christ’s side was pierced. You certainly see by what method the pagans stood guard and kept watch for their gods. Yet these guardians, however vigilant, could not prevent the pagans’ temples and images from being plundered and carried off, or [Col. 1044C] from becoming objects of ridicule and contempt to very many. Nevertheless, the pagans were thoroughly persuaded that these images either were their gods themselves or were at least their dwelling, seat, and habitation. We have made numerous observations on these matters elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Arnob.*, ch. 20, art. 5; and *in Lactant.*, ch. 35, art. 5), and shall speak of them again below.

Suppose, if you wish, that the pagans did not believe these images to be gods, but only to represent them. What will you gain from this? For it is established that these very gods of the pagans, whoever they were, depended upon the emperor’s will and judgment. Many of them, as we demonstrated above, experienced this very thing. Indeed, several gods, as Tertullian said, “had Caesar angry with them” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 29, p. 439), because he had forbidden not only the construction of their shrines and statues but even their being regarded as gods. [Col. 1044D] Nothing, however, could be more absurd than to seek the emperor’s safety from gods subject to the emperor, who were more capable of obtaining their own safety from him than he was of obtaining his from them. Tertullian therefore addresses the pagans in these words: “But you are irreligious, for you seek that safety where it does not exist; you ask it from those by whom it cannot be given” (*Ibid.*, p. 440). So the text reads in our codices and in the old editions. Rigault, however, contrary to the authority of them all, ordered *religiosi* (“religious”) to be printed, doubtless because he thought the pagans were there being called religious ironically. But is it permissible, because of this conjecture, to alter an author’s text on one’s own authority—especially when both readings have the same meaning?

Again, Tertullian demonstrates that Christians were more concerned than the pagans for their emperor’s safety [Col. 1045A] (*Ibid.*, ch. 33, p. 448), since they knew with certainty that he had been chosen by their true God. “For from the same source,” he says, “he has his power as he has his spirit and life” (*Ibid.*, ch. 30, p. 442). He had recognized this not only by the light of nature, but also from our writers inspired by the divine Spirit (Idem, Book I, *De Praescriptionibus*, ch. 2). For in the book of Wisdom, as Tertullian says, Solomon addresses kings themselves as follows: “Power was given to you by the Lord, and strength by the Most High” (Wisdom 6:4). Paul, moreover, wrote to the Romans: “There is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been ordained by God” (Romans 13).

Tertullian therefore rightly and deservedly concludes from this: “Caesar is more truly ours, since he was appointed by our God” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 33, p. 448), than he belonged to any gods of the pagans, who could never [Col. 1045B] make anyone a Caesar or emperor. Consequently, by praying to his God, by whom the emperor was appointed, the Christian contributes more to the emperor’s safety than does the pagan by praying to his fictitious gods, who cannot confer even the smallest benefit upon either the emperor or any other human being.

### ARTICLE III  
#### How vainly the pagans objected that Christians were guilty of treason against human majesty because they refused to swear by the emperor’s Genius, although they nevertheless swore by his safety, albeit in a manner entirely different from that of the pagans

The pagans persisted in arguing that Christians were shown to be guilty of offending Caesar’s majesty because they refused to swear by the emperors’ Genii; or, as our Author says elsewhere: [Col. 1045C] “We are called irreligious toward the Caesars because we neither propitiate their images nor swear solemnly by their Genii” (Tertullian, Book I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 17, p. 583).

According to Festus (Festus, Book XX, *De Significatione Verborum*, s.v. “Genius”), the pagans called “Genius” not only the god of each particular place, but also the god who possessed the power of directing everything that was to be done. In confirmation of this belief he cites these words of Aufustius:

> Genius is the son of the gods and the father of men, from whom men are begotten; and therefore he is called my Genius, because he begot me.

Servius somewhere observes that the ancients called the natural god of each place, thing, or person its Genius; and elsewhere that the *Manes* are called by Virgil the Genii allotted to everyone together with life (Servius on Book I of the *Georgics*, p. 85, and Book VI of the *Aeneid*, p. 440). Varro also, as cited by Augustine (Augustine, Book VII, *De Civitate Dei*, ch. 13, p. 172), had said that the Genius was the god [Col. 1045D] appointed over, and possessing the power of, the generation of all things. More is said concerning Genii by Lipsius (Lipsius, Book I, *Physiologia*, ch. 19, p. 554 ff., vol. IV), Natalis Comes (Natalis Comes, Book IV, *Mythologia*, ch. 3), Vossius (Vossius, Book I, *De Origine et Progressu Idololatriae*, ch. 18), and others, as well as by us in our earlier dissertations.

What we touched upon briefly there demonstrates more than sufficiently that the pagans were persuaded that the emperor’s Genius was his own particular god, by whom they thought he had been begotten and was governed. It is therefore unsurprising that they swore by it and accused Christians who refused to swear of treason.

But the Christians, although very frequently provoked by the pagans to take this impious oath—sometimes by flattery, [Col. 1046A] sometimes by threats and tortures—never consented to take it. Do you wish this to be demonstrated by an example? Eusebius is at hand, who relates more than once that the most holy martyr Polycarp was challenged by the proconsul to swear by Caesar’s Genius: Ὄμοσον (“Swear”), he said, τὴν Καίσαρος τύχην (“by Caesar’s Fortune”) (Eusebius, Book IV, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 15, p. 132). But Eusebius continues that the proconsul received from him no answer except this: “You pretend not to know that I am a Christian, to whom that oath is completely and utterly forbidden.”

We are not unaware that the Greek word τύχη (*tychē*, “Fortune”) signifies Fortune, but Caesar’s Fortune is meant there—the Fortune that the pagans believed to be governed by his Genius. We discussed this pagan oath, and its refusal by Christians, in our earlier dissertation on the [Col. 1046B] *Octavius* of Minucius (*Dissert. in Minut. Octav.*, ch. 12, art. 3).

Christians therefore detested every oath sworn by the Genii of emperors, since those Genii either did not exist or were demons subject to those very Christians, who adjured them in Christ’s name and cast them out of the bodies of those whom they possessed (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 32). It was therefore not lawful for Christians to swear by beings over whom they possessed supreme power. Moreover, pagans who swore by Caesar’s Genius very frequently perjured themselves. For traitors and perjurers habitually broke the loyalty they had pledged to Caesar by this oath (Idem, Book I, *Ad Nationes*, ch. 17, p. 583). Who, then, is guilty of treason: one who is implicated in an impious oath or infamous perjury, or another who steadfastly refuses to take that most unjust [Col. 1046C] oath (above, ch. [number omitted], art. [number omitted])?

Thus, in rejecting this oath, Christians were all the less guilty of treason against human majesty because they all the more willingly swore by the emperor’s safety, which, our Author says, “is more august than all the Genii,” as we explained shortly above (*Ibid.*). As a witness to this oath we have Athanasius, who wrote to the emperor Constantius concerning Syrianus, doubtless a Christian: Μαρτυρομένος τὴν σὴν σωτηρίαν (“Having called your safety to witness”), “having called your safety to witness” (Athanasius, *Apology to Constantius*, p. 310, vol. I), he promised that he would cause no further disturbance but would report the matter with far greater solemnity to his piety.

At the Conference of Carthage, moreover, the count Marcellinus bound himself by the same oath in these words:

> Whether I participate in judging this matter with another or alone, [Col. 1046D] I promise that I shall judge nothing except what the examined representations of the parties have been able to demonstrate and what the assurance of truth has discovered—by the wondrous mystery of the Trinity, by the sacrament of the Lord’s Incarnation, and by the safety of the aforementioned princes, Honorius and Theodosius.

(*Collatio Carthaginensis*, after Optatus’s book, p. 481.)

You certainly see how weighty this oath was, since in a celebrated assembly of bishops it was joined with two other oaths than which nothing could be more sacred. Numerous examples of this oath, whether in public or private affairs, and confirmed by the laws of the emperors, occur frequently and are sufficiently accessible to all (Theodosian Code, law VIII, *De Pactis et Transactionibus*). Nothing therefore need be added by us except [Col. 1047A] that the custom of taking this oath is extremely ancient. For in sacred Scripture (Genesis 42:15–16), the patriarch Joseph is said twice to have sworn by Pharaoh’s safety.

Basil, however, wished to caution us that, although this bears some appearance of an oath, it is not properly called an oath, but is rather a means by which a matter may more easily be made persuasive to its hearers (Basil, Homily on Psalm 14, §2). Yet Tertullian openly declares that Christians swore by the emperor’s safety because “we desire,” he says, “that he whom God has willed should be safe, and we regard this as a weighty oath” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 32, p. 447).

You therefore understand how great a difference there was between Christians and pagans when they swore in this manner. By this public religious oath, Christians affirmed that they [Col. 1047B] sincerely desired and prayed for the safety of the emperor whom God had placed over them. Pagans, however, with impious flattery declared that the emperor—whose death they desired—was God and a participant in true divinity. Horace was certainly both a witness to and a participant in such wicked adulation, for he wrote these verses to Caesar Augustus, as we have noted elsewhere (*Dissertat. in Minut.*, ch. 12, art. 3):

> While you are present, we bestow timely honors upon you,  
> and set up altars at which men must swear by your name.

(Horace, Book I, Epistle 1, vv. 15–16.)

### ARTICLE IV  
#### Christians were wrongly accused by the pagans of treason against human majesty because they refused to call the emperors gods; how the emperor himself was reminded of his frailty amid the greatest glory of a triumph; how Caesar Augustus refused the title of god and lord; how emperors were called lords by Christians in the ordinary sense of the word; and how it was unlawful for pagans to call an emperor a god before his death and apotheosis

[Col. 1047C]

There was certainly no greater perversity than that of the pagans, who brought an accusation of treason against the Christians because they entirely refused to call the emperors gods or to confer divine honors upon them. For, as Tertullian acutely argues: “He who calls him a god denies that he is an emperor. Unless he is a man, he is not an emperor” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 33, p. 449). For who can deny that an emperor begotten by a human father and mother cannot be God, who must necessarily be altogether eternal? Christians, therefore, who rightly detested the most shameful flattery of the pagans in calling their emperors gods, could not on that account be prosecuted by anyone for treason against human majesty.

Indeed, by the celebrated example of an emperor celebrating a triumph, Tertullian most plainly convicts the pagans who customarily called him a god of a most disgraceful falsehood. For at this highest summit of human glory—or, as Tertullian expresses it, “in the most exalted chariot of his triumph”—he is reminded of his frailty. For someone behind him whispers: “Look behind you; remember that you are a man” (*Ibid.*). Tertullian does not say who it was that gave this reminder, doubtless because the person was then as well known to everyone as he is known to few today. Tzetzes, however, calls him an attendant, slave, [Col. 1048A] and public servant. For concerning the triumphal chariot and this admonisher he wrote:

> Καὶ πῶς ἐπὶ τοῦ ἅρματος δημόσιος οἰκέτης,

Ὄπισθεν τοῦτον ἴσταται τὸν στέφανον ἀνέχων,

Καὶ λέγων τούτῳ πρός τε οὗς· καὶ τὸ κατόπιν βλέπε

(“And how, upon the chariot, a public servant stands behind him, holding up the crown and saying to him—apparently into his ear—‘Look also at what is behind you.’” The recovered words πρός τε οὗς appear corrupt and are uncertain.) And how, in the chariot, a public servant  
> stands behind him, holding up the crown  
> and saying into his ear: “Look at what is behind you.”

(Tzetzes, *Chiliades* 13, §461.)

Pliny, however, calls him a physician of envy and glory:

> Hanging beneath the chariot of those celebrating triumphs, he defends them as a physician against envy; and a similar remedy of the tongue commands them to look back, lest Fortune, the executioner of glory, be appeased behind them.

(Pliny, Book XXVIII, *Natural History*, ch. 4, p. 570.)

But if this passage of Pliny is sound and complete, it means that the envy customarily aroused [Col. 1048B] in others by those celebrating triumphs—or the vice of vainglory by which the triumphators themselves could be bewitched—was cured by the remedy of the attending slave’s tongue, as he reminded the triumphator of his human condition. For elsewhere the same Pliny explicitly said that the triumphator’s crown was held up by a slave (*Ibid.*, Book XXXIII, ch. 1).
