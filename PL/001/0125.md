Those who make such a concession ought to reconsider the status of the word itself—what “woman” means from the very first pages of the sacred writings; for they will find that it is the name of the sex, not of a rank within the sex. Indeed, God called Eve, who had not yet known a man, both “woman” and “female” (Gen. 2:23): “female,” insofar as it designates the sex generally; “woman,” insofar as it designates a particular rank of the sex.

> **Note [Col. 1185D]: Eve, who had not yet known a man, etc.** To show that Tertullian understands married and unmarried females alike by the term γυνή (*gynē*, “woman”), he adduces an example from Genesis 2:23. There, speaking of Eve, God says, according to the Septuagint translators: αὐτὴ κληθήσεται γυνή, ὅτι ἐκ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς αὐτῆς ἐλήφθη, “She shall be called woman, because she was taken from her man”—as Tertullian himself most aptly translates elsewhere, following the version of the Seventy. The Vulgate translator, however, has: “She shall be called *virago*, because she was taken from man.” The divine writer had also said above: “The Lord God fashioned the rib which he had taken from Adam into a woman,” εἰς γυναῖκα, “into a woman.” In both places Eve, although still a virgin and not yet having known a man, [Col. 1186B] is called γυνή, “woman.” Therefore this word does not signify a rank of the sex, that is, the order of married women, but the female sex generally; and hence [Col. 1186C] both virgins and married women are commanded by the Apostle to be veiled. —MURAT.

Thus even then, while Eve was still unmarried, the designation “woman” belonged to her, and that designation [Col. 1185B] was thereby made common to the virgin also. Nor is it surprising if the Apostle, being moved by the same Spirit by whom all divine Scripture—including the book of Genesis—was composed, used the same term, writing “woman,” a word which, by the example of unmarried Eve, applies also to a virgin.

The remaining arguments, finally, carry no weight. For by the very fact that he did not name virgins—just as in another passage, where he teaches about marriage—he sufficiently declares that what was said concerned every woman and the entire sex, and that no distinction was drawn with respect to the virgin, whom he does not name at all.

> **Note [Col. 1186B]: “Divine matters,” Murat.** This makes no sense.

> **Note [Col. 1186C]: Thus in another passage.** He refers to the same First Epistle to the Corinthians, chapter 7, verse 34, where Paul speaks as follows: “The unmarried woman and the virgin thinks about the things of the Lord, that she may be holy in body and spirit. But she who is married thinks about the things of the world, how she may please her husband.” If, says Tertullian, the Apostle had wished to distinguish unmarried women from married women when commanding women to be veiled, he would have distinguished them by some term, just as he did where he truly wished to distinguish them. It is therefore apparent that both married and unmarried women were comprehended under the single designation “woman,” or γυνή, when Paul did not wish to distinguish them. Tertullian answers in the same way in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 5. —MURAT.

For he who remembered elsewhere to distinguish—where, of course, the difference required it, distinguishing each kind by its own designation—when he does not distinguish, by failing to name the two separately, intends no difference to be understood. Moreover, in the Greek language, in which the Apostle wrote his letters, it is customary to call females γυναῖκες (*gynaikes*, “women”) rather than θήλειαι (*thēleiai*, “females”).

> **Note [Col. 1186C]: Rather than females.** That is, *in preference to* “females.” This is an elegant Hebrew mode of expression, also employed by Tertullian elsewhere. —MURAT.

Therefore, if this word is commonly employed as the name of the sex, being used in translation for the term “female,” he named the sex when he said γυναῖκα (*gynaika*, “woman”). But the virgin too is included within the sex.

There is also the explicit pronouncement: “Every woman,” he says, “who prays and prophesies with her head uncovered dishonors her head” (1 Cor. 11:5). What does “every woman” mean, if not every age, every rank, every condition? By saying “every,” he excepts no woman—just as neither man nor the obligation concerning veiling; for in the same way he says “every man” (ibid.). [Col. 1186B]

> **Note [Col. 1186C]: Just as neither man nor the obligation concerning veiling.** In the cited First Epistle to the Corinthians, chapter 11, verse 4, the Apostle says: “Every man praying or prophesying with his head covered dishonors his head.” But below he adds: “A man indeed ought not to cover his head.” Paul therefore says that it is disgraceful for any man whatsoever to be covered when he prays; and by naming every man he means all males, of whatever rank, age, or condition. Thus under the designation “every woman” he comprehends all females—virgins, married women, and widows alike. No one, however, will doubt that these words have been corrupted in our text. They might perhaps be restored, or at least some light may be shed upon them, if we also quote what Tertullian reports in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 8:
>
> “The Apostle says, ‘Every woman.’ What is ‘every,’ unless every kind, every rank, every condition, every dignity, every age? For ‘every’ is whole, complete, and lacking in no part of itself. The virgin, moreover, is a part of woman. Likewise, concerning the man who is not to be veiled, he says ‘every.’ See: two different names, man and woman; each is ‘every.’ Two laws corresponding to one another: on the one side veiling, on the other uncovering. Therefore, if by the expression ‘every man,’ the name ‘man’ is common even to one who is not yet a man—to the unbearded male—and since that name is common by nature, the law of not being veiled is also common to him who is a virgin among men,” etc. —MURAT.

Just as, therefore, in the male sex, under the designation “man,” even the unbearded youth is forbidden to be veiled, so also in the female sex, under the designation “woman,” even the virgin is commanded to be veiled. In each sex alike, the younger age should follow the discipline of the older; otherwise let male virgins also be veiled, if female virgins are not veiled, since the former too are not expressly bound by name. Let “man” and “unbearded youth” be different things, if “woman” and “virgin” are different things.

He says, of course, that she ought to be veiled “because of the angels” (ibid.), since the angels fell away from God on account of the daughters of men. Who, then, would contend that only women—that is, women already married and deprived of virginity—are objects of desire, unless perhaps virgins are not also permitted to excel in beauty and to find lovers? [Col. 1187A]

> **Note [Col. 1187B]: And deprived of virginity, objects of desire.** A word seems to me to be missing here, such as “kindle the fires” or something similar, so that the sense would be: Can only married women who lack virginity kindle the desire of angels and men? Are virgins not also allowed to excel in bodily beauty and find lovers for themselves? —MURAT.

Indeed, we see that they did not desire virgins alone, since Scripture says “the daughters of men” (Gen. 6:2), although it could have named “the wives of men,” or “females” without distinction.

> **Note [Col. 1187B]: Indeed, we see.** Indeed, says Tertullian, the angels appear to have desired virgins rather than married women, since Scripture testifies in Genesis 6:2: “The sons of God, seeing that the daughters of men were beautiful, took wives for themselves from all whom they chose.” Thus it was not the wives of men, nor females without distinction, who were sought by the sons of God—or by angels, as Tertullian interprets them, though I do not inquire how soundly—but virgins, women who were “free,” that is, not yet married. Therefore I think Tertullian’s passage should read: “Indeed, we see that they desired virgins alone.” Below too, no one will deny that “wives” should be read instead of “wife.” The same passage of Genesis is treated by Tertullian in the aforementioned *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 7. —MURAT.

Furthermore, the statement “And they took them as wives” (ibid.) supports this point, because those who are free are taken as wives. Concerning women who were not free, Scripture would have spoken differently. Thus women are “free” both through widowhood and through virginity: so completely, by naming the sex generally as “daughters,” Scripture blended the individual kinds within the genus.

> **Note [Col. 1187C]: Thus women are free.** That is, women are said to be “free” when they are either virgins or widows. —MURAT.

Likewise, when he says that nature itself teaches that females are to be veiled, since it assigned hair to women as a covering and an adornment, has not the same covering and the same honor of the head been assigned to virgins [Col. 1187B] as well?

> **Note [Col. 1187C]: Likewise, when he says, etc.** In the same chapter 11 of the First Epistle to the Corinthians, verse 14, the Apostle says: “Does not nature itself teach you that, if a man lets his hair grow, it is a disgrace to him? But if a woman lets her hair grow, it is a glory to her, since hair has been given to her as a covering.” Therefore, since hair has also been given by nature to virgins as a covering, they too ought to use a veil. —MURAT.

If it is disgraceful for a woman to be shorn (1 Cor. 11:6), it is equally so for a virgin. Therefore, where one and the same condition of the head is assigned, one and the same discipline of the head is also required, even for those virgins whom childhood protects; for she has been called “female” from the beginning. Finally, Israel too observes this practice.

> **Note [Col. 1187D]: Finally, Israel too observes this practice.** Tertullian elsewhere also testifies that it was customary for Jewish women to walk with covered heads—namely, in chapter 4 of *The Chaplet*, whose words are: “Among the Jews the covering of their women’s heads is so established a custom that they are recognized by it.” He then adds the examples of Rebecca, still a virgin, who went veiled to her bridegroom, and of Susanna, who, when brought to trial, was covered with a veil according to the custom of the other Hebrew women. He repeats similar statements in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 11. Rabbi Nathan, in the *Arukh*, also mentions a certain thin veil with which women’s heads were customarily covered. —MURAT.

But even if Israel did not observe it, our law, enlarged and supplemented, would claim this addition for itself. Let it be excused for imposing a veil upon virgins also.

> **Note [Col. 1187D]: But even if Israel did not observe it.** Although Jewish virgins might not have had the custom of veiling their heads, such a practice would nevertheless have to be observed under the law of Christians; for in the New Testament the old laws have been both enlarged and supplemented. —MURAT.

For the present, let the age which does not yet know its own sex retain the privilege of innocence. For both Eve and Adam, when knowledge came to them, immediately covered what they had come to recognize.

> **Note [Col. 1188B]: For the present, let the age which does not yet know its own sex.** From this it is clear that girls who had not yet attained the use of reason were permitted to appear with uncovered heads. Tertullian calls this “the privilege of innocence.” But in those who had grown toward adolescence, as nature changed, discipline too properly had to change. See Tertullian himself in the cited chapter 11 of *On the Veiling of Virgins*. —MURAT.

> **Note [Col. 1088B]: Covered.** I read *texerunt*, “covered,” as in Genesis 3:7, rather than *texuerunt*, “wove.” —MURAT.

Certainly, in those whose childhood has changed, age ought to be as generous toward discipline as it is toward nature. For they are disclosed as women both in their bodily members and in their functions. No one is a virgin from the time when she is capable of marrying, since age in her has already married its own husband, that is, time.

But some woman has vowed herself to God. [Col. 1188B] From that moment she transforms the arrangement of her hair and changes her entire attire into that of women.

> **Note [Col. 1188B]: But some woman has vowed herself to God.** That from the very infancy of the Church there were virgins who dedicated their virginity to Christ can be shown most clearly both from several passages of Tertullian in the aforementioned *On the Veiling of Virgins* and from this chapter. One may therefore marvel either at the blindness of some people in a manifest matter, or at their audacity in condemning a practice approved by the usage of so many centuries; for they deny either that this custom flourished in the ancient churches of the Christians or that it should be praised in more recent churches. Yet here we read that certain women vow themselves to God and that this holy institution is practiced through God’s grace. Below he says: “You have married Christ; you have delivered your flesh to him. Conduct yourself according to the discipline of your husband”—that is, Christ. “If Christ commands the brides of others to be veiled, how much more surely his own.” To these add what the most learned Pamelius says in his preface to the same book *On the Veiling of Virgins*. In a matter sufficiently established, I refrain from citing further testimonies of the ancients. —MURAT.

> **Note [Col. 1188C]: From that moment she transforms the arrangement of her hair.** We have said that when virgins dedicated their chastity, they “transformed” their hair. I do not think Tertullian means by this expression that their hair was cut; for the author himself states that it is disgraceful for virgins also to be shorn: “If it is disgraceful for a woman to be shorn,” he says, “it is equally so for a virgin.” He repeats the same statement in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 7. I am well aware that in some regions consecrated virgins were accustomed to lay aside their hair, particularly in Syria and Egypt, as Jerome testifies in Letter 48 to Sabinianus, together with other records. But nowhere do I find that such a custom prevailed in Africa during at least the first three centuries of the Church. I therefore judge that nothing else is meant here than the modest arrangement of the hair which virgins began to observe when they dedicated themselves to Christ.
>
> In antiquity, virgins customarily went about with their hair loose and free. But as soon as they bound themselves in marriage, their hair was gathered and arranged, so that they passed into the sober dignity of matrons. Those, therefore, who married a divine bridegroom were rightly to show that they were married to God by hair that was arranged, restrained, and transformed; and by changing all their attire into that of women—that is, by clothing themselves in matronly garments—they were to be counted among matrons or married women. Other virgins, however, who were being raised in hope of worldly marriage, scarcely beyond childhood, “turn their hair, and with a rather wanton pin fasten up their tresses, parting their hair from the forehead and openly professing their womanhood.” These [Col. 1189B] are Tertullian’s own words in chapter 12 of *On the Veiling of Virgins*. Yet when, in chapter 11, he says, “Look at that virgin of yours already married, in soul through expectation and in flesh through transformation,” I do not know whether this adds darkness to the present passage or whether those words should be corrected from this one. If anyone wishes to understand “transforming the hair” here as referring to the aforementioned wantonness of virgins in arranging, fastening, and parting their hair, let him show that in this passage Tertullian is not speaking about virgins dedicated to God. —MURAT.

Let her therefore affirm the whole and fulfill the whole character of a virgin: what she conceals for God’s sake, let her cover completely. [Col. 1189A]

> **Note [Col. 1189B]: Let her therefore affirm the whole.** That sacred virgins are being discussed here is made more convincing to me by these words. For the sense is: If, from the time when she dedicated her virginity to God, a virgin ought to restrain the freedom of her hair and display sobriety in her clothing, why does she afterwards present herself with uncovered head to be seen in the sacred assemblies of the people? Let her therefore show herself altogether married to Christ and preserve the whole modesty of a sacred virgin, completely overshadowing the virginal head which she conceals for God’s sake. —MURAT.

It is our concern to commend to God’s knowledge alone what God’s grace accomplishes, lest we receive from human beings the recompense we hope for from God. Why do you uncover before God what you cover before human beings? Will you be more modest in public than in the Church?

> **Note [Col. 1189C]: Why do you uncover before God?** See what Tertullian considered utterly intolerable. Virgins consecrated to Christ went about in public with veiled heads just like matrons, but not in church. Tertullian does not believe that those virgins were free from the fault of ostentation: as though glorying in themselves, they appeared to boast of their own virtue before the other virgins, advertising that divine grace had chosen them to make a vow of continence. Some answered that they did this so that by their example they might encourage others to make the sacred vow of virginity; but Tertullian says that not even this was free from the danger of ambition. —MURAT.

If it is God’s grace and you received it, “why do you boast,” he says, “as though you had not received it?” (1 Cor. 4:7). Why do you judge others by displaying yourself? Or do you invite others to the good by your own glory? Yet you yourself run the risk of losing it if you boast, and you compel others toward the same dangers. What is assumed out of a passion for glory is easily chosen.

Veil yourself, virgin, if you are a virgin; for you ought to blush. If [Col. 1189B] you are a virgin, do not endure many eyes. Let no one marvel at your face; let no one perceive your fiction. You successfully pretend to be married if you veil your head.

> **Note [Col. 1189D]: You successfully pretend to be married.** It is established that all married women, in obedience to the Apostle, covered their heads with a veil both in public places and in sacred buildings. This was therefore a mark of married women. Since sacred virgins wished to be regarded as married to Christ, they used in public the same garments, ornaments, and veils as matrons and women married in the world; in the Church, however, they removed the veil to distinguish themselves. Tertullian therefore says: “Let no one marvel at your face; let no one perceive your fiction.” That is, he calls it a fiction—though certainly a pious one—to pretend that they were married when they were still virgins. If sacred virgins had also used a veil in church, no one would have distinguished them from married women; and virgins could successfully pretend to be married only if they veiled their heads everywhere. —MURAT.

Indeed, you will not seem to be pretending; for you have married Christ and delivered your flesh to him. Conduct yourself according to the discipline of your husband. If he commands the brides of others to be veiled, how much more surely his own. [Col. 1190A]

> **Note [Col. 1189D]: Indeed, you will not seem to be pretending.** Tertullian corrects himself for having said that sacred virgins simulated marriage: for they had truly married Christ and dedicated their bodies to him, and therefore ought to attend the divine offices with veiled heads, in the manner of married women. He says the same in chapter 16 of *On the Veiling of Virgins*: “Fulfill the attire of a woman, so that you may preserve the state of virginity. Pretend outwardly something of what is within, so that you may display the truth to God alone. Yet you are not pretending to be married; for you have married Christ, you have delivered your flesh to him, you have betrothed your maturity to him. Walk according to the will of your bridegroom. It is Christ who commands both the brides of others and married women to be veiled: surely, much more his own.” I therefore have no doubt that Tertullian is speaking here not about all virgins but only about those dedicated to Christ, from the words “But some woman has vowed herself to God” down to the following words, “But let no one suppose.” —MURAT.

[The text is corrupt:] But let no one suppose that [the matter] is to be influenced by the practice of each predecessor.

> **Note [Col. 1190C]: But let no one suppose.** I perceive that an error lies hidden in these words, but I do not dare repair it. Perhaps *uniuscujusque*, “of each person,” should be read. The sense, however, is that the custom of virgins who went before ought not to be adduced as a reason. —MURAT.

Many consign their judgment to another’s custom, and add to it their own constancy, so that they may not be compelled to be veiled.

> **Note [Col. 1190C]: Many consign their judgment to another’s custom.** Here too I think the words are corrupt. I would prefer to read *multae*, “many women.” He says that numerous virgins, so that they might not be compelled to be veiled in the church, defended themselves by appealing to the custom of preceding virgins, considering themselves sufficiently prudent if they did what those women had consistently done. —MURAT.

> **Note [Col. 1189B]:** Muratori already understood that this passage was corrupt, and that it would not be adequately restored by writing *uniuscujusque et multae*, “of each person, and many women.” A. Neander emended it to: *Institutionem unusquisque*, “Let each person [regard] the institution.”

Certainly, voluntary virgins ought not to be prohibited, since they cannot deny that they too are virgins; they are content to abuse their reputation, secure in their conscience before God.

> **Note [Col. 1190C]: Certainly, those acting voluntarily.** I would interpret it as follows. It is altogether necessary for women bound either by worldly marriage or by marriage to Christ to be veiled. But women who practice voluntary virginity until they obtain a husband should not be absolutely forbidden to uncover their heads; they should merely be admonished to embrace the better counsels of discipline, although they sometimes pretend that they are virgins. We are compelled to explain the corrupt text by conjectures alone. —MURAT.

Concerning those, however, who are said to be betrothed, I can confidently pronounce and affirm beyond my measure that they ought to be veiled from the day when they first trembled at contact with a man’s body through a kiss and the joining of right hands.

> **Note [Col. 1190D]: Concerning those, however.** Nevertheless, Tertullian thinks that women who are called voluntary virgins ought to be veiled from the day on which they were betrothed to men and began to prepare themselves for future marriage. He contends that they differ in no respect from women already married. He makes similar statements in chapter 11 of *On the Veiling of Virgins*. We have said that betrothals among Christians were customarily performed by the giving of a kiss and the joining of right hands. Moreover, the matters presented briefly in this chapter were afterwards treated more fully by Tertullian himself in the frequently mentioned book *On the Veiling of Virgins*. —MURAT.

For everything in them has already entered into marriage beforehand: age through maturity, flesh through age, spirit through consciousness, modesty through the experience of the kiss, [Col. 1190B] hope through expectation, and mind through willing. Rebecca is a sufficient example for us (Gen. 24:65): when her bridegroom was pointed out to her, she was veiled for marriage merely through becoming aware of him.

## CHAPTER XXIII

> **Note [Col. 1191B]:** Title in Muratori’s manuscript: *On Bending the Knee.*

> **Note [Col. 1191B]:** Chapter XVIII in Leopold’s edition.

[Col. 1191A]

Prayer also suffers variation in the observance concerning the bending of the knee, because of a certain few who abstain from kneeling on the Sabbath.

> **Note [Col. 1191B]: They abstain from kneeling on the Sabbath.** The practice of bending the knees in prayer is exceedingly ancient. We have examples in 1 Kings 8:54; Luke 22:41; Acts 7:60, 20:36, and 21:5. Indeed, Eusebius of Caesarea calls this practice distinctive of Christians in *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 5, where he says that Christian soldiers prayed γόνυ θέντας ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν κατὰ τὸ οἰκεῖον τοῖς χριστιανοῖς τῶν εὐχῶν ἔθος, “placing the knee upon the ground, according to the custom of prayer peculiar to Christians.” Some, however, abstained from bending the knee on the Sabbath, although the other churches maintained the contrary practice. Tertullian held the latter opinion and for that reason [Col. 1191C] wished this diversity of ritual to be removed. I think it certainly was removed, since among the ancients I encounter no one who forbids bending the knees on the Sabbath.
>
> The same opinion nevertheless pleased later Greeks, as John the Monk attests in the *Canonical Collection* appended to Morinus’s books *On Penance*. It contains the following: τὰς πάσας σαββατοκυριακάς, καὶ ἑορτὰς δεσποτικάς, καὶ δωδεκαήμερον, ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ τὰς ἀπὸ τοῦ Πάσχα ἡμέρας ἕως τῶν ἁγίων πάντων μὴ γονυκλινεῖν ἐν ταῖς εὐχαῖς, “On every Saturday and Sunday, on the feasts of the Lord, during the twelve-day festival, and likewise on the days from Easter until the feast of All Saints, it is not permitted to bend the knees in prayer.”
>
> In book III of the *Greco-Roman Law*, where Luke the Patriarch is discussed, no. 9, we also read: οἱ ἀποστολικοὶ κανόνες κολάζουσι τὸν ἐν τῷ τυχόντι σαββάτῳ, ἢ τῇ κυριακῇ γονυκλιντοῦντα, ἢ νηστεύοντα, “The Apostolic Canons punish anyone who bends the knee or fasts on any Sabbath or Sunday.” Although this little Greek is mistaken—for no such provision is found in the Apostolic Canons—it is nevertheless evident that [Col. 1191D] many centuries after Tertullian this custom had again become established in the East. —MURAT.

Since this disagreement is currently pleading its case most forcefully among the churches, the Lord will give his grace so that they either yield or employ their own opinion without scandal to others.

We, however, as we have received the practice, ought on the Lord’s Day alone, the day of the Resurrection, to guard ourselves not only from kneeling but from every posture and duty expressive of anxiety, postponing even our business so that we may give no place to the devil.

> **Note [Col. 1191D]: On the Lord’s Day alone.** The same statements are made in *The Chaplet*, as things received from the storehouse and authority of tradition: “On the Lord’s Day,” he says, “we regard it as unlawful to fast or to worship on our knees. We rejoice in the same immunity from Easter Day until Pentecost.” This practice was then common among Christians. Therefore, when some departed from it, the Fathers of the First Council of Nicaea thought it proper to settle the matter in its final canon, lest the churches disagree on it. St. Hilary, in the Prologue to the Psalter; St. Jerome, chapter 4 *Against the Luciferians*; the author of the *Questions to the Orthodox*, among the works of St. Justin, question 113; St. Basil, chapter 17 of *On the Holy Spirit*; St. Augustine, Letter 119; Cassian; Germanus of Constantinople; Balsamon; Zonaras; [Col. 1192B] and not a few others mention the suspension of kneeling both on every Lord’s Day and on the days from Easter to Pentecost. —MURAT.

We do the same throughout the season of Pentecost, which we observe as the same solemnity of exultation.

> **Note [Col. 1192B]: But from every posture of anxiety.** A verb such as “to abstain” seems to me to be missing here. Since bending the knee and fasting expressed sadness and penitence, Christians abstained from that practice on the Lord’s Day, which was a day of exultation. —MURAT.

> **Note [Col. 1192B]: Which we discharge as the same solemn period of exultation.** We have said that Christians did not bend their knees during Pentecost either. I do not know whether it should be read: “which is the same solemnity of exultation, we discharge.” In *On Patience*, Tertullian uses *expunxit* for “performed”; in chapter 5 of *On the Flesh of Christ*, *dispuncta est* for “was completed”; and in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, *expunget* for “will accomplish.” In *Against the Jews* he likewise says: “Let us therefore *discharge* the order we have begun,” that is, let us complete it. Thus *tantumdem dispungimus* here signifies nothing other than “we perform and complete the same.” —MURAT.

For the rest, who would hesitate to prostrate himself before God every day, at least during the first prayer with which we enter the daylight?

> **Note [Col. 1192C]: At least during the first prayer.** The ἑωθιναί (*heōthinai*), or morning prayers, are famous among the ancients. On them consult the *Apostolic Constitutions*, book VIII, chapter 34; Chrysostom’s homily on Psalm 109 and elsewhere; and Basil’s *Longer Rules*, question 37—to omit others. —MUR.

During fasts and stations, however, no prayer is to be celebrated without kneeling [Col. 1191B] and the other customary expressions of humility. For we do not merely pray, but also make supplication and satisfaction to God our Lord. Concerning the times of prayer, nothing whatever has been prescribed, except, of course, to pray at every time and in every place. [Col. 1192A]

> **Note [Col. 1192C]: At every time.** He alludes to chapter 2 of the Epistle to Timothy, where Paul says: “I desire, therefore, that men pray in every place.” But he makes no mention of time. —MURAT.

## CHAPTER XXIV

> **Note [Col. 1192C]:** Title in Muratori’s manuscript: *On Place.*

> **Note [Col. 1191B]:** Chapter XIX in Leopold’s edition.

But how are we to pray in every place (1 Tim. 2:8), when we are forbidden to pray in public? “Every place,” he says, means every place which opportunity or even necessity might require. For what was done by the Apostles is not regarded as contrary to the precept: in prison, with the guards listening, they prayed and sang to God; likewise Paul, who celebrated the Eucharist aboard ship in the presence of everyone.

> **Note [Col. 1192C]: In the case of Paul.** It would seem that the reading ought to be “or by Paul.” He is referring to Acts 27:35. For when Paul was aboard ship, he took bread, gave thanks to God in the sight of all, broke it, and began to eat. —MURAT.

## CHAPTER XXV

> **Note [Col. 1192C]:** Title in Muratori’s manuscript: *On Time.*

> **Note [Col. 1192B]:** Chapter XX in Leopold’s edition.

Concerning time, however, the external observance even of certain hours will not be without purpose. I mean those common hours [Col. 1192B] which mark the divisions of the day: the third, sixth, and ninth, which are found to be more solemn in the Scriptures.

> **Note [Col. 1192C]: The third, sixth, and ninth.** Different authorities established different times to be devoted to prayer. The author of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, book VII, chapter 34, says that prayer should be offered six times daily: around dawn, at the third, sixth, and ninth hours, in the evening, and around cockcrow. Chrysostom, in his homily on Psalm 109, likewise teaches that prayer should be offered six times, though he does not employ the same division of hours. St. Basil determines that prayers are to be poured out to God seven times a day.
>
> Clement of Alexandria, however, in *Stromata*, book VII, praises this division of hours but adds other observations not to be disregarded: “Some establish fixed and definite hours for prayer, such as the third, sixth, and ninth. But the person endowed with knowledge prays throughout his entire life, as he strives through his prayers to live in communion with God.”
>
> Tertullian elsewhere, namely in *On Fasting*, chapter 10, again assigns the third, sixth, and ninth hours to prayer and confirms his opinion by the same examples. Maimonides, in *On Prayer and the Priestly Blessing*, chapter 1, states that in the synagogue prayer was likewise practiced three times every day. St. Cyprian, in *On Prayer*, and St. Jerome, in his letters to Laeta and Demetrias, testify that the same was customarily done three times in the Church of God. —MURAT.

The Holy Spirit was first poured out upon the assembled disciples at the third hour (Acts 2:15). On the day when Peter experienced the vision of the universal community in that vessel, he had gone up to the upper room at the sixth hour for the purpose of praying (Acts 10:9). The same Peter, together with John, was going to the temple at the ninth hour, where he restored the paralytic to his health (Acts 3:1).

Although these observances stand simply, without any command requiring them, it may nevertheless be good to establish a certain presumption which both enforces a reminder to pray and, as though by law, sometimes wrenches us away from our business for such a duty. Thus, as we also read that Daniel observed—surely from the discipline of Israel (Dan. 6:10)—we should worship no fewer than three times a day, being debtors to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

This is in addition, of course, to the prescribed prayers [Col. 1193B] which, without any reminder, are owed at the coming of daylight and of night.

> **Note [Col. 1193C]: In addition, of course, to the prescribed prayers.** He means the morning and evening prayers prescribed by law. Chrysostom speaks of them in Homily 22 on the Epistle to the Hebrews. See also Cyprian, the final chapter of *On Prayer*, where he says that one must necessarily pray in the morning and at the setting of the sun. —MURAT.

It is also fitting for the faithful neither to take food nor to enter the bath before prayer has intervened. For the refreshments and nourishment of the spirit must take precedence over those of the flesh, and heavenly things over earthly things.

## CHAPTER XXVI

> **Note [Col. 1193C]:** Title in Muratori’s manuscript: *On the Departure of Brethren.*

> **Note [Col. 1193C]:** Chapter XXI in Leopold’s edition.

Do not allow a brother who has entered your house to depart without prayer. “You have seen,” he says, “a brother; you have seen your Lord”—especially if he is a stranger, lest perhaps he be an angel.

> **Note [Col. 1193C]: You have seen, he says, a brother.** These words appear to be quoted from the sacred Scriptures, but I do not know what passage is intended. What is clear is that he is referring to Hebrews 13:2, where the Apostle, commending hospitality, says that people have sometimes received angels as guests. His words are: “And do not forget hospitality, for through it some, having received angels as guests, were unaware.” [The transmitted Latin is corrupt; it appears to record *latuerunt* (“were unaware”), with *placuerunt* (“pleased”) as a variant.] He alludes to the angels who presented themselves as guests to Abraham and Lot.
>
> Hence St. Ambrose, in *On Abraham*, book I, chapter 5, says: “How do you know whether you may receive God when you think you are receiving a guest? Abraham, while offering hospitality to travelers, received God and his angels as guests. Even when you receive a guest, you receive God,” etc. Ambrose repeats the same in *On Duties*, book II, chapter 21. But something may be missing from Tertullian’s words. —MURAT.

Nor should the visitor himself, once received by the brethren, place earthly refreshments before heavenly ones.

> **Note [Col. 1193C]:** “Once received” should be written instead of “removed.” —LEOP.

For immediately your faith will be judged. Or how will you say, in accordance with the command, “Peace to this house” (Luke 10:5), unless you also exchange mutual peace with those who are in the house? [Col. 1194A]

## CHAPTER XXVII

> **Note [Col. 1193D]:** Title in Muratori’s manuscript: *On Appending a Psalm.*

> **Note [Col. 1194C]:** Chapter XXII in Leopold’s edition.

Those who are more diligent in praying are accustomed to append “Alleluia” to their prayers, as well as psalms of the kind whose closing phrases those who are present may answer.

> **Note [Col. 1193D]: Alleluia in their prayers.** Others have already treated sufficiently the antiquity and repeated use of this word in sacred prayers. St. Augustine mentions it in his works on Psalms 106 and 110; St. Ambrose in the *Apology for David*; Sozomen in book VII, chapters 15 and 19; as do St. Paulinus, St. Jerome, Isidore, Sidonius, and others. It passed from the Jews to the Christians, for it is frequent in the Psalms of David; and we learn from St. Gregory, book II, Letter 63, that it was once used especially in the Church of Jerusalem. —MURAT.

> **Note [Col. 1194C]: Whose closing phrases they may answer.** I would wrong the reader’s learning if, after the most learned and deeply devout Cardinal Bona, I wished to discuss the very ancient practice of psalmody and of either reciting or singing the Psalms of David. Whoever wishes to be fully satisfied should consult his book *On Psalmody*. Here I merely observe in passing how ancient was also that practice whereby psalms are recited antiphonally in choir and in other ecclesiastical supplications. —MURAT.

And certainly every institution is excellent which is suitable for setting something before God and honoring him—bringing forward a prayer enriched to fullness as though it were the finest sacrifice.

> **Note [Col. 1194C]: A prayer enriched to fullness.** Just as Cicero spoke of a “meager prayer,” although in another sense, so Tertullian calls a prayer “enriched to fullness” when it joins prayers with the recitation of psalms. This is the finest, the well-fattened victim which we bring to the divine altar. —MURAT.
