## Headless Fragment of an Uncertain Author on the Canon of Holy Scripture (S)

[Col. 0173C]

[Note: [Col. 0173D] **I.—At which, nevertheless, he was present.** If the deeds of Christ the Lord, rather than those of the Apostles themselves, are meant here, the author of the fragment would contradict the words of Papias. As we saw above, Papias, following the ancient presbyter, denied this of Mark—namely, that Mark had either heard the Lord himself or followed him. Papias received this from the presbyter’s own lips, and you have Jerome subsequently confirming it by his own testimony in the preface to his *Commentary on Matthew*. I pass over the other Fathers in silence.

Nevertheless, the contrary tradition concerning Mark is very well known—although I doubt whether it is also entirely certain—which Epiphanius records in *Heresy* LI, § 6, according to which Mark is said to have been [Col. 0174D] one of the seventy-two disciples. The same Epiphanius also relates this of Saint Luke in § 11, just as he had earlier asserted it of both evangelists in *Heresy* XX, § 4, following Hippolytus, if the testimonies placed by Mill before the Gospels are to be trusted.

Many very learned men have considered this scarcely reconcilable with the beginning of Saint Luke’s Gospel, nor do I wonder at that. If Luke had ever been a disciple of the Lord, he certainly does not appear to have been one of “the eyewitnesses from the beginning” [τῶν ἀπ᾽ ἀρχῆς αὐτοπτῶν] who would write as follows: “just as those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and became ministers of the Word delivered them to us” [καθὼς παρέδοσαν ἡμῖν οἱ ἀπ᾽ ἀρχῆς αὐτόπται καὶ ὑπηρέται γενόμενοι τοῦ Λόγου].

The author of the fragment agrees with those learned men, as you will shortly see, by denying that Luke was a disciple; Eusebius also agrees in book III of the *Ecclesiastical History*, chapters 4 and 24. In book I, chapter 12 of the same work, he says that no catalogue of the seventy disciples [τῶν ὅ] survives, although it has been handed down that Barnabas was one of them.

After I had written these notes, I saw that the distinguished Freindaller, in his recent edition of this fragment, judged that the passage should be interpreted as meaning that Mark had attended Peter’s discourses. Yet he afterwards says:

> “Perhaps someone might form the opinion that Mark had associated with Christ and later committed to writing those things at which he himself had been present, and that this is what the author of the fragment meant by those words. But the most ancient presbyter Aristion expressly denies this in Papias; nor is it permissible to confuse Mark the evangelist with that other John Mark who is mentioned in chapter XII of the Acts of the Apostles. With regard to the latter, however, there might appear to be some ground for holding such an opinion.”

Thus Freindaller.

Nevertheless, whatever conclusion is reached concerning Mark’s discipleship or concerning John Mark, it may still be that the author of the fragment actually wrote something of this sort:

> Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, set in order the deeds done by the Lord—according to what he had heard Peter relate—at which he himself nevertheless had been present, and thus committed them to writing.

(Up to this point I have reproduced Jerome’s words, *On Illustrious Men*, chapter 8.)

Besides John the presbyter in Papias, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Eusebius, and Epiphanius say the same thing about Peter and Mark. Tertullian likewise says in book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 5:

> What Mark published is affirmed to belong to Peter, whose interpreter Mark was.

Furthermore, Eusebius, book III of the *History*, chapter 24, relates that this Gospel was approved by the apostle John. As for Eusebius’s statements about Mark’s Gospel in book II of the *History*, chapter 15, and book IV, chapter 14—which in both places are taken from one and the same Clement, if indeed those *Hypotyposes*, which contained at least many frivolous and absurd things, were Clement’s—they seem to me to be reconciled and interpreted in the following manner. The Romans asked Mark, who had long been Peter’s follower, to write down the things Peter had said and afterward to communicate them to them. When Peter learned of this request, he neither prohibited it from being done nor encouraged its execution. But when the work had been completed and Peter learned that the Gospel had been composed, he willingly approved the book by his own authority so that it might thereafter be read in the churches.

For the rest, I noted above, in connection with the Fragments of Papias, vol. I, p. 36, how highly the testimony concerning Mark’s Gospel, which Papias received from the lips of John the presbyter, ought to be valued. Papias was also the first of all writers expressly to mention Saint Matthew’s Gospel.

But how is this passage to be explained? In Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, chapter 8, after mentioning the Gospels of Mark and Matthew, Irenaeus writes:

> Luke also, the follower of Paul, committed to writing the Gospel preached by Paul.

Might our fragmentary author perhaps have intended to say the same thing—namely, that Luke, in his own name, gave the Gospel deeds preached by Paul written form, according to the opinion of most writers, for example Irenaeus? The ancients commonly reported that Luke was a physician, and some also said that he was a painter. Finally, Callistus Nicephorus relates a great many things about him in the *Historical Breviary*, book II, chapter 45. But I do not remember ever reading anywhere that he was also a student of law and that, in that capacity, he was attached to Paul as a companion, as the cited passage of the fragment says. Perhaps the Apostle did this so that, with a legal expert as his associate, he might obtain easier access either to learned men or to magistrates.

Above all, however, the genuine meaning of the added word *according to* [*secundum*] is difficult to divine. Does it mean “on a second occasion”? The resulting interpretation would then be that after Paul had associated Luke with himself a second time as a legal expert, Luke wrote this Gospel. Or has some other word, either preceding or following this expression, been lost? I think this last possibility comes closest to the truth. Thus Freindaller.

The passage is exceedingly corrupt, but perhaps it should be cleansed as follows:

> When Paul had taken him along with himself as one also devoted to law [καὶ τοῦ δικαίου, “and to justice”], he composed it under his own name in order [καθεξῆς σοι γράψαι, Saint Luke 1:3, “to write to you in order” in the Vulgate translation; see also below, where it is said of John that “he professes himself the writer, in order, of all the Lord’s wonders”].

Many ancient writers handed down that Luke, Paul’s follower, committed to writing the Gospel preached by Paul, including Irenaeus, book III *Against Heresies*, chapter 1, Origen, Eusebius, and Jerome. “For they are also accustomed to ascribe Luke’s arrangement to Paul,” says Tertullian, *Against Marcion* 4.5, wisely adding that what disciples published could be regarded as belonging to their teachers.

As for the words “also devoted to law,” it may be that some comparison is made here between jurisprudence—the knowledge of what is equitable and good—and the medical art that Luke practiced. Certainly the beginning of the *Commentary on the Deeds of Luke*, first published by Combefis in his *Supplement to the Fathers*, tends to confirm the integrity of the words. There the Metaphrast says:

Clement had written in his *Hypotyposes* that John, the last of all the evangelists, seeing that the matters pertaining to Christ’s body had been handed down in the other Gospels, was himself inspired by the divine Spirit and composed a spiritual Gospel at the request of his intimate associates. Eusebius himself likewise relates in the passage just cited, book III of the *History*, chapter 24, that after approving the other Gospels, John, when requested, handed down in his Gospel the deeds done by Christ before the Baptist’s imprisonment. Thus Clement and Eusebius.

Victorinus, who flourished toward the end of the third century, comes very close to this account. In the commentary attributed to him on the Apocalypse, p. 1253, in vol. I of the Paris *Library of the Fathers*, he writes concerning the apostle John:

Epiphanius also, *Heresy* LI, no. 12, while mentioning this purpose of establishing Christ’s divine nature, continues:

P. 434, Petavius’s edition.

Jerome also says in *On Illustrious Men*, chapter 9, that John wrote the Gospel last of all, at the request of the bishops of Asia, against Cerinthus and other heretics, and especially against the then-rising doctrine of the Ebionites, who assert that Christ did not exist before Mary. Jerome has the same and more in the preface to his *Commentary on Matthew*, which is very closely related to this brief fragment. There he adds that ecclesiastical history relates that when John was compelled by the brethren to write, he answered that he would do so if all would proclaim a fast and pray to God in common. When this had been completed, filled with revelation, he poured forth that prologue coming from heaven:

> In the beginning was the Word; and the Word was with God; and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God.

As for the proclaimed fast, which some have regarded as suspect because no one before Jerome had mentioned it, this author, who preceded Jerome by nearly two centuries, has now at last become a witness to it for us. Indeed, Acts of the Apostles 14:22 provides an example of this apostolic custom observed in matters of great importance:

> And when they had appointed presbyters for them in each church and had prayed with fastings, they commended them to the Lord.

**Let us relate it to one another.** That is, whether or not the Gospel was to be written at their request.

**That same night it was revealed to Andrew**, etc. An extraordinary subject for the narrative. Saint Jerome relates something similar concerning John’s Gospel; and Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, chapter 24, is no less worthy of reading on this matter. The one feature found nowhere else, however, is Saint Andrew’s participation in this evangelical labor.

Did Andrew survive so long? According to Eusebius and Jerome, John wrote his Gospel very late, after his return from exile, under the emperors Nerva or Trajan. Yet that chronological indication is not so firmly attested that it leaves no room for another, shorter interval after John’s banishment to the island of Patmos under Domitian. Who will deny that Andrew could have survived into these years? The many very extensive journeys of this Apostle, which are recorded in the most ancient histories, provide reason to suppose that he reached an extremely advanced age, until Aegeas, proconsul of Achaia, condemned him to be suspended upon a cross. **Freindaller.**

That Andrew preached the Gospel in regions not far removed from Asia is attested by the competent authority Gregory of Nazianzus, *Oration* XXV, p. 438, Paris edition, where he says that Epirus was the province assigned to this Apostle. Jerome further relates, chapter 7 of *On Illustrious Men*, that Saint Luke’s bones were transferred to Constantinople together with the relics of the apostle Andrew in the twentieth year of Constantius. On this passage Fabricius noted from Philostorgius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, chapter 2, that the relics of Saints Andrew and Luke were transferred from Achaia.

**John should describe everything under his own name.** Clearly “John.” **Freindaller.**

Concerning this divine Gospel, it is worthy of note that words drawn from it appear to have been cited by Ignatius at the beginning of the Church’s second century, in the *Epistle to the Philadelphians*, § 7. Papias relates that the same was done by presbyters who had been disciples of the Apostles—or at least Irenaeus himself does so in book V, the final chapter, *Against Heresies*. Likewise, in two passages of Celsus preserved by Origen, book II, chapters 37 and 54, John’s Gospel is either indicated or cited during Hadrian’s reign. Add Justin Martyr, who in the middle of the second century cites the same Gospel in his *Apology to Antoninus Pius*, Hadrian’s successor, § 61, p. 71, Benedictine edition. There is no need to proceed further in this matter.

**The beginnings are taught.** “Let it be seen,” says Tertullian, “whether the arrangement of the narratives varies”—that is, among the evangelists—“provided that they agree concerning the chief point of faith,” book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 2. For I would be more inclined to think that the word *beginnings* [*principia*] means chief points or propositions, since everyone commonly speaks of being taught the principles or chief points of faith, rather than prologues or prefaces. Nevertheless, chapter 3 of the *Responses* attributed to Saint Polycarp by Victor, bishop of Capua, seems to suggest the contrary:

> The evangelists reasonably employ different beginnings, although their one and identical purpose in evangelizing is established. Matthew, writing to the Hebrews, traced the order of Christ’s genealogy in order to show that Christ descended from that lineage from which all the Prophets had sung that he would be born. John, however, established at Ephesus among those who, being of Gentile origin, were ignorant of the law, took the beginning of his Gospel from the cause of our redemption, etc., etc. Nothing contrary is therefore found where, though the writings are different, the same homeland is nevertheless reached.

P. 32 of the Appendix to the *Ignatian Writings*, edited by Ussher at London in 1647. These words attributed to Polycarp had first been transferred by Feuardentius, in his notes on Irenaeus, book III, chapter 3, from a very ancient Catena on the four Gospels.

> “This is indeed an outstanding testimony for posterity to the inspiration conferred upon these evangelical writers. The author lists them in such an order that he reduces everything to the history of the birth of Jesus Christ, his Passion, his Resurrection, his association with the disciples—namely, after the Resurrection—and his twofold advent, as the principal heads.”

**Just as, separately, he clearly declares Peter’s passion to the Father, but Paul’s completion**, etc. But how are the words that immediately follow to be explained?

**And separately [*semote*] he clearly declares Peter’s passion.** Perhaps these words indicate Peter’s imprisonment and miraculous deliverance (Acts 12); for these could in every respect be regarded as a kind of prelude to Peter’s impending martyrdom.

**But Paul’s journey from the city as he set out for Spain.** The Acts in our Bibles are entirely silent about this journey of Paul from Rome to Spain. But the unquestionably mutilated passage prevents us from knowing whether this reference inserted there pertains to the Acts of the Apostles or rather to another book. Nevertheless, these words assure us that Paul departed from the city of Rome for Spain, undoubtedly after he had been acquitted by Nero—that is, after the first imprisonment whose history is read in the final chapter of Acts. Was it perhaps our fragmentary author’s intention to indicate that the book of Acts ended with this event, just as it was designated as beginning with the words, “Most excellent Theophilus”? **Freindaller.**

Perhaps instead of *semote* one should read *semota*, “separate things,” so that the word is understood of narratives placed separately and apart—that is, elsewhere than in the Acts of the Apostles. The passage would consequently be restored as follows:

> Just as separate writings clearly declare Peter’s passion—namely Saint John 21:18–19—and also Paul’s journey from the city as he set out for Spain.
