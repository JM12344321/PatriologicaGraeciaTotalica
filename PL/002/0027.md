> “No,” the Marcionites say, “our God, although not from the beginning and although not through creation, was nevertheless revealed through himself in Christ Jesus.”

A separate book will also be devoted to Christ and to every aspect of his condition; for the subjects must be distinguished so that they may be examined more fully and systematically. Meanwhile, for the present stage it will suffice to meet the argument by showing, and indeed in a few words, that Christ Jesus was the agent of no God other than the Creator.

In the fifteenth year of Tiberius, Christ Jesus deigned to flow down from heaven as a saving spirit—the salvation of Marcion [Note: [Col. 0267C] Pamelius reads *salutis* (“of salvation”); Semler conjectures the same], who so willed it [Note: [Col. 0267C] Semler conjectures *valuit*, “was able”]. [Note: [Col. 0267D] In precisely what year of the elder Antoninus. What he said above, in *On the Prescription against Heretics*, ch. 30, simply as “approximately in the reign of Antoninus,” is explained here, since the text states in which year of Antoninus Marcion’s Christ began to be revealed. We said something there as though the heretic Marcion had first appeared under Antoninus the Philosopher, because it was under Anicetus. But because I later discovered that Anicetus occupied the Roman see for about six years before the death of Antoninus Pius, I am compelled to agree with the author here that Marcion began under the elder Antoninus, that is, Pius, as immediately follows; all the others borrowed from him either the name or the praenomen Antoninus. This is especially so because Eusebius writes, both in his *Chronicle* and in *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, ch. 13, that Polycarp came to Rome at the beginning of Anicetus’s pontificate, and likewise writes that Marcion encountered Polycarp there. PAM.] In precisely what year of the elder Antoninus that dog-day breeze [Note: [Col. 0267C] Junius reads *aula* (“court”) instead of *aura* (“breeze”)] exhaled from his native Pontus, I have not troubled to investigate. This much, however, is certain about him: he is an Antonine heretic, [Col. 0267B] impious under Pius. [Note: [Col. 0267D] But from Tiberius to Antoninus, the years, etc. [Col. 0268D] If the reading were “one hundred and five years,” it would come closer to historical truth, since that number is found between the fifteenth year of Tiberius, which he mentioned shortly before and from which he undoubtedly intended to begin his computation, and the time of Marcion. He is counting, as he adds, the intervening years between Christ and Marcion. PAM.] From Tiberius to Antoninus, moreover, there are approximately one hundred and fifteen and a half years, together with half a month: they place just that much time between Christ and Marcion.

Since, therefore, Marcion was the first to introduce this god under Antoninus, as we have proved, the matter is immediately clear, if you have any sense. The chronology establishes in advance that what first emerged under Antoninus had not emerged under Tiberius; that is, the god of the Antonine reign [Note: [Col. 0267C] Pamelius reads “of Antoninus, of the reign”] did not exist in the Tiberian reign, and accordingly was not revealed by Christ, since it is agreed that he was first [Note: [Col. 0267D] Semler and Oberthür read “for the first time”] proclaimed by Marcion.

To prove now that this is an established fact, I shall take the remaining evidence from our adversaries themselves. The separation of the Law and the Gospel is Marcion’s peculiar and principal work. His disciples will not be able to deny what they possess in their chief instrument, by which, in short, they are initiated and hardened [Note: [Col. 0267D] Semler and Oberthür read “are identified”] in this heresy. For these are Marcion’s *Antitheses*, that is, opposing contradictions, which attempt to set the Gospel at variance [Note: [Col. 0268C] Semler omits “of the Gospel”] with the Law, so that from the diversity of the propositions in the two Testaments they may also argue for a diversity of gods.

Therefore, since it is precisely that separation of the Law and the Gospel which insinuated the existence of one God of the Gospel in opposition to the God of the Law, it is evident [Col. 0268A] that before that separation the God who became known through the argument of separation was not known. Accordingly, he was not revealed by Christ, who existed before the separation, but was devised by Marcion, who instituted the separation against the harmony of Gospel and Law. From Christ’s appearance down to Marcion’s audacity, that harmony had assuredly been preserved unharmed and unshaken by the principle which maintained that no other God [Note: [Col. 0268C] The Paris edition reads “and God”] of both Law and Gospel existed besides the Creator, against whom the separation was launched so much later by the man from Pontus.

## CHAPTER XX

Against the clamorous objections of the opposing party, a defense of this very straightforward proof is also necessary on our part. For they say that Marcion did not so much introduce a novel rule by separating the Law and the Gospel as restore a rule that had previously been corrupted.

O Christ, most patient Lord, who endured the suppression of your preaching for so many years, until Marcion, of course, came to your aid! [Note: [Col. 0268C] Pamelius reads “of course, came to your aid”; “to you” is absent from Semler.]

For they also cite the fact that Peter himself and the others, the pillars of the apostolate, were rebuked by Paul (Gal. 2) for not walking with a straight step according to the truth of the Gospel. Yet this was the very Paul who, still inexperienced in grace and in fact anxious lest he had run or was running in vain (ibid.), was only then conferring for the first time with the apostles who preceded him.

Therefore, if in his fervor as one still a neophyte [Note: [Col. 0268D] Pamelius reads “still as”; Semler omits “still”] he judged that something in their conduct toward Judaism deserved rebuke—namely, indiscriminate association [Note: [Col. 0268D] “Indiscriminate association,” that is, promiscuous association. See ch. 7. SEML.]—although later by practice he himself would become all things to all people, so that he might gain all, being to the Jews as a Jew, and to those under the Law as though under [Note: [Col. 0268D] Semler and Oberthür read “in”] the Law (1 Cor. 9), you wish that rebuke, which concerned conduct alone and which would afterward be approved even by its own accuser, to be regarded as suspect [Note: [Col. 0268D] Rhenanus reads “undertaken”] of treachery in the preaching about God as well.

On the contrary, concerning unity of preaching, as we read above, they had joined right hands; and by the very distribution of their duties [Note: [Col. 0268D] “They had agreed upon fellowship in the Gospel.” *Condixerant* means “had agreed”; for *condicere* also means “to announce” and “to determine.” A little later we restore the reading, “Long ago proclaiming this very thing.” RHEN.], they had agreed upon fellowship in the Gospel. So too elsewhere (1 Cor. 15:11): [Note: [Col. 0268D] “Whether therefore it was I,” he says, “or they, so we preach.” The author translated literally as *praedicamus* (“we preach”) what in Greek is κηρύσσομεν (*kēryssomen*, “we proclaim”), as did Ambrose in his *Commentary*, blessed Augustine in *Against Faustus the Manichaean*, book II, ch. 2, the Syriac edition itself, and the more accurate Latin manuscript codices, instead of the reading in others, *praedicavimus* (“we preached”). The author also agrees with these below, in *Against Marcion*, book IV, ch. 4. PAM.] “Whether therefore it was I,” he says, “or they, so we preach.”

And even if he described certain false brethren as having slipped in, who wished to transfer the Galatians to another gospel (Gal. 1:7), he himself shows that this corruption of the Gospel was directed not toward transferring them to faith in another God and another Christ, but toward preserving [Note: [Col. 0269D] Others read “observing”] the discipline of the Law. He was, of course, rebuking those who championed circumcision and observed the seasons, days, months, and years of Jewish ceremonies (Gal. 4:10), which they ought already to have recognized as abolished under the Creator’s renewed dispensation. The Creator had long ago proclaimed this very thing [Note: [Col. 0269D] Semler and Oberthür omit “this very thing”] through his prophets, as through Isaiah (Isa. 43:19): “The old things have passed away,” he says; “behold the new things that I now perform”; and elsewhere (Jer. 31:32): “And I shall establish a covenant, not like the one I established with your fathers when I led them out of the land [Col. 0269B] of Egypt.”

So too through Jeremiah (Jer. 4:4): “Renew for yourselves a new renewal, and be circumcised to your God, and circumcise the foreskins of your heart.” Therefore, by now establishing this circumcision and this renewal, the Apostle was also dissuading them from those antiquated ceremonies which their own Founder had declared would one day cease, through Hosea (Hos. 2:11): “And I shall turn away all her delights, her feast days, her new moons, her sabbaths, and all her ceremonies.”

Likewise through Isaiah (Isa. 1:14): “Your new moons and sabbaths and great day I cannot endure; my soul hates your holidays and fasting and your feast days.” But if the Creator had already long ago rejected all these things, and the Apostle was pronouncing that they must now be rejected, the Apostle’s very pronouncement, being in agreement with the Creator’s decrees, proves [Col. 0269C] that the Apostle proclaimed no other God than the one whose decrees he desired now to be acknowledged. In this matter, he brands both the apostles and the brethren as false who transferred the Gospel of the Creator’s Christ away from the new order foretold by the Creator and back to the old order rejected by the Creator (Gal. 1:6ff.).

## CHAPTER XXI

Moreover, if in proclaiming what was supposedly a new God he wished to exclude the law of the old God, why does he lay down nothing at all concerning the new god, but only concerning the old Law, unless because, while faith in the Creator remained, only his Law had to be brought to an end? Thus that Psalm had also sung beforehand [Note: [Col. 0269D] Pamelius reads “had sung beforehand”]: “Let us break their bonds from us [Note: [Col. 0270D] Pamelius reads “their bonds from us”], and let us cast their yoke away from us” (Ps. 2:3)—because, of course, “the nations raged, and the peoples devised [Col. 0270A] vain things; the kings of the earth took their stand, and the magistrates assembled [Note: [Col. 0270D] Semler and Oberthür read “surrounded”] together against the Lord and against his Christ” (ibid. 1–2).

And assuredly, if another god were being proclaimed by Paul, there would have been no dispute over whether the Law should be observed, since it would not, of course, belong to the new lord who was hostile to the Law. The very novelty and difference of that god would have removed not merely the question of the old and alien Law but every mention of it. But the entire issue under dispute was that, although the same God of the Law was being proclaimed in Christ, his Law was being diminished. Faith, therefore, always remained fixed upon the Creator and his Christ, while conduct and discipline were unsettled.

[Note: [Col. 0269D] For some also disputed about eating food sacrificed to idols, etc. He alludes to the Apostle’s First Epistle to the Corinthians, in which he argues at length against disputes of this kind: about food sacrificed to idols in ch. 10, women’s veiling in ch. 11, marriages or divorces in ch. 7, and the hope of resurrection in ch. 15. PAM.]

For some also disputed about eating food sacrificed to idols, others about women’s veiling, others about marriage or divorce, and some even about the hope of resurrection; about God, no one disputed. For if [Note: [Col. 0270D] Semler omits “if”] this question too had been disputed, it would itself have been found in the Apostle’s writings—and all the more so, in proportion to its fundamental importance.

But if, after the apostles’ time, the truth suffered corruption concerning the rule of God, then the apostolic tradition had suffered nothing in its own time concerning the rule of God; and no other tradition of the apostles is to be acknowledged than that which is today set forth among the apostles’ own churches. Yet you will find no church of apostolic pedigree that does not profess Christianity in the Creator. Or, if those churches which date from the beginning have been corrupted, which ones will be sound? The Creator’s adversaries, no doubt. Produce, then, some one of your own churches that possesses apostolic doctrine, and you will have prevailed [Note: [Col. 0269D] “And you will have prevailed.” That is, “you will have proved your case.” RHEN. PAM.].

Therefore, since it is established in every way that from Christ down to Marcion no other god than the Creator existed in the rule of this sacrament, our proof is now sufficiently fortified: we have shown that knowledge of the heretics’ god began with the separation of the Gospel and the Law. Our earlier definition has also been substantiated: no god whom a human being has fashioned from his own conceptions is to be believed—unless, of course, that human being is a prophet, that is, one who does not speak from his own conceptions. If Marcion can be called one, that too will have to be proved.

There should have been no need to examine anything further, [Note: [Col. 0269D] “By this wedge of truth.” That is, by this argument, arranged like a wedge-shaped battle formation in defense of the truth. He used this metaphor drawn from military discipline in ch. 2 of *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*: “With what wedge-shaped formation must we meet them?” That type of battle line was invented by Philip of Macedon to break through even the most densely packed ranks of the enemy. By another name the wedge is called a phalanx, and in Greek ἔμβολος ἱππική (*embolos hippikē*, “cavalry wedge”) and στίφος (*stiphοs*, “compact body”). LE PR.] for every heresy is driven out by this wedge of truth, when Christ is shown to be the agent of no god other than the Creator.

## CHAPTER XXII

But how will Antichrist be utterly overthrown [Note: [Col. 0270D] Rhenanus reads “ventilated” or “exposed”] unless room is also given for refuting his other insinuations, by relaxing the defense based on preliminary objections? Let us therefore proceed from here to the very person of his God—or rather, to the shadow and phantom [Note: [Col. 0270D] “Or rather, to the shadow and phantom,” etc. He alludes to Marcion’s error that Christ became man as a phantom, against which he argued above in *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 5. PAM.], corresponding to his own Christ—and let him be tested by precisely that quality through which he is preferred to the Creator.

There will assuredly be definite rules for testing God’s goodness. But first I must find that goodness and lay hold of it, and only then bring it to the rules. For when I survey the course of time, it is nowhere to be found from the beginning of created substances and [Note: [Col. 0271D] Semler inserts *in* (“in”).] from the inception of the causes, together with which it ought to have been found, acting from that point onward upon what [Note: [Col. 0271D] Semler, Pamelius, and Oberthür read “whereby”] it had to act.

For death already existed, as did sin, the sting of death, and the Creator’s own supposed wickedness. Against these the goodness of another god ought to have come to the rescue, satisfying this first rule of divine goodness: if it wished to prove itself natural, it should have brought assistance immediately, as soon as the cause arose. For all things in God must be natural and unbegotten, so that they may be eternal in accordance with his condition, lest they be regarded as adventitious and foreign, and therefore temporal and alien to eternity.

Thus goodness too will be required to be perpetual and continuous [Note: [Col. 0271D] “And continuous” is absent from the Paris edition] in God. Stored up and ready in the treasury of his natural properties, it should have preceded its own causes and objects; and, if it did precede them, it should have taken up the very first one rather than disregarding and abandoning it.

In short, I shall ask no less here [Note: [Col. 0271D] Pamelius reads “here no less.”] why his goodness did not operate from the beginning than we asked concerning God himself why he was not revealed from the beginning. And why should I not? If he had existed, he would have had to be revealed through his goodness. [Note: [Col. 0271D] “It is not permissible for God to be unable to do anything.” The sense is: It is not permissible for God to lack the power to do anything, much less to be unable to exercise his natural properties. RHEN.] It is not permissible for God to be unable to do anything, much less to be unable to exercise his natural properties. If they are restrained from running their course, they will not be natural.

For in its own nature it knows no inactivity; hence it is judged by whether it acts [Note: [Col. 0271D] Pamelius reads “if it is acted upon”] and whether it does not appear meanwhile to have been unwilling to exercise goodness under the name of nature. For nature cannot refuse itself [Note: [Col. 0271D] Thus others read.]; it directs itself in such a way that, if it ceases, [Col. 0271C] it does not exist.

But in Marcion’s god goodness at one time ceased to operate. [Note: [Col. 0272D] The Paris edition and Rhenanus read “goodness’s operation.”] Therefore, the goodness that could at one time have ceased was not natural, because natural properties are not permitted to cease. And if it is not natural, it must no longer be believed eternal, nor equal to God, because it is not eternal insofar as it is not natural. Indeed, it gives no assurance of its own perpetuity, either by establishing it from the past or by promising it for the future. For it did not exist from the beginning, and without doubt it will not exist until the end. It can at some time cease to exist, just as at one time it did not exist.

Therefore, since it is established that in the beginning that God’s goodness ceased to act—for he did not liberate humanity from the beginning—and that it ceased through choice rather than weakness, the will that suppressed goodness will now be found to be the ultimate form of malignity. For what is so malignant as to be unwilling to help when you can? As to be tormented by another’s benefit [Note: [Col. 0272D] Rigault reads “to torment benefit”]? As to permit injury?

[Note: [Col. 0271D] “In short, the Creator’s entire indictment will be entered against him.” By *elogium* he means a name or title; and by *rescribere*, “to enter against,” what in the following book he will call *transcribere*, “to transfer”: “But even if you transfer the indictment of evil from the human being to the devil.” RIG.]

In short, the Creator’s entire indictment will be entered against the one who, by delaying his goodness, assisted the Creator’s cruelties. For when it lies in someone’s power to prevent something from happening, it is already charged to him when [Note: [Col. 0272D] Pamelius inserts “already”] it happens.

Humanity is condemned to death [Note: [Col. 0271D] “Humanity is condemned to death,” etc. A beautiful description of original sin, although he expresses the following in the plural—“and from that point sins advance (or *make progress*, as the Vatican manuscript z reads) together with punishments”—because many other sins also arise from it. He also says elegantly, “those who know not a single sod of paradise,” that is, those who have never entered the soil of paradise. PAM.] for tasting from one little tree, and from that point sins advance together with punishments, and now all perish who know not a single sod of paradise. And does some “better” God either not know this or tolerate it?

If [Note: [Col. 0272D] After “if,” supply “he tolerated it.” EDD.] he did so in order to be found better by contrast, in proportion as the Creator was regarded as worse, then in this design too he is sufficiently malicious: he wished the Creator to be burdened with the consequences of his permitted actions [Note: [Col. 0272D] Rhenanus reads “allowed him to be honored by his works”], and he kept the world in affliction.

What judgment will you pass on a physician of this kind, who nourishes a disease by delaying his assistance and prolongs the danger by postponing the remedy, so that he may cure it at a higher price or with greater fame? The same sentence must be pronounced upon Marcion’s god: he is a permitter of evil, a supporter of injury, a procurer of favor, and a betrayer of benevolence, because he did not display that benevolence to its cause immediately—as he certainly would have done if he were good by nature rather than by accession; if supremely good by innate character rather than by training; if God from eternity rather than from Tiberius—or rather, to speak more truthfully, [Note: [Col. 0272D] “From Cerdo and Marcion.” He joins the two because Marcion was Cerdo’s disciple. PAM.] from Cerdo and Marcion. Let that god now grant this much to Tiberius: that divine goodness was inaugurated upon earth under his reign.

## CHAPTER XXIII

[Note: [Col. 0272D] “I set out another rule against him.” I employ another argument against him and explain to him a rule of truth. LE PR.]

I set out another rule against him: just as all things in God must be natural, so they must also be rational. [Note: [Col. 0272D] Semler and Oberthür omit “must.”] I demand a reason for his goodness, because nothing else may be regarded as good unless it is rationally good, much less may goodness itself be found irrational. An evil in which some rational ground is present will more readily be taken for good than a good deserted by reason will escape being judged evil.

I deny that Marcion’s god possesses rational goodness, first of all for this very reason: it has gone forth for the salvation of a human being who belongs to another. I know what they will say: “On the contrary, this is the principal and perfect goodness, since without any obligation arising from familiarity it is voluntarily and freely poured out upon strangers, in accordance with that goodness by which we are commanded to love even our enemies, and those who on that account are already strangers.”

Since, therefore, he did not regard humanity from the beginning, although humanity was a stranger from the beginning, by his inaction he established in advance that he had nothing to do with a stranger. Moreover, the command to love the stranger [Col. 0273A] or enemy was preceded by the command to love one’s neighbor as oneself. [Note: [Col. 0273D] “The command to love one’s neighbor,” etc. He derives this partly from Matt. 5, “You have heard that it was said to those of old: You shall love your neighbor,” and partly, and more directly, from Lev. 19, to which Christ was alluding there. PAM.]

Even though this is from the Creator’s Law, you too will have to accept it as something not destroyed by Christ but rather built up by him. For in order that you may love your neighbor all the more, you are commanded to love your enemy and the stranger. The latter is an intensification of owed goodness, a demand for goodness not owed. But what is owed precedes what is not owed, because it is primary and more worthy than its attendant and companion, namely, what is not owed.

Therefore, since the first and primary rationale of goodness is that it be displayed toward what is one’s own as an act of justice, while the second is that it be displayed toward what belongs to another from the overflowing of righteousness beyond that of the scribes [Note: [Col. 0273D] “From the overflowing of righteousness beyond that of the scribes.” I see that *super* is used here in the sense of “more than,” as Suetonius did in his *Augustus*. Although this is not especially elegant, it is a form of novelty. LE PR.] and Pharisees, what sense does it make to attribute the second rationale to one who lacks the first—to one who has no human being of his own and is therefore himself deficient [Note: [Col. 0273C] Pamelius reads “of one who has”; Semler conjectures “deficient”]? Moreover, how did a goodness deficient because it possessed nothing of its own overflow upon what belonged to another?

[Col. 0273B] Produce [Note: [Col. 0273D] Semler adds *re-*; Pamelius reads “to produce”] the primary rationale, and then claim the one that follows. Nothing can be vindicated as rational without order; so far is reason itself from losing order in any matter.

Let us now suppose that the rationale of goodness begins at the second degree, that is, toward a stranger. Not even its second degree will stand rationally, since it is destroyed in another way. For goodness toward a stranger, even at the second degree, will be regarded as rational only if it operates without injury to the one whose property is involved. Whatever the form of goodness, justice first makes it rational. Thus at the primary degree it will be rational when displayed toward one’s own property, provided it is just; and likewise toward another’s property it will be able to appear rational, provided it is not unjust.

But what sort of goodness is established through an injury, and that on behalf of a stranger? Perhaps an injurious goodness might to some extent be regarded as rational on behalf of a member of one’s own household. But on behalf of a stranger, to whom even honorable goodness was not lawfully owed, by what reasoning will such unjust goodness be defended as rational?

For what is more unjust, more inequitable, and more dishonorable than to benefit another man’s slave in such a way that he is snatched from his master, claimed for someone else, and suborned against the person of his master—and, what is still more disgraceful, while he is yet in his master’s own house, still living from his master’s granaries and still trembling under his master’s lashes? Such a champion of liberty would be condemned even in the secular world [Note: [Col. 0273D] “Such a champion of liberty would be condemned even in the secular world.” An *assertor* is one who vindicates another’s freedom. LE PR.], much more a kidnapper [Note: [Col. 0273D] “Much more a kidnapper.” He contrasts the kidnapper with the champion of liberty. *Plagium*, from which *plagiator* derives, is the abduction and concealment of a free person or slave—either because, according to Suidas, it is committed πλαγίς (*plagis*, “obliquely”) or δολίως (*doliōs*, “deceitfully”), or, as Isidore says, from ἀπὸ τοῦ πλαγίου (*apo tou plagiou*, “from the oblique”). LE PR.].

Marcion’s god acts no differently when he breaks into another’s world and snatches the human being from God, the son from his father, the pupil from his educator, and the servant from his master, in order to make him impious toward God, irreligious toward his father, ungrateful toward his educator, and wicked toward his master.

I ask you: if rational goodness makes a person like this, what sort of person would irrational goodness make? I do not suppose [Note: [Col. 0274C] “Irrational.” The Paris edition reads *an* (“whether?”); Rhenanus and Oberthür read *at* (“but”)] it could make anyone more shameless than the man who is baptized for one god in another God’s water, stretches out his hands to one god beneath another God’s heaven [Note: [Col. 0274D] “Stretches out his hands to one god beneath another God’s heaven,” etc. Christians therefore stretched out their arms toward heaven when praying. RHEN.], prostrates himself before one God upon another God’s earth [Note: [Col. 0274D] “Prostrates himself before one God.” This was the practice of penitents. RHEN.], performs acts of thanksgiving to one God over another God’s bread [Note: [Col. 0274D] “Performs acts of thanksgiving to one God over another God’s bread.” Irenaeus spoke in the same sense: “But how will it be established for them that the bread over which thanks have been given is the body of their Lord, and the cup his blood, if they do not say that he is the very Son of the Maker of the world, that is, his Word?” RIG.—“Over another God’s bread, to one God,” etc. This refers to the sacrament of the Eucharist, in which it is certain that the bread does not remain but is changed into Christ’s body. PAM.], and from another God’s goods performs works in the name of almsgiving and love on account of a different god.

Who is this god so good that a human being is made evil by him? So gracious that he makes another god—and indeed the human being’s own Lord [Note: [Col. 0274C] Semler and Oberthür omit “his own”]—angry with him?

## CHAPTER XXIV

But God, just as he is eternal and rational, is also, I think, perfect in every respect: “For you shall be perfect, just as your Father who is in heaven” (Matt. 5:48). [Col. 0274B] Produce, then, his perfect goodness as well.

That his goodness is imperfect is already sufficiently established, since it is found to be neither natural nor rational. It will now be exposed by another line of argument: it is not merely imperfect but defective, meager, and exhausted—smaller than the number of its own objects, though it is supposedly displayed toward all. For not all are saved, but fewer than the total number of both Jews and the Creator’s Christians.

Since the greater number perish, how is a goodness that remains inactive toward the greater part defended as perfect? It is something to a few and nothing to the many, yielding to perdition and sharing in ruin. But if the greater number will not be saved, it will no longer be goodness, but rather malignity, that is more perfect. For just as the operation of goodness is that which saves, so the operation of malignity is that which does not save. But that which does not save, insofar as it saves only a few, will be more complete in withholding assistance than [Col. 0274C] in giving it.

Nor can you refer to the Creator the failure of goodness toward all. Since you regard him as a judge, you may perhaps show that he must be understood as an administrator of goodness, not as its indiscriminate dispenser—which is what you claim for your god [Note: [Col. 0274D] “God” is absent from Semler.]. So completely do you prefer him to the Creator on account of this goodness alone; yet if he professes this goodness as both his only quality and his whole nature, it ought to have been lacking to no one.

But I no longer wish to argue that Marcion’s god is imperfect in goodness from the greater portion of those who perish. It is enough to show his goodness imperfect from the fact that even those whom he saves are found to possess an imperfect salvation: namely, they are saved only as far as the soul [Note: [Col. 0274D] “Namely, they are saved only as far as the soul, while they are lost in the flesh,” etc. This is what the author said above in *On the Prescription against Heretics*, ch. 51: “He affirms the resurrection of the soul alone but denies that of the body.” Against this heresy he argues at length in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*. As “division of salvation” is read here, so above, in *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 1: “Why do you divide Christ?” The statement below, “to rise in part,” has the same bearing. PAM.], while they are lost in the flesh, which according to him does not rise again.

Whence comes this division [Col. 0275A] of salvation, if not from the deficiency of goodness? For what is so characteristic [Note: [Col. 0275C] Semler and Oberthür read, “What belonged to perfect”; Latinus reads, “For what is so characteristic of perfect love”] of perfect goodness as to restore the entire human being to salvation—the whole human being condemned by the Creator and the whole human being enticed by the supremely good God?

As far as I know [Note: [Col. 0275C] Pamelius, Semler, and Oberthür read “But if now”], the flesh too is baptized among his followers [Note: [Col. 0275D] “The flesh too is baptized among his followers.” We read *tinguitur* here, in Tertullian’s manner, for *tingitur*. Similarly, above in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 8: “But the flesh too is washed,” etc. Virginity and widowhood, etc., are offered to God from the goods of the flesh; for this is what he adds here, “and the flesh is withdrawn from marriage.” At these and certain other passages we have noted that continence from marriage is confirmed—a practice which some today do not blush to attack—and likewise in *On the Veiling of Virgins* and *On the Dress of Women*, ch. 9. PAM.]; the flesh too is withdrawn from marriage; and the flesh too is savaged in confessing the Name.

But even if sins are charged to the flesh, the guilt of the soul precedes them, and the primacy of fault must rather be ascribed to the soul, which the flesh attends in the capacity of a servant. Indeed, flesh deprived of the soul sins no further. Thus in this respect too his goodness is unjust, and thereby also imperfect: it abandons to destruction the more innocent substance, which sins through obedience, not through free judgment.

Although his Christ did not assume the flesh’s reality, as your heresy has decided, he nevertheless deigned to assume its image. The very fact that he falsely represented it means that he must have owed it something. [Note: [Col. 0275C] Junius reads “had”; others, “I ought.”]

But what is a human being other than flesh? For the name “human being” was assigned by its author to the bodily, not the animate material: “And God made the human being,” he says (Gen. 2), “clay from the earth”—not the soul. For the soul came from the breath [Note: [Col. 0275D] “For the soul came from the breath.” It is a common etymology that *anima* (“soul”) is so named as though from ἄνεμος (*anemos*, “wind”). Tertullian may have had this in mind. LE PR.]: “And the human being became a living soul.” Who became so? Assuredly the one made from clay. “And God placed the human being whom he had formed in paradise”—the one he molded, not the one he breathed; the one who was flesh, not the one who was soul.

Accordingly, if this is so, with what face will you contend for the title of perfect goodness, when that goodness has failed not merely with respect to a particular part of the human being to be delivered, but with respect to the human being’s generic identity? If that grace is complete and that mercy substantial which brings salvation to the soul alone, this present life provides more, since in it we enjoy life as whole and complete persons.

Moreover, to rise in part will be to suffer a penalty, not to be liberated. It would also have belonged to perfect goodness that the human being, once liberated [Col. 0275C] into faith in the supremely good God, should immediately be removed from the dwelling and dominion of the savage god.

But even now, Marcionite, you suffer fevers; the pain of your flesh still produces for you the other thistles and thorns; and you remain exposed not only to the Creator’s thunderbolts, wars, plagues, and other blows, but also to his scorpions. In what respect do you suppose yourself liberated from the kingdom of one whose flies still trample upon you? If you have been rescued from the future, why not from the present also, so that you may be liberated perfectly [Note: [Col. 0275C] Semler adds “be”]?

Our condition [Col. 0276A] is different, since we stand before the Author, the Judge, and the offended ruler of our race. You put forward a God [Note: [Col. 0276C] “God” is absent from Franeker.] who is only good. Yet you cannot show him to be perfectly good [Note: [Col. 0276C] Rhenanus adds “God”] when you are not perfectly liberated by him.

## CHAPTER XXV

As regards the question of goodness, along these lines we have demonstrated that it is by no means commensurate with God, since it is neither unbegotten, nor rational, nor perfect, but rather dishonorable and unjust, and now unworthy even of the very name of goodness. For to precisely the degree that such goodness is compatible with God, it follows that the one commended even for such a kind of goodness is not God—and not merely for such a kind, but for it alone.

For it is now proper to examine this point as well: whether God should be characterized by goodness alone, while the other attendant qualities [Note: [Col. 0276D] “While the other attendant qualities, feelings,” etc. Here he presses Marcion and employs those terms adopted by Marcion’s disciples, such as calling God the Creator savage. LE PR.], feelings, and affections are denied. The Marcionites drive these away from their god and into the Creator; we, however, acknowledge them in the Creator as worthy of God. On this ground too we shall deny [Note: [Col. 0276C] Junius reads “we deny”] that he is God in whom all qualities worthy of God are not found.

If Marcion desired to give the title of God, under the name of Christ, to some deity from Epicurus’s school [Note: [Col. 0276D] “Some deity from Epicurus’s school.” Namely, as follows, one immovable and stupefied, and—as in ch. 26—utterly foolish. To the same point belongs the following statement about God: “he made trouble both for himself and for others, against Epicurus’s wishes.” PAM.], so that, because the blessed and incorruptible being causes trouble neither for itself nor for another—Marcion, ruminating upon this maxim, removed severity and judicial powers from him—then he ought either to have conceived a God wholly immovable and insensible; and what would such a God have to do with Christ, who caused trouble both for the Jews through his teaching and for himself through his suffering [Note: [Col. 0276C] Rhenanus reads “Jesus”]? Or else he ought also to have acknowledged the other emotions in him [Note: [Col. 0276C] Wouwer reads “he would have acknowledged”]; and then what would he have to do with Epicurus, who was necessary neither to himself nor to Christians?

For see: by the very fact that he who was previously at rest and had meanwhile taken no care through any work to make himself known, after so long an age developed a concern for human salvation—assuredly through an act of will—was he not then moved by a new will, so that he appears liable to other emotions as well?

And what will exists without the spur of desire? Concern too will accompany the will. Who will will [Note: [Col. 0276D] “Who will will what he does not desire?” etc. Rhenanus deleted this passage because it was not present in the Gorze codex. We restore it from the second Vatican manuscript because it contributes to the explanation. Whether one reads *concupivit* twice with those manuscripts or *concupiit* with the printed editions makes no difference. “Its proper functionaries” and “unwillingness,” which Rhenanus says he sets in opposition to will and applies to the human being, are Tertullianic expressions. PAM.] what he does not desire? For who will will something and desire it, yet not care about it?

Therefore, since he both willed and longed for human salvation, he made trouble both for himself and for others [Col. 0277A], against Epicurus’s wishes, with Marcion as his adviser [Note: [Col. 0277D] Others read “of Marcion”]. For he also established for himself an adversary—the very thing against which he willed, longed, and took care to act, whether sin or death; and above all the very arbiter of these things and lord of humanity, the Creator.

Moreover, nothing that will not be without an adversary can proceed without rivalry. In short, in willing, desiring, and taking care to liberate humanity, by this very fact he now rivals both the one from [Note: [Col. 0277D] Rhenanus and Oberthür read “with respect to”] whom he liberates it—since he is going to liberate it for himself in opposition to him—and the things from which he liberates it, since he is going to liberate it into other things. Accordingly, rivalry’s own functionaries must necessarily join it [Note: [Col. 0277D] Semler and Oberthür read “meet it”] against the objects of its rivalry: anger, discord, hatred, disdain, indignation, bile [Note: [Col. 0277D] Rhenanus reads “indignant bile”], unwillingness, and offense. If all these attend rivalry, while rivalry serves the liberation of humanity, and the liberation of humanity [Col. 0277B] is an operation of goodness, that goodness cannot exist without its endowments—that is, without the feelings and affections through which it is administered against the Creator—lest it be condemned as irrational on this ground also, because it lacks the feelings and affections properly owed to it. We shall defend these matters much more fully in the case of the Creator, in whom they are also made grounds for reproach.
