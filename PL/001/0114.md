The pagans, leaving no stone unturned against the Christians, further objected that they were wholly useless to the commonwealth and, for that reason alone, ought to be utterly exterminated. But they, says Tertullian, are neither Brahmans nor Indian Gymnosophists, forest-dwellers and exiles from ordinary life (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42). But who, someone may perhaps ask, were those Brahmans and Gymnosophists? Were they the same people, or peoples distinct from one another? For Apollonius of Tyana and Damis, cited by Philostratus (Philostratus, *Life of Apollonius*, bk. III, ch. 4, p. 119, and bk. VI, ch. 4, p. 265), assert that [Col. 1079D] the Brahmans were inhabitants of India and that, as he says elsewhere, the γυμνούς (“naked men”), or Gymnosophists, inhabited a hill not far from the banks of the Nile. Jerome, however, speaks in one place of the Brahmans of India and the Gymnosophists of Egypt (Jerome, Epistle 57, to Laeta, and bk. I *Against Jovinian*); elsewhere, both he and other authors whom we cited in another dissertation call them the Gymnosophists of India (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. 2, ch. 19, art. 1, p. 1160 ff.). In the same place, moreover, we noted that there were at least two kinds of them. If anyone should further prove that they established dwellings not only in India but also in Egypt, he will easily reconcile Tertullian with the other authors praised by Philostratus. In any event, they, like the [Col. 1080A] Brahmans, lived apart from other human beings and pursued an extremely harsh manner of life and abstinence from food, which we explained in the passage already cited.

But the Christians’ abstinence was very different, as our Tertullian rightly asserts: they received with thanksgiving the foods given them by God, though always observing the laws of temperance. “For,” he says, “we do not reject any fruit of God’s works; we certainly exercise restraint, lest we use them beyond measure or wrongly” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42). Nor were these same Christians inhabitants of forests or, like the Brahmans and Gymnosophists, “exiles from ordinary life” (*ibid.*), wholly separated from association with other human beings. For they lived intermingled with the pagans in towns and cities, as we have already said, and everywhere joined them in common [Col. 1080B] activities—in the forum, the market, fairs, fields, baths, and shops (*ibid.*)—not wine shops, to be sure, but buildings which, as Festus says (Festus, s.v. *Tabernacula*), were constructed from planks and, according to Nonius, served popular use (Nonius, s.v. *Taber.*). They also practiced their crafts in workshops open to anyone and stayed in *stabula* (Tertullian, *ibid.*), either inns and lodgings where guests and travelers were received, or rather dwellings and the private homes of certain people, such as farmers, of which Virgil sings:

> He seeks the lofty roof of the dwelling, and from its highest ridge  
> Sounds the shepherd’s signal.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VII, v. 512.)

And thus the Christians, says our Author, “shared the habitation of this age” [Col. 1080C] or, as our manuscripts read, “lived together in this age.”

For this reason he presses the pagans themselves as follows: “We too sail with you, and with you” (this latter phrase is absent from our manuscripts) “we serve as soldiers, farm, and trade; accordingly, we mingle our crafts and make our services available for your use” (the manuscripts and editions before Rigaltius read “our work”), and thus by reciprocal labors we assist one another. And all these things brought the greater benefit to the entire commonwealth in proportion as the number of those same Christians was then greater, as we saw above. Nothing, therefore, could have been devised more remote from the truth than the claim that Christians were useless to public affairs.

## ARTICLE II. That no harm was inflicted upon the commonwealth by Christians who did not attend the pagans’ ceremonies, did not bathe at dawn during the Saturnalia but at the proper hour, and did not recline at public banquets during the Liberalia, as was customary for beast-fighters eating their last supper

[Col. 1080D]

However clearly and certainly Tertullian demonstrated that Christians were of the greatest benefit to public affairs, the pagans nevertheless did not hesitate to protest that they were unproductive for the commonwealth because, scorning all their ceremonies and the more sacred rites of their religion, they absolutely refused to take part in them. But although Tertullian concedes this, he utterly denies that Christians were therefore of no use or advantage to the commonwealth. [Col. 1081A] He therefore addresses any pagan as follows: “If I do not attend your ceremonies, I am nevertheless a human being on that day too” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42)—one who, as was just said, by living together with the pagans and combining my crafts with theirs, supplies you and others with the many things necessary for your life and for human society, so that everyone derives the greatest benefit from my labors.

No one can fail to admit that this wedge utterly demolishes the wholly foolish accusation of the pagans. Nevertheless, Tertullian goes beyond what is sufficient and makes it evident to everyone through several examples that, even if Christians did not attend the pagans’ mysteries, either no harm was thereby brought upon public affairs or, if any was brought, it was abundantly made good by them from another source. [Col. 1081B]

But let us, if you please, hear him begin with the pagans’ Saturnalian festivities: “I do not bathe,” he says, “at dawn during the Saturnalia, lest I lose both night and day” (*ibid.*). And this is indeed how these words appear in our manuscripts and older printed copies. Rigaltius, however, rejected the adverb “at dawn” and ordered the text to read, “I bathe toward night”; but he offers us sheer nonsense, contrary to the genuine text (the same author, *On Idolatry*, ch. 10) and thought of our Author. To make this more readily recognizable, everyone should observe that the festival of the Saturnalia, as Tertullian himself elsewhere, Varro (Varro, *On the Latin Language*, bk. V, p. 51), and Festus (Festus, s.v. *Saturnia*) attest, was instituted in honor of Saturn, whom they regarded as guardian of the fields. [Col. 1081C] Livy discloses its origin and the appointed day of its celebration in these words: “By then it was the month of December; a sacrifice was offered at the temple of Saturn in Rome, a lectisternium was commanded, the senators spread the couch, and a public banquet was held; throughout the City the cry ‘Saturnalia!’ was raised by day and night, and the people were ordered to keep and observe that day as a festival forever” (Livy, bk. II, § 1). Macrobius (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, bk. I, chs. 8 and 10), and later Lipsius (Lipsius, *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 3, vol. III), debated whether these rites were held for only one day or for seven consecutive days, as they consider more probable. At that time, according to Lucian (Lucian, *Dialogue on the Saturnalia*, p. 1021), it was forbidden to conduct any serious private or public business; instead, people drank, became drunk, shouted, played, contended at dice, sang naked, [Col. 1081D] danced with lascivious movements of the body, sometimes plunged into water with faces smeared with soot, and admitted their servants to the banquet, just as Accius sang in these verses transcribed by Macrobius:

> The greater part of Greece, and Athens most of all,  
> Perform rites to Saturn, which they call the Cronia;  
> And when they celebrate the day, throughout the fields and nearly all the cities  
> They feast in gladness and each attends to his own servants.  
> The same custom has likewise been handed down from there to us:  
> That servants then feast there together with their masters.  
> (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 7.)

It is therefore no wonder that Christians condemned these abominable acts of idolatry, wantonness, drunkenness, and the other detestable practices which we have enumerated from the writers cited above, and refused to lose night and day [Col. 1082A] celebrating such impious festivals. “For this reason,” says Tertullian, “I do not bathe at dawn during the Saturnalia” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42, p. 482), in order to prepare myself to perform those rites. For who does not know that it was the custom of the pagans to bathe before a banquet? Christians, however, did not believe it permissible to prepare themselves at dawn, when winter was raging, in an untimely bath for the celebration of those abominable banquets and mysteries.

Yet they thereby brought no harm upon public affairs. “For I bathe,” says our Author, “at an honorable and healthy hour.” Rigaltius preferred “the proper” to “an honorable hour,” but against the protest of all the manuscripts. Could not our Author have said that Christians did not reject baths, but went to bathe at an honorable and healthy hour—namely, the hour at which everyone customarily bathed consistently with [Col. 1082B] decency and health? In winter, according to Pliny, this was the ninth hour, and in summer the eighth (Pliny, bk. III, Epistle 1, p. 90), or, as others observe, from noon until evening. Horace sang:

> But when the fiercer sun warned me, weary as I was, to go and bathe,  
> I flee the hours of the raging constellation.  
> (Horace, bk. I, Satire 6, v. 125.)

Does Tertullian not therefore appear to designate the midday hour, which in December, when the Saturnalia were held, was honorable and healthy, “so that,” he says, “it may preserve my warmth and blood”? “I do not bathe,” he continues, “at dawn on these cold days of the impious Saturnalia, since after the bath I can stiffen and grow pale when I am dead.” By these words he undoubtedly alludes [Col. 1082C] to another ancient custom, that of washing dead human bodies, and perhaps to these verses of Virgil concerning the funeral of Misenus:

> Some prepare hot water and cauldrons seething over the flames,  
> And wash and anoint the cold body.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VI, v. 217 ff.)

Servius, in his comments on these verses, cites the following line composed by Ennius:

> A good woman washed and anointed the body of Tarquinius.  
> (Servius, p. 411.)

Tertullian therefore says that he can indeed stiffen and grow pale in that bath after death, but that while alive he does not wish to anticipate it with the untimely winter bath with which the Saturnalia began. Although this reasoning is merely natural, it stung the pagans all the more sharply [Col. 1082D] because of the absurdity of their objection that Christians were useless to the commonwealth because they rightly fled from and execrated this bath and the impious ceremonies that followed it.

He next passes to the Liberalia, that is, the days and games dedicated to Liber or Bacchus, as follows: “During the Liberalia I do not recline at table in public” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42, p. 492). If you ask him the reason, you will find it given in another book in these words: “Since the games were indiscriminately called Liberalia, they manifestly proclaimed the honor of Father Liber. For they were first held for Liber by country people because of the benefit which they attribute to him, the gift of wine having been revealed” (the same author, *On the Shows*, ch. 5). We have indeed discussed this matter more than once. Since, therefore, these festivals [Col. 1083A] were celebrated in honor of a false god, not without idolatry and other crimes—especially gluttony and drunkenness—they were an object of the greatest horror to Christians. Our Author added: “During the Liberalia I do not recline at table in public, as is customary for beast-fighters eating their last supper” (the same author, *Apology*, loc. cit.). This is how we read it in the Puteanus manuscript and the first Colbert manuscript, but the Harleian and second Colbert manuscript have “the last” in a different grammatical form. Junius suspects that the text should read “toward the first hour,” because, he says, the beast-fighters, whom we discussed above, fought at midday. Bulenger, however, dismisses this conjecture not without laughter (Bulenger, *On Hunting in the Circus*, ch. 34), because beast-fighters fought not only at midday but also in the morning. For what could be clearer than these words of Suetonius concerning the emperor Claudius? [Col. 1083B] “He took such delight in beast-fighters and the midday spectacles that he would go down to the show even at first light and, when the people had been dismissed at noon, remain seated” (Suetonius, bk. V, *Life of Claudius*, § 34). Seneca likewise says: “In the morning men are exposed to lions and bears; at noon, to their own spectators” (Seneca, Epistle 70, p. 295). Do you wish to hear other, no less transparent words of his? Here they are: “By chance I came upon the midday spectacle, expecting some entertainment.... In the morning men are exposed to lions and bears; at noon, to their own spectators. They are ordered to be exposed to those who will kill them after they themselves have killed, and they reserve the victor for another death” (*On Anger*, bk. III, ch. 43, p. 583). Again, elsewhere: “Recently, in the school of the beast-fighters, one of the Germans, while being prepared for the morning spectacle, withdrew” (the same author, Epistle 7, pp. 172–73). Finally, he repeatedly calls them the “morning spectacles” of the arena (*On Anger*, [Col. 1083C] ch. 43, p. 583). It would not be difficult to gather many other passages here; but since these suffice, we shall add nothing except this poem of Martial:

> Recently seen in the morning arena,  
> Mucius.  
> (Martial, bk. X, Epigram 25.)

Nothing, therefore, should be changed in Tertullian’s text. He aptly calls the beast-fighters’ meal their “last,” or final, supper, because, since they were to fight the next day with other gladiators or wild beasts, they ought to have expected death rather than victory. For, as the Seneca already cited warns, “the outcome for those who fight is death” (Seneca, Epistle 7, p. 173). Even if some escaped unharmed, they nevertheless did not dare hope for this and believed that on the preceding day they were eating their last supper. From the author of the [Col. 1083D] *Acts of Saints Perpetua and Felicity*, moreover, we learn that this meal was called the “free supper,” doubtless because it was extremely lavish and the gladiators gorged themselves on foods set before them at will.

Christians therefore rightly rejected those public banquets, since they were given in honor of Bacchus and with intemperance. Yet no harm thereby came to public affairs. “Wherever I dine,” Tertullian thus addresses any pagan, “I dine from your provisions”—that is, from food which I have been accustomed to purchase from your fellow pagans in any public place not consecrated to false gods.

## ARTICLE III. How foolishly the pagans objected that Christians were unproductive for the commonwealth because they did not buy garlands, attend spectacles, or purchase incense, although they consumed more and costlier goods purchased from the Sabaeans in burying the dead than the pagans did in offering fumes to their gods

[Col. 1084A]

The use of garlands was undoubtedly frequent in Tertullian’s time, since the pagans had a particular complaint about its rejection by Christians, as though this inflicted great harm upon the commonwealth. But he answers any pagan as follows: “I do not buy a garland for my head—but what concern is that of yours?” For what harm thereby comes upon public affairs? Plainly none. “I use purchased flowers,” he says, “but I think them more pleasing when free, loose, and wandering in every direction” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42, p. 493), rather than bound into a garland. For the fragrance of flowers is perceived not by the hair [Col. 1084B] but only by the nostrils. We have treated these matters more fully in another dissertation, where we further showed that Christians rejected the use of garlands of this kind because it derived its origin from idolatry (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 13, art. 3).

The pagans insisted that Christians inflicted great harm upon the commonwealth by not attending public spectacles (Tertullian, loc. cit.). It was, to be sure, a great evil to flee things which Tertullian proves in another book to have arisen “from the general guilt of idolatry, their mother” (the same author, *On the Shows*, ch. 7). In other dissertations, moreover, we noted that these spectacles were polluted not only by idolatry but also by many other crimes, from which Christians rightly fled no differently than from a public plague. [Col. 1084C] What loss to the public good, therefore, resulted from this? A great one indeed, said the pagans. For those spectacles continued for a long time; spectators therefore bought at the entrance of the amphitheater many kinds of food with which they could refresh themselves from time to time. Christians, accordingly, incurred none of these expenses.

“But if we buy nothing there,” says Tertullian, “whatever is sold at those gatherings, should I desire it, I shall more gladly obtain from its proper places” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42, p. 493). Certain old editions read, “I obtain”; some more recent ones, “they will be obtained.” The reading “I shall obtain,” found in our manuscripts and the other editions, is better. Nothing was therefore lost to the public good if [Col. 1084D] Christians purchased in other places lawful for them the things sold at the pagans’ unlawful gatherings.

Nor, whatever those same men might still allege, does the commonwealth suffer loss “if,” says Tertullian, “we do not buy incense” (*ibid.*), which the pagans burned to false gods. For this was wholly forbidden to us, and the price of incense was too small for the pagans ever to have had reason to complain of such a loss. “For the Arabians,” he continues, “have better grounds for complaint.” But if the Arabs have any complaint about the failure to sell their more costly incense, the “Sabaeans,” he says, “will know,” or will answer on our behalf, “that their merchandise, of greater quantity and value, is expended in burying Christians than in fumigating the gods” (*ibid.*). Concerning Sabaean incense Virgil writes:

[Col. 1085A]

> India sends ivory, the soft Sabaeans their incense.  
> (Virgil, *Georgics*, bk. I, v. 57.)

And in another book, concerning Venus:

> ...where she has a temple, and a hundred altars glow  
> With Sabaean incense.  
> (The same author, *Aeneid*, bk. I, v. 420.)

Servius notes the following on these verses: “The Sabaeans are peoples near Syria and Arabia, called Sabaeans ἀπὸ τοῦ σέβεσθαι (“from worshiping”) politically, because incense, with which we placate the gods, grows among them.”

Tertullian, moreover, spoke not only of this Sabaean incense, concerning which Pliny records a great deal (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. VI, ch. 28, p. 726), but also of their other wares, or spices, as will become evident from what follows. Indeed, there was such an abundance of these in their region [Col. 1085B] that, as Strabo relates (Strabo, *Geography*, bk. XVI, p. 776), they used cinnamon, cassia, and other sweet-smelling substances of this kind as brushwood and material for making fire. We likewise read in Pliny: “The Sabaeans cook their food with incense wood” (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. XII, ch. 17, p. 32). But our Tertullian, not only in his *Apology* but also in another book, openly declares that Sabaean incense was used by Christians chiefly in burying the bodies of the dead: “Bodies,” he says, “treated with preserving spices, are set apart for burial, mausoleums, and monuments, from which they will come forth into a second life when the Lord commands” (Tertullian, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 27). Elsewhere, and more closely to our present purpose, he says: “Let the pagans see whether the same wares—I mean incense [Col. 1085C] and the other imported goods used for sacrifice to idols—also serve human beings as medicinal ointments, and serve us besides for the consolations of burial” (the same author, *On Idolatry*, ch. 11).

Let it be so, someone will argue, and let it be held demonstrated more clearly than the noonday sun that Christians used Sabaean incense, the most precious of all, in burying the bodies of the dead. Could Tertullian nevertheless assert without great hyperbole that the Sabaeans sold a greater quantity and value of their incense for Christian burials than to the nations for fumigating their gods? Did not a great many pagans, he will say, burn Sabaean incense to their gods with a full and lavish hand? But whoever makes this objection ought to observe that these people were richer than others and far fewer in number than the poorer majority, [Col. 1085D] who offered incense with only three fingers or incense worth a single *as*, as will be said below (below, ch. 29, art. 2). In addition, we have observed that the multitude of Christians was greater than that of the pagans. If Christians therefore embalmed the bodies of their dead with Sabaean incense, the Sabaeans must have sold them more incense for this pious office than they sold to the pagans, the greater part of whom customarily gave their gods only a very few grains of cheaper, ordinary incense.

## ARTICLE IV. How undeservedly the pagans complained that the revenues of their temples had been dwindling since the Christians arose, although Christians did not have enough to supply both their gods and their human beggars, and paid their taxes with as much care as the same pagans used deceit in evading them; that no one could complain of the unproductiveness of Christians except infamous pimps, procurers, bathhouse-panders, assassins, poisoners, magicians, haruspices, soothsayers, and astrologers; and that, if Christians had perhaps inflicted any harm upon the commonwealth, they made it good elsewhere with interest

[Col. 1086A]

Although what we have thus far related is clear and evident to everyone, it could not restrain the blind assaults of the pagans or extinguish their unbridled fury against the Christians. For they still shouted everywhere with a great clamor that the Christians had arisen for the destruction of the commonwealth. Ever since their religion was disseminated throughout the world, they said: “The revenues [Col. 1086B] of our temples are daily becoming insolvent” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42, p. 493), that is, are gradually diminishing. For every Christian, as Tertullian himself admits, was forbidden to “undertake the management of temples or administer their revenues” (the same author, *On Idolatry*, ch. 17). Since the number of Christians was greater than that of pagans, as we have frequently said, far fewer people were consequently paying the revenues of the temples.

But even if matters were so, the pagans ought to have remonstrated not only with the Christians but also with their own adherents. For Tertullian asks them: “How few now cast a contribution?” (the same author, *Apology*, loc. cit.). A *stips*, moreover, was a stamped coin. “For,” says Varro, “when they give *asses* to gods with treasuries, they call it a *stips*” [Col. 1086C] (Varro, *On the Latin Language*, bk. IV, near the end). Hence Ovid sang:

> The people contributed bronze; from it Metellus built  
> The shrines, he says: hence the custom of giving contributions remains.  
> (Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. IV, § 3.)

If the pagans, therefore, did not give contributions to the temples, they certainly harmed them no less than the Christians did.

The pagans persisted, and, as Arnobius says, diviners, soothsayers, prophets, and others [Col. 1086D] bitterly lamented the emptiness of their temples (Arnobius, *Against the Pagans*, bk. II), and that even paltry contributions could not be extorted from their very few clients. We know that Pliny the Younger wrote in his letter to the emperor Trajan that temples and solemn rites had begun to be frequented again in his time. For these are his exact words: “It is certainly sufficiently established that the temples, which had by now been almost deserted, have begun to be frequented, that the solemn rites long discontinued are being resumed, and that victims are everywhere on sale, though until now a buyer was very rarely found” (Pliny, bk. X, Epistle 101, p. 376). His admission that the temples and sacred rites of the pagans had been almost deserted is entirely true. But in adding that they had begun to be frequented again, he ineptly flattered Trajan, whose clemency he wished to extol by fair means or foul. For as the number of Christians increased, pagan temples were less frequented and fewer revenues were paid.

Tertullian therefore freely admits this and answers the pagans [Col. 1087A] that, however great that loss might be, it was abundantly repaid by Christians. “For we do not have enough,” he says, “to bring aid both to human beings and to your beggar gods” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42, p. 494)—that is, all our goods and all our resources do not suffice to bring aid to your beggars, both gods and human beings, whom we have discussed elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 784). Although, therefore, we cast no contribution into the treasury of your temples, into which it is sacrilege for us to enter together with you, elsewhere we much more frequently and abundantly bestow it upon anyone who asks (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 20, art. 30). “For,” he says, “our compassion spends more street by street”—that is, gives more to all who beg throughout the streets and roads of the cities—[Col. 1087B] “than your religion does temple by temple” (Tertullian, loc. cit.), that is, than it gives in each of your temples.

Moreover, Christians paid the taxes imposed by the emperors with much greater care and better faith than the pagans. “Indeed,” he says, “the taxes will thank the Christians, who faithfully pay what is due, because we refrain from defrauding another of his property” (*ibid.*). For they knew Christ’s command: “Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s” (Matt. 22:21). Hence Justin Martyr openly said to the pagans: “Φόρους καὶ εἰσφορὰς τοῖς ἐφ` ὑμῶν τεταγμένοις πανταχοῦ πρὸ πάντων πειρώμεθα φέρειν. Indeed, we strive everywhere, above all others, to pay taxes and assessments to the collectors appointed by you” (Justin, *Apology to Antoninus*). The pagans, however, defrauded those taxes to such a degree that, “if an account is taken,” [Col. 1087C] in Tertullian’s words, “of how much is lost to the taxes through the fraud and falsehood of your declarations, it may readily be reckoned that the complaint concerning one category is compensated by the advantage furnished under the other accounts” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 42, p. 495). Here the term “declaration” certainly means the public disclosure or statement of all the property which each person possessed. But this does not remove the entire difficulty of this rather obscure phrase, which more acute interpreters explain in two ways. Some think Tertullian’s meaning to be this: if one considers how much harm the pagans bring upon the commonwealth through “the fraud and falsehood of their declarations” (*ibid.*), or through a false declaration of their property, it will at once be recognized from this category or this single example that such harm is greater than whatever benefit [Col. 1087D] they confer by other means. Others explain Tertullian’s meaning thus: Christians pay taxes with as much care and good faith as the pagans employ fraud and falsehood in evading them; and this single example fully establishes that the pagans’ complaint about the uselessness of Christians is most unjust, because Christians paid taxes more accurately than all others, and, if they brought any harm from another quarter, it was abundantly compensated by the advantage of the other considerations that have been indicated. Now, if Tertullian is not there comparing the pagans with the Christians, the first interpretation seems preferable, for from it one gathers that they complained entirely undeservedly of the harm these brought upon the commonwealth, since they conferred another and greater benefit. But if he there compared Christians and pagans [Col. 1088A] with one another, you may, so far as we are concerned, prefer the second exposition.

Furthermore, to press the pagans more closely, our Author (the same author, *ibid.*) freely concedes to them that certain persons could be found among them who complained of what he calls the Christians’ “unproductiveness.” But who, good God, were these men? Certainly none other than abandoned men, infamous for their vices and crimes, whom it is of the highest benefit to the commonwealth neither to please nor favor. He divides them into three classes. The first consisted of the most disgraceful ministers of abominable lust, whom he calls pimps, procurers, and bathhouse-panders. Cicero mentions the first two when inveighing against Verres: “Let there be silence concerning his nocturnal bacchanals and vigils; let no mention [Col. 1088B] be made of pimps, gamblers, and procurers” (Cicero, *Against Verres*, bk. II, p. 68, line 3). The distinction between pimps and procurers was that the former brought women who did not resist to fornication, while the latter brought unwilling women. Hence Horace sang:

> “Once she has praised you,” says the fornicator, “beware lest she ask more;  
> Hand easy Penelope over to the better man. Do you think  
> One so virtuous and modest can be procured?”  
> (Horace, bk. II, Satire 5, v. 75 ff.)

The *aquarioli* were of the same sort; according to Festus, this was the name given to “the sordid attendants of shameless women” (Festus, bk. XX, *On the Meaning of Words*, s.v. *Aquarioli*). Hence Apuleius mentions “the embittered *aquariolus* of his shameless and meretricious wife” (Apuleius, *Apology*, before the middle, p. 366). Men of this most filthy kind [Col. 1088C] carried water to women for washing away filth. But consider, please, whether the emperor Commodus should be enrolled in this most impure herd of men; Lampridius has recorded the following about him: “He lived together with gladiators and carried water as a pimp’s servant” (Lampridius, *Life of Commodus*). Some, however, hold that the term *aquariolus* means a bath attendant who guarded the clothes of bathers and acted as a pander. Hence Martial sang:

> When the bath attendant, once the lamp is extinguished, admits you  
> Among the tomb-haunting adulteresses.  
> (Martial, bk. III, Epigram 62.)

But the complaint of such men about the unproductiveness and uselessness of Christians was certainly a commendation and proclamation of both the Christians’ virtue and their usefulness.

[Col. 1088D] Nor can any different judgment be passed upon the assassins, poisoners, and magicians who make up Tertullian’s second class (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 43). For these lay snares for the lives of others and very frequently took them away. Assassins were so called from the *sica*, or shorter sword, which they carried to kill people by ambush. How frequently others were secretly and treacherously killed by these savage men you may learn from these few words of Cicero: “These are everyday things: daggers and poisons” (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. III, p. 251, line 15). But to come nearer Tertullian’s times, Cyprian says: “Hence poisoners, hence assassins, as swift to sin as they are free from punishment after sinning” (Cyprian, book *To Demetrianus*).

[Col. 1089A] It is now evident why those who administer poison to others, prepare it, or sell it are next called poisoners by our Author. Magicians, who are assigned the third place in this class, were undoubtedly not unlike them. For by diabolical arts, maleficent practices, and deceptions they sought to harm others and kill them. These are the persons whom Horace marks in the following verses:

> What witch, what great god, with Thessalian poisons,  
> Will be able to release you?  
> (Horace, bk. I, Ode 27, vv. 21–22.)

Finally, Tertullian places in the third class haruspices, soothsayers, and astrologers (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 43), whom Arnobius, cited by us shortly above, calls empty diviners. For by their detestable arts, frauds, and deceptions they advertised that they could [Col. 1089B] foretell the future. The soothsayers, or *farioli*, whom we discussed above, pretended to prophesy by divine inspiration, and the haruspices by inspecting sacrificial victims. Terence, however, stings both with the barbs of these verses:

> A hen crowed; the soothsayer forbade it.  
> The haruspex prohibited beginning any new  
> Business before midwinter.  
> (Terence, *Phormio*, act IV, scene 4.)

Cicero certainly spared neither these nor the others when he wrote: “Μαντικὴ your art, which in Latin is called divination, by which we would be steeped in such great superstition if we wished to listen to you: haruspices, augurs, soothsayers, prophets, and diviners would have to be cultivated by us” (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. I, p. 203, line 36). But these matters have been discussed elsewhere and will again be discussed later.

[Col. 1089C] That criminal band, hostile and wholly destructive to the commonwealth, is completed by the astrologers. “The common people,” says Aulus Gellius, “call ‘astrologers’ those who should be called ‘Chaldaeans’ by their national designation” (Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights*, bk. I, ch. 9). Earlier, Cicero had explained their art and profession in these words: “Named not from their art but from the name of their people, they are thought, through long observation of the stars, to have created a body of knowledge by which it could be predicted what would happen to each person and under what destiny each was born” (Cicero, *On Divination*, bk. I, beginning, p. 255, line 4). Hence Juvenal:

> Your nativity is known to the astrologers.  
> (Juvenal, Satire 14, after the middle.)

But this same Cicero [Col. 1089D] calls them and their science “monstrosities of the Chaldaeans,” and warns us that Eudoxus, their leader, recorded in writing that “the Chaldaeans are by no means to be believed in their prediction and description of each person’s life from the day of birth” (Cicero, *On Divination*, bk. II, p. 291, line 49). To omit much else, our Tertullian asserts without hesitation in another book that evil angels, or demons, were the teachers of this impious art, and then adds: “Let the astrologers be expelled, like their angels: disciples and teachers suffer the same penalty of exile” (Tertullian, *On Idolatry*, ch. 9). Suetonius relates that these astrologers were driven from the City and Italy by the emperor Vitellius and were afterward condemned to death (Suetonius, bk. VII, § 14). But earlier, according to Tacitus, [Col. 1090A] decrees of the senate had been issued concerning this exile of the astrologers (Tacitus, *Annals*, bk. II, before the middle). Finally, Ulpian records many things concerning them and may be consulted (Ulpian, *On the Office of the Praetor*, bk. VII).

These, then, were the men of the three classes who complained that Christians were useless to the commonwealth because they abhorred their abominable works and brought them no profit. “But to be unprofitable to the most abandoned men,” says Tertullian, “is undoubtedly a great profit to the commonwealth” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 43).

Finally, he demonstrates by this further argument that Christians were of the greatest benefit to the pagans: “How many do you have”—the old editions read “how many” in the accusative, Lacerda “when”—“I do not mean those who now cast demons out of you; I do not mean those who also lay prayers before the true God on your behalf, because perhaps you do not believe it, but simply those from whom you can fear nothing?” (*ibid.*). Rigaltius, however, on his own authority and contrary to the testimony of the manuscript and printed copies, removed the words “because perhaps you do not believe it” as superfluous or displeasing to him. But what harm, I ask you, was done by those words, through which the Author signifies that the pagans were by no means persuaded that Christians prayed to the true God on their behalf? Yet this, he says, was as certain as it was plainly established that demons had been cast by Christians out of the bodies of those same pagans, as we have most evidently proved.
