## CHAPTER XXXVIII

One thing is desired by nature, [Note: [Col. 0717C] “It is one thing to be desired by nature,” Fran., Gagn.] another by necessity; one according to its own proper condition, another for the sake of something else.

The soul will therefore desire food for itself, certainly, because of necessity, but for the flesh by reason of the flesh’s natural property. For the flesh is the soul’s house, and the soul is the flesh’s tenant. The tenant will therefore desire, by reason of and under the necessity of that name, whatever benefits the house throughout the whole period of his tenancy—not so that he himself may be built up, nor so that he himself may be fortified, [Note: [Col. 0717C] *Gloriandus* (“glorified”), Fran.] nor so that he himself may be propped up, but only so that he may be contained, because he cannot otherwise be contained unless the house is kept standing.

Otherwise, when the house has fallen apart, the soul will be able to depart unharmed despite the loss of supports that were proper to the house, since it possesses its own foundations and the nourishment of its own condition: immortality, rationality, sentience, intelligence, and freedom of choice.

## CHAPTER XLI

The soul’s evil, therefore, apart from what is built upon it through the arrival of an evil spirit, precedes that arrival through the defect of its origin; it is, in a certain sense, natural. [Note: [Col. 0720D] **“In a certain sense, natural.”** He distinguishes what is “natural in a certain sense,” so called from the corruption of nature, from what is properly natural. He still acknowledges remnants of the latter—namely, of that good which was originally primary in the soul before the human fall, and which, in the now-corrupted human being, is not so much extinguished as overshadowed. PAM.] For, as we have said, the corruption of nature is another nature, possessing its own god and father—namely, the very author of corruption.

Yet there is also present within the soul its good: that primary, divine, genuine good which is natural in the proper sense. For what comes from God is not so much extinguished as overshadowed. [Note: [Col. 0720D] **“For what comes from God is not so much extinguished,” etc.** He takes a fine metaphor from light, for God made the soul luminous, as above in ch. 9. Observe in passing that Tertullian was unwilling to say that free choice was extinguished in the human being after sin, as our recent heretics particularly wish it to be regarded as not merely diminished but destroyed. LAC.] It can be overshadowed because it is not God; it cannot be extinguished because it comes from God.

Accordingly, just as light obstructed by some obstacle remains but does not appear, if the obstacle’s density is great enough, [Note: [Col. 0720C] *Via* (“way”) added, Codex Agobardinus.] so also the good in the soul, oppressed by evil, according to evil’s degree either remains altogether inactive, its light concealed, [Note: [Col. 0720C] *Occulta* (“concealed”), Fran.] or shines wherever it is permitted, when it finds freedom. Thus there are some very wicked and some very good people, and yet there is one kind of soul in them all. Thus there is something good even in the worst and something bad even in the best.

For God alone is without sin, and the only human being without sin is Christ, [Note: [Col. 0720D] **“The only human being without sin.”** By nature, namely; for the blessed Virgin too was without sin, but by the gift of grace, not by what was due to nature. EDD.] because Christ is also God. Thus the soul’s divinity also bursts forth in presentiments, [Note: [Col. 0720C] *Praesentia* (“present things”), Ven.] by virtue of that prior good, and its consciousness of God comes forth in testimony: “God is good!” “God sees,” and “I entrust it to God.”

For this reason no soul is without guilt, because none is without a seed of good. Accordingly, when it comes to faith, reformed by a second birth from water and power from above, [Note: [Col. 0720C] “And” inserted, Jun.] the curtain of its former corruption having been removed, it beholds all its light. It is also received by the Holy Spirit, [Col. 0720C] just as at its former birth it was received by a profane spirit. [Note: [Col. 0720C] *Profanos* (“profane spirits”), Jun.]

The flesh follows the soul as it marries the Spirit, like property transferred as a dowry; and now it is no longer the soul’s servant but the Spirit’s. O blessed marriage, if it admits no adultery!

## CHAPTER XLII

[Col. 0721A]

It remains now to discuss death, so that the subject may end there [Note: [Col. 0721C] **“So that the subject may end there.”** That is, so that this discourse on the soul may end there. RIG.] where the soul itself completes its account. [Note: [Col. 0721D] **“Where the soul itself completes its account.”** Where the soul, departing from the body, counts each one of its deeds performed in the body and collects their total, as though it were a sum of money composed from many accounts. RIG.]

Epicurus, however, following a sufficiently common opinion, denied that death concerns us. “For what is dissolved,” he says, “lacks sensation and is nothing to us.” Yet what is dissolved and lacks sensation is not death itself but the human being who undergoes it. But Epicurus has assigned passivity to the thing to which the action belongs. And if it belongs to the human being to undergo death, which dissolves the body and destroys sensation, how absurd it is to say that so great a force does not concern the human being!

Seneca is much more strained: “After death,” he says, “all things come to an end, even death itself.” If this is so, death will now concern itself as well; if it too comes to an end, it concerns the human being all the more, since, while bringing everything in him to an end, death itself also comes to an end in him.

“Death is nothing to us”: therefore life too is nothing to us. For if that by which we are dissolved lies outside us, then that by which we are put together also lies outside us. If the loss of sensation is nothing to us, neither is the acquisition of sensation anything to [Col. 0721B] us. But let the one who also destroys the soul destroy death as well. [Note: [Col. 0721C] *Si et* (“if also”), Jun.]

We, however, shall treat death as a posthumous life and [Note: [Col. 0721C] An uncertain and apparently corrupt variant is reported: *Interimat, quia et anima in nobis ut depositum habitat, et ut*, Pam.; *A nobis ut depositum avita, et*, Codex Agobardinus.] as another province of the soul; [Note: [Col. 0721D] **“We, as concerning a posthumous life and another province of the soul.”** Septimius speaks with great elegance: “we,” namely Christians, treat death as though treating a posthumous life. For we know that eternal life remains, and that one passes to it from death. It therefore comes after death. RIG.] and we ourselves pertain to death, if death pertains to us. [Note: [Col. 0721C] *Pertinet* (“concerns”) omitted, Rig., Ven.] Finally, sleep, death’s mirror, is no foreign subject either.
