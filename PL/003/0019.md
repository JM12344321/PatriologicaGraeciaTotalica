## IV

In these same times Irenaeus was especially distinguished for piety and learning. Eusebius adduces a notable passage from the second book of his *Against Heresies*, in which he testifies that “examples of divine and wondrous power,” he says, [Note: [Col. 0109C] “In the second book of the same work, he indicates in the following words that examples of divine and extraordinary power still survived down to his own time in certain churches:

> ‘So far are they from raising the dead, as the Lord raised them and the apostles did through prayer! Indeed, among the brotherhood it frequently happened, when necessity required and the whole church in a particular place petitioned with much fasting and supplication, that the spirit of the deceased returned, and the person was granted in answer to the prayers of the saints.’

And again, after other remarks, he says:

> ‘But if [Col. 0109D] they say that the Lord performed such deeds merely in appearance, we shall refer them to the prophetic writings and demonstrate from them that all these things were both foretold concerning him and certainly came to pass, and that he alone is the Son of God. Therefore, in his name those who are truly his disciples, having received grace from him, perform works for the benefit of the rest of mankind, according as each has received his gift from him. For some assuredly and truly drive out demons, so that those very persons who have been cleansed of evil spirits frequently come to believe and remain in the Church; others have foreknowledge of future things, visions, and prophetic utterances; still others heal the sick through the laying on of hands and restore them to health. Indeed, as we have said, even the dead have been raised and remained with us for many years. And what more? It is impossible to state the number of gifts which the Church throughout the whole world, having received them from God, exercises each day in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, for the benefit [Col. 0110D] of the nations, neither deceiving anyone nor making money from them. For as she has freely received from God, so she also freely ministers.’

And in another place the same author writes:

> ‘We also hear of many brethren in the Church who possess prophetic gifts, who through the Spirit speak in every kind of tongue, bring the hidden things of men into the open for their benefit, and expound the mysteries of God.’

These statements concern the continuance among the worthy, down to the times indicated, of the various gifts.” *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 7.]

still survived in some churches in his own age, in the following words:

> “So far are they,” he says (*Against Heresies*, II.57), “from raising a dead person through prayer, as the Lord and the apostles did! Indeed, it has very frequently happened among the brethren, when some necessity required it and the whole church in a particular place petitioned with much fasting and prayer, that the soul of the deceased returned to the body, and the person’s life was granted in answer to the prayers of the saints.”

And a little later:

> “But if,” he says, “they are going to claim that the Lord did these things by some simulation and trickery, we shall lead them to the oracles of the prophets and demonstrate from them that all these things were most certainly both foretold concerning him and performed by him, and that he is the Son of God. Hence also, in his name, all who are his true disciples, having received grace from him, [Col. 0110B] perform these things for the salvation and benefit of all others, according as each has received his gift from him. Some drive out demons most certainly and truly, so much so that those who have been delivered from evil spirits of this kind frequently embrace the faith and remain in the Church. Others have been allotted foreknowledge of the future, visions, and prophetic predictions. Others heal the sick through the laying on of hands and restore them to their former health. Moreover, as we said above, the dead have sometimes been raised and afterward remained with us for many years. What more need be said? No reckoning can be made of the gifts which the Church throughout the whole world, having received them from God, exercises daily in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, for the assistance of the nations, neither deceiving anyone [Col. 0110C] nor amassing money. For as she has freely received from God, so she also freely ministers.”

“The same man,” Eusebius says, “writes thus in another place:

> ‘Likewise, we hear of many brethren in the Church who have obtained gifts of prophecy, who through the Spirit speak in every kind of tongue, bring hidden things into the light for the benefit of men, and expound the secret mysteries of God.’

From these statements, therefore, it is apparent that the manifold variety of spiritual gifts remained until those times among those who were worthy.”

Since this was the state of the Church at that time, and there was so great a variety and abundance of divine charisms that their number could not be reckoned; since all who were ἀληθῶς αὐτοῦ μαθηταί, “truly his—that is, Christ’s—disciples,” abounded in prophetic gifts and either raised the dead, cast out demons, foresaw future events, penetrated the secrets of hearts, restored the sick to health through the laying on of hands, or spoke through the Spirit in every kind of tongue: do not those act imprudently who measure the affairs of the Christians of that time by the present state of affairs [Col. 0111A] and express such great astonishment that a holy woman, an ἀληθῶς αὐτοῦ μαθήτρια, “a true disciple of his,” and thus accustomed to these divine charisms—one who “knew that she conversed with the Lord”—released now from every earthly affection and actually setting out upon martyrdom, should have assumed such confidence in the divine favor, “whose benefits she had so greatly experienced,” that she confidently promised to report to her brother whether what was to come would be martyrdom or a reprieve?

In Basnage’s judgment, this is far removed from the customs of those times. But is it not still farther removed from present custom that which Irenaeus testifies happened πολλάκις, “frequently among the brethren”: that, “when some necessity required it and the whole church in a particular place petitioned with much fasting and prayer, the soul of the deceased returned to the body, and the person’s life was granted in answer to the prayers of the saints”? [Col. 0111B]

## V

But so that Basnage may understand with what confidence the ancient Christians administered these divine charisms and powers, let him hear what Tertullian boldly declares concerning their authority over demons [Note: [Col. 0111D] *Apology*, chapter 23]:

> “Let someone be produced here before your tribunals who is clearly possessed by a demon. If commanded by any Christian whatsoever to speak, that spirit will as truly confess itself to be a demon as elsewhere it falsely claims to be a god. Likewise, let someone be brought forward from among those who are thought to be under divine influence, who breathe upon altars and conceive the divinity from the smell of the sacrifice, who labor to belch forth their inspiration and utter their prophecies while gasping. Let that very *Virgo Caelestis* (“Heavenly Virgin”), the promiser of rains, and that very Aesculapius, the revealer of medicines, the supplier of remedies to Socordius, Denatius, and Asclepiodotus, who otherwise would have died—unless they confess themselves to be demons, not daring to lie to a Christian, shed the blood of that most insolent Christian on that very spot. What is more manifest than this deed? What is more trustworthy than this proof? The simplicity of truth stands openly before you. Its own power supports it. No suspicion will be possible. You will say that it is done by magic or some fraud of that kind—if your eyes and ears permit you. But what can be alleged against that which is displayed in naked sincerity?”

Is this not great confidence: to bargain with tyrants over the blood of any Christian whatsoever, if he cannot exercise over the demons whatever power he chooses and at his own discretion? Will Basnage dare to say that this confidence also was proper to the family of Montanus alone, because Tertullian was attached to that sect?

But there is no place here for cavils of that kind. This is not a matter of hidden and private [Col. 0111D] visions or revelations, but of something publicly put to the test under the very tribunals of pagan magistrates. The honor of the Christian religion and the blood of the Christian man are placed in jeopardy unless the proffered sign proceeds as intended. Does not Tertullian conduct himself and speak as though Christians kept the Holy Spirit for restraining demons and subjecting them to themselves, like a certain necklace enclosed in their own casket, ready at their command to employ its power and authority whenever they wished and at their own discretion? From what source, moreover, did Christians derive such confidence, if not from daily experience of powers of this kind?

“Fearing Christ,” the cited author continues, [Col. 0112A] “and God in Christ, they are subjected to the servants of God and Christ. Thus, by our touch and by our breath . . . even from bodies they depart at our command, unwillingly and in pain, and blushing in your presence.”

In his book *On the Soldier’s Crown*, moreover, he clearly and openly indicates that not only clerics or more distinguished Christians but Christians of every kind indiscriminately, even laymen, were endowed with this spirit and this power. For he deters Christians—namely, those whom the discipline of the catholic Church permitted to serve—from entering or continuing military service, among other arguments, on the ground that soldiers would have to stand guard at night before the temples of demons over whom the Christian exercised authority by day and whom he put to flight by exorcisms [Note: [Col. 0112D] Chapter 11]:

> “And will he defend by night those whom by day he put to flight with exorcisms, reclining and resting upon the spear with which Christ’s side was pierced?” [The OCR reading *luncho* is uncertain.]

Irenaeus, as we have seen, agrees with him, saying without any distinction that Christ’s true disciples were endowed, among other gifts, with the power to cast out demons βεβαίως καὶ ἀληθῶς, “certainly and truly.”

And Justin, who was earlier than both, calls the pagans themselves as witnesses of these powers, since they were performed openly and before their eyes [Note: [Col. 0112D]

> “Even now you can learn this from what takes place before your eyes. For throughout the world and in your own city, many of our people—Christians, I mean—have exorcised in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, many persons possessed by demons whom all your other exorcists, enchanters, and sorcerers could not cure; and they have cured them and continue to cure them even now, rendering powerless and driving out the demons that possess those persons.”

*Second Apology to the Senate*]:

> “You can understand this very thing from what takes place before your eyes. For throughout the entire world and in this city of yours, very many of our people—Christians, I mean—have, by adjuring them in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, [Col. 0112C] healed many persons seized by the violent disorders of demons whom your other adjurers, enchanters, and sorcerers were unable to cure; and they continue to heal them even now, disarming and expelling from men the demons that had besieged them.”

And in the *Dialogue with Trypho* [Note: [Col. 0112D]

> “For we call him our helper and redeemer; even the demons tremble at the power of his name, and today, when adjured in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, the man who became procurator of Judea, they submit to us.”

And:

> “Even now we who believe in our Lord Jesus, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, adjure all demons and evil spirits and hold them subject to us.”]:

> “For we call him our helper and redeemer; even the demons tremble and recoil at the power of his name, and today as well, when adjured in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, the man who served as procurator of Judea, they obey us.”

And again:

> “Even now we who believe in our Lord Jesus, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, adjure all demons and malignant spirits and hold them subjected beneath our power.”

The great Athanasius speaks about this matter with no less confidence than Tertullian, although in the fourth century these charisms of the Holy Spirit did not abound with the same fullness as at the beginning [Col. 0113A] of the third [Note: [Col. 0113D]

> “These things which we say do not consist in words alone, but also possess the testimony of truth from experience itself. Let whoever wishes come forward and behold the mark of virtue in Christ’s virgins and in young men who practice chastity in continence, and the proof of immortality in so great a company of his martyrs. Let whoever wishes to test what has been said come, and amid the very illusions of demons, the deception of oracles, and the wonders of magic, let him use the sign of the cross, which they ridicule, merely naming Christ; and he will see how through him demons flee, oracles cease, and all magic and sorcery are rendered powerless.”

Book *On the Incarnation of the Word of God*, no. 48, new edition]:

> “The things we say do not consist in words alone; experience itself also gives splendid testimony to their truth. Let whoever wishes approach and contemplate the marks of virtue in Christ’s virgins, as well as in young men who profess chastity, and the proof of immortality in so great a company of Christ’s martyrs. Let whoever desires to test what we have said come, and amid the very illusions of demons, the deceptions of oracles, and the wonders of magic, let him employ the sign of the cross—which among them is an object of ridicule—and merely name Christ. He will immediately see how quickly demons are put to flight through him, oracles cease, and every magical art and every sorcery vanish.”

If it is not incredible that the ancient Christians exercised so great a power against demons and possessed the power of the Holy Spirit for restraining malignant spirits, like a sword hanging from the thigh, ready at their command to draw it whenever they wished and at their own discretion, why does it appear incredible that a woman filled with the divine Spirit should have conducted herself as though she possessed that Spirit in her breast, like a certain necklace enclosed in a casket, ready at her command to employ its illuminations whenever she wished and at her own discretion?

I do not see what can be answered to this argument. It rests not upon uncertain or obscure examples, not upon doubtful or perhaps fabricated revelations, not upon vague rumors or traditions of the common people, but upon the reliability of deeds performed openly and before the eyes of all, attested and recorded in the clearest monuments. The ancient Christians employed the power of the Holy Spirit at their discretion to repress [Col. 0113C] demons. “What is more manifest than this deed?” Why, then, could a candidate for martyrdom not obtain the illuminations of the Holy Spirit at her discretion? “What is more trustworthy than this proof? The simplicity of truth stands openly before us; its own power supports it; nothing may be suspected”—neither Montanism nor fanaticism nor anything of that kind.

## VI

In the same persecution of Severus as Perpetua, Saint Potamiaena, the most celebrated among the Alexandrian martyrs of this period, was put to death. In Eusebius there survives not only a notable apparition of hers but also a remarkable example of confidence in God, no less worthy of admiration than Perpetua’s. As usual, I shall append Eusebius’s words [Note: [Col. 0113D]

> “As soon as she had received the sentence pronounced against her, Basilides, one of those enrolled in military service, took charge of her and led her away to death. When the crowd attempted to molest her and insult her with licentious words, he restrained them, driving away those who insulted her and displaying very great pity and humanity toward her. She, accepting the man’s sympathy for her, urged him to take courage, for after her departure she would petition her Lord on his behalf, and before long would repay him for what he had done for her. . . . Such was the contest accomplished by that renowned maiden. Not long afterward Basilides . . . declared himself to be a Christian and openly confessed it. . . . When the brethren in God came to him and inquired about the cause of this sudden and extraordinary impulse, he is said to have replied that Potamiaena had appeared to him at night three days after her martyrdom and placed a crown upon his head. She said that she had entreated the Lord on his behalf, had obtained her request, and would take him to herself before long. Thereupon the brethren imparted to him the seal in the Lord; and on the following day, after distinguishing himself in his testimony to the Lord, he was beheaded.”

Book VI of the *Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 5]:

> “Accordingly, immediately after she had been condemned by the judge’s sentence, [Col. 0114A] Basilides, one of the attendants, led her to execution. When the crowd attempted to harass her and mock her with obscene words, Basilides drove off those who were insulting the virgin and gave many indications of mercy and humanity toward her. She, gladly accepting the man’s kindness toward her, told him to be of good courage: after her death she would obtain his salvation from the Lord and would shortly repay the benefits bestowed upon her. . . . Such was the contest fought by the most noble virgin. Shortly afterward Basilides . . . openly professed himself a Christian. . . . When certain brethren in the Lord came to him and asked the cause of that sudden and unexpected conversion, [Col. 0114B] he is said to have replied that Potamiaena, appearing to him by night on the third day after her martyrdom, had placed a crown upon his head and said that she had prayed to the Lord on his behalf and had obtained what she sought, and that not long afterward he himself would migrate to the realms above. After this, having received the seal of the Lord from the brethren, on the following day he gloriously confessed Christ and was beheaded.”

Did Potamiaena promise Basilides salvation and certain recompense for his benefits with less confidence than Perpetua promised Saturus a prediction of the future event? Does not Potamiaena also conduct herself as though she held the ordinances of divine predestination in ready money, or as though after death she would become the arbiter of the divine counsels, employing at her own discretion the aids of grace that is victorious of itself, by which Christ would grant Basilides “not only to believe in him, [Col. 0114C] but also to suffer for him”?

If Potamiaena hoped that her prayers for Basilides would shortly be heard, why could Perpetua not also have been led to the same hope? If it is counted to Potamiaena’s praise that she gave Basilides a sure hope of future salvation, why is it charged against Perpetua as a fault that she gave Saturus a sure hope of a future revelation? If no one has ever brought Potamiaena’s prophecy under suspicion of Montanism, why shall we drag Perpetua’s similar prophecy into the odium of Montanism? For how do these statements differ: “Be of good courage; after my death I shall obtain salvation for you,” and, “Tomorrow I shall report to you”?

After the persecution of Severus ended, a long peace followed until Decius, interrupted only by the persecution of Maximinus, which was brief and local. During this interval, visions and revelations were not absent from the Church, as is evident from certain examples which Eusebius inserted into his history because they were especially notable and concerned the public condition of the Church and likewise public and particularly celebrated persons. Such were those by which Alexander was chosen bishop of Jerusalem [Note: [Col. 0115D] See Eusebius, book VI, chapter 11], and that by which Saint Fabian was declared pontiff of the Roman Church [Note: [Col. 0115D] See Eusebius, ibid., chapter 29]. But I pass them over because they have scarcely anything in common with our argument, as I also pass over those of Dionysius of Alexandria which the same Eusebius relates [Note: [Col. 0115D] See his letter to Germanus in Eusebius, ibid., chapter 40, and his letter to Philemon in the same author, book VII, chapter 7], since they appear to offer little support to the present case.

## VII

I shall therefore come to Cyprian, for no one conducted himself more as though he possessed the Holy Spirit, like a certain necklace enclosed in his own casket, ready at his command to employ its illuminations whenever he wished and at his own discretion. Two things must be considered in his revelations and visions: their variety and frequency, and the confidence in divine favor and familiarity which that most holy and equally prudent man conceived from them. So that the reader may observe these things more carefully and conveniently, I have thought it proper to set forth here the holy martyr’s visions in the very words in which he himself relates them in his letters.

The eleventh letter—or the eighth according to the numbering of Pamelius’s edition—contains scarcely anything except visions and revelations. It was written from his retreat to the presbyters and deacons and exhorts them to pour forth prayers continually. Hence Augustine, mentioning it in book IV of *On Baptism*, gave it this title: “To the Clergy, on Praying to God.” The Decian persecution was raging with extreme savagery at that time. Cyprian says, moreover, that he was moved especially by divine revelations to write:

> “For you ought to know,” he says, “that what especially persuaded and compelled me to write this letter to you was that—as the Lord deigns to show and reveal—it was said in a vision: ‘Ask, and you shall obtain.’ Then the people who were present were commanded to pray for certain persons specifically designated to them. In their prayer, however, there were discordant voices and conflicting wills; and this greatly displeased him who had said, ‘Ask, and you shall obtain,’ because the disunity of the people was discordant and there was not among the brethren one agreement and a simple and united concord.”

He adds that this lack of unanimity, peace, and concord [Col. 0115D] among the brethren had been the cause of the raging persecution, which he testifies had been shown to him in the following vision long before it was decreed:

> “Indeed, these evils would not have come upon the brethren if the brotherhood had been united in one purpose. For this also was shown: the master of a household was seated, with a young man sitting at his right hand. The young man, anxious and somewhat indignant and sorrowful, sat supporting his cheek with his hand and wearing a mournful countenance. [Col. 0116A] Another, standing on the left, carried a net which he threatened to cast so as to catch the people standing around. When the person who saw this wondered what it meant, he was told that the young man sitting thus on the right was grieved and sorrowful because his commandments were not being observed, but that the one on the left rejoiced because an opportunity was given him to receive from the master of the household the power to rage. This was shown long before the tempest of the present devastation arose. And we have seen the fulfillment of what was shown.”

Just as in time of peace he foresaw the future persecution, so while the persecution raged he likewise understood in a vision that peace would soon come. Of this he writes in the same letter:

> “Finally, he deigned, in his goodness toward us, to give this message to his least servant—one burdened, indeed, with very many offenses and unworthy of his favor: ‘Tell him,’ he said, ‘to be secure, because peace is coming. There is only a short delay in the meantime, because there still remain some who are to be tested.’”

He testifies that he had been admonished by heavenly revelations not only concerning the continual pouring forth of prayers but also concerning other works of piety and duties of religion:

> “We are also admonished by divine favors concerning sparing food and sober drink, etc.”

Indeed, the most holy bishop was so secure about the future fulfillment of his prophecies that, although no ray of the desired tranquillity yet appeared, he nevertheless ordered this letter to be promulgated and also communicated to the people by the clergy to whom it had been written:

> “I ought not to have concealed each of these things and kept them hidden within my conscience alone. . . . [Col. 0116C] You yourselves certainly should not keep this letter hidden among you, but should cause it to be read to the brethren.”

But the minds of the afflicted people were so far removed from every hope of peace that most gave no credence to the letters and revelations of their most venerable pastor. He reproves their unbelief at the beginning of his book *On the Lapsed*, composed after tranquillity had been restored to the Church through the unexpected and sudden death of the Decii:

> “Behold, dearest brethren, peace has been restored to the Church, and our security—which recently appeared difficult to unbelievers and impossible to the faithless—has been reestablished by divine aid and vengeance.”

Letter sixteen in the most recent edition—letter ten according to the numbering of Pamelius—abounds no less in visions. Indeed, it embraces more numerous and varied visions, though in fewer words: [Col. 0116D]

> “Thus the divine judgment does not cease to chastise us either by night or by day. For in addition to nocturnal visions, by day also the innocent age of children among us is filled with the Holy Spirit and in ecstasy sees with its eyes, hears, and speaks those things by which the Lord deigns to admonish and instruct us. And you shall hear everything when the Lord, who commanded me to withdraw, has caused me to return to you. In the meantime, certain rash, incautious, and swollen men among you, who neither take account of man nor fear God, should know that if they persist further in the same conduct, I shall employ that admonition which the Lord commands me to employ, so that for the present they may be prohibited from making offerings.”

Who, on reading this, would not ascribe to our Cyprian that splendid commendation which Polycrates had long before bestowed upon Melito of Sardis [Note: [Col. 0117D] Letter to Pope Victor, in Eusebius, book V, chapter 24]: Τὸν ἐν ἁγίῳ Πνεύματι πάντα πολιτευσάμενον, “one who conducted all things under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit”? Hence Jerome reports from Tertullian, “in the seven books which he wrote against the Church on behalf of Montanus,” that Melito was “regarded by many of our people as a prophet” [Note: [Col. 0117D] *Catalogue*, under Melito].

Yet all these things apply no less to our Cyprian. For what did he do during the persecution that he did not accomplish under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit? So that he might prepare his people for the coming war, he perceived it by divine revelation long before it broke out. When war had been declared, he had God as the author of his decision to seek concealment and avoid the persecution; and, as he himself testifies, and as the deacon Pontius testifies in his *Life*, he did not withdraw without divine revelation.

When the tempest arose, he learned by divine revelation both by what exercises of virtue it was to be overcome and by what duties of piety tranquillity was to be obtained. By divine revelation he likewise foreknew the coming peace no less than the approaching war. He understood what discipline he ought to employ toward the lapsed, both through his own nocturnal visions and through the daytime raptures and ecstasies of innocent children. He likewise received from God, through an open and manifest admonition, the correction with which he ought to punish certain incautious and rash presbyters. Relying upon these continual gifts and favors of the Holy Spirit, he also awaited a heavenly revelation concerning his own return:

> “And you shall hear everything when the Lord, who commanded me to withdraw, has caused me to return to you.”

That this was not withheld from him [Col. 0117C] we shall state afterward.

Meanwhile, before he returned from his retreat to Carthage, he enrolled Aurelius and Celerinus—men distinguished by the glory of their confession—among the clergy. This was not done without the customary visions and divine revelations. For although the practice of those times required clerical ordinations to be celebrated only with the testimony of the people and the vote of the entire clergy, he explains why, while absent, he advanced them to the clergy. Speaking of Aurelius, he gives this reason: one who had been approved by divine testimony did not require human testimony [Note: [Col. 0117D] Letter XXXVIII according to Pamelius, no. 33]:

> “In clerical ordinations, dearest brethren, we are accustomed to consult you beforehand and to weigh the character and merits of each individual by common counsel. But human testimonies are not to be awaited when divine votes precede them. Our brother Aurelius, a distinguished young man, has already been approved by the Lord, etc.”

And in the following letter, speaking of the ordination of Celerinus, he testifies that it too was performed through the spirit of prophecy and not without visions and revelations:

> “For when,” he says, “he hesitated to consent, he was compelled in a vision by night by the admonition and exhortation of the Church herself not to refuse our persuasion; for she who compelled him possessed the greater authority.”

Cyprian himself frequently relates in his letters what disturbances Felicissimus, the author of the schism at Carthage, and the five presbyters who fostered his ruinous faction stirred up against that most holy man. Learned men infer from the following words of the holy martyr that he had received something from God concerning this matter and the persecution that threatened from it, and that a heavenly revelation had not been lacking:

> “This is another persecution and another temptation; and those five presbyters are nothing other than the five leading men who recently had been associated with the magistrates by edict, so that they might overthrow our faith and divert the frail hearts of the brethren, through betrayal of the truth, into deadly snares. The same design and once again the same overthrow are now being introduced, through the five presbyters allied with Felicissimus, for the ruin of salvation.”

Pamelius says that Cyprian appears to allude to some vision in which five leading men seemed to appear with the magistrates and jointly publish an edict against the bishops. Rigaltius does not oppose this interpretation, and Dodwell manifestly embraces it [Note: [Col. 0118D] Fourth *Cyprianic Dissertation on Visions*, etc., §18].

In the same letter—now numbered XLVIII, formerly XL—the last of those which he composed in retirement, he clearly and plainly indicates, as it seems to me, that he had already received the revelation concerning his return which, as we said above, he had been awaiting. For first he complains:

> “That the malice and perfidy of certain presbyters had made it impossible for him to come to his people before Easter.”

He was, of course, taking care lest a greater tumult arise in Carthage from his arrival because of the threats and plots of the faithless, and lest, although a bishop ought in all things to provide for peace and tranquillity, he himself should appear to have furnished material for sedition and inflamed the persecution anew.

Afterward he adds [Col. 0118C] that this is the final persecution and the last temptation, which, he says,

> “will itself also quickly pass, with the Lord’s protection, so that I may be restored to you with my colleagues after Easter Day.”

Do we not have manifest and clear indications of a prophetic revelation? Before Easter he remained in his retreat, fearing lest by his arrival he furnish material for sedition and appear to have inflamed the persecution anew. Yet he confidently promises that he will return after Easter. But could not the same danger threaten after Easter? By no means, for he knew that this last temptation of the persecution would quickly pass. But from what source did he know this, so as to make so certain a promise? Surely from none other than divine revelation.

After the Decii had been removed, Gallus and Volusianus brought upon the Church the second persecution of Cyprian’s time. Saint Cyprian understood that this persecution too would come before it raged—indeed, even before it was decreed—not through one or two, but through many frequent and manifest disclosures and visions. Through the synodical letter of the second Council of Carthage to Cornelius, he made it known not only to his own Church but also to the Roman Church [Note: [Col. 0118D] Letter LVII, formerly LIV]:

> “But since we see that the day of another assault has again begun to approach, and since we are admonished by frequent and continual disclosures to be armed and prepared for the contest which the enemy declares against us, let us also prepare by our exhortations the people entrusted to us by divine favor, etc.”

Afterward:

> “For we must obey just disclosures and admonitions, so that the sheep are not abandoned by their pastors in time of danger, etc.”

Again:

> “It has seemed good to us, at the suggestion of the Holy Spirit and with the Lord admonishing us through many manifest visions—since it is foretold and shown that the enemy threatens us—to gather the soldiers of Christ within the camp, etc.”

The distinguished Dodwell adds the following to these words of Cyprian [Note: [Col. 0119D] *Dissertation on Visions*, etc., §19]:

> “Atheists will perhaps not believe these things. But what reason do they have for refusing to regard them as worthy of belief? Here we have visions that are not merely numerous but also manifest and confirmed by the event. Nor were they recorded upon the obscure testimony of some private person, but by the consenting vote of an entire synod, in writings destined—had they proved false—to remain as everlasting memorials of the matter, writings ‘clearer than the sun,’ as Jerome says. I do not see what more they could desire for the confirmation of a matter of fact, provided they wished to judge impartially and not deprive men of all credibility whatsoever.”

Again, concerning the same persecution he writes thus to the people of Thibaris [Note: [Col. 0119D] Letter LVIII, formerly LVI]:

> “For since, by the favor of the Lord who instructs us, we are repeatedly and still more urgently impelled and admonished, we ought also to bring the solicitude of our admonition to your attention. For you ought to know, believe with certainty, and hold fast that the day of tribulation has begun to hang over our heads. . . . We do not think that the things now coming are such as those which have passed. A struggle more severe and more savage now threatens.”

It is not without reason that one may wonder how the persecution of Gallus was to be more severe and savage than that of Decius, since in fact it was briefer and far milder. I resolved this difficulty as follows in my *Dissertation on Penance, or on Capital Absolution* [Note: [Col. 0119D] Section 3].

Through these visions Cyprian had been shown not only the coming persecution but likewise all the evils that threatened the Church no less than the commonwealth: namely, the incursions of the barbarians and the resulting captivities of Christians and pagans alike, and the most ruinous plague which raged widely throughout the Roman Empire. The severity and innumerable devastations of this calamity are lamented both by Saint Cyprian himself, by the deacon Pontius in his *Life*, and by other authors. These and other calamities, therefore, combined with the persecution, made the reign of Gallus and Volusianus far more grievous for Christians than that of Decius. Since not all the causes of these evils had been clearly and distinctly revealed to the holy martyr, he readily persuaded himself that all the calamities and deaths of Christians which he had foreseen would arise from the persecution alone.

Thus far we have seen how frequent and familiar heavenly revelations were to Cyprian. Let us now see to what confidence the holy martyr was led by their frequency, and whether he too, no differently from Perpetua, conducted himself as though he possessed the Holy Spirit, like a certain necklace enclosed in his own casket, ready at his command to employ its illuminations whenever he wished and at his own discretion.

A sign of such confidence is the assurance he displayed that a heavenly revelation would not fail him which would indicate the proper time for his return—a point we have already noted. I shall now offer another no less evident argument for the same conclusion.

In that most weighty letter in which he reproves Florentius Pupianus, a seditious and turbulent man, with the greatest severity, he at last promises him pardon, if he comes to his senses, upon this condition: that God should permit it and signify his judgment and will in the matter by some perceptible sign [Note: [Col. 0120D] Letter LVI, formerly LXIX]:

> “If, even at this late hour, you begin to repent of your rashness, pride, and insolence . . . we shall be able to take account of your communion, while nevertheless retaining among us respect and fear for the divine judgment, so that I may first consult my Lord whether, by his disclosure and admonition, he permits peace to be given to you and you to be admitted to the communion of his Church. For I remember what has already been shown to me—indeed, what was commanded by dominical and divine authority to an obedient and God-fearing servant. Among the other things which he deigned to show and reveal, he also added this: ‘Thus he who does not believe Christ when he appoints a priest will afterward begin to believe Christ when he vindicates the priest.’”

Dodwell adds the following to these words of the holy martyr [Note: [Col. 0120D] Fourth *Cyprianic Dissertation*, §20]:

> “Divine revelations were so frequent in that age that he did not dare to promise even what he would determine concerning a repentant Pupianus, lest perhaps something else should be commanded by God. Thus he held that God must first be consulted by means of a sign; and at last he awaited God’s punishment of the rebel, precisely such a punishment as learned men acknowledge to have accompanied apostolic censures.”

Again, concerning the presbyters who had communicated with the lapsed without consulting him, he says [Note: [Col. 0120D] Letter XVI, formerly X]:

> “I shall employ that admonition which the Lord commands me to employ, so that for the present they may be prohibited from making offerings, etc.”

Cyprian was not unaware that visions of this kind were held in ridicule and contempt by impious and rash men, such as we now experience in no small number. Nevertheless, he held it so certain that his revelations came from God that he had no doubt about future events. He was not afraid not only to publish the revelations already granted to him but even openly to promise himself new ones. Hence, in the same letter, he immediately adds:

> “Although I know that dreams appear ridiculous and visions foolish to certain persons—namely, to those who prefer to believe against a priest rather than to believe the priest. But this is no wonder, since Joseph’s brothers said of him: ‘Behold, that dreamer comes. Come now, therefore, let us kill him.’ Yet afterward the dreamer obtained what he had dreamed, while his killers and sellers were confounded, so that those who had not previously believed words afterward believed deeds.”

There remains another prophetic vision of Cyprian concerning his martyrdom, but I shall reserve it for the following chapter. Meanwhile, I ask: does he not appear to have possessed the Holy Spirit in his breast as though enclosed in a casket, since even in exercising ecclesiastical discipline he awaited visions and dared determine nothing [Col. 0121A] unless he had first consulted the Lord and had been made certain of his will by some manifest indication? And was Perpetua therefore to be accused of Montanism because she too, being accustomed to divine revelations, conducted herself with the same confidence?
