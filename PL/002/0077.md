Behold, once again we are compelled to set forth our view even concerning the examination of the dreams by which the soul is tossed about. And when shall we arrive at death? Here too I would say: when God grants it; there can be no long delay in what is bound to happen.

Epicurus judged dreams altogether vain, freeing the divinity from all business, dissolving the order of things, and scattering everything into passivity, so that all things are exposed to chance [Col. 0726C] and are fortuitous. But if that is so, then some chance event will also prove true, since truth alone cannot be exempted from the chance to which all things are subject.

Homer assigned two gates to dreams: the horn gate of truth and the ivory gate of deception. For, they say, one can see through horn, whereas [Col. 0727A] ivory is opaque.

[Note: [Col. 0727B] **Aristotle, assigning the greater part to falsehood.** Aristotle considers dreams for the most part false, though not always. LE PR.]

Aristotle, assigning the greater part to falsehood, nevertheless acknowledges some truth.

[Note: [Col. 0727B] *Balaridem* Pam. Rhen. Seml.]

[Note: [Col. 0727B] **The Telmessians, etc.** Those people of Caria reject no dreams as empty, but interpret them. LE PR.]

The Telmessians dismiss no dreams as empty, but blame the weakness of the interpretation. Yet who is so alien to human experience that he has not at some time perceived some trustworthy vision? By touching briefly upon a few of the more notable ones, I shall put Epicurus to shame.

Herodotus relates that Astyages, ruler of the Medes, dreamed that the bladder of his still-virgin daughter Mandane produced a flood that inundated Asia; and likewise, a year after her marriage, that a vine sprang from those same parts and spread over all Asia. Charon of Lampsacus, who preceded Herodotus, also reports this. Those who interpreted her son as the meaning of so great an event were not deceived, since Cyrus both submerged and overmastered Asia.

[Note: [Col. 0727B] **Philip the Macedonian, not yet a father.** Plutarch: [Col. 0727C] ὑστέρῳ χρόνῳ μετὰ τὸν γάμον, “at a later time after the marriage.” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0727C] **He had seen that he sealed his wife’s generative part.** Plutarch: “He saw in a dream that he himself was placing a seal upon his wife’s womb”: Εἶδεν ὄναρ αὐτὸν ἐπιβάλλοντα σφραγῖδα τῇ γαστρὶ τῆς γυναικός, “He saw in a dream that he himself was placing a seal upon his wife’s womb.” RIG. and EDD.]

Philip the Macedonian, not yet a father, dreamed that he had sealed with a ring the generative part of his wife Olympias [Col. 0727B]; the device upon it was a lion.

[Note: [Col. 0727C] **He had believed that offspring was precluded.** That is, that his wife’s womb would be barren. RIG.]

He had believed that offspring was precluded,

[Note: [Col. 0727C] **I suppose because the lion becomes a father only once.** A false popular opinion became widespread that the lion gives birth only once, and to only one cub. Indeed, Homer describes a lion περὶ οἷσι τεκέεσσιν, “among its offspring,” and ἄγων ἐν ὕπῃ νήπια, “leading its young in the thicket.” Nor could I readily say that our Septimius here shared the popular belief, or that a Carthaginian was ignorant of facts very well known to Gaetulian and Numidian hunters. But when Septimius says that a lion is a father only once, we must understand it as meaning with a single female—unless someone prefers to think that so severe a censor of marriage approved even of monogamous lions. But with Alexander not yet a father, how could a lion’s becoming a father only once signify that offspring was precluded, since even a lion cannot become a father once unless offspring has been brought forth and the womb has already been opened? Let us therefore say that Alexander’s inference was that his wife would not give birth, because the animal whose form he had seen in his dream was believed to produce offspring only once. Lions, however, are rare, and therefore the lion gives birth rarely. What happens rarely is popularly said to happen only once in a lifetime. Asconius observes that “once” is close to “almost never”; and in that sense Lucilius wrote that Crassus—the grandfather of the Crassus killed among the Parthians—laughed once in his life. Pliny renders this without qualification as “never laughed.” RIG.—**I suppose because the lion becomes a father only once.** Certain writers attest that this belief had spread among the common people: that a lioness tears open her own womb with her claws in giving birth. Aristotle, however, taught that this is false. Aelian, Book IV, chapter 23, says that the lioness gives birth five times: first to five cubs, then four, then three, afterward two, and finally one. LE PR.]

I suppose because the lion becomes a father only once.

Aristodemus or Aristophon, interpreting the dream—

[Note: [Col. 0728C] **Rather, that nothing empty is sealed.** Plutarch: οὐδὲν γὰρ ἀποσφραγίζεσθαι τῶν κενῶν, “for nothing empty is sealed.” RIG.]

or rather, that nothing empty is sealed—

declared that a son was portended, and one of the greatest force.

[Note: [Col. 0728C] **Those who know Alexander.** That is, those acquainted with the history of Alexander. It circulated everywhere, variously written by various persons and interwoven with generally very foolish fables. Of this sort is the manuscript volume identified long ago by Casaubon which falsely bears the name of Callisthenes. RIG.]

Those who know Alexander recognize the lion on the ring: Ephorus records the story. [Col. 0728A]

A certain woman of Himera also dreamed of Dionysius’ tyranny over Sicily: Heraclides reported it. And Laodice, before she had yet given birth to Seleucus, foresaw his rule over Asia: Euphorion made the account public. I also learn from Strabo that Mithridates gained possession of Pontus as the result of a dream.

[Note: [Col. 0728C] **And that the Illyrian Baraliris ruled from the Molossians as far as Macedonia because of a dream.** This is precisely the reading of the Agobardine manuscript, through the increasingly easy slippage of scribes from *Bardiem* or *Bardirem* at first, next to *Biradirem* and *Baralirem*. From this flowed the reading *Balaridem* found in the printed editions. Five copies of Frontinus’ *Stratagems* consistently present *ab Ardie Illyrio*, which means “by Bardies,” and comes very close to the truth, Bardylis. The Greeks appear to have preserved the correct form of this name, Βάρδυλιν, “Bardylis.” Hence Cicero has *Bargulus* for *Bardylis*, according to the very certain conjecture of that exceptionally upright and learned man Henri de Valois. Bardylis the Illyrian, who rose from brigandage to become commander or prince of the Illyrians and Molossians, was checked by Philip when he also attacked the Macedonians, and was confined within the borders of the Illyrians. The source is Diodorus, cited by our Valois. RIG.]

And from Callisthenes I learn that Baraliris the Illyrian ruled from the Molossians as far as Macedonia as the result of a dream.

The Romans too knew dreams of this truthful kind. Marcus Tullius knew from a dream the future restorer of the empire, while he was still a boy and a private person, known only as Julius Octavius and personally unknown to Cicero himself; he already recognized in him both Augustus and the one who would lay civil convulsions to rest.

[Note: [Col. 0728D] **It is preserved in Vitellius’ memoirs.** Suetonius cites certain details concerning the Vitellian family from a little book by Q. Eulogius, addressed to Q. Vitellius, Augustus’ quaestor. RIG.]

It is preserved in Vitellius’ memoirs.

Nor will this class of dreams predict only the highest powers, but also dangers and deaths. Thus, when Caesar was ill during the battle at Philippi against the rebel forces of Brutus and Cassius, he nevertheless escaped another, greater danger by abandoning his tent because of Artorius’ vision.

[Note: [Col. 0728B] *Verum* Pam. Rhen. Seml. Oberth.]

Thus too the daughter of Polycrates of Samos foresaw his crucifixion through his being anointed by the sun

[Note: [Col. 0728D] **Through his being anointed by the sun.** Herodotus explains this in Book III, writing that the daughter of Polycrates saw in a dream her father raised aloft in the air and anointed by the sun, by which she foresaw that he would be put upon a cross and dried out by the sun’s heat. PAM.]

and washed by Jupiter.

[Note: [Col. 0728D] **And washed by Jupiter.** This is what Herodotus writes in the same place that she dreamed: that her father was washed by Jupiter—that is, drenched by rains and showers, which the pagans attributed to Jupiter. PAM.]

Honors and talents too are revealed in sleep; remedies are provided, thefts disclosed [Col. 0729A], and treasures bestowed. Indeed, Cicero’s nurse, while he was still a very small child, had already beheld his future distinction.

[Note: [Col. 0729B] *Suam* Par. and others.]

[Note: [Col. 0729B] **The swan from Socrates’ bosom, etc.** Laertius relates this in his *Life of Plato*: Socrates is said to have dreamed that he held a young swan between his knees, which immediately spread its wings and flew away, uttering a delightful cry; and that on the following day Plato was commended to him, whereupon Socrates said that this was the very swan. PAM.]

The swan from Socrates’ bosom, charming humanity, is his disciple Plato. Cleonymus the boxer

[Note: [Col. 0729B] **Cleonymus the boxer.** See Homer. *Pyctes*, “boxer,” is a familiar word. PAM.]

is cured by Achilles in a dream. When a golden crown had disappeared from Athena’s citadel, Sophocles the tragedian recovered it through a dream. Neoptolemus the tragic actor, at Rhoeteum near Troy, was warned in dreams by Ajax himself and freed Ajax’s tomb from collapse; and when he cleared away the decrepit mass of stones, he returned rich in gold.

[Note: [Col. 0729B] *Erithraeum* Par. and others; *Rhaetum* Jun.]

[Note: [Col. 0729B] **At Rhoeteum near Troy, Ajax’s tomb.** With Leopardus, *Emendations*, Book III, chapters 15 and 16, I restored this passage from the former reading “at Erythraeum.” For Pliny confirms it in these words, Book V, chapter 30: “Outside the gulf,” he says, “are the Rhoetean shores, inhabited by the towns Rhoeteum and Dardanium.” And shortly afterward: “In Sigeum there was also Aeantion, founded by the Rhodians; on the other promontory is the tomb of Ajax.” PAM.]

How many commentators and guarantors there are for this subject! Artemon, Antiphon, Strato, Philochorus, Epicharmus, Serapion, Cratippus, Dionysius of Rhodes, Hermippus—the entire literature of the age.

[Note: [Col. 0729C] **Artemon, Antiphon, etc.** Pliny, Book XXVIII, chapter 1, appears to have copied certain things from Artemon the physician. Artemon of Clazomenae is also cited by the Suda and Aelian.—**Antiphon.** The Suda says of him: Antiphon the Athenian interpreter—or diviner, as the Suda elsewhere calls such a person—wrote on the interpretation of dreams.—**Strato.** Laertius attests that this man, namely Strato of Lampsacus, wrote *On Sleep* and *On Dreams*.—**Philochorus.** This man too, an Athenian seer and diviner, wrote in part four books *On Divination*; and divination was also practiced through dreams. The Author speaks of him again shortly afterward: “with Philochorus the Athenian.”—**Epicharmus.** You have certain poems of Epicharmus. Laertius, however, also relates that he wrote commentaries *On Nature* and *On Medicine*. It is therefore likely that in these he also wrote something about dreams. They may be consulted at Rome in the Vatican Library. Certainly the Author says shortly afterward: “Moreover, Epicharmus raised dreams even to the highest pinnacle among forms of divination.”—**Serapion.** Pliny noted that he took certain things from one Serapion for Book II, and again for Book IV, where he calls him an Antiochene. There were, however, other men named Serapion.—**Cratippus.** Cratippus, a Peripatetic philosopher, wrote books on divination and dreams.—**Dionysius of Rhodes.** The Suda notes that Dionysius, son of Musonius, was called a Rhodian by some; he was a historian and priest of the temple of the Sun.—**Hermippus.** The Author speaks of him shortly afterward. The Suda relates that he was a disciple of Philo of Byblos, lived under the emperor Hadrian, and wrote many works. PAM.]

I shall laugh only, perhaps, at the man [Col. 0730A] who thought he could persuade us that

[Note: [Col. 0729D] **Saturn dreamed before everyone else.** Because melancholic persons—whom for this reason they call πολυονείρους, “much-dreaming,” on account of the intensity of their mental power—are occupied by many kinds of visions. According to Aristotle, *On Divination through Dreams*, chapter 2. LAC.]

Saturn dreamed before everyone else—

[Note: [Col. 0729B] *Somniavit* (“dreamed”) Jun.]

unless Aristotle too lived before everyone else. Forgive my laughter.

Moreover, Epicharmus, together with Philochorus the Athenian, even raised dreams to the highest pinnacle among forms of divination. For the world has also been furnished with oracles of this kind: that of Amphiaraus at Oropus,

[Note: [Col. 0729D] **Of Amphiaraus at Oropus.** With Leopardus we read *Oropum* in place of *Horopum*, because Oropus, a town on the border of Boeotia, is mentioned by Pliny, Book IV, chapter 7. He also mentions Amphiaraus, who died near Thebes one generation before the Trojan War, and whose son Tiburtus was said to have been inaugurated beside three holm oaks in the territory of Tibur, which were older than Tiburtus himself. PAM.]

that of Amphilochus at Mallus,

[Note: [Col. 0730B] **Of Amphilochus at Mallus.** With the same Leopardus we read *Amphilochi* in place of *Ampliloci*, for Clement of Alexandria also celebrates the oracle of Amphilochus, as does Justin Martyr in his *Second Apology*. The Author implicitly means Mallus, a nearby town of Cilicia mentioned by Pliny in Book V, chapter 27. PAM.]

that of Sarpedon in the Troad,

[Note: [Col. 0730B] **Of Sarpedon in the Troad.** Pliny writes as follows about this, Book XIII, [Col. 0730C] chapter 13: “Furthermore, Mucianus, three times consul, reported that while governor of Lycia he had read, in a temple, a document written at the time of Sarpedon’s departure from Troy.” On his oracle, however, see Philostratus, *Heroicus*. PAM.]

that of Trophonius in Boeotia,

[Note: [Col. 0730C] **Of Trophonius in Boeotia.** In Book XXXIV Pliny praises a work of Eutychrates, a statue of Trophonius at the oracle, concerning which he speaks more fully in Book XIII, chapter 2: “In Boeotia, at the shrine of the god Trophonius, beside the river Orchomenus, there are two springs, one of which brings memory and the other forgetfulness, and from them the divine powers were discovered.” From this arose the proverb, “To prophesy in Trophonius’ cave,” about which Erasmus writes at length in the *Adages*. Plutarch also referred to this oracle in his book *On the Decline of Oracles*, cited by Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, Book V, chapter 8. Nowhere in those parts is an oracle now found except at Lebadeia in Boeotia. PAM.]

that of Mopsus in Cilicia,

[Note: [Col. 0730C] **Of Mopsus in Cilicia.** I do not know why Rigaltius retained “Sicily,” since the reading appears to be “Cilicia.” For Pomponius Mela mentions a certain seer Mopsus, who founded the city of Phaselis on the borders of Pamphylia. LE PR.]

that of Hermonia in Macedonia,

[Note: [Col. 0730D] **Of Hermonia in Macedonia.** The Suda says of the oracle of Hermione: “Hermione is a town in the Peloponnese, a sanctuary of Ceres and Proserpina.” It is therefore likely that oracles were customarily pronounced there. PAM.]

and that of Pasiphaë in Laconia.

[Note: [Col. 0730D] **Of Pasiphaë in Laconia.** This must certainly be read in place of *Pasiphaeae*, as Leopardus correctly noted in *Emendations*, Book VIII, chapter 4. Cicero, *On Divination*, Book I, and Plutarch, in the *Life of Agis and Cleomenes*, agree. “Those who governed the Lacedaemonians,” Cicero says—hence the Author adds “in Laconia”—“not content with their waking deliberations, lay down to sleep in the temple of Pasiphaë, which is in the countryside near the city, because they regarded dreams as true oracles.” Pausanias also agrees with Plutarch at the end of his *Laconica*, writing: “At Thalamae”—which Stephanus says was a city of Messenia—“there was an oracle and temple of Jupiter, in which those practicing divination foresaw the future; and in that part of the temple which is beneath the open sky there were bronze statues erected, one of Pasiphaë”—for thus he too should correctly be restored in place of “Paphia”—“and another of the Sun.” Nor is this surprising, since Pasiphaë, wife of Minos, king of Crete, was the daughter of the Sun. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0730B] *Pasipheae* Par.]

Hermippus of Berytus will set forth all the rest—with their origins, rites, and reports, together with the whole subsequent history of dreams—in the fullest possible manner in five volumes.

[Note: [Col. 0730B] *Berytensis* Jun. Ven.; the Agobardine manuscript has *Beriensis*; Wouwer’s manuscript, *Bergensis*; Ursinus’ manuscript likewise.]

[Col. 0731A]

[Note: [Col. 0731D] **But the Stoics too prefer to think that God, etc.** As the Epicureans above rejected everything because they removed divine providence from consideration, so the Stoics, conversely, believed in dreams because they exalted divine providence itself. Cicero, *On the Divination of the Gods*, Book I, and Plutarch, *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*, Book V, chapter 1, confirm this Stoic religious regard for dreams. LAC.]

But the Stoics too prefer to think that God, in his supreme providence for the instruction of humanity, implanted dreams in us among the other aids supplied by the divinatory arts and disciplines, as a distinctive comfort in the form of a natural oracle.

This much was necessary for us too to confirm the credibility of dreams and to interpret them in this manner.

[Note: [Col. 0731C] *Aliter* Fran. Pam.]

[Note: [Col. 0731D] **For concerning the other oracles too, etc.** From this it is evident that Tertullian intended above to recount only those oracles that answered sleepers through dreams. Eusebius, Clement, and Lactantius enumerate many other oracles belonging to another kind of prophecy. LAC.]

For as to the other oracles, at which no one falls asleep, what else shall we pronounce than that their system is demonic—the work of those spirits which either already inhabited those very human beings at that time, or

[Note: [Col. 0731C] *Ad* added by Fran. Pam.]

appropriated their memories for every scene in their own malicious drama? In this form too they counterfeit divinity, and with the same contrivance deceive even through the benefits of remedies, warnings, and predictions—benefits which do greater harm by helping, since through the things by which they help they lead people away [Col. 0731B] from seeking the true divinity, by insinuating a false one.

And indeed this power is not confined or circumscribed by the boundaries of shrines. It is wandering, swiftly flying, and meanwhile at liberty. Therefore no one need doubt that houses too lie open to demons, and that they beset people with images not only in sanctuaries but also in bedrooms.

## CHAPTER XLVII

We therefore determine that dreams are very largely inflicted by demons, even though at times they are true and favorable, yet courting and captivating for the purpose of which we have spoken;

[Note: [Col. 0731D] **How much more the vain.** That is, false dreams, although the words might be referred to the vanity of superstitions practiced by those who rather diligently use dreams to arrange for future events. LAC.]

how much more those that are vain, deceptive, confused, mocking, and impure. Nor is it surprising if the images belong to the same beings to whom the realities belong.

Those dreams, however, will be assigned to God—which correspond to his grace—since he promised the grace of the Holy Spirit to all flesh, and promised that his male and female servants would dream just as they would prophesy [Col. 0731C]: dreams that are honorable, holy, prophetic, revelatory, edifying, and invitational. The bounty of such things is accustomed to drip down even upon the profane, since God distributes his rains and suns equally to the just and the unjust. Thus even Nebuchadnezzar dreams by divine agency.

[Note: [Col. 0731D] **And almost the greater part of humanity learns of God from visions.** Through visions and dreams they are called [Col. 0732D] to the Christian faith. RIG.]

And almost the greater part of humanity learns of God from visions.

Therefore, just as God’s favor extends even to the pagans, so the Evil One’s temptation extends even to the saints. He does not desist from them even by day, so that he may steal upon them while they sleep by whatever means he can, if he cannot do so while they are awake.

A third class will consist of the dreams which the soul itself appears to induce in itself through its concentration upon surrounding circumstances. But since dreaming does not lie within our choice—for Epicharmus too holds this view—how will the soul itself be the cause of any vision? Should this class, then, be left to the natural order, preserving the principle that the soul undergoes its own experiences even in ecstasy?

But those things that appear to occur neither from God, nor from a demon, nor from the soul,

[Note: [Col. 0731C] *Cum* Rhen. Seml.; *quam* Pam.; *qua* in the old Wouwer manuscript.]

[Note: [Col. 0731D] *Occidere* Par. and others.]

and which lie beyond conjecture, beyond interpretation, and beyond the capacity for explanation, will properly be set apart for ecstasy itself and its own principle.

## CHAPTER XLVIII

They maintain that more certain and more refined dreams occur toward the end of the night [Col. 0732B], as though the vigor of souls were already rising again as sleep grew prolonged.

As to the seasons of the year, the soul is said to be more tranquil in spring,

[Note: [Col. 0732D] **More tranquil in spring.** Almost all writers relate that in springtime the soul becomes more tranquil and is occupied by more peaceful dreams. LE PR.]

because summer relaxes souls, winter in some fashion hardens them, and autumn—otherwise a season that puts health to the test—dissolves them with the wine-laden juices of fruits.

Likewise they distinguish according to one’s position in sleep: provided that one lies neither on one’s back nor upon the right side,

[Note: [Col. 0732D] **Provided one lies neither on one’s back nor upon the right side.** Philosophers observed that sleep comes more readily to those who lie upon the right side. Hence Aristotle, section 6, problem 5, asks why sleep comes more quickly when we lie upon the right side of the body. Sleeping flat upon the back is also less healthy, since serious illnesses arise from that position. LE PR.]

lest, when the internal organs are pressed backward, as though their chambers had been overturned,

[Note: [Col. 0731D] *Repulsis localis* in the Agobardine manuscript.]

the seat of the senses should float,

[Note: [Col. 0732D] **The seat of the senses.** He means the region around the heart, περικάρδιον, and the blood surrounding the heart, discussed above. RIG.]

or the mind be constricted by pressure upon the liver.

[Note: [Col. 0732D] **Or be constricted by pressure upon the liver.** So the manuscript reads. He uses *compressam*, “compressed,” for *compressionem*, “compression,” just as *remissam*, “relaxed,” may stand for *remissionem*, “relaxation.” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0732C] Others incorrectly read *sagina*, “fattening.”]

But I think these things have been ingeniously conjectured rather than firmly proved, even though Plato was the one who conjectured them; and perhaps they occur by chance. Otherwise dreams will be under our control, if they can be directed.

For the rule that presumption in one case and superstition in another now prescribes for dreams regarding the selection or rejection of foods

[Note: [Col. 0732C] *Decurandis* Rig. Ven., that is, “regarding them as of no concern”; others read *decorandis*.]

must be examined. Superstition does so when fasting is enjoined upon those who are about to sleep at oracles, so as to induce ritual purity.

[Note: [Col. 0732D] **Fasting is enjoined upon those who are about to sleep at oracles.** The pagans sometimes also sought to please their demons by fasts, and these were appointed or imposed upon those consulting oracles. LE PR.]

Presumption does so when the Pythagoreans reject beans on this ground as well, as a heavy food that causes flatulence.

And yet those three brothers together with Daniel, content with legumes alone so that they would not be defiled by the royal dishes, obtained from God—besides all their other wisdom—the grace especially associated with dreams: both the ability to receive them and the ability to expound them.

When fasting, however [Col. 0733A]—unless I alone experience this—I dream so much that I do not perceive that I have dreamed.

[Note: [Col. 0732D] *Jejunus* (“when fasting”) Jun.]

“Then sobriety contributes nothing to this matter,” you say? On the contrary, it contributes the more to this matter in proportion as it contributes to everything. If it contributes even to superstition, much more does it contribute to religion.

For demons too demand sobriety from their dreamers, so as to promote their counterfeit divinity, because they know that it is familiar to God.

[Note: [Col. 0733C] *Norant* (“they knew”) Par.]

[Note: [Col. 0733C] **Because Daniel too again kept a station for three weeks.** I am surprised that Pamelius—otherwise a diligent and very learned man—expresses surprise at what the Author here affirms concerning Daniel’s fast for three weeks, since he asserts that no mention of it occurs anywhere in Daniel, although it is found very clearly in Daniel, chapter 10: “In those days I, Daniel, was mourning for the days of three weeks. I ate no desirable bread, and flesh and wine did not enter my mouth; neither was I anointed with oil, until the days of three weeks were completed.” LAC.]

For Daniel too (Dan. 10), during a renewed observance of three weeks, dried himself out by his diet;

[Note: [Col. 0733D] **He dried himself out by his diet.** This signifies *xerophagia* (ξηροφαγία, “dry eating”), which in the book *On Fasting* he calls “a dry diet” and “dry foods,” with the food deprived of every kind of meat, every broth, and all juicier fruits. Daniel himself says: “I ate no desirable bread.” RIG.]

but he did this to draw God by acts of humility, not to equip the soul that was about to dream with perception and wisdom, as though it were not going to act in ecstasy. Thus sobriety will not serve to remove ecstasy, but to commend the ecstasy itself, so that it may take place in God.

## CHAPTER XLIX

[Col. 0733B] Those who suppose that infants do not dream, although all the soul’s functions are discharged in life according to the measure of their age, should observe their jolts,

[Note: [Col. 0733C] *Successus* Rig. Ven.]

their nods, and their smiles during sleep, so that they may understand from the fact itself that the movements of the dreaming soul readily break through to the surface because of the tenderness of the flesh.

But as for the report that the Atlantes, a Libyan people, pass the night in dreamless sleep,

[Note: [Col. 0733D] **The Atlantes, a Libyan people, in dreamless sleep, etc.** Pliny, Book V, chapter 8: “The Atlantes, a Libyan people, have degenerated from human customs. They see no dreams such as the rest of mortals see.” Herodotus: λέγονται δὲ οὐδὲ ἐνύπνια ὁρᾶν, “they are said not even to see dreams,” etc. RIG.]

the nature of the soul is certainly being censured by it. Either the report deceived Herodotus, being sometimes slanderous toward barbarians, or a great force of demons of this kind dominates that region.

For if Aristotle also notes a certain hero of Sardinia who deprived those sleeping in his shrine of visions, this too will lie within the caprice of demons: to remove dreams as well as to introduce them. Thus arose the notable cases of Nero, who began dreaming only late, and of Thrasymedes. [Col. 0733C]

But we also derive dreams from God. Why, then, would the Atlantes receive no dreams from God either, since no nation is now foreign to God,
