# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, ON BAPTISM, AGAINST QUINTILLA (C)

## Argument

QUINTILLA HAD SPREAD THE HERESY OF THE CAIANITES AT CARTHAGE AND TAUGHT THAT WATER WAS NOT NECESSARY IN BAPTISM. IN THIS TREATISE TERTULLIAN, WHO WAS STILL A CATHOLIC, ATTACKS THIS ERROR. THE TREATISE IS DIVIDED INTO TWO PARTS: THE FIRST PART CONCERNS THE DOCTRINE OF THE NECESSITY AND EFFICACY OF BAPTISM; IN THE SECOND PART, SEVERAL DISCIPLINARY QUESTIONS CONCERNING BAPTISM ARE DISCUSSED.

The excellence of the water of Baptism, chs. I, II, III. The matter and power of Baptism, IV. The effects of Baptism, V, VI. The customary anointing after Baptism, VII. The first reason Christians were called “little fishes” after the pattern of our ἸΧΘΥΣ, Jesus Christ, derives from the life-giving water of Baptism. “For since,” says the most learned Mamachi in *Origins and Antiquities of the Christians*, book I, ch. 1, §6, p. 54, “our ancestors understood that we are regenerated in the water of Baptism unto a new and spiritual life, they drew a likeness from fish, which are born in [Col. 1199B] water and cannot live without water, and gave the name ‘little fishes’ to all who had been baptized and embraced the Christian religion.”

> Fisher of mortals,

> pure fish [Col. 1199C]  
> from the hostile wave—  
> baiting them with sweet life.

## CHAPTER IV

But it will be enough to have outlined in advance those matters in which we also recognize the first rationale of Baptism—that original one, by which, even then, through its very disposition, it foreshadowed a figure of Baptism: that the Spirit of God, who from the beginning was borne over the waters, would re-form those immersed in them. [Note: [Col. 1203B] Franeker reads: “which even then—over the waters—would re-form those immersed.” Rigault reads: “was borne above, destined to remain over the waters of those being immersed.”]

The Holy Spirit was assuredly borne over something holy; or else that which bore him borrowed holiness from him who was borne above it. For every underlying material must necessarily take on the quality of that which hangs above it—especially corporeal matter taking on the quality of spiritual substance, which by the subtlety of its nature can readily penetrate and settle within it. Thus the nature of the waters, sanctified by the Holy One, itself conceived the power to sanctify.

Let no one say: “Surely we are not immersed in the very same waters that existed then [Note: [Col. 1204B] “Now,” Franeker; “themselves,” Rigault.] in the beginning?” Not in precisely the same waters, certainly, except insofar as the genus is one, [Note: [Col. 1203C] **The genus is one.** He preserves the terminology of law, not the elementary teaching of Porphyry; for interpreters of law call a species a “genus,” and an individual a “species,” so that “human being” and “horse” are genera, while Titius and Bucephalus are species. Thus Julian, law LIV, *Digest*, *On Verbal Obligations*, and Ulpian, law XXX, *Digest*, *On Legacies*. See Boerius on the military, forensic, and legal discipline of topics. Gothofredus, however, on law LIV, attempts to draw Aristotle into agreement with Julian and the jurists. He thus notes that Aristotle called species τελευταῖα γένη ἐπὶ τοῖς ἀτόμοις, “the last genera above individuals,” and individuals ἔσχατα εἴδη, “ultimate species.” Yet this will seem to dialecticians a less than logical assertion. By ἔσχατα εἴδη, “ultimate species,” which they themselves render “lowest species,” they understand not individuals but those universal, essential things which are predicated only of several things differing in number, such as “human being”; thus they contain beneath themselves no other species, but only individuals. They call the lowest genus that which truly is a genus but is predicated of several things differing only in species, so that it can by no means contain another genus beneath itself but is instead subordinated to the other genera. LAC.] while the species are many. Now what has been attributed to the genus also flows over into the species.

Accordingly, it makes no difference whether someone is washed in the sea or a pool, a river or a spring, a lake or a basin; nor is there any distinction between those whom John immersed in the Jordan and those whom Peter immersed in the Tiber. [Note: [Col. 1203D] **It makes no difference whether someone is washed in the sea or a pool; nor is there any distinction between those whom John immersed in the Jordan and those whom Peter immersed in the Tiber.** No one has said greater things about Christ’s Baptism than Paulinus said concerning John’s baptism in his *Poem on John the Baptist*:

> Proceed, then, to the holy waters of the pure Jordan, etc.

And yet these words of Tertullian mean only that in the matter of Baptism there is no difference among waters, nor is one more excellent than another. Therefore, it makes no difference whether someone is washed in the sea or a pool, a river or a spring, a lake or a basin. For the Tiber, in which Peter immersed people, conferred no more salvation than the Jordan, in which John did; nor was the chance water encountered on the road, in which Philip baptized the eunuch, for that reason either more or less salutary. This is certainly most true. For waters, whatever their kind, do not sanctify; rather, all waters, when God in three Persons has been invoked, receive the sacrament of sanctification, and through them God sanctifies the human being. Septimius says only this. RIG.—**Peter immersed people in the Tiber.** Peter, then, was at Rome at some time—a fact Calvin dared to deny, or at least to put forward as doubtful. Tertullian, who lived near those times and was a diligent observer of Christian affairs, leaves the matter in no doubt here and in *On Prescription*. LE PR.]

Nor did that eunuch whom [Col. 1204A] Philip immersed along the road in water encountered by chance consequently receive more or less salvation. Therefore all waters, by virtue of the ancient prerogative of their origin, receive the sacrament of sanctification when God is invoked. For the Spirit immediately comes upon them from heaven and remains over the waters, sanctifying them from himself; and thus sanctified, they absorb the power of sanctifying. [Note: [Col. 1204B] Wouwer’s old manuscript appends to these words: “in place of filth, may be washed away by the waters.” Wouwer thinks the text should read: “they absorb, so that offenses too, in place of filth, may be washed away by the waters.”]

The likeness, however, corresponds to the simple act: since we are defiled by offenses as though by filth, we are washed clean by waters. But offenses do not appear upon the flesh, since no one carries upon his skin a stain of idolatry, [Note: [Col. 1204C] **Of idolatry, fornication, or fraud.** These are the principal offenses which, at the very beginning of *On Idolatry*, he calls crimes destructive and devouring of salvation. In the same place he adds murder—which he omitted here—because murderers commonly bear the stains of a killing they have committed upon some part of their own body. RIG.] fornication, or fraud. Rather, persons of this kind are filthy in the spirit, which is the author of the offense. For the spirit rules and the flesh serves; nevertheless they share the guilt between them—the spirit because of its command, the flesh because of its service. Therefore, when the waters have in some manner been medicated [Col. 1204B] through the intervention of the angel, the spirit is bodily washed clean in the waters, and the flesh is spiritually cleansed in those same waters.

## CHAPTER V

But the foreign nations, strangers to all understanding of spiritual things, ascribe power to their idols through the same efficacy; yet they deceive themselves with widowed waters, [Note: [Col. 1204C] **But they deceive themselves with widowed waters.** Waters, that is, widowed of the angel and the Holy Spirit, and therefore barren. But upon our waters the Spirit comes from heaven, sanctifying them from himself; and thus, once sanctified, they conceive the power of sanctifying and consequently give birth—namely, to Christians, human beings destined for eternal life. Paulinus, Letter XII to Severus:

> Into this river the Holy Spirit descends from heaven.
