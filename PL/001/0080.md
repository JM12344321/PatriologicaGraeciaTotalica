Tertullian further insists that their gods are most unjust if, because of the crimes of the Christians, they afflict and destroy them with such great evils. Nor does he allow this weapon to be turned against the Christians, whom God permitted to be overwhelmed by the same calamities as the Gentiles. For, as he himself declared, God will not distinguish the just [Col. 0720C] from the unjust until the final judgment after the end of the world. He therefore willed that, in this course of human life, all things should be common to all: adversities serving as admonition for the good, but as chastisement for the wicked. Moreover, Christians were harmed by no adversities, since nothing was more desirable to them than to depart from this world. Whatever affliction shook them, they renewed their hope and confidence through the predictions of the divine Scriptures. The Gentiles, by contrast, having no consolation from their gods in adversity, either in this life or in the life to come, plainly worshipped gods who were unjust and ungrateful (ch. 41).

What, moreover, could be more absurd than another complaint made by the Gentiles against the Christians, in which they protested that Christians were unproductive and useless to the commonwealth? For they were not [Col. 0720D] Brahmans or forest-dwellers, but everywhere engaged in every kind of commerce with those same Gentiles; and, excepting only the latter’s superstitious ceremonies, they so combined their crafts and services that they lived both with them and from them. “But the revenues of our temples,” the Gentiles said, “are diminished.” “For whom?” asks Tertullian. “For your beggar-men and beggar-gods?” Do Christians not always bring assistance to those who ask it, and does not their mercy distribute more street by street than the Gentiles distribute temple by temple? As for all other taxes, those same Christians pay them with as much good faith as the Gentiles use dishonesty in evading them (ch. 42).

But who, our author continues, complained that Christians were useless? Was it not pimps, procurers, bath-house attendants, assassins, poisoners, magicians, [Col. 0721A] and other men of the same stamp? Yet to be unproductive for them is itself a gain. Besides, if Christians caused any loss to the commonwealth, they more than adequately compensated for it by expelling demons from the bodies of the Gentiles themselves. But the Gentiles killed Christians with a cruelty as great as the injury they thereby did to the commonwealth, although the Christians’ innocence ought to have been evident to everyone, if only from the fact that, while the prisons were full of criminals, no Christian was found there except solely on account of his name (chs. 43, 44).

The integrity of their lives and morals is proved not only by this fact, but also by their divine laws, which far surpass all human laws in sanctity and authority. For the latter forbid homicide, the former [Col. 0721B] anger; the latter adultery, the former lust; the latter wrongdoing, the former evil speech. The latter command that injury be avenged, while the former forbid repaying injury in kind. To this is added the fact that everything approved in the human laws of the Gentiles was drawn from the divine statutes of the Christians. Moreover, it is plainly established that the authority of the latter is greater than that of the former, because a human law can easily be broken, whether because offenders can conceal themselves and feel compelled to sin, or because punishment is brief and cannot be inflicted beyond death. But absolutely nothing can be hidden from God, who exacts eternal punishments from all who are conscious of crimes. What wonder, then, if—as has already been demonstrated—the Christians who feared him possessed such great innocence, while the Gentiles who feared the proconsul displayed such depravity [Col. 0721C] (chs. 45, 46)?

“But our philosophers,” they said, “professed the same innocence, justice, patience, sobriety, and chastity as the Christians.” If that is so, Tertullian replies, why are so many philosophers, who spurned the superstitions of the Gentiles, praised and rewarded with statues and stipends, while Christians are tortured and killed for precisely the same reason? Was Socrates—who made use of the services of a demon that continually attended him, and who sacrificed to Aesculapius—better than Christians, who put demons to flight and expel them? Is the knowledge of Thales, who when questioned about God gave no answer, or of Plato, who said that God is difficult to discover and describe, greater or better than that of any Christian artisan, who has found God and immediately explains who he is [Col. 0721D]? Is the chastity of Christians, who preserve continence or desire no woman except a wife, less or worse than that of Plato, corrupter of young men; of Diogenes, desperately enamored of the prostitute Phryne; of Speusippus, who died in adultery; or of Democritus, who blinded himself lest he look upon women with corrupted eyes? And what modesty was there in Diogenes, who trampled Plato’s pride with another kind of pride, or in Pythagoras and Zeno, who aspired to tyranny? What equity was there in Lycurgus, who killed himself because his laws had been amended? Why say more? Is not Aristotle remembered for having shamefully expelled Hermias from his position, just as he flattered Alexander? Is not Plato remembered as having sold himself for the sake of his belly, Aristippus for his dissipation, [Col. 0722A] and Hippias as having been killed in an ambush which he himself had prepared for another? But the Christian, utterly removed from all these things, has never attempted anything of the kind. For if anyone departed from the rule of discipline, he thereby ceased to be a Christian.

The Gentiles therefore boasted in vain that their philosophers were equal to Christians in knowledge. For those very philosophers, like the poets and sophists, had indeed drawn many teachings from the sacred fountains of our divine Scripture; but, despising its simplicity and its language, which was then obscure, they corrupted those teachings with their own perverse opinions. From this sacred Scripture they learned that there is only one God; but the Platonists taught that he is incorporeal, the Stoics that he is corporeal, Epicurus that he arose from atoms, Pythagoras that he arose from numbers, and Heraclitus that he arose from fire. In the same way, [Col. 0722B] Plato affirmed his divine providence, which Epicurus denied. Some considered him to be outside the world, others to be situated within it. For the same reason, their opinions varied concerning the origin and duration of the world and the nature of the human soul. Nor should it be objected that the recent teaching of Christ was also adulterated by certain Christians. For these, says Tertullian, are heretics, and against their errors we prescribe the most certain rule of truth, which has been transmitted to us by the disciples of Christ himself.

Furthermore, through the malicious cunning and wicked prompting of demons, the Gentile philosophers and poets devised certain fables, so that, through a false resemblance to the truth handed down in the sacred Scriptures, other men would place their faith in them rather than in the Christians, whom they hated. [Col. 0722C] Thus they invented a tribunal in the underworld, the river Pyriphlegethon, and the Elysian Fields, because they had learned from our people that there would be a final judgment of all humanity, eternal punishments for the wicked, and everlasting happiness for the devout in paradise. But who does not know that our writings, as the more ancient, should be believed rather than the more recent writings of others, just as the archetype should be believed rather than its image (ch. 47)?

And certainly the complete restoration of our bodies is far truer than the Pythagorean transmigration of souls. For no one will ever deny that a human body can be reconstituted by him from whom the soul is falsely imagined to have received the power to migrate from one body into another. But that migration of souls is plainly fictitious, since no one has ever [Col. 0722D] recognized that he had been transformed into a beast. Moreover, in this passage of the soul from one body into another, one part of the human being remains alive while the other always remains dead. Yet the whole human being must necessarily return to his former life, so that he may receive God’s final judgment concerning the good or evil deeds he performed in this life. What of the fact that the soul can suffer nothing without the body, although it nevertheless merited nothing apart from it? Nor should you say that this can never be done by anyone. For he who, when you did not exist, created you, like the whole world, from nothing, can assuredly reconstitute you entire, just as you were, after you have ceased to exist. Finally, light, darkness, the stars, the fruits of the earth, seeds, and very many other things revive; how much more will man return [Col. 0723A] to life—man who, if he knows himself according to the oracle of the Pythia, is lord of all things that die and rise again? Wherever, therefore, or in whatever manner, he has been reduced to nothing, he will be recalled to life by the power of him to whom nothingness belongs, just as does the whole.

“Must one therefore,” someone will argue, “always die and rise again?” Certainly, says Tertullian, if it had so pleased God. But he who created all things from diverse and contrary realities plainly willed that dead human beings should be raised once to their former life at that intervening point of time which, at the end of the world, lies between this temporal age and the eternity that will follow.

Then the worshippers of God will obtain the reward of eternal happiness, just as the profane and wicked will be tormented in everlasting fire. Examples of this fire, [Col. 0723B] wholly different from our earthly fire, are furnished to us by lightning bolts and perpetually burning mountains. Although the philosophers acknowledged these things, and others did not reject them, they nevertheless mocked this opinion of the Christians. Grant, however, if it pleases God, that it is false. Yet surely the very hope of eternal punishment or reward is useful and necessary for everyone. Whatever that hope may be, Christians were still not for that reason to be savaged with swords, beasts, crosses, and fires while the populace exulted. The Gentiles, therefore, displayed an utterly foolish eagerness and gloried in the punishments of men who, choosing condemnation rather than falling away from God, celebrated an immortal triumph (chs. 48, 49).

But the Gentiles again cried out even more violently that Christians had no cause for complaint if they suffered voluntarily. Our author replies to them that Christians suffer in the manner of soldiers who, although they pray to be spared the dangers of war, nevertheless fight with all their strength and ultimately rejoice when they obtain spoil, victory, and glory. [Col. 0723C] For the Christians’ victory possesses the glory of pleasing God and the spoil of eternal life. In this glorious contest, the very instruments by which they are tortured become for them the attire of victory, the palm-embroidered robe, and the triumphal chariot. Thus they could not be called desperate or ruined by Gentiles who not only extolled with the highest praises the courage and constancy amid torture and the cruelest death of Mucius, Empedocles, Dido, Regulus, Anaxarchus, the Attic courtesan, Zeno of Elea, the Laconians, and others, but also adorned them with statues, images, and inscriptions, effecting a kind of recall from death to life [Col. 0723D] and an eternity of glory. So far, therefore, is the Christian—who expects from God, for whom he suffers, a true eternity—from being mad, that by this very fact he proves his own innocence and the unjust cruelty of the Gentiles. Did not those same Gentiles, by condemning a Christian woman to a pimp rather than to a lion, openly confess that she and other Christians considered the stain upon chastity more dreadful than every kind of torture and even death itself? Yet their savagery profited the Gentiles not in the least, since the blood of the martyrs was the seed of Christians, through which their number continually increased in a marvelous fashion. Indeed, by their death they made far more disciples than Cicero, Seneca, Diogenes, [Col. 0724A] Pyrrho, and Callinicus, who exhorted their followers to endure pain and death. For the Gentiles first admired the Christians’ unconquered constancy. Then they recognized the cause of such constancy and the truth of the Christian religion. Once they had recognized it, they embraced it without delay and were inflamed with a marvelous ardor for martyrdom, by which not only are all sins forgiven, but the entirety of God’s grace is also purchased. They therefore gave thanks for the judgments of the Gentiles, by which they were condemned but absolved by God (ch. 50).

## ARTICLE II. It Is Shown That Tertullian Is the Author of This Book; Who He Was; What His Native Country Was; Who His Parents Were; and in Whose False Religions He Was First Instructed

It is certainly established, and has hitherto been the common opinion of all, with no one dissenting, [Col. 0724B] that Tertullian is the true and genuine author of this *Apologeticus*. Yet if anyone desires certain arguments in support of this opinion, it is not difficult to satisfy him abundantly. For besides numerous manuscript codices of both earlier and more recent date, in which this very book is inscribed with Tertullian’s name, Eusebius—whose reliability in this matter ought to be suspect to no one—after transcribing certain words from this book, added in the same place: [non-Latin text omitted] (Euseb., *Ecclesiastical History*, bk. II, ch. 2, p. 41). Rufinus translated this into Latin as follows: “[Col. 0724C] Tertullian, a man thoroughly versed in Roman laws and institutions and exceedingly renowned among our writers, says these things in his *Apologeticus*, which he writes against the Gentiles on behalf of our faith” (Ruf., bk. II, ch. 2). This is certainly not a bad translation, if he had not omitted certain words that should have been rendered into Latin thus: “afterward translated into the Greek language.” Eusebius, therefore, and Rufinus himself as well, were persuaded that this book had been composed by Tertullian. The same Eusebius, mentioning Tertullian by name, transcribed still more from this same book (Euseb., bk. III, ch. 23, p. 91), and elsewhere added: [non-Latin text omitted]. “Moreover, from Tertullian’s *Apologeticus*, composed in Latin, which we mentioned above, this narrative has been taken by us” [Col. 0724D] (*ibid.*, ch. 35, p. 105). Thus Valesius rendered Eusebius’s Greek words into Latin. Finally, in yet another book, when similarly quoting the words of our Tertullian, he prefaced them with the following (*idem*, bk. V, ch. 5, p. 169): [non-Latin text omitted]. “But Tertullian also,” as Valesius translates, “is a competent witness to the same matter in the *Apologeticus*, which he composed in the Latin language for the defense of our faith and addressed to the senate of the city of Rome, and which we also mentioned above; there he confirms this account with a stronger and clearer argument.”

[Col. 0725A] In addition, Jerome says in his letter to Magnus (Jerome, Epist. 83, to Magnus, p. 656): “What is more learned than Tertullian, what more acute? His *Apologeticus* and his little books against the Gentiles embrace all the learning of the age.” But there is no need to heap up further evidence for a matter so certain and evident, unless one wishes to bring light to the noonday sun itself.

Before, however, we proceed to examine this book in our customary manner, it is worthwhile to investigate who its author was. First, then, these four names of his are transmitted, with no one yet disputing them: Quintus, Septimius, Florens, Tertullianus. No one should be troubled by this multitude of names. For many people were designated by just as many names, so that they might be distinguished from other members of their clan and family. The first [Col. 0725B] was the *praenomen*, by which the person upon whom it was conferred could be distinguished from all his brothers. For, as Varro has taught us, “*praenomina* were established as individual designations for practical use, by which clan names might be distinguished: from numbers, such as Prima, Secunda, Tertia, Quarta; among men, Quintus, Sextus, Decimus; and similarly from other circumstances, when there were two or more Terentii, in order to distinguish them they were marked by some individual designation—for example, one born in the morning was called Manius; one born by daylight, Lucius; and one born after his father’s death, Postumus” (Varro, bk. III, *On the Latin Language*, p. 126).

The *nomen* was the clan name, or one derived from the clan from which a person traced his origin. The *cognomen*, which many do not distinguish from the *agnomen*, was given so that, when several persons of the same [Col. 0725C] name belonged to a single family, it might be known from which branch each had been born. Some, however, believe that the *agnomen* was so called because it was added to the three names already mentioned on account of some illustrious deed, some virtue, a defect of character, a bodily mark, or another similar reason. Sigonius, Onuphrius Panvinus, and others discuss these matters more fully (Sigon., *On Roman Names*, ch. 5; Onuphr., *On Roman Names*, §§ 13 and 16).

Tertullian’s *praenomen*, therefore, was Quintus, undoubtedly because he was born fifth in order among his brothers. His *nomen* was Septimius, as he himself indicates in the words with which he concludes the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*: “To those who read these things in good peace, preferring truth to custom, may peace and grace from our Lord Jesus overflow together with Septimius Tertullianus, whose little work this is” [Col. 0725D] (Tertull., *On the Veiling of Virgins*, ch. 17). He bore the name Septimius because he sprang from the Septimian clan. Since, however, there were several men of this family, the cognomen Florens Tertullianus was added to him—unless the *agnomen* is shown to be distinct from the *cognomen* and to have been added to him for some particular reason, as we have noted.

Yet although he possessed four names, he is nevertheless called simply Tertullian by Eusebius, as we have already seen, and by Jerome and others, as we shall observe more than once below. And under that single name his *Apologeticus* is also inscribed in our manuscript codices.

Jerome teaches us in what province and city he was born [Col. 0726A] in these words: “Tertullian . . . of the province of Africa, of the city of Carthage” (Jerome, *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115). Hence in Eusebius’s *Chronicle* he is called “Tertullian the African” (*Chronicle of Eusebius*, at the year of Christ 208), and by Optatus, “Tertullian the Carthaginian” (Optatus, bk. I, *On the Schism of the Donatists*, p. 8). But what need is there of further witnesses, when Tertullian himself indicates his native country in these words, which we read in the *Apologeticus* under discussion? “In Africa infants were openly sacrificed to Saturn down to the proconsulship of Tiberius . . . as witnessed by the military force of our own country, which performed that very service for that proconsul” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 9). Moreover, in another book, which he addressed to the Carthaginians, he testifies that he was born at Carthage. [Col. 0726B] There he reproaches them for the fault of forgetfulness because, although they had formerly used the battering ram in wars: “Yet,” he says, “when the last days of their native city approached, and the Roman ram now dared to assault walls once its own, the Carthaginians immediately stood amazed at it as a novel and foreign contrivance” (*idem*, *On the Pallium*, ch. 1). Can it not be inferred from this passage that in these words he designates his own native country no less clearly than that of those whom he addresses?

Tertullian was therefore undoubtedly born at Carthage, his father being, as Jerome says, a proconsular centurion; or, as we read in Eusebius’s *Chronicle*, “he was the son of a proconsular centurion.” The proconsul’s centurions and soldiers, however, were not held in great honor, since they were his attendants and customarily performed menial services (Jerome, *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115; [Col. 0726C] *Chronicle of Eusebius*, at the year of Christ 208).

Born of this father and his other, now unknown, Gentile forebears, Tertullian imbibed their abominable superstitions with his mother’s milk and afterward openly professed them. For when he discusses the true doctrines of the Christians, he addresses those same Gentiles thus: “We too once laughed at these things; we were of your number: Christians are made, not born” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 18). In another book, moreover, he laments in these words the blindness and ignorance of the true God under which he then labored: “Repentance, as men of this kind—such as we ourselves once were—blind and without the Lord’s light, know it merely by nature, is a certain passion of the mind which arises from dissatisfaction with a previous judgment” (beginning of *On Repentance*, ch. 1).

[Col. 0726D] At that time, therefore, as learned men have observed, being deprived of the light of the true God and of the Christian religion, he delighted in profane spectacles. For of these he said somewhere: “I prefer not to give a full account rather than to remember” (*On the Spectacles*, ch. 19). Indeed, he confesses, not without anguish, that he had fallen into shameful sins of the flesh: “For I know,” he said, “that I did not commit adulteries in one kind of flesh, nor then”—read *now*—“strive for continence in another kind of flesh” (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 59). He declares no less openly that he had repented of his other sins: “You, a sinner like me—indeed, less than me. For I acknowledge my own preeminence in transgressions” (*On Repentance*, ch. 4). And again later: “Since I am a sinner of every mark, born for nothing [Col. 0727A] except repentance, I cannot easily remain silent on that subject” (*On Repentance*, ch. 12). Yet no one will doubtless find it surprising if a Gentile man who worshipped adulterous gods, and gods worse than adulterers, wished to follow their examples, as Chaerea does in Terence (*Eunuch*, act III, scene 5, v. 43). But as much pleasure as these crimes once gave Tertullian while he was a Gentile, so much horror did they afterward cause him when he professed the Christian religion, as is evident from his books, and especially from this *Apologeticus*.

## ARTICLE III. By What Considerations Tertullian Was Led to the Christian Religion, and When He Professed It

[Col. 0727B] If anyone asks by what considerations Tertullian was impelled to embrace the faith of Christ, he will hear from George of Amiens, the Capuchin, that there were three: the innocence of the Christians, the excellence of sacred Scripture, and the Christians’ marvelous power over demons (George, *Vindication of Tertullian*, part II, questions 1 and 2). Yet, however weighty these considerations may be, he destroys them with the same hand by which he had established them. For he immediately added that Gentiles and Jews—such as Ulpian and Suetonius, Josephus and Philo—and other very learned men, having weighed these considerations with minds no less attentive than Tertullian’s, could not be led to profess the Christian religion. He therefore supposes that Tertullian, while still wavering and fluctuating, was impelled by some [Col. 0727C] singular and divine vision to give his name to the Christian religion. But from where, I ask, did George learn that those proofs of the Christian religion were examined and weighed by the Gentiles and Jews whom he mentions with as much attentiveness, fairness, and sincerity as by Tertullian? From where, moreover, did he learn that Tertullian was granted that singular vision, without which, despite the strongest possible arguments, he could never have been led to the Christian religion? Is it thus permissible for a Christian and religious man, by a mere and extremely slight conjecture, to weaken the force and strength of those arguments by which, as we shall show below, the truth of the Christian religion is demonstrated most invincibly? But that learned man had doubtless not paid sufficient attention to these words of Tertullian in the [Col. 0727D] *Apologeticus* concerning the endurance of the martyrs: “Who is not stirred by contemplating it to inquire what the reality within the matter may be? Who, once he has inquired, does not join us? Once he has joined us, he longs to die” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50)? And again, he concludes another book thus: “Anyone who witnesses such great endurance is struck by some uneasiness and is kindled to inquire what the cause may be; and when he has learned the truth, he himself immediately follows it” (*To Scapula*, final chapter). But if any Gentile who was neither wholly obstinate nor an unfair evaluator of matters, having thus recognized the truth of the Christian religion, embraced it, how much more readily would Tertullian, who far surpassed others in mental acuteness and fairness?

What of the fact that he was especially [Col. 0728A] an eyewitness of the Christians’ power over demons, or was at least so firmly persuaded of it that he challenged the Gentiles to test it publicly, and declared that the blood of those Christians should be shed unless the demons adjured by them confessed that they were not gods? “But what,” he says, “is more manifest than this operation, what proof more trustworthy? The simplicity of truth stands openly before us” (*idem*, *Apologeticus*, ch. 23). He therefore regarded the force of this argument and experience as fully established, and believed that no one could resist it unless utterly blinded by false prejudices.

No less evident to him was the authority of sacred Scripture, in which, he says, “whoever approaches will find God; whoever also endeavors to understand will be compelled to believe” (*ibid.*, ch. 18). Did he not, therefore, find God there, [Col. 0728B] recognize the false gods of the Gentiles, and discover that the Christian religion was true? For this truth shone with such great light that it was immediately received by all who were not blinded by voluntary obstinacy. But I would have you hear him recount these things himself: “It is a testimony of ignorance, which, while excusing injustice, condemns it: for all who formerly hated because they did not know the nature of what they hated, as soon as they cease to be ignorant, also cease to hate. From these persons Christians are made, assuredly upon ascertainment of the truth; and they begin to hate what they had been and to profess what they had hated” (*ibid.*, ch. 1). But surely, if so many Gentiles, by Tertullian’s own admission, became Christians through all or any one of these considerations, how much more did he, to whom the falsity of the Gentiles’ religion and the truth of the Christian religion were so clearly established? To reject the former and [Col. 0728C] embrace the latter, therefore, he had no need of the divine vision invented by George.

It is not so easy to say when he gave his name to the Christian religion. Pamelius believed that the book *On the Pallium* was published by him in the year of Christ 195, immediately after he had been initiated into the Christian religion. But this writer’s opinion regarding the date of this book is false; more expert scholars now believe it was written much later, namely after his defection from the Catholic Church and around the year 211.

The beginning of his profession must therefore be investigated in another way. We learn from Jerome that he published a treatise on the troubles of marriage “while he was still a young man” (Jerome, [Col. 0728D] bk. I, *Against Jovinian*, p. 157). Although Jerome said that he had written playfully on this subject in his youth, he nevertheless exhorts the virgin Eustochium to read this work, just as he exhorts her to read other works of Cyprian, Damasus, and Ambrose: “And now,” he says, “I give the same advice: if it pleases you to know from how many troubles a virgin is free and by how many a wife is bound, read Tertullian’s book addressed to a philosopher friend” (*idem*, Epist. 18, to Eustochium, p. 37). Yet it may not unreasonably be doubted whether this book, which we lost long ago, was composed by Tertullian as a Gentile or as a Christian. But to whom, someone will object, will it appear plausible that Jerome wished to be the adviser and advocate of a Christian virgin’s reading this work, just as she was to read the books of Cyprian, Damasus, and [Col. 0729A] Ambrose, if he had seen in it anything contrary to the established laws of the Christian religion? And how could Tertullian have composed such a book unless he had been fully instructed in those same laws? Yet, by his own testimony, no one recognized the Christian religion and its laws without immediately wishing to profess it. Moreover, Jerome recorded this further fact concerning him: “He remained a presbyter of the Church”—that is, the Catholic Church—“until middle age” (*idem*, *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115), and therefore until before he was infected by the heresy of Montanus. Then Jerome added in the same place: “He is said to have lived to a very advanced age,” namely to his eightieth year, or, if you prefer, his ninetieth. He therefore remained an orthodox presbyter until approximately the fortieth year of his life. [Col. 0729B] Yet he does not appear to have been elevated to this sacred order either for the first time then or immediately after professing the Christian religion. He therefore appears to have become a Christian from a Gentile while still a young man. Let all who are expert judge how much weight this entire argument carries.

## ARTICLE IV. On Tertullian’s Marriage and His Supreme Love of Continence; on His Priesthood; Whether, After Becoming a Presbyter, He Lived with His Wife in the Manner of Married Men and Did Not Distinguish Those Initiated into Sacred Orders from the Laity; What Manner of Life He Followed and How Harsh His Character Was; How and When He Fell into the Heresy of the Montanists; and in What Year of His Life and of Christ He Brought His Long Life to an End

[Col. 0729C] Tertullian certainly had a wife, and she was a Christian, as is most clearly demonstrated by the two books he wrote to her. Writers of our own age, however, dispute whether he, while still a Gentile or already a Christian, married her while she was a Gentile or a Christian. For at that time it was indeed customary among Christians, as Tertullian himself testifies, to contract marriages with Gentile women; and no trace exists in his writings or those of others by which it can be determined whether, after already taking a wife, he entered the camp of Christ. But if it can be inferred from what we said a little earlier that he swore allegiance to the laws of Christ while still a young man, can it not also be concluded from this that he married afterward? For once he had become a Christian, he always burned with too great a zeal for observing Christian law [Col. 0729D] to take any woman in marriage except a Christian. And indeed, in the books addressed to his wife already cited, he endeavors to persuade her that, if he should depart this life first, she should either remain in widowhood or marry a Christian man if she wished to be bound by a second marriage. Is it not altogether probable that he himself obeyed the laws which he prescribed for his wife? But we leave these conjectures to the judgment of others.

Tertullian undoubtedly lived with his wife in the ordinary manner of married couples when he addressed to her the books just commended. For he exhorts her as follows to preserve continence in widowhood if he should die first: “What we have not been able to attain in marriage, let us pursue in widowhood. The opportunity which removes what necessity commanded must be embraced” [Col. 0730A] (Tertull., bk. I, *To His Wife*, ch. 7). For why would he have addressed these words to her if he and his wife had always abstained from the customary use of marriage? Yet no one can fail to understand from this how greatly Tertullian was always inflamed with zeal for continence and chastity.

It ought to appear less surprising to everyone that he exhorts his wife to continence in these books, if the opinion of certain heterodox and Catholic writers concerning the time when they were written is true. They suspect that he published them when his own age was advancing, while his wife’s was still flourishing and vigorous. They therefore suppose that in them he drew up, as it were, the tablets of his testament. But they offer no other argument for their opinion except these words of Tertullian, which are read at the beginning of the first [Col. 0730B] book as follows: “For we are occupied with secular affairs, and we desire provision to be made for each of us. If we make arrangements for such matters, why should we not all the more provide for our posterity in divine and heavenly matters, and, as it were, bequeath in advance as a legacy an admonition and exposition of those things which are assigned from immortal goods and from the inheritance of heaven?” (*ibid.*, ch. 1). But because, as Rigaltius advises us, the word *talibus* (“for such matters”) is read twice in the Agobard manuscript, he wished it to be emended and read: “we arrange testamentary tablets for such matters”; and from this he infers that these books were composed by Tertullian in the manner of a testament, when he was already far advanced in age (Rigalt., note 1 on this book).

But even if the matter were so, have wills never been made and sealed by human beings except when their age was already advancing? Has no prudent [Col. 0730C] man, especially a Christian, ever reflected—while still in the flower of youth—upon death, which is always uncertain, and upon drawing up his will? Tertullian certainly had still greater reason to think about making it at any period of his life, because, with the Gentiles then raging against Christians, he could not have been unaware that his life was in daily danger. Could not he also, always inflamed—as we have said—with so great and singular a love of continence and chastity, have exhorted his wife at any period of his life to preserve it if she should ever become a widow? Moreover, if she was still vigorous, as they say, when Tertullian was engaged in publishing these books, can it be concluded from an invented meaning of some of his words that he was old or decrepit? Nor should those be heard who object that several things occur in [Col. 0730D] these books by which Tertullian reveals that his mind was already inclined toward the Montanists. Is it merely because he exhorts his wife to continence? But did not the apostle Paul himself also exhort Christians to the same virtue? It is enough for us that they concede that, when Tertullian wrote these books to his wife, he had not yet fallen into the error of the Montanists. For until middle age, as Jerome, whom we have cited, testifies, he was a presbyter of the Catholic Church. He therefore composed those books long before his old age.
