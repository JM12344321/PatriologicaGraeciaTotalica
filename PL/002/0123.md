Remedied. On this word see above, Chapter XV. Moreover, this episode is narrated in Exodus 15:23ff. as follows: “And they came to Marah, and they could not drink the waters of Marah, because they were bitter; hence he also gave the place a fitting name, calling it Marah, that is, ‘bitterness.’ … And the Lord showed him a piece of wood; and when he had cast it into the waters, they were turned to sweetness.”

Retro, “previously,” as often elsewhere.

Remedying. The ancient Fathers also interpret it thus, among them St. Ambrose, *On Those Who Are Initiated*, ch. 3:

> Marah, he says, was a very bitter spring; Moses cast wood into it, and it became sweet. For water without the preaching of the Lord’s cross—behold the wood—would be of no use for future salvation; but when it has been consecrated by the mystery of the saving Cross, it is then tempered for use as a spiritual washing and a saving drink. Therefore, just as Moses cast wood into that spring—that is, the Prophet—so into this font the priest casts the preaching of the Lord’s Cross, and the water becomes sweet unto grace.

[Col. 1171A]

Moreover, Junius thinks that this passage of Tertullian should be read thus: “the water of Baptism, namely, remedying by itself the most unhealthy veins.” But I firmly retain our reading. For it was not the water of Baptism that remedied those bitter waters, as though they were most unhealthy veins; rather, the wood—or Christ crucified upon the wood—converted those waters, which were destined for Baptism but were otherwise unhealthy, that is, contained nothing salutary or conducive to our salvation, into sweetness by itself, or by its own proper power, as a remedy for sins and for the salvation of our soul.

Flowed down. For when the people of Israel had come from the wilderness of Sin to Rephidim and, because of the bitter thirst afflicting them, had murmured against Moses, God commanded him to take the very rod with which he had previously divided the Red Sea and to strike the rock in order to draw water from it, etc.; Exodus 17:1ff. A similar miracle also occurred at Kadesh, as may be seen in Numbers 20:13ff. St. Paul had both in view in 1 Corinthians 10, explaining them thus:

> All drank the same spiritual drink—for they drank from a spiritual rock that followed them [ἔπινον γὰρ ἐκ πνευματικῆς ἀκολουθούσης πέτρας]. They drank, moreover, from the spiritual rock that followed them; and the rock was Christ—but with most of them, etc.

[Col. 1171B]

From this, not only the Rabbis but also quite a few Christian interpreters maintain that that rock, or at least the waters of that rock, thereafter always accompanied the Israelites in the wilderness. Thus our Author says in *On Patience*, ch. 5: “After the edible rain of manna, after the watery attendance of the rock,” etc. But what necessity is there to assert here so enormous a miracle, one by no means indicated by Moses? For the Apostle is not speaking here of that material rock which was at Rephidim, but of a spiritual rock—and thus only in a spiritual or mystical sense—namely Christ, prefigured by that rock, who as the living Rock continually accompanies Christians, the better Israelites, through the wilderness of this world, becoming for them a fountain of water springing up into eternal life; John 4:14. For this reason our Author rightly places this water, flowing forth from the rock at Rephidim, among the figures of our Baptism.

Be blessed. The passage is certainly obscure. Something seems to have dropped out and must be supplied. Accordingly, I think it should be read thus: “Water in Christ, in whom we see Baptism blessed”; this sense is sufficiently clear. But what does this mean, “Baptism is blessed”? It may be understood either of that very ancient rite, persisting even to our own times, namely the blessing of the baptismal font itself, which is customarily performed on Holy Saturday and the Vigil of Pentecost—see above, Chapter IV, note “God being invoked”—or of the very formula of Baptism, as in Chapter VI, note “Through the blessing.”

[Col. 1171C–D]

The grace of water, meaning its beauty or dignity, as in Chapter III, note “Or grace”; or its kindness, favor, or esteem with God, to whom water has always been more acceptable than the other elements, etc.

Confirmation, so that Baptism may be established or more strongly commended through water as its matter.

Water inaugurates, by changing it into wine, which was his first miracle: “Jesus made this the beginning of signs at Cana in Galilee,” etc.; John 2:11.

He invites to his water. For thus Jesus speaks to the Samaritan woman in John 4:14: “The water that I shall give him will become in him a fountain of water springing up into eternal life.” And John 7:37: “If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. He who believes in me, rivers of living water shall flow from his belly.”

Concerning the agape. Ἀγάπη in Greek means “love.” Elsewhere *agapae* also means those charitable meals of the earliest Christians which they customarily celebrated before the consecration of the Holy Eucharist, both in memory of Christ’s Last Supper, at which he instituted that sacrament, and to foster mutual love. Paul sharply censures their abuse among certain people in 1 Corinthians 11:20ff.

[Col. 1172A]

He approves. Thus, in commending brotherly love in Matthew 10, the final verse, he says: “Whoever gives one of these little ones only a cup of cold water to drink in the name of a disciple, amen I say to you, shall not lose his reward.”

Gladly. “But in the fourth watch of the night Jesus came to them, walking upon the sea”; Matthew 14:25.

He crosses the strait. “And entering into a boat, he crossed over and came into his own city”; Matthew 9:1. Similarly: “When they”—the disciples with Jesus—“had crossed over, they came into the land of Gennesaret”; Matthew 14:34.

Spear. “One of the soldiers opened his side with a spear, and immediately blood and water came out”; John 19:34. St. Ambrose teaches, on Luke 23, that Baptism was prefigured by this water:

> From that body, incorrupt though dead, there remained the life of all. For water and blood came forth: the former to wash clean, the latter to redeem.

And St. Augustine, *Tractate 120 on John*, accordingly says that Christ’s side was opened on the cross:

> So that there, as it were, the door of life might be opened, from which flowed the sacraments, without which no one enters into the life that is true life. That blood was shed for the remission of sins; that water tempers the saving cup: it supplies both washing and drink.

[Col. 1172B]

## Notes on Chapter X

Constantly, that is, “continuously” or “immediately”; as our Author likewise says of Christ in *On Patience*, ch. 3: “It was not enough that he even had his betrayer with him and did not immediately denounce him.” Elsewhere, however, he uses it for “boldly” and “fearlessly”; for example, in *On the Shows*, ch. 26, where a demon says from within a certain possessed woman: “I acted boldly, and indeed with perfect justice.”

Believing. He is certainly alluding to what appears in Isaiah 7:9, where the Hebrew text reads literally: “If you do not believe, neither shall you be faithful,” or “have confidence.” Symmachus translates it: Ἐὰν μὴ πιστεύσητε, οὐ διαμενεῖτε, “If you do not believe, you shall not endure”; and from this our Vulgate has: “If you do not believe, you shall not remain.” Theodoret has it somewhat differently: οὐδ’ οὐ μὴ πιστευθείητε, “You shall by no means be believed.” Finally, the Septuagint has: ἐὰν μὴ πιστεύσητε, οὐδὲ μὴ συνῆτε, “If you do not believe, you shall by no means understand”; in accordance with this wording John Chrysostom, Cyprian, Augustine, and other Fathers frequently cite the passage. That saying in John 6:69 also seems to accord with it, where Peter says to Christ: Καὶ ἡμεῖς πεπιστεύκαμεν, ἐγνώκαμεν κτλ., “And we have believed and have known that you are the Christ,” etc. And John 10:38: ἐὰν ἐμοὶ μὴ πιστεύητε, τοῖς ἔργοις πιστεύσατε, ἵνα γνῶτε, καὶ πιστεύσητε ὅτι ἐν ἐμοὶ ὁ Πατὴρ κτλ., “If you are unwilling to believe me, believe the works, so that you may know and believe that the Father is in me,” etc.

[Col. 1172C–D]

Accordingly, if we wish to understand the mysteries revealed by God, we must first believe, so that faith must precede and understanding follow from faith. Tertullian confirms this elsewhere as well, as in *On the Soldier’s Crown*, ch. 2: “I praise the faith which believes that a thing must be observed before it has learned it.” And in *Against Marcion* IV, ch. 20, after similarly employing that testimony of Isaiah, he immediately adds: “This was the faith which had also bestowed understanding.” Finally, in the same work, ch. 25, he confirms it again, saying: “He concealed it, after first supplying the prophetic instrument of obscurity, the understanding of which faith would merit; for unless you believe, you shall not understand.”

But what of our Protestants? They wish first to understand and then to believe, measuring their faith by the light of their own reason and paying no attention at all to the common saying: “The learner must believe”—before, that is, he understands what he is learning. See, then, the difference between us and them. They do not believe unless they understand; we believe in order that we may understand. This is what the Apostle admonishes us in 2 Corinthians 10:5: to be “destroying counsels”—human counsels—“and every height that exalts itself against the knowledge of God”—that is, against those things concerning God that become known to us through faith—“and bringing every understanding into captivity unto the obedience of Christ.”

[Col. 1173A]

By command, however. *Mandatus*, fourth declension, is found among the jurists. The word could therefore easily have adhered to Tertullian from civil jurisprudence; for we demonstrated in the epitome of his Life prefixed by us to the book *On Prescription* that he was highly skilled in civil law.

By condition. That is, according to its intrinsic nature or, as we otherwise say, according to its substance. That Baptism was therefore divine only extrinsically, namely with regard to that command of God by which it was administered by John. But it was not divine intrinsically and with regard to its own nature; rather, it was devoid of any power imparted to it.

It furnished. John’s Baptism therefore had no efficacy for producing anything supernatural in the soul, whereas Christ imparted to his Baptism the power to produce sanctifying grace and through it to abolish sins. John himself clearly indicated this difference between the two Baptisms in Mark 1:8, saying: “I have baptized you with water, but he shall baptize you with the Holy Spirit.”

[Col. 1173B]

It ministered beforehand. For just as John himself was merely the Forerunner of the Lord, preparing his way, Mark 1:2, so too John’s Baptism was merely a kind of precursor to Christ’s Baptism: through elicited acts of repentance it called people away from sins and disposed them toward a better life, but did not itself confer that life. Thus the author of *Twenty-two Responses to the Orthodox*, Response 2 to Question XXXVII, says: “John’s Baptism was the prelude to the Gospel of grace.” And St. Basil, *Homily 13, Exhortation to Baptism*, says: “John’s Baptism belonged to those being initiated; this one belongs to those being perfected. That one called away from sin; this one joins a person to God and makes him intimate with God.”

By power, or liberty, aided, namely, by the powers of grace.

To require. For faith, as the Council of Trent teaches in Session VI, *On Justification*, ch. [number absent in the source], is a certain disposition necessarily preceding repentance itself, as is explained there more fully. Thus, in Luke 7:29, “all the people hearing, and the publicans, justified God”—believing, namely, Christ as he so notably commended John’s qualities—“having been baptized with John’s Baptism.” And the Evangelist immediately continues: “But the Pharisees and the lawyers rejected the counsel of God against themselves”—being unbelieving, namely, toward John as he preached a Baptism of repentance and toward Christ himself as he urged it so effectively—“not having been baptized by him.” Nor were they worthy of this Baptism of repentance, because they were destitute of faith, which they had so obstinately rejected.

[Col. 1173C]

Human. That is, a thing necessarily dependent on human choice and its free acts.

It is necessary. Equally human, namely, as he said above. See the note “By condition.” John’s Baptism therefore did no more than arouse repentance, by bestowing the helps of grace necessary for it; it did not itself efface sins. That had to be accomplished through an act of perfect repentance or contrition, as a work of the person acting.

If it had been heavenly. Just as Baptism conferred sanctifying grace itself and the remission of sins by a divine power intrinsic to itself—or, as we say, *ex opere operato*—as we have already said elsewhere.

God alone. This must indeed be understood of principal and authoritative power. Nevertheless, this power can also be communicated to human beings as instruments through whom sins are forgiven in Christ’s name and the Holy Spirit is conferred; and it is clear from John 20:22ff. that Christ communicated it to the Apostles, to their successors the bishops, and through these to priests. There Christ breathed upon the Apostles and said: “Receive the Holy Spirit. Whose sins you shall forgive, they are forgiven them,” etc.

[Col. 1173D–1174A]

Nevertheless, as St. Pacian says in Epistle 1 to Sympronianus: “What Christ does through his priests is his own power.” And in Epistle 3:

> What I do, I do not by my own right, but by the Lord’s; for we are God’s fellow workers. Therefore, whether we baptize, compel to repentance, or grant pardon to those who ask it, we administer this with Christ as its author.

These points had to be noted against our Protestants, who assert that priests possess no true power of forgiving sins, but merely a declaratory power regarding a remission already effected by God.

Would descend. “If I do not go away,” Christ says in John 16:7, “the Paraclete will not come to you; but if I go, I shall send him to you.” Tertullian employs this testimony to prove that John’s Baptism could not yet confer the grace of the Holy Spirit, because the Holy Spirit had not yet poured himself forth into the Church before Christ’s ascension into heaven. From this, quite a few even among our own maintain that neither did Baptism instituted by Christ possess the efficacy of conferring grace before the Passion and Resurrection—indeed, even before his Ascension. But more on this in the following chapter.

They had not even heard of him. In Acts 19:2ff., Paul says to his disciples: “Did you receive the Holy Spirit when you believed?” But they said to him: “We have not even heard whether there is a Holy Spirit.” He said: “Into what, then, were you baptized?” They said: “Into John’s Baptism.” Paul then said: “John baptized the people with a Baptism of repentance,” etc.

[Col. 1174B]

From this it is easily inferred that John’s Baptism was not conferred in the name of the Holy Trinity; otherwise, they could not have been ignorant of the Holy Spirit, since they would previously have had to believe in him as the one through whose grace the remission of sins was to take place and sanctification be conferred upon the soul. These, therefore, were Ephesian Jews who had once been baptized by John in the Jordan and had meanwhile returned to Ephesus. Before Paul arrived there, they had heard nothing about Christ’s Baptism, hitherto conferred by the Apostles, nor about the laying on of hands—that is, the sacrament of Confirmation, which was then joined immediately to Baptism—nor about the gifts of the Holy Spirit accompanying it.

Paul clearly did not think John’s Baptism had sufficed for them, since through it they had not yet been initiated into Christ’s Church. Therefore Acts 19:5–6 adds: “When they had heard these things, they were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus. And when Paul had laid his hands upon them, the Holy Spirit came upon them, and they spoke with tongues and prophesied.” See, then, how much weaker John’s Baptism was than Christ’s.

The spirit of prophecy. For John was a prophet—indeed, as Christ told the crowds in Matthew 11:9, more than a prophet. See also ch. 21:26. For he prophesied, not—as the ancient prophets had—that Christ would come long afterward, but that he would come immediately after him.

The transfer. Namely, when Christ, baptized by John, saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming upon him; Matthew 3:16.

[Col. 1174C]

To inquire. Namely, in Matthew 11:3, when John sent two of his disciples to Christ and said to him: “Are you he who is to come, or do we await another?” From this Tertullian believed that John later began to doubt whether Christ—whom he had so often and so openly proclaimed as the Messiah who was to come—was the true Messiah, even when Christ himself was exercising his ministry of preaching. Our Author sufficiently discloses this in *On Prescription*, ch. 8, and expresses it still more openly and at greater length in *Against Marcion* IV, ch. 18, where he says:

> While the Lord himself of powers, by the word and Spirit of the Father, was working and preaching upon earth, the portion of the Holy Spirit which, in accordance with the measure of the prophetic allotment, had operated in John in preparing the ways of the Lord, necessarily had now to withdraw from John, having been brought back—that is, recalled—into the Lord, as into the total sum of its mass, in whom, namely, the entire mass and fullness of grace resided. Accordingly, John was now an ordinary man, because, now left to himself by the prophetic spirit, he was now one of the crowd, etc.

[Col. 1174D–1175A]

Thus far, and further, Tertullian.

But other Fathers and interpreters more probably judge that John never doubted Christ. Rather, he sent his disciples—whom he had found reporting Christ’s miracles to him, yet perhaps still doubting his divinity—to question Christ, so that by his presence and by the miracles performed before them they might be fully convinced of his divinity. See also St. Ambrose on Luke, ch. 7, where he additionally offers mystical and allegorical meanings of that mission.

A candidate for remission. Among the Romans, those who aspired to public honors and went about among the individual tribes in the assemblies, soliciting votes for themselves, were called *candidati* from their white garment. Hence Tertullian says in *On Idolatry*, ch. 18: “Those who enjoyed familiarity with kings were called the kings’ ‘purple-clad men,’ just as among us candidates are so called from the white toga.”

This designation later began to be extended more widely, not because of similarity of dress but of office. Thus those who recited the prince’s letters and decrees in the senate were likewise called *candidati*, because this office gave them access to higher honors and to the magistracies themselves, as Ulpian teaches in the sole Law, §4, *Digest*, *On the Office of the Quaestor*. Likewise, younger men who were being trained in an apprenticeship and were shortly to leave adolescence were called *candidati*; in nearly this sense Quintilian, *Institutes*, Book V, calls them “candidates of eloquence.”

[Col. 1175B]

Accordingly, our Author also calls those people “candidates of the angels” in *On Prayer*, ch. 3, who have merited that heavenly utterance toward God and are learning the office of future glory, because such persons, according to Christ’s promise in Matthew 22:30, will be like God’s angels in heaven. Thus too in *To His Wife*, ch. 7, he calls one who is, as it were, undergoing an apprenticeship in the fear of God a “candidate of fear,” and elsewhere he has similar expressions. In the same way, therefore, our Author calls John’s Baptism a “candidate for remission,” because those initiated by it were prepared and disposed for Christ’s Baptism, through which full remission of sins takes place.

Moreover, in a worse sense, in *On the Crown*, ch. 7, he calls idolaters “candidates of the devil,” and in *On the Soul*, ch. 39, he calls certain offspring still in the mother’s womb “candidate progeny of demons.”

In Christ. Namely, in Christ’s Baptism.

It was declared. Namely, a remission that would occur not through the power of John’s Baptism itself, but through that of Christ’s Baptism which was afterward to follow; for this alone remitted sins *ex opere operato*, or by its own intrinsic power, as we have already said often elsewhere.

By the Spirit and fire. For thus John says in Luke 3:16: “I indeed baptize you with water; but one stronger than I shall come, and he will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.” He certainly did not exclude water from Christ’s Baptism. John intended only to indicate that his washing was merely simple, performed outwardly with simple water, whereas in the future Baptism of Christ it would be elevated to a higher mystery and also to an interior washing of the soul, which cannot occur without the grace of the Holy Spirit.

[Col. 1175C]

But the question of what that Baptism “in the Holy Spirit and fire” (ἐν Πνεύματι ἁγίῳ καὶ πυρί) might be has exercised many and has even given some a serious occasion for error. For there were certain ancient heretics who thought that fire was to be understood simply and literally of our true elemental fire, and therefore that fire was to be employed in Baptism no less than water. Clement of Alexandria—or whoever else is the author of this writing—testifies thus in the *Prophetic Extracts*: ἔνιοι δὲ, ὥς φησιν Ἡρακλέων, πυρὶ τὰ ὦτα τῶν σφραγιζομένων κατεσημήνατο; that is: “Some, as Heracleon says, branded with fire the ears of those being sealed”—the baptized. The author of the poem *Against Marcion*, Book I, also records a certain fiery baptism of the heresiarch Valentinus, who “taught that one should be immersed twice, the body being passed through flame.”

St. Augustine too, in *On Heresies, to Quodvultdeus*, heresy 59, reports that the Seleucian heretics conferred Baptism by fire. Whatever the truth about these matters, it is certain that the Apostles never employed fire in Baptism, and therefore that it was by no means commanded by Christ; hence the Church has always abhorred this practice.

[Col. 1175D–1176A]

Accordingly, some Catholic interpreters judge more correctly that “in the Spirit and fire” denotes Christ’s own Baptism, upon which the Holy Spirit came in the form of a dove and at the same time—as St. Justin Martyr reports in his *Dialogue with Trypho*, p. 246 of my edition—κατελθόντος τοῦ Ἰησοῦ ἐπὶ τὸ ὕδωρ, καὶ πῦρ ἀνήφθη ἐν τῷ Ἰορδάνῃ, that is: “When Jesus had descended into the water, fire was also kindled in the Jordan.”

But an invisible fire is also kindled in our Baptism, when the grace of the Holy Spirit flows into our hearts and, as St. Ambrose says in the final chapter of *On Elijah and Fasting*, consumes our sins and purifies the soul from their dross. Then there are those who, with St. Augustine on Psalm 65, understand it of the exorcisms customarily preceding our Baptism; for he says: “Why do unclean spirits so often cry out, ‘I burn,’ if that is not fire? But after the fire of exorcism one comes to Baptism.”

Others, with the author of the *Incomplete Work on Matthew*, Homily 3, take that Baptism of fire to designate, metaphorically, the tribulations and persecutions through which the constancy of the faithful righteous in faith and their patience in adversity are tested like gold in a furnace of fire. St. Basil, in his *Homily on Baptism*, maintains that this fire must be understood of the word of teaching, which both reproves the malice of sins and manifests the grace of justifications. These interpretations are sufficiently suitable, but only metaphorical.

Finally, our Tertullian explains it of the day of judgment and the infernal fire of the damned, as we shall see shortly.

[Col. 1176B]

Meanwhile I confess that I greatly prefer the opinion of those who understand it literally of the Holy Spirit’s visible descent in the form of fire, not only upon the Apostles but also upon their recently baptized disciples. A sufficiently brilliant and illustrious example of this occurs in Acts 11:15, where St. Peter says: “And when I had begun to speak, the Holy Spirit fell upon them, as also upon us at the beginning”—and thus also in the form of fire, just as he fell upon the Apostles on the day of Pentecost.

The best and entirely literal sense of St. John the Baptist’s words therefore seems to be this: “I indeed baptize you with water”—a barren element, namely, possessing no power of grace within itself—etc. “But he will baptize you”—not with water alone—“but in the Holy Spirit”—who makes the water of Baptism fruitful for the generation of grace—“and fire,” namely, with the graces of the Holy Spirit which will come upon you in fire through the laying on of hands.

This is that fiery Baptism which Christ himself foretold in Acts 1:5: “John indeed baptized with water, but you shall be baptized with the Holy Spirit not many days hence.” And shortly afterward, v. 8: “You shall receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you,” etc.; and we know that this was fulfilled exactly ten days later, when the days of Pentecost were completed.

[Col. 1176C]

The faith of the water. That is, the faith which is directed toward water, or the Baptism of water, is stable, perseveres firmly, and perpetually bestows what is necessary for our salvation. Or: that water is faithful, keeping its promises, in the sense in which it was said in Isaiah 33:16: “His waters are faithful.” Or, finally, the sense is: only that faith is true and sincere in which we are washed unto salvation, etc.; but a feigned and unstable faith—one that is not serious and will soon be abandoned again—is immersed unto judgment.

Unto judgment. Tertullian seems to have drawn the sense of the text explained at greater length above from St. Paul, 1 Corinthians 3:13ff.:

> The work of each shall be made manifest; for the day of the Lord shall declare it, because it shall be revealed in fire; and the fire shall test what kind of work each has done. If anyone’s work remains—he shall receive a reward; if anyone’s work is burned, he shall suffer loss; but he himself shall be saved, yet so as through fire.

The same thing had been foretold by Isaiah 4:4: “The Lord shall wash away the filth of the daughters of Zion—in the spirit of judgment and the spirit of burning,” or fire. On this passage St. Basil says: “John’s Baptism was a cleansing of filth, but this is regeneration itself through the Holy Spirit; that testing, however, takes place through the fire of judgment.” And in *On the Holy Spirit, to Amphilochius*, ch. 35, he says: “The Baptism of fire is that testing which shall take place at the judgment.”

[Col. 1176D–1177A]

## Notes on Chapter XI

As though in fact. My edition has *because*, certainly with an obscure and corrupt sense; I therefore preferred to restore *as though* from other editions, so that the sentence instead expresses irony.

Had preached. When John said of Christ in Matthew 3:11, “He shall baptize you,” etc., he by no means added “by himself” or “with his own hands.”

He posted, that is, promulgated it or affixed it to the doors of the senate-house, etc. Its sense is surely that he ordered or caused it to be promulgated or affixed—namely, through another.

He is said to do it. According to that common rule of the jurists: “What anyone does through another is deemed to be done by himself.”

[Col. 1177B]

Moreover, *is ministered beforehand* is used by our Author in the same sense in which, in *On Prayer*, ch. 1, he called John Christ’s *pre-minister*, as one going before or ministering in advance. Apuleius likewise, in his *Apology*, called the tongue the pre-minister of lies and bitterness.

Through him. Namely, in his name and by his authority.

Into him. That is, into his power and name, so that you pass into Christ’s name and are henceforth to be called Christians. Thus St. Paul says in Galatians 3:27ff.: “As many of you as have been baptized in Christ”—in Greek, εἰς Χριστόν, “into Christ”—“have put on Christ. … For you are all one in Christ Jesus.” See further discussion in St. Basil’s cited *Homily on Baptism*.

He himself did not immerse. Nevertheless, there are Fathers who plausibly suppose that the Apostles—or at least their chief, Peter—were baptized by Christ’s own hand. Thus St. Augustine, in that very elegant Epistle 108 (now 265) to Seleuciana, *On the Baptism and Repentance of Peter*, says:

> Nor did he fail to perform the ministry of baptizing, so that he might have baptized servants through whom he would baptize others: he who did not fail to perform that memorable ministry of humility when he washed their feet and, when Peter asked that he wash not only his feet but also his hands and head, replied: “He who is washed has no need,” etc.

The entire epistle certainly deserves to be read on this subject.

[Col. 1177C]

He bestowed it by a word. For he absolved the paralytic in Matthew 9:2 and the woman in Luke 7:[48] with no other outward rite, but only with a few simple words, saying: “Your sins are forgiven you.”

He concealed it through humility. Thus, when Christ spoke directly of himself, he never openly called himself the Son of God but “the Son of Man,” as in Matthew 8:20, ch. 9:6, and frequently elsewhere. Hence, when in Luke 4:41 demons came out of many people, crying out and saying, “You are the Son of God,” he rebuked them and did not permit them to speak. And in John 8:54 he said: “If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing.”

They had built. For this was first built by the Apostles after they received the Holy Spirit on the day of Pentecost, when, after Peter’s first preaching, “those who received his word were baptized; and about three thousand souls were added on that day”; Acts 2:41.

With John’s Baptism. Tertullian was therefore of the opinion that before the Passion and Resurrection the Apostles administered only John’s Baptism, while Christ’s Baptism was first instituted afterward, as he affirms more explicitly a little later.

There were certainly Holy Fathers who followed this opinion of Tertullian, especially St. Chrysostom, Homily 28 on chapter 3 of St. John’s Gospel, and likewise St. Leo the Great, who writes in Epistle 4 to the bishops of Sicily:

> The Lord Jesus Christ himself, after he had risen from the dead, gave his disciples—in whom all the prelates of the Churches were being instructed—both the formula and the power of baptizing, saying: “Go, teach all nations,” etc. He could certainly have instructed them about this even before the Passion, had he not wished it to be properly understood that the grace of regeneration began from his Resurrection.

[Col. 1177D–1178A]

Some theologians afterward followed this opinion, and the Most Illustrious Melchior Cano, *On Theological Topics* VIII, ch. 5, strongly inclines toward it.

But St. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* III, q. 66, a. 2, teaches the contrary, following his Augustine, who speaks thus in the cited epistle to Seleuciana:

> Christ baptized, not by himself but through his disciples, whom we understand to have already been baptized, whether with John’s Baptism or, as is more credible, with Christ’s Baptism.

Augustine also affirms the latter absolutely in Tractates 5 and 13 on John and in *On the Origin of the Soul* III, ch. 9. Nor furnished. That is, while the washing with water had not yet been furnished with its own efficacy and power of washing away sins through baptismal grace.

Concerning the danger to the others. The passage seems rather obscure to me, but it may perhaps be somewhat clarified from 1 Timothy 5:21, where the Apostle says:

> I testify before God … that you observe these things “without prejudgment” (χωρὶς προκρίματος), doing nothing “by inclining toward one side” (κατὰ πρόσκλησιν).

Πρόσκλησις properly means advocacy, by which one side is assisted more than another, as occurs in partiality.

[Col. 1178C–D]

It must not be concealed, however, that in many manuscripts, according to Calmet, the reading is πρόσκλισιν, which properly means an inclination or propensity, especially of the mind, and is equally pertinent to our subject. For the passage seems capable of being understood in precisely this sense: the other Apostles were exempted from that common law by a certain antecedent judgment and a singular propensity and favor of mind, so that thereafter their salvation would not be endangered by the absence and lack of Baptism. Thus the prescription, or law, concerning Baptism in John 3:5 never affected the Apostles, since they had already been exempted by God, but thereafter remained immovable only with respect to others.

Yet what need is there for so elaborate a circumlocution? Why might *prejudged* not rather be understood as “foreguarded,” that is, protected by some singular privilege against the danger to salvation?

Be rescinded. That is, for that law to be invalidated and revoked.

It has been established. The force of the entire argument raised against Baptism therefore seems to be this: if the Apostles did not receive Baptism and nevertheless attained salvation, then either they must have been specially exempted by Christ—though we read nowhere in the Gospel of such an exemption—or the gate of salvation also lies open to others who are unbaptized, and thus that law concerning the reception of Baptism possesses no binding force.
