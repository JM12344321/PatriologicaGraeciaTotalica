Moreover, they prayed while looking up to heaven and with their hands, as Tertullian says, outstretched; or, as he puts it in another book: “We, however, not only raise them, but also spread them out, patterned after the Lord’s Passion” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 30, p. 441; idem, *On Prayer*, ch. 11), that is, in the manner and form of Christ affixed to the cross. We have discussed this custom of praying elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius Felix’s Octavius*, ch. 12, art. 3). But because the pagans also used to pray to their gods in the same manner, as we have already shown, our Author therefore added, after the words “with hands outstretched,” “because they are innocent” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, loc. cit.). For the Gentiles raised in prayer hands polluted by impiety, idolatry, and other crimes; [Col. 0943B] Christians, however, raised toward heaven hands no less wholly clean and pure than their breast and mind.

They also prayed, Tertullian further says, with uncovered head, “because we do not blush” (*ibid.*). Here again he contrasts them with the Gentiles, whose custom it was to pray and sacrifice with covered head. Hear, I pray, Virgil’s verses concerning this rite:

> Indeed, when the fleets have crossed the seas,  
> And altars have been set up and you now fulfill your vows upon the shore,  
> Cover your hair, veiled in a purple mantle,  
> Lest amid the sacred fires, in the worship of the gods,  
> Some hostile face appear and disturb everything.  
> Let your companions retain this rite of sacrifice, and retain it yourself;  
> In this observance let your descendants remain pure.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book III, v. 403 ff.)

On these verses Servius makes the following observation: “It must indeed [Col. 0943C] be known that those sacrificing to all the gods customarily covered their heads, so that amid the religious rite something might not present itself to their wandering gaze; with the sole exception of Saturn, lest it appear to be an imitation of the deity, and of Hercules in his own temple, because he himself is represented with covered head, or because these altars were consecrated by Juno and Evander before the arrival of Aeneas” (Servius on the cited verses, p. 292). That exception sufficiently proves this to have been a rite and custom of the Gentiles, which they also observed when saluting and praying to the gods. Brissonius, whom you may consult, has more concerning it (*On Formulae*, book I, p. 140 ff.).

Christians, therefore, on the contrary, prayed with uncovered head, because they knew the Apostle’s saying: “Every man praying or prophesying with his head covered dishonors his head” (Paul, 1 Corinthians 11:4). But someone will ask why Tertullian [Col. 0943D] did not adduce this reason from the Apostle, but instead said, “because we do not blush.” We shall answer that he appears to have given this reason because it was especially suited to his purpose. For there, as we have said, he contrasts Christians with the Gentiles. But the latter, as he adds in the same place, when sacrificing to false gods, as also when praying, brought, after all their pollutions, “even a filthy conscience” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 30, pp. 443, 445), contaminated with every kind of crime; and on its account, had any sense of shame remained in them, they ought perpetually to have been suffused with blushes. Christians, by contrast, offered to their almighty God prayer proceeding “from chaste flesh,” as we noted shortly before (art. above), “from an innocent soul, from the Holy Spirit” [Col. 0944A] (Tertullian, *ibid.*), as we explained at greater length in the preceding dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius Felix’s Octavius*, ch. 12, art. 2; and *On Lactantius*, ch. 33, art. 1). So far were Christians from blushing when they prayed to God in this manner that, as our Author says: “While we are stretched out toward God, let claws tear us, crosses suspend us, flames lick us, swords cut off our throats, beasts leap upon us: the very posture of a Christian at prayer is ready for every punishment” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, loc. cit.). For the Christian was ashamed neither to profess his religion nor to pray to God. And they poured forth their prayers openly and in public synaxes not only with uncovered head but also with body prostrate, as we have shown in another dissertation (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 8, art. 2).

In addition, they prayed facing east, or, as [Col. 0944B] Tertullian puts it, “toward the region of the east” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 16, p. 369). Nor was this custom, which we have discussed elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, diss. 2, ch. 8, art. 7, p. 1040), unknown to the pagans, who were likewise accustomed to pray in the same manner, though in a wholly and utterly perverse way. Hence our Author addresses them in these words: “Many of you, on occasion, with a pretension of worshipping even the heavenly bodies, quiver your lips toward the sunrise” (Tertullian, loc. cit.). He repeats the same statement in the first book *To the Nations* (book I, ch. 13, p. 579). If you seek a witness to this custom, you may hear Virgil singing as follows of Aeneas:

> He rises, and, gazing upon the dawning light  
> Of the ethereal Sun, duly lifts water from the river  
> In his hollow palms and pours forth such words to heaven.  
> [Col. 0944C]  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book VIII, v. 68 ff.)

And in another book, concerning others performing sacrifices:

> They, turning their eyes toward the rising Sun,  
> Give salted grain with their hands, mark with the sword  
> The brows of the beasts, and pour libations from bowls upon the altars.  
> (Ibid., book XII, v. 72 ff.)

Servius, moreover, remarks upon these last verses: “He certainly did not mean that the sun was now rising, for it had long been daylight; rather, he followed the discipline of the ceremonies, so that he might say that one who was about to pray looked toward the east” (Servius, p. 676). Hence Latinus Pacatus, at the beginning of the panegyric that he delivered to Theodosius in his presence, said: “When engaged in divine rites, we turn our faces toward that region of heaven from which light has its beginning” (Pacatus, *Panegyric to Theodosius*, p. 246). Finally, to [Col. 0944D] pass over many others, Ovid sang concerning the goddess Pales:

> With these the goddess must be appeased; facing the sunrise,  
> Say these words four times, and wash your hands in living dew.  
> (Ovid, *Fasti*, book IV, v. 365.)

Yet although this rite was common to Christians and Gentiles, the latter prayed to their gods facing east for reasons different from those for which the former prayed to the one true God. For the Gentiles, as Tertullian notes, did so “with the thoroughly wicked pretension, on occasion, of worshipping even the heavenly bodies” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 16, p. 371; and *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 13, p. 579). Since, therefore, they worshipped heavenly things—namely, the stars and planets—and thought them gods, they consequently turned themselves in prayer toward the sun, [Col. 0945A] the greatest and chief of them all.

Christians, however, prayed in this manner for very different reasons, and chiefly because they ought to look toward paradise, from which they had been expelled, and toward Christ the Lord, the Sun of righteousness. Cardinal Bona has reported several other reasons from various writers; they may readily be read in his books and need not be collected here (Bona, *On the Divine Psalmody*, ch. 6, § 3, p. 177). Nevertheless Origen, as we have observed elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1040), candidly admits that no sufficiently compelling reason for this rite can be offered. Yet no one will easily be persuaded that Christians adopted it for no reason at all, since it was observed with such public and unanimous consent by all that [Col. 0945B] it was not unknown even to the pagans themselves. For from this some of them suspected, as our Author says, that the corporeal sun was the God of the Christians and was worshipped by them. But he crushes this absurd calumny in these few words: “We might perhaps be classed with the Persians, even though the sun we worship is not painted on linen, since we have it everywhere in its own shield” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 16, p. 369). It is well established that the Persians worshipped the sun, which they regarded as the true God. Witnesses to this impiety are Herodotus, Strabo, Justin the historian, and many others, whom, lest we repeatedly treat what others have already treated, we have not thought it necessary to name (Herodotus, book I, § 131; Strabo, *Geography*, book XV, p. 732; Justin, *Histories*, book I, ch. 10). No one, therefore, should think it strange if these worshippers of the sun [Col. 0945C] had it painted upon their shields, since soldiers customarily carried painted or sculpted images upon their shields. Indeed, if we believe Pliny, the very name *clypeus* was derived from this. He adds: “The origin of the custom, full of valor, was that the face of each person who had used a shield should be reproduced upon it. The Carthaginians regularly made both shields and images from gold and carried them with them in camp” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXXV, ch. 3, p. 177). Who, moreover, is unfamiliar with what Homer sang in Greek verse about the shield of Achilles, and Virgil in Latin verse about the shield of Aeneas? (Homer, *Iliad* XVIII, v. 478 ff.; Virgil, *Aeneid*, book VIII, v. 925 ff.). In the same way, therefore, the Persians bore upon a shield the likeness of the sun, which they considered the greatest god.

Grant, you will say, and I concede that this truly was a Persian custom. But how can one infer from it [Col. 0945D] what Tertullian’s true meaning is? Some indeed interpret his words thus: the Persians paint the sun upon linen, whereas Christians have it upon their shield—that is, in that which is the eye of the world and like a shield; or, as others maintain, they behold the face of the sun as though upon a shield. But this explanation, surely more subtle than sound, without doubt never entered Tertullian’s mind. The simpler sense of his words, as is clear from the foregoing observations, appears to be this: the pagans falsely supposed that the sun was worshipped by Christians as it was by the Persians. For the Persians everywhere had upon their shields the fashioned and represented image of their sun-god, whereas Christians did not even have it painted anywhere upon linen.

## ARTICLE IV

### Why Christians joyfully kept the Lord’s Day, which the pagans had dedicated to the Sun, as a feast, and thus differed at only one point from the Gentiles and Jews who solemnly observed the Sabbath day

[Col. 0946A]

Another and surely no less serious error belonged to the Gentiles, who further supposed that Christians worshipped the sun as God because they joyfully observed the Lord’s Day, which the pagans had dedicated to the Sun. Tertullian therefore refutes and chastises them as follows: “Likewise, if we devote the day of the Sun—that is, the Lord’s Day—to gladness, we do so for a reason far different from religion or worship of the Sun” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 16, p. 371). He does not, indeed, state what that other reason was for which they celebrated the day gladly and [Col. 0946B] even festively. But Barnabas has supplied it for us: “For this reason,” he says, “we observe the eighth day—that is, the Lord’s Day—with gladness, the day on which Jesus both rose from the dead and, after appearing, ascended into heaven” (Epistle of Barnabas, § 15, p. 16). Justin Martyr beautifully confirms this, for, no less than Barnabas and our Tertullian, he openly declares that this day on which Christians customarily assembled was called the day of the Sun: “[non-Latin text omitted] on the day called the Sun’s day” (Justin, *First Apology*). It is designated by the same name in various laws of Constantine the Great, Valentinian the Younger, and Theodosius, upon which Gothofredus and others elsewhere have made many observations pertinent to our subject (Theodosian Code, laws 1 and 2, *On Holidays*, p. 118 ff.).

Sometimes it was also called “the Lord’s Day,” [Col. 0946C] “the Lord’s solemnities,” or simply “the Lord’s.” For our Tertullian left it written: “How shall we celebrate the Lord’s solemnities?” (Tertullian, *On Flight*, final chapter). Cyprian, moreover, wrote in his letter to the presbyters, deacons, and entire people: “Meanwhile he read for us on the Lord’s Day—that is, he inaugurated peace while dedicating the reading” (Cyprian, epistle 38, p. 57). And what, I ask you, is this reading other than that which, as we shall say in the following chapter, customarily took place in the public assemblies of Christians? But if you desire something clearer, hear Eusebius, who recorded that this custom of celebrating the Lord’s Day with a solemn rite derived from Christ the Lord: “[non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0946D] [non-Latin text omitted]. He commanded that, assembling together each week, they should keep the Lord’s Day as a feast and should not fatten their bodies but refresh their minds with divine precepts” (Eusebius, *Oration in Praise of Constantine*, ch. 17, p. 664). Valesius thus translates this passage of Eusebius; he does not doubt that, through a copyist’s carelessness, the particle [non-Latin text omitted] was omitted before the word [non-Latin text omitted], because [non-Latin text omitted] is not appropriate to Christians. Later Epiphanius committed the following to writing concerning the same celebration of the Lord’s Day: “[non-Latin text omitted]. The catholic Church has appointed every Lord’s Day festive and joyful, announces solemn assemblies in the morning [Col. 0947A], and observes no fasts” (Epiphanius, *Exposition of the Faith*, § 22, p. 105). Elsewhere he tells us that this custom originated with the apostles (idem, *Heresy of Arius*, § 6, p. 910). There is plainly no need to accumulate more concerning a matter known to everyone. It is enough to have proved to all that this day was rightly and deservedly instituted in honor of Christ, who was raised from the dead on this very day.

Tertullian therefore continues: “We stand next to those who assign Saturn’s day to idleness and feasting, though they too deviate from the Jewish custom, which they do not understand” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 15, p. 357). But he explained this more fully and clearly elsewhere, where he addresses the Gentiles in these words: “You assuredly are those who have even admitted the Sun into the register of the seven days and have selected its day from among them [Col. 0947B] as the day on which you refrain from bathing, or postpone it until evening, or attend to leisure or dining. You do this while yourselves deviating from your own religions toward others. For Jewish are the feasts, the Sabbaths, the ‘pure meal,’ the Jewish rite of lamps, the fasts with unleavened bread, and prayers on the shores—all of which are certainly alien to your gods. Therefore, to return from the digression: you who reproach us with the Sun and its day, acknowledge the proximity. We are not far removed from your Saturn and Sabbaths” (idem, *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 13, p. 579). But it is not easy to say whether these words, “prayers on the shores,” are genuine and uncorrupted (Philo Judaeus, *Against Flaccus*, p. 992D). For if Tertullian wrote them, it is still more difficult to say what they signify, and no one has attempted to explain them in his notes. Did our Author perhaps have in view certain [Col. 0947C] prayers of the Jews, which they customarily poured forth upon the banks of rivers or fountains before washing in their waters? He rebukes the Gentiles, however, because they charged Christians as a fault with keeping the Lord’s Day as a feast. For the Christians did not differ greatly from the Sabbaths of the Jews and the rite of the Gentiles themselves, who had also named one day of the week after the sun.

Moreover, as Philo Judaeus writes, although individual peoples and nations lived under their own distinct laws, there was nevertheless none that did not honor “[non-Latin text omitted] that sacred day recurring each week, which brings relief from labor and a holiday to the head of the household with his family, [Col. 0947D] not only the freeborn but also slaves, and even beasts of burden” (Philo, *Life of Moses*, book I, near the beginning, p. 657). We have another witness of the same nation in Josephus, who similarly declared: “[non-Latin text omitted]. There is no city of Greeks or barbarians, and no nation, to which the observance of the seventh day, on which we rest, has not extended, together with fasts and lighted lamps” (Josephus, *Against Apion*, book II, p. 1081). Finally, we have observed elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, diss. 2, ch. 16, art. 1, p. 1117) that Clement of Alexandria and Eusebius prove by testimonies from various writers that the seventh [Col. 0948A] day of the week was sacred to Jews and Greeks.

It ought therefore to be certain to everyone from what has thus far been said, first, that Christians celebrated the day of the Sun, or Lord’s Day, for a reason and with a rite far different from those of Jews and Gentiles, of whom Lactantius also speaks, whatever some have said; and secondly, that Christians devoted this day to gladness—spiritual gladness, of course—because they gathered from every direction, as we have noted elsewhere from Justin Martyr (*ibid.*, p. 23), into the same place and held their sacred synaxis in the manner we have described. But our Tertullian has transmitted more concerning it, which must now be explained.

## CHAPTER XV

### On the Sacred Synaxes of Christians

## ARTICLE I

### For what reason Christians entered sacred assemblies; concerning the prayers poured forth there, and especially for the postponement of the end; concerning the reading of Sacred Scripture and exhortations; concerning the presidents—approved elders who obtained their honor not by any payment but by testimony

[Col. 0948B]

There was plainly nothing in the entire discipline and customs of Christians that the pagans assailed with harsher calumnies than their synaxes and solemn assemblies for celebrating the sacred mysteries. Tertullian therefore judged that the shameless audacity of their slanderers could be utterly restrained by no stronger argument than a sincere and straightforward exposition of what took place in those assemblies. He first explains [Col. 0948C] the end for which Christians gathered: “We come together,” he says, “into an assembly and congregation so that, as though forming a body of troops, we may surround God with prayers as we pray” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 467). Although these words occur verbatim in all our manuscripts and ancient editions, they appeared too lengthy and perhaps too obscure to Rigault. Therefore, contrary to the evidence of the older manuscript that he himself used, he wished only the following to be retained in the text: “We come together to God so that, as though forming a body of troops, we may surround him with prayers.” Yet he gives not even the slightest reason why he deleted so many words against the unanimous agreement of the manuscripts. Was Rigault, then, permitted to correct the book of so ancient a writer as though it were his own, according to his whim, and to remove and amputate from it whatever he pleased? Tertullian said, “We come together into an assembly and congregation,” [Col. 0948D] just as Cicero, after Terence, said, “We come together into the Piraeus” (Cicero, book VII, epistle 3 to Atticus, p. 275, line 8; Terence, *Eunuch*, act III, scene 4, v. 1), while Virgil sang:

> . . . All the valor of the allies comes together as one.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book X, v. 410.)

Thus assembled, Christians, as though forming a body of troops, surrounded and encompassed God from every side, so that, praying together, they might bend his divine mercy by their prayers. Nor did they exercise against him an unwelcome force: “For this force,” says our Author, “is pleasing to God” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 467)—the force by which Christians, through the united prayers of all, compelled him, as it were by a kind of violence, to lay down the fearsome weapons [Col. 0949A] of justice and not deny them the requested gifts of divine mercy.

They prayed in this manner not only for themselves but also, as he further says, “for the emperors, for their ministers, and for the authorities” (Tertullian, *ibid.*)—that is, for magistrates and governors, whether of cities or provinces, and for all whose service they employed in governing peoples; “for the condition of the age,” that is, of the empire, so that it might be firm and stable and not shaken by wars or seditions; and “for the peace of affairs,” so that all might always enjoy the desirable blessing of peace. For Christians knew what the Apostle had commanded: that “supplications, prayers, petitions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, for kings [Col. 0949B] and all who are in high station, that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life in all piety and chastity” (1 Timothy 2:1–2). We have discussed elsewhere these prayers that Christians customarily poured forth for themselves and others in their synaxes (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 8, art. 2).

It is therefore worth observing here only what Tertullian added in the same place: that in these synaxes Christians further prayed to God “for the postponement of the end” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 467). What end, I ask you, was this if not that of the whole world, which they believed would be destroyed with the fall of the Roman Empire (idem, *To Scapula*, ch. 2)? Although this opinion, about which something must be said later (below, ch. 21, art. 1), is altogether false, from it we nevertheless understand [Col. 0949C] that Christians prayed for the continuance of the Roman Empire as often as they prayed for the delaying of the world’s end. For they had become thoroughly convinced—wrongly, indeed—that “the final termination of the age,” to use the very words of our Author, “is delayed by the continuance of the Roman Empire” (idem, *Apologeticus*, ch. 32, p. 447).

But as false as these things are, so true are those practices of the sacred synaxes of Christians that he records as follows: “We come together for the commemoration of the divine writings” (*ibid.*, ch. 39, p. 34), that is, for their reading and recitation. For in this assembly of the faithful, as Justin Martyr says: “[non-Latin text omitted] [non-Latin text omitted]. The memoirs of the apostles, or the writings of the apostles, [Col. 0949D] are read for as long as time permits” (Justin, *First Apology*, no. 67); or, as Tertullian says: “if the character of the present times”—in which the pagans were raging against Christians with such cruelty—“compels us either to be forewarned or to review” (Tertullian, loc. cit.). By these sacred words they nourished faith, raised up hope, and established their confidence and assurance. By repeatedly reading and hearing the precepts of conduct handed down in them, they also made their discipline and manner of living rightly more steadfast.

The reading of Sacred Scripture was followed by “exhortations,” says our Author. Earlier Justin Martyr had said: “[non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0950A] [non-Latin text omitted]. Then, when the reader has ceased, the president delivers an admonition by which he urges and exhorts the others to imitate such beautiful things” (Justin, *ibid.*). Thus he relates more explicitly what these exhortations were, by whom they were given, and for what reason.

These, Tertullian continues, were immediately followed by “rebukes and divine censure” (Tertullian, *ibid.*), which Justin Martyr omitted entirely, either for brevity’s sake or because they contributed little to his purpose. Origen, however, expressly declares first that the Christians of his own time devoted every effort to filling their assemblies and synaxes with intelligent persons, to whom everything excellent and divine that we possess was explained in popular sermons [Col. 0950B] and according to each person’s capacity (Origen, *Against Celsus*, book III).

Tertullian then immediately adds that those who were stained by sins, and especially by lust, were utterly expelled from those same assemblies (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 469). There were three classes of sins and crimes, as we shall show in the proper place, on account of which a sentence of excommunication was passed against the guilty, following the Apostle’s example (Paul, 1 Corinthians 5:3 ff.), and they were barred from entering the church. Nor could they be absolved until they had performed public penance for these crimes.

Beforehand, however, their sins had to be examined and ascertained with the greatest diligence. Hence, our Author says, “Judgment is conducted with great gravity, as among those who are certain of [Col. 0950C] the sight of God; and it is the supreme anticipation of the future judgment if anyone has sinned in such a way that he is banished from participation in prayer, assembly, and every holy fellowship” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 469). With the utmost care, therefore—a care greater than which there could be none—they investigated what the sins of the accused were and of what kind, and passed sentence upon those convicted as though they had God, the supreme judge of all, before their eyes. Such a sentence was therefore the greatest anticipation of the judgment to be pronounced by God at the very end of the world. For, as Cyprian later wrote to Pomponius: “Among the faithful servants of God, the proud and contumacious are slain with the spiritual sword of excommunication when they are cast out of the Church. For they cannot live outside, since the house of God is one, and no one can have salvation except in the Church” (Cyprian, [Col. 0950D] epistle 5, to Pomponius).

Tertullian continues: “The approved elders preside over these assemblies of Christians” (Tertullian, loc. cit.). He does not explain to us who these presiding elders were; just as here he calls them by this name alone, so in another passage, where he discusses the same synaxes, he calls them simply “presidents”: “The sacrament of the Eucharist,” he says, “both at the time of meals and as commanded to all by the Lord, we receive even at assemblies before dawn, and from the hand of none but the presidents” (idem, *On the Soldier’s Crown*, ch. 3). Justin Martyr likewise, as we saw shortly before, calls the president of each synaxis only [non-Latin text omitted]. But Tertullian himself, when discussing the administration of sacred baptism conferred in these same synaxes, [Col. 0951A] openly indicates who these presidents were. There we read his very words: “The chief priest, who is the bishop, has the right to confer baptism; then the presbyters and deacons, yet not without the bishop’s authorization, for the sake of the Church’s honor, by whose preservation peace is preserved” (idem, *On Baptism*, ch. 17). Bishops, therefore, presided over these and other synaxes of Christians, and, by their authority, presbyters and deacons.

Why, however, were they called “elders” by him? This may be learned from Cyprian’s words: “Long since, throughout every province and in each individual city, bishops have been ordained who are advanced in age, sound in faith, approved under oppression, and proscribed in persecution” (Cyprian, epistle 55 to Antonianus). You see there that bishops are called advanced in age, [Col. 0951B] and therefore elders. Did Tertullian not, then, designate by that name the bishops, at least very many of whom in his time were elders and advanced in years? Moreover, Cyprian says that they were “approved under oppression,” that is, approved through tortures steadfastly endured for the faith of Christ. Yet in another sense our Author calls them simply “approved”: namely, approved and elected by the suffrage of others because of the holiness of their lives. For he added in the same place: “They obtained this honor not by payment but by testimony. For no thing of God has a price” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 469 ff.). No one, we think, will deny that by the words “this honor” Tertullian designates the sacred orders of God’s ministers, which ought to be conferred upon approved and elected men for no payment whatsoever, however slight, but upon the certain and weighty testimony of religious [Col. 0951C] persons. If you seek the reason, receive it from Ambrose, in words entirely consonant with those of Tertullian: “You have the Lord’s command and the example of the prophet Elisha punishing Gehazi: to give ministry freely and not to sell it, but to offer it; for God’s grace is not assessed at a price, nor should one seek profit in the sacraments, but the service of the priest” (Ambrose, *Exposition of Luke*, book IV, § 52). And shortly afterward: “For the guilt of a ministry and grace sold is inexpiable: heavenly vengeance passes to one’s descendants” (*ibid.*, § 53). Later he confirms this by the example of Simon Magus (*ibid.*, book IX, § 19), from whom simony—so often condemned by the canons of the Church and the writings of the holy Fathers—received its name.

## ARTICLE II

### On the contribution that Christians offered in their synaxes; where it was deposited; whether their charity had grown cold in Tertullian’s time; and to whom and for what uses their gifts were distributed

[Col. 0951D]

Although in Tertullian’s time every simoniacal stain was utterly banished from the Church, nevertheless in that house to which Christians customarily flocked to hold their synaxes there was deposited “a kind of chest,” as he says, containing “no dishonorable sum, as though religion had been purchased” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 469). Thus indeed the text reads in two of our older manuscripts. But in two more recent Colbert manuscripts it reads “not from an honorarium.” [Col. 0952A] In old and some more recent printed editions it reads “not a burdensome sum.” Worst of all, in Pamelius’s edition, it reads “not an ordinary sum.” For, as will shortly be said, that sum was brought at least every month. Rigault therefore preferred the first reading to the others, both because it occurs in the oldest manuscript and because he thinks the word “dishonorable” signifies a sum that was neither venal nor, as Tertullian says, the price by which religion was purchased, and thus involved no dishonor. The two words “from an honorarium” were therefore less correctly fashioned by copyists from this word, unless by them you wish it to be signified that Christians, who utterly abhorred pagan ostentation, did not give this sum in pursuit of any human honor. But if you judge that the reading should be “not a burdensome sum”—one, namely, that was neither troublesome nor burdensome to Christians—this [Col. 0952B] reading can be supported no less than Rigault’s by Tertullian’s following words: “Each person deposits a modest contribution on the monthly day, or whenever he wishes, and only if he wishes and only if he is able. For no one is compelled; each contributes voluntarily” (*ibid.*). Thus the modest sum of money that each person offered according to his pleasure and means could carry neither great honor nor great burden. When he added that it was “not as though religion had been purchased” (idem, *ibid.*, chs. 13 and 42), by these words he censured the pagans, who, as he himself says and as we shall show below, held all sacred things for sale and demanded payment for them.

Tertullian next attests that this sum or contribution was given by Christians “on the monthly day” (*ibid.*, ch. 39, p. 469), that is, once each [Col. 0952C] month. Marcian explains a “monthly contribution” thus: “Those of limited means are permitted to contribute a monthly payment, provided that they assemble only once a month; the deified Severus also declared by rescript that this was to have application not in the City but in Italy and the provinces” (*Digest*, book XLVII, title 22, law 1). Tertullian was writing these words, as noted above, during the reign of this same Severus.

What then, someone will ask: had the charity of Christians grown cold at that time? Had the Apostle not written to the Corinthians: “Concerning the collections made for the saints, as I directed the churches of Galatia, so do you likewise. On the first day of the week, let each of you set something aside, storing up whatever has pleased him, so that collections may not be made only when I come” (Paul, 1 Corinthians 16:1–2)? Nor does he speak only there, but in almost [Col. 0952D] all his epistles, says Jerome, and command the churches of the Gentiles that every first day of the week—that is, on the Lord’s Day—all should contribute what is to be sent to Jerusalem for the relief of the saints (Jerome, book *Against Vigilantius*). The Apostle therefore commanded that these collections be made every Lord’s Day and that the money be deposited with certain persons until it was sent to the poor and to other wretched and needy persons. Justin Martyr also committed to writing that this custom was observed in his time, and that at each synaxis assembled on the day of the Sun, or Lord’s Day: “[non-Latin text omitted]. Those who possess more abundant means and are willing contribute, each according to his own judgment, whatever he thinks fit, and what is thus collected [Col. 0953A] is deposited with the president” (Justin Martyr, *First Apology*, no. 67).

If, therefore, in Tertullian’s age Christians disbursed this contribution only on a monthly day, and a modest one at that, had their compassion and charity not, as we noted before (above, ch. 12, art. 3), been considerably diminished and weakened? Nor does Cyprian, whom we cited there, conceal this fact; he complains as follows about the rather hard heart of a Christian matron toward the poor and wretched: “You are wealthy and rich, and you think that you celebrate the Lord’s Day, though you take no notice whatever of the contribution-box, though you come to the Lord’s assembly without a sacrifice, though you take a portion of the sacrifice that the poor person offered” (Cyprian, *On Works and Almsgiving*). Yet although some persons at that time, seized by the vice of avarice, contributed nothing on that day, many [Col. 0953B] nevertheless bestowed numerous gifts for distribution to the poor, not only on the monthly day but, as Tertullian says, whenever they wished. For there he declares that Christian liberality was so great that “the exercise of such very great love brands us, in the eyes of certain persons”—namely, the Gentiles—“with the reproach, without doubt, of an excessive outpouring of money” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 471).

How great that generosity and beneficence of Christians was you may further learn from Tertullian’s following words. He openly affirms that the money, “as a deposit of piety,” was disbursed “for feeding and burying the needy; for boys and girls destitute of means and parents; for elderly persons now confined to the household; likewise for shipwrecked persons; and if any are in the mines”—that is, condemned to the mines—“if any are on [Col. 0953C] islands”—that is, banished to islands—“or in custody,” that is, in prisons, “provided it is for the cause of God’s sect, they become dependents of their confession” (*ibid.*). That is, adherents of the Christian religion who had suffered these penalties for confessing it were supported with money liberally collected by Christians. Hence he begins his book *To the Martyrs* as follows: “Among the nourishment for the flesh, blessed designated martyrs, which both Lady Mother Church supplies to you in prison from her breasts and individual brothers from their own resources, accept something also from us that may serve to nurture the spirit as well” (idem, *To the Martyrs*, ch. 1). And shortly afterward: “The flesh does not lose what is its due, through the care of the Church and the love-feast of the brethren” (*ibid.*, ch. 2). Earlier Justin Martyr, whom we quoted, had said that with this money the president, with whom it had been deposited, assisted “[non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0953D] [non-Latin text omitted] orphans and widows, those in need because of illness or some other cause, those who are in chains, and strangers arriving from abroad” (Justin, *First Apology*, no. 67).
