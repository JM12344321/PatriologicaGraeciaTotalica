## ARTICLE III. On the Scholia, Paraphrases, Notes, and Observations of Various Authors on Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*

[Col. 0752D]

Many writers distinguished by varied learning, who devoted their attention to publishing Tertullian’s books or were engaged in some other undertaking, attempted by means of their observations, scholia, paraphrases, and commentaries either to explain and elucidate, or to confirm, the more obscure or particularly noteworthy passages of that *Apologeticum*. First, Beatus Rhenanus prefixed to his edition of this book an outline, though one far briefer than was fitting. He thought, however, that this brevity could be remedied by adding new chapter summaries, as they are called, through which he endeavored to correct others presented in the manuscripts. In addition, in that same—albeit very brief—outline of the book, [Col. 0753A] he attempted to cast some light upon a very few of its obscure passages.

Afterward François Zephyrus, having observed how useful this book could be to everyone, and wishing to prevent young students from being deterred from reading it by the obscurity of its language and by passages corrupted through scribal negligence, composed and published a paraphrase of it. In this paraphrase—which is indeed rather awkwardly intermingled with Tertullian’s text—he endeavors to explain several passages difficult to understand and to remove various impediments caused by obscurity. Moreover, Pamelius valued this paraphrase so highly that he judged it should be inserted into our Author’s text in precisely the same manner as in the preceding edition.

Nevertheless, this very paraphrase was wholly and completely rejected by others who subsequently presided over various editions [Col. 0753B] of this book, and especially by Luis de la Cerda. But lest anyone should ever be able to complain of this omission, that learned man published a new interpretation of the Tertullianic text. He also appended his own notes to the individual chapters. Although otherwise deserving of praise, these notes make it difficult to read the text itself without continually interrupting the reader’s attention. But many also complain of certain useless and overly lengthy notes and of digressions foreign to Tertullian’s purpose. Others, nevertheless, praise them and rank them above all others published thus far. They therefore find fault with them only because he did not correct Tertullian’s text against manuscript codices; because he did not publish all his works; and because, in keeping with his learning, he did not explain and confirm [Col. 0753C] the teachings of the Christian religion transmitted by him.

After Heraldus published this *Apologeticum* of Tertullian, he appended a book which he calls a commentary, though he composed it solely of notes entirely disconnected from one another. To avoid straying too far in them from Tertullian’s purpose, however, he added two books of digressions, in which he strives to smooth out Tertullian’s difficult and rugged passages. In all of these the author’s secular learning can be discerned, but one would look in vain for an explanation of the sacred and more obscure dogmas of the Christian religion transmitted by Tertullian. He also left untouched several other matters that undoubtedly required explanation and confirmation.

[Col. 0753D] Laurent de la Barre likewise published many observations on all of Tertullian’s works, which he states that he excerpted from those in which Beraldus, Alciatus, Crinitus, Turnebus, Vetranius, Homanus, Cujacius, and others attempted in scattered places to explain certain expressions of Tertullian that were difficult to understand. But because these matters pertain to grammar, he there appended Rhenanus’s notes, which he indicates that he had first corrected himself, since they had not escaped the censure of the Romans and Spaniards. But because he added very few of his own, and those extremely brief, and gave scarcely any to this *Apologeticum*, one can readily see what should be expected from him on theological matters.

Pamelius’s annotations on this [Col. 0754A] and Tertullian’s other books are therefore held to be of greater value. For, as they say, they are sprinkled with no small measure of sacred and secular learning, which can be useful to readers. Yet they fault them because Pamelius too frequently departs from his subject, amassed much that is useless, and, by interposing Zephyrus’s paraphrase, as we have said, interrupted Tertullian’s text, which in this and his other books he ought, with the aid of better codices, to have restored, if possible, to its original integrity.

Rigault therefore scorned and set aside all of these and wrote new notes and observations on this *Apologeticum* and Tertullian’s other works. Yet because he omitted, altogether without justification, very many observations originating with learned men, these were again made public in a single volume [Col. 0754B] published in 1635. They were collected from Pamelius, Latino Latini, Beatus Rhenanus, Jean Mercier, Edmond Richer, Théodore Marcile, Johann Wouwer, and Gabriel de L’Aubespine, Bishop of Orléans.

Certain learned men were persuaded that, if this volume were joined to Rigault’s published edition of Tertullian’s works, the latter would be more perfect than all the rest. But aside from the fact that this volume of notes omits the no less useful notes and explanations of others, such as La Cerda, what, I ask you, can be expected of Rigault, who, in correcting Tertullian’s text, took care neither to read nor to follow either the very manuscript codices he had at hand, as we have said (preceding Article), or any others? Moreover, in writing his notes he displayed no small [Col. 0754C] audacity, which he was compelled to excuse in his preface. But one would find very few people who find his excuse wholly acceptable.

Less rash, indeed, but no more approved are the extremely long observations heaped up to the point of nausea by Georges d’Amiens. For into them he thrust everything that he had gathered for another purpose and, it appears, for public sermons. Some nevertheless admit that they contain useful material, especially in the chapter scholia that he presents on the page facing Tertullian’s text.

Finally, Le Prieur selected various observations by learned men—Rhenanus, Pamelius, La Cerda, Salmasius, L’Aubespine, and certain others—and from time to time interspersed among them [Col. 0754D] some devised by himself. But these are of such little importance that scarcely anyone can be found who has approved them. Nevertheless, he deserves some measure of praise for having all these observations and notes printed in the lower margins of the pages without any interruption of Tertullian’s text. For in this way better provision was made for the reader’s convenience. But who will ever pardon the fault of negligence in a man who, undertaking to reprint Tertullian’s works in new type, compared no manuscript codex whatsoever with the printed editions, but followed Rigault’s edition alone, word for word?

From what we have thus far discussed concerning so many and such varied editions of Tertullian’s works, [Col. 0755A] who will not admit with us that there is absolutely none that can deservedly be called perfect and complete in every respect? He will therefore undoubtedly render good service to the republic of letters who, being thoroughly imbued with sacred and secular learning, prepares a new edition of the *Apologeticum* and Tertullian’s other works. But before all else, its text must, with the utmost fidelity and diligence, be reviewed and corrected against French, Italian, German, and English manuscript codices and the early editions. For there still survive several codices of excellent quality, at least of the *Apologeticum*, which no one has thus far compared with the printed editions. But if he faithfully presents the variant and noteworthy readings excerpted from them, it will be possible to render a more certain judgment concerning Tertullian’s true and genuine text.

# CHAPTER IV. On Tertullian’s Two Books *To the Nations*

[Col. 0755B]

## ARTICLE I. Analysis of the First Book *To the Nations*

In the two books *To the Nations*, Tertullian once again pleaded the cause of the Christians, and he quite clearly divided each book into two parts. In the first part of the first book, he shows that Christians, though neither heard nor permitted a defense, were harassed, condemned, and most cruelly killed by the gentiles on the basis of the falsest and most unjust prejudgments (Book I, ch. 1 and following). In the second part, however, he makes it plain to everyone that the crimes of which Christians were accused were entirely false and that the gentiles themselves were in reality guilty of them (ch. 9 [Col. 0755C] and following). In the first part of the second book, he demonstrates how empty and absurd is the threefold theology of the pagans devised by Varro, which he calls physical, mythical, and popular or gentile (Book II, ch. 1 and following). In the second part, however, he demonstrates that the gods of the Romans are false and entirely fictitious (ch. 9 and following). But these matters must be explained somewhat more clearly.

First, therefore, he censures the pagans because they persecuted Christians with implacable hatred, although they were utterly ignorant of who and what Christians were and had never wished to know. For as soon as they had come to know them, they immediately ceased to hate them and willingly embraced the Christian religion. Nor could this be denied, as their daily complaint concerning the multiplied number of Christians loudly proclaimed. Our Author does not permit it to be objected that they had changed for the worse, as sometimes happens. For the profession of the Christian religion bears no mark of evil: no one is either ashamed of it or regrets it, and everyone rejoices and glories in it (Book I, ch. 1).

But if Christians had committed any crimes, the pagans surely ought to have compelled them by interrogation and torture to disclose them. Yet, on the contrary, by immediately believing those who either voluntarily denied their designation or were forced to deny it, they also had to admit that nothing evil could be found in their religion. Furthermore, judges were bound by their own laws to question those accused of any crimes concerning what, of what kind, and how serious those crimes were, [Col. 0756A] who their accomplices and harborers were, and finally to state these matters expressly in the written sentence of the condemned. But nothing of the kind was ordinarily done in the condemnation of Christians, nor was anything pronounced in their sentence except the name “Christian.” The name alone, therefore, was the crime; yet this very name signifies nothing barbarous, ill-omened, or dishonorable, but either anointing or at least sweetness or goodness, since even the gentiles corruptly called them *Chrestians* (chs. 2–3).

But those who alleged that they hated the Christian sect solely because of its founder’s name were plainly witless (ch. 4). For they did not hate the Platonists or Pythagoreans because of the name of Plato or Pythagoras, nor physicians because of the name of Erasistratus, nor grammarians because of that of Aristarchus. Who also does not know that it is wrong to hate any sect unless one first observes [Col. 0756B] what there is in it deserving hatred? Yet the pagans neither knew the Christian sect or its founder nor wished to learn about them. Moreover, according to their own practice, any pagan was permitted to embrace whichever philosophical sect he wished, even one hostile to the state and its rulers. They also harassed only those philosophers who, by teaching the truth in part, spurned their gods. Thus indeed they condemned Socrates, although he had been proclaimed by Apollo’s oracle the wisest of all mortals. It is therefore no wonder if the truth engendered their hatred of Christians. But observe, I ask, the frenzy of their deranged minds. They admitted that all who had renounced the worship of false gods and enrolled themselves in the Christian religion had become better people. Yet for this name alone [Col. 0756C] they raged more cruelly against them and preferred them to be guilty and wicked rather than Christian.

The pagans, however, objected that some Christians were thoroughly wicked and ruined by vices and crimes (ch. 5). But these, Tertullian says, were very few; and, like blemishes on a body, they thereby showed the excellence of their sect. Whoever they were, and however many, they—just like philosophers who disgraced their own sect by shameful deeds—were true deserters, Christians in name alone, indeed unworthy of that name.

The gentiles insisted that the Christian sect was truly evil, since it had been condemned by the emperors’ laws (ch. 6). But did not, our Author replies, the same just laws of the emperors and others establish that no one was ever to be condemned unless he had first [Col. 0756D] been duly convicted of some crime? Why, then, did the pagans condemn Christians without either hearing or convicting them? Were not many exceedingly unjust laws likewise enacted by the lawgivers of those same pagans, and afterward amended or rendered obsolete? Finally, an unjust law, such as that enacted against Christians, requires no honor or obedience.

Nor could the pagans insist that the laws against Christians had been enacted by men who had learned of their crimes through common and popular report. For is not rumor, by the admission of those same pagans, an evil than which none is swifter? Yet it lives only so long as it remains uncertain, false, and mendacious. For where a matter is certain, there rumor ceases. Its origin must therefore be investigated, and [Col. 0757A] one must await time, which reveals all things. But from the very beginning of the Christian religion, who ever discovered, exposed, or disclosed the infanticides, incest, and other crimes alleged against Christians in their assemblies? Was it the Christians themselves? But they concealed their mysteries in profound silence, nor could they, had they been conscious of such horrendous crimes, disclose them without endangering their lives. Was it the gentiles? Certainly not. For although they kept Christians under daily surveillance in their assemblies, no one ever discovered these things, nor did any member of their households, corrupted by money, ever testify that he had either seen or concealed them.

Moreover, Christians promised eternal happiness to their followers who diligently observed Christ’s laws. But tell me, I ask, could it appear credible to anyone not utterly insane [Col. 0757B] that it had ever been promised to those who perpetrated such great crimes? No gentile endowed with even the slightest sense of humanity would ever have dared to commit them. Therefore neither would any Christians, who shared the same nature as other human beings and were neither Cynopenae, Sciapods, nor Antipodes (ch. 7).

Who would not laugh at the pagans, who endeavored to prove that Christians were later than the Phrygians and other nations by the ridiculous tale of a boy reared by a mute nurse and uttering, as the first sound of his voice, βέκκοις (“bekkois,” interpreted as “bread”)? For who does not know that the Christian people, which derived its origin from the Jews, is more ancient than the others? Yet even if it were more recent than others, it was nevertheless a third nation after the Greeks and Romans, and was not addicted to the most inhuman superstitions (ch. 8).

[Col. 0757C] The pagans insisted that Christians, because of their contempt for the gods, were the authors of every disaster and must therefore be exterminated (ch. 9). But by various examples Tertullian demonstrates that the world had been disturbed and overwhelmed by the gravest calamities—such as inundations of water, fire from heaven, and the universal flood—before the pagans themselves had fashioned their gods and before the cities in which those gods were born, dwelt, and buried had been founded. What of the fact that afterward these eminent gods could not rescue their worshippers from other disasters?

In the second part of this book, our Author shows that the gentiles themselves were conscious of the very crimes of which they vainly attempted to accuse Christians. [Col. 0757D] For those who unjustly complained that the Christians had repudiated the ancient but impious rites had themselves departed from their ancestors’ laws, institutions, and customs in worship, diet, and even speech, and had rejected the antiquities that they could never praise highly enough.

Furthermore, Christians could not have despised gods whom they knew not to exist. The pagans, however, utterly spurned those whom they advertised as true gods and claimed to worship. For after scorning and repudiating many, they honored only certain ones selected by themselves. Were not the god Alburnus, Liber, Serapis, Harpocrates, and Anubis also contemptuously expelled by the senate, and their statues overthrown? What could be more insulting to the gods than that they could not be worshipped [Col. 0758A] without a decree of the senate? What more ignominious than for any private individual to pawn his household gods, while public gods were auctioned beneath the spear? What more shameful than to charge a fee for the temple site, its approach, doorposts, and doors? What greater disgrace to the gods than to consecrate an old man in Saturn, a beardless youth in Apollo, a soldier in Mars, and a smith in Vulcan; than to make dead men equal to the greatest gods; than to punish those who swore falsely by the gods, but not those who did so by Caesar’s genius; or than to sacrifice to them all the worst parts of the victims? Finally, what could be more unworthy of the gods’ majesty than for Homer and other poets to sing with impunity of Venus wounded, Mars bound, Jupiter assailed by other gods, weeping, indulging his lust, and enumerating his own adulteries? Why say more? Were not these same gods mocked [Col. 0758B] in the theaters and in the comedies of the Lentuli and Hostii, where, to universal applause, mimes and actors played the utterly ridiculous part of Cybele’s sighing shepherd, or sang of Jupiter’s glories and disgraces? Were not the gods defamed by depraved men who in the arena represented Attis castrated, Hercules burned, Dis conducting the gladiators’ funerals, and Mercury examining the dead? Who, then, will deny that gods of this sort were far more despised and mocked by the very gentiles who fashioned them than by Christians (ch. 10)?

Come now: the pagans, deceived by the mendacious Tacitus, falsely asserted that Christians worshipped the head of an ass (ch. 11). But the gentiles themselves actually worshipped whole asses together with their Epona, and consecrated [Col. 0758C] beasts together with their mangers. By what reasoning, moreover, did they charge Christians with a crime for worshipping Christ’s cross, when they themselves revered the Attic Pallas and Pharian Ceres, whose forms did not differ from the upright beam of a cross? Did they not also place their gods upon part of an upright cross, while upon complete crosses they placed figures of Victory, *cantabra*, *siphara*, and military standards, which they held in the highest honor (ch. 12)?

Some of them wrongly supposed that Christians worshipped the Sun because they prayed to their true God with their faces turned toward that same Sun and indulged in spiritual rejoicing on the Lord’s Day (ch. 13). Yet these shameless slanderers could not deny that it was the custom of the pagans to pray to their fabricated gods in a similar fashion and to dedicate to them one day of the week, which they celebrated on the day nearest the Lord’s Day, according to a rite far different from that of the [Col. 0758D] Jews.

Tertullian then inveighs sharply against a certain beast-fighter, an apostate from the Jewish religion, who carried around in the Circus a picture of the Christians’ God in the form of an ass, with this inscription: *Onochoetes* (ch. 14). For the further that image departed from the truth, the more certain it was that the pagans worshipped horned gods, goat-born gods, and gods altogether resembling monsters.

Nothing more false could likewise have been invented than that Christians killed an infant amid their sacred mysteries and drank its blood. Yet it was plainly established that the pagans, contrary to their own laws, slaughtered newborn infants, or with the utmost cruelty exposed them to be killed by the injuries of the open air, [Col. 0759A] or drew out their blood and devoured premature infants whole (ch. 15).

What, indeed, could be more shameless than for Christians to be accused of wholly fictitious nocturnal incest by those who could not deny that pagan Persians openly had intercourse with their own mothers, and who praised and encouraged with great applause Oedipus when he represented that crime in the theater? Indeed, these very men frequently mingled in infamous incest with their own children, whom, as we have said, they had exposed as infants and no longer recognized (ch. 16). Lest anyone object that this was invented without evidence, our Author confirms it by a well-known example that occurred during Fuscianus’s term as prefect of the City, a considerable time before he wrote these books.

He proceeds and makes it plain that Christians were vainly proclaimed public [Col. 0759B] enemies by the pagans because they did not worship the images of the Caesars or swear by their genius (ch. 17). For he clearly shows that those rebellious nations conquered by the emperors, which had procured for them the surnames Parthicus, Medicus, and Germanicus, were justly charged with this very crime. Were not the Romans also public enemies who conspired against their emperors and recently killed them in their palaces in Gaul? Must not those also be called public enemies who assailed them with every kind of insult at the circus games, or in defamatory placards hung upon their statues? Finally, who were the public enemies: the Christians, who refused to call the Emperor a god, or the gentiles, who against their conscience and not without mockery called him a god, [Col. 0759C] and, swearing by his genius, did not shrink from perjury?

Moreover, they imputed a new crime to Christians: namely, their marvelous constancy in enduring every kind of torture for the truth of their religion (ch. 18). But with what effrontery and audacity, I ask? For they themselves extolled with the highest praises Regulus, who was the first to end his life by the punishment of the cross; the queen of Egypt, who ended hers through the bite of beasts; and Hannibal’s wife, who, like Dido, ended hers by voluntary fire. Did they not also praise the Attic woman who, after the bitterest torments, spat her tongue at the tyrant rather than betray a secret? Did they not likewise account it praiseworthy in gladiators to be killed by one another or by beasts, in certain men to let themselves be burned alive, and in the Laconians steadfastly to endure being beaten and mangled [Col. 0759D] with scourges?

Yet surely the Christians’ unconquered fortitude under horrendous tortures was all the more praiseworthy because, unlike the pagans, they endured them not to pursue the empty glory of men, but because they most certainly expected the future resurrection of bodies, God’s final judgment, and an eternal reward according to their deserts (ch. 19). Nor were they to be hissed off by the pagans because of this expectation, since the latter asserted that human souls would migrate even into other bodies, including those of beasts; that there would be a future judgment of Minos and Rhadamanthus; and that afterward the impious would pass eternity in fire, but the pious would pass eternal life in a pleasant place.

[Col. 0760A] Finally, from what has thus far been argued, Tertullian concludes that even if Christians had been guilty of all the crimes of which they were falsely accused, they nevertheless ought not to be punished and harassed by pagans who are proved actually to have committed the same things (ch. 20). Finally, even if Christians had declared themselves guilty of those crimes, the pagans should nevertheless have investigated with the utmost diligence whether the charges were true and should have heard and examined lawful witnesses to and accomplices in those crimes. But they refused to observe these provisions of their own laws, lest, once the Christians’ innocence had been recognized, they should love those whom they had resolved to hate and remove from their midst. Thus, through voluntary ignorance, they loved their own errors and hated the truth.

## ARTICLE II. Analysis of the Second Book

[Col. 0760B]

In the second book, likewise divided into two parts, Tertullian shows that the gods of the gentiles are false and that their forms of worship are depraved and to be rejected (Book II, ch. 1). However firmly they may appear to be established by ancestral authority, their falsity is so manifest that it is most readily recognized by all who have not voluntarily blinded themselves. Therefore, to break their unyielding obstinacy, Tertullian first refutes what Varro—easily foremost in learning among all the men of his age—had set forth in his books on religion. There he distinguishes three classes of gods: the first physical, or that of the philosophers; the second mythical, or that of the poets; and the third gentile, or that of the peoples. But the first, says [Col. 0760C] our Author, rests upon uncertain conjectures, the second upon mere fables, and the third upon ridiculous adoptions of gods and an insane and utterly shameful cult. All these things, therefore, are false, while everyone’s opinion concerning the true God ought to be one and shared, and their consensus unanimous.

For there was the greatest disagreement among the physicists or philosophers concerning God’s nature, which arose from nothing other than their ignorance of him (ch. 2). They therefore professed wisdom in vain, since its beginning is the fear of God. But how could they have feared him when they did not know who or what he was? Yet it was easy for them to know this true God from our Sacred Scriptures. But, led by a blind lust for glory, they corrupted with various sophistries what they had drawn from these pure fountains, and consequently split into various factions. [Col. 0760D] The Platonists taught that all things were governed by God’s providence; the Epicureans, by contrast, wholly and completely denied it. The same Platonists asserted that God was situated within the world, but the Stoics that he was outside it. Diogenes admitted that he did not know what was happening in heaven or who the gods were; while earlier Thales of Miletus could make absolutely no reply when Croesus questioned him about the gods. Socrates did not, indeed, believe that they existed, yet ordered that a rooster be sacrificed to Aesculapius.

Why say more? Dionysius divided the gods into three classes: first, those that fall within our sight, such as the sun and moon; second, others that are unseen, such as Neptune; and third, others who were deified [Col. 0761A] from among human beings, such as Hercules and Amphiaraus. Arcesilaus likewise devised a threefold but different class of gods, namely the Olympians, the Stars, and the Titans. Xenocrates, however, admitted only the latter two, just as most Egyptians admitted no more than the sun and moon, heaven and earth. Democritus suspected that all the gods arose together with the heavenly fire, which Zeno held nature to resemble. Hence Varro taught that the soul of the world was fire, since when it is extinguished we die.

All these opinions, however diverse they may be, seem to conspire toward this one conclusion: those philosophers thought there were no gods other than the elements of the world or things arising from them (ch. 3). Tertullian, however, demonstrates that these are the dreams of men who are awake. [Col. 0761B] For God exists from himself, unbegotten and eternal, having no beginning and never to have an end. But the elements are parts of the world, which Plato admits was created, and which Epicurus alone dreamed was uncreated.

Moreover, if the world and the elements are gods, they must be living beings, and by this very fact are mortal and devoid of divinity. Nor should Varro be heard when he prattled that they were indeed living beings, but ones that move of themselves and should therefore be regarded as immortal gods. For however these elements may be moved, they are certainly corporeal; and therefore, since they can be destroyed, they cannot be gods. Furthermore, even if they moved themselves, they nevertheless received the principle of their motion from another, who, although unseen, is the first author of all motions and therefore the true and only God.

[Col. 0761C] Nor does Tertullian permit the objection that the Greek name Θεός (“God”) is in fact derived from the running and motion with which the elements are endowed (ch. 4). For the true designation of God does not signify some quality visible to our eyes, as running and motion are, but supreme majesty and substance. What of the fact that many pagans proclaimed a great number of their gods to be motionless? Moreover, no one will ever deny that the elements are composed of matter, something that God’s utterly simple nature cannot admit. And indeed Zeno separated the world’s matter from God and supposed that he passed through it as honey passes through the comb. But I should like you to say by what reasoning the natural philosophers asserted that the world and the elements contained within it were gods. For Plato judges this world itself to be spherical and without a head. Epicurus, however, used to say, [Col. 0761D] “What is above us has nothing to do with us,” and babbled that the sun was only a foot across; the Peripatetics, by contrast, thought it far larger than the whole terrestrial globe. Is it not, therefore, the height of madness to fashion gods of this kind? Finally, Thales of Miletus fell into a well while scrutinizing the heavens. What does that fall indicate except the vanity of the natural philosophers’ curiosity, since, after fruitlessly investigating the nature of things, they failed to know its true craftsman, almighty God?

Varro insisted that divinity should be attributed to the elements because they either bind human beings to themselves through the greatest benefits or oppress them with hardships (ch. 5). Tertullian, however, replies that the elements are merely instruments of prosperous or adverse circumstances and that the true [Col. 0762A] God, whom they serve, is their author. For just as a sick person’s cure is to be attributed not to the medicines but to the physician, and a person’s death not to the sword or spear but to the robber or enemy, so prosperous and adverse events must be ascribed not to the elements but to God, the equitable judge of deserts. It is also evident that the elements diminish, perish, and are ruled by someone (ch. 6). But the true God can neither be diminished nor undergo anything, and he rules and governs all things.

Tertullian passes from there to the second class of gods, which Varro chose to designate the mythical class, or that of the poets (ch. 7). The poets imagined that these gods were heroes who, after passing through the mortal life of human beings, obtained the reward of divinity through their illustrious deeds. Following this utterly pernicious example, the Romans [Col. 0762B] likewise proclaimed their kings to be gods, while others perjured themselves. But who, I ask you, had those gods of the poets been? Human beings, certainly, born from a sordid or criminal origin, who throughout the course of their lives wallowed in every kind of crime. For this reason Plato judged that the poets, and Homer himself, should be expelled from the state as shameless accusers of the gods. Indeed, when the gentiles—shameless as they were—were reproached by Christians for such great infamy among their gods, they answered nothing except that these were mere fables of the poets. But although they loudly declared that they did not believe the poets, they not only venerated the gods fashioned by them—Ceres, the Idaean Mother, Saturn, and Hercules—but also performed horrendous sacrifices to them. Who, then, will not admit that these gods of the poets were worshipped and vaunted by the gentiles [Col. 0762C] with the greatest impiety?

Certainly no less senseless was the third class of gods, which Varro taught was gentile or popular (ch. 8). For these gods were none other than those whom certain peoples had adopted according to their own whim and caprice, and whom others could distinguish neither by sight, nor by hearing, nor even by name. Tertullian lists as such Atargatis, Caelestis, Versutina, Obodas, Dusares, Belenus, Delventinus, Visidianus, Numentinus, Ancharia, Praeveris, and Nortia. He thinks that by the same license the Egyptians, who worshipped beasts, regarded and honored the patriarch Joseph as a god under the name Serapis. He therefore rightly concludes from what has been said that those gods of the peoples are utterly false, since the one true God, present everywhere [Col. 0762D] and Lord of all, is equally to be worshipped and adored by everyone.
