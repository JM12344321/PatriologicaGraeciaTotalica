## CHAPTER V. On Tertullian’s Book *To Scapula*

### ARTICLE I. Analysis of This Book

Tertullian addressed this book—or rather, this petition—to Scapula, proconsul of Africa, in order, if it were at all possible, to deter him from torturing and mangling Christians in defiance of every law, human and divine (Tertull. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 1). First, [Col. 0774B] however, he declares that he wrote this petition not because he feared for the Christians, who endured punishments willingly and cheerfully, but because he feared for their pagan enemies, whom, on this ground alone, those same Christians were bound to love and for whom they were bound to pray to God. He therefore considered it his duty to make clear to everyone that Christians were wholly innocent and that the pagans themselves were in reality guilty of precisely the class of crimes for which they falsely reproached the Christians.

For Christians do not worship fictitious gods, as the pagans do, but the one true God, whom all people know naturally and revere. Christians, therefore, ought not to be compelled to worship another, since it is no part of religion to compel religion. Nor can any of those who are represented as gods desire sacrifices [Col. 0774C] from the unwilling. Christians, therefore, are accused wholly without justification of impiety, sacrilege, theft, or sedition because they refuse to worship the gods. The pagans, by contrast, strip the temples of their own gods with impunity and treat those gods with contempt and ridicule.

What, moreover, of the fact that, during the seditions of Albinus, Niger, and Cassius, pagans alone were found who at that very time swore by the emperors’ guardian spirits, immolated victims for their safety, and offered sacrifices? Christians, however, are enemies of absolutely no one, much less of the emperor, whom they know to have been appointed by God, and whom they therefore sincerely honor and wish to remain safe and unharmed together with the entire Roman Empire. Nevertheless, they do not revere him in the pagan manner as a god, but because he is greater than all false gods and inferior only to the true God, [Col. 0774D] whom they therefore entreat for his safety with a daily sacrifice of prayer. Furthermore, although the multitude of those same Christians is exceedingly great, individuals are nonetheless scarcely recognized except by their modesty, silence, and correction of their former vices (cap. 2).

Tertullian says that Christians do not endure the punishments inflicted upon them by the pagans with a restless or impatient spirit, for they await vengeance from God. They do, however, grieve that signs of that vengeance, now imminent, have already been sent beforehand: namely, the harvests lost recently under the governorship of Hilarianus, excessive and untimely rains, nocturnal fires hanging over the walls of Carthage, unusual thunder, and an altogether extraordinary eclipse of the sun. Vigellius Saturninus, who was the first to order Christians [Col. 0775A] to be killed in Africa, was deprived of his eyesight. In Cappadocia, too, Claudianus Herminianus died of plague, with worms erupting from him, although through an all-too-late repentance he had almost become a Christian. Finally, when Caecilius Capella, during the Byzantine departure or destruction, had cried out in mockery of the Christians, “Rejoice,” he paid the penalty for his shameless cruelty. If any governors of this kind escaped punishment, they will be punished at God’s final judgment. Scapula, therefore, our author says, had reason to fear, because shortly before that Byzantine destruction he had condemned Mavillus of Hadrumetum to the beasts (cap. 3).

He nevertheless earnestly warns Scapula that he recalls these things not because he himself fears anything, nor in order to terrify Scapula, but because he desires everyone [Col. 0775B] to be saved. He consequently exhorts him to comply with the laws, which direct that no accused person be punished unless he has confessed his crimes or has been convicted of them. He then sets before him the examples of Cincius Severus, Vespronius Candidus, Asper, and Pudens, who had refused to condemn innocent Christians. Scapula’s advocates, upon whom Christians had certainly conferred benefits of no small importance, could suggest to him how he might imitate these men. For Christians had not only expelled demons from the advocates’ clerk and from another of their relatives, but had also miraculously healed others afflicted by diseases.

Indeed, when the emperor Severus, father of Antoninus, who had been brought up on Christian milk, had once been similarly healed by oil given to him by Proculus Torpacion, a Christian and procurator of Euhodia, he not only retained him in his palace until the day of his death, [Col. 0775C] but also never harmed the most distinguished women and men who were likewise followers of the Christian religion, and resisted the populace when it openly raged against Christians. Marcus Aurelius, too, during his German expedition, obtained by the prayers of Christians rain that was absolutely necessary for his army; and Christians had obtained it for others in a similar circumstance.

What, then, could be more unjust than to order that Christians, who devote diligent attention to every virtue, should not merely be struck down by the sword, as they were by the governors of the legion and of Mauretania under the prescription of the ancient laws, but should even be burned alive? Tertullian, however, openly declares that Christians desire these punishments more than they fear them. For when Arrius Antoninus was raging more cruelly against them, and all the Christians in a city of Asia voluntarily presented themselves to him, [Col. 0775D] he replied: “If you wish to perish, you have cliffs or ropes.” What, then, will Scapula do with the almost innumerable Christians of Carthage? What will he do with members of his own rank, or with relatives and friends who will voluntarily offer themselves to him to be killed? Tertullian therefore exhorts him to spare Carthage and the province, which he has made subject to extortions by soldiers and by each person’s enemies. For God alone is the Christians’ master; he existed before Scapula and cannot suffer any harm from human beings. Scapula’s masters, by contrast, are human beings who will one day die. Finally, whatever he may do, the Christian sect will not fail, for it grows even when it appears to be cut down. Whoever investigates and learns why Christians so steadfastly [Col. 0776A] endure every kind of torture then comes to know the truth and embraces their religion (cap. 4).

### ARTICLE II. It Is Shown That Tertullian Is the Author of This Book; the Reasons That Impelled Him to Publish It After the *Apologeticum* and the Books *To the Nations*; When He Wrote It; and Certain Pagan Proconsuls and Prefects Who Treated the Christians Either More Cruelly or More Leniently

Everyone acknowledges, with a truly great and unanimous consensus, that Tertullian is the genuine author of this book. Indeed, who would dare deny what the book itself, in its entirety, openly attests? For it contains the same style of expression as the *Apologeticum* and the books *To the Nations*, [Col. 0776B] the same distinctive forms of speech peculiar to Tertullian, the same unusual words, and generally the same arguments in defense of Christians against the pagans. Just as the *Apologeticum* and the books *To the Nations* were sent either to all pagans or to their governors, so it is entirely clear that this book was addressed to Scapula, prefect of Carthage and of all Africa, whom Tertullian attempted to dissuade from torturing and slaughtering wholly innocent Christians.

But someone may ask what need Tertullian had to write, besides the *Apologeticum* and the books *To the Nations*, another treatise or book against the pagans and on behalf of Christians. We answer that the monstrous cruelty of Scapula, governor of Africa, against Christians compelled him to present not exactly a book, but a petition. [Col. 0776C] For although Severus, who became emperor in the year of Christ 193, greatly favored Christians during the first years of his reign, nevertheless, after Cassius, Niger, and Albinus had been defeated and after his solemn entry into the city of Rome in 197, his attitude completely changed. For then, with his initial approval, or at least without his prohibition, the terrible persecution of Christians began to revive. Subsequently becoming still more hostile, he enacted new laws against them in 202; by the authority of these laws, Eusebius says, martyrdoms occurred everywhere throughout the Churches. Accordingly, the surge of this most cruel persecution ravaged Africa no less than the other provinces (Euseb. lib. VI. *eccles. Hist.* cap. 2. pag. 201. Hieronym. *Catalog. scriptor. eccl.* § 54. pag. 115).

[Col. 0776D] Tertullian, ever concerned for the safety of Christians, had published the *Apologeticum* and the books *To the Nations* before these laws were issued, as we have said; and the pagans’ fury against the Christians, however fiercely kindled, should undoubtedly have been calmed by reading them (Euseb. ibid. cap. I. pag. 201). But absolutely no weight of reasoning, even if clearer than the noonday sun, could extinguish that fury or prevent Christians in Africa from being mangled under the utterly unjust laws enacted by Severus. Tertullian therefore further presented this petition to Scapula, proconsul of that province, so that by both the very great weight of his arguments and the notable domestic examples of other governors and even emperors, [Col. 0777A] he might dissuade him from harassing and torturing Christians.

This book, moreover, supplies us with several arguments by which it is quite clearly established that it was written after the emperor Severus’s persecution of Christians had begun. For it presents examples of certain governors who, having at that time subjected Christians to various punishments, had already paid the penalty for their cruelty. Tertullian begins with Hilarianus as follows: “Under the governorship of Hilarianus, when they had shouted concerning the grounds of our burial places, ‘There must be no burial grounds,’ their own threshing floors ceased to exist; for they did not gather their harvests” (Tertul. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 3). Here, by a by no means wholly inept antithesis, he contrasts an *area*, meaning an empty place or barren field in which the dead were buried, [Col. 0777B] with an *area*, or threshing floor, on which crops are threshed and beaten out. Thus he said that at the very time when the pagans were raging more fiercely against Christians and shouting that grounds for burying them must be denied, those same pagans lacked harvests and grain to thresh upon their own threshing floors. He openly asserts that this barrenness occurred while Hilarianus was governor of Africa. Now from the Acts of the holy martyrs Perpetua and Felicitas—which were published as true and genuine by several editors and, finally, by the Bollandist associates and by our Theodoric Ruinart—we learn that these most holy martyrs were exposed to the beasts by order of the procurator Hilarianus, “who at that time,” as we read there, “had received the power of the sword in place of the deceased proconsul Minutius Firmianus” (Bolland. 7. *Martyr.* pag. 633 et seqq. Ruinart. *Act. Martyr.* cap. [Col. 0777C] 85 et seqq.). Some think that they received the crown of martyrdom in 202; others in 203; and others in 205, undoubtedly after the persecution had been stirred up by the emperor Severus. Tertullian himself, moreover, recorded the following concerning Perpetua in another book: “Perpetua, that most courageous martyr, in a revelation on the day of her passion saw there in paradise only her fellow martyrs” (Tert. lib. *de Anim.* cap. 55). We also learn from Augustine, who frequently mentions her and Felicitas, that the feast day of them both was celebrated annually in Africa. Is it not sufficiently and clearly established from this that they won the palm of martyrdom in Africa while Hilarianus was discharging the office of proconsul (Augustin. serm. 280 et seqq. pag. 1134 et seqq.)?

Tertullian subsequently says: “Vigellius Saturninus, [Col. 0777D] who was the first here to wield the sword against us, lost his eyesight” (Tertull. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 3). He is thought to have been the proconsul of Africa who, in the year 200—or, as others prefer, 205—condemned Speratus and the other Scillitan martyrs at Carthage to be beheaded. For we read this recorded in the acts of their martyrdom, which were first published by Baronius as true and authentic and were subsequently enlarged and corrected by our Ruinart (Bar. ad ann. Christ. 202, § 2 et seqq. Ruinart. *Act. Mart.* p. 77 et seqq.). When Tertullian added that Saturninus was the first in Africa to wield the sword against Christians, it must undoubtedly be understood to mean after the persecution had been stirred up by the emperor Severus. For it seems entirely beyond dispute [Col. 0778A] that several had previously been put to death. Tertullian’s meaning, therefore, is that Christians were tortured by Saturninus before they were tortured by Hilarianus, and consequently that, when listing here the prefects of Africa who were the Christians’ executioners, he does not follow chronological order.

Less certain, therefore, seems the opinion of those who have supposed that Cincius Severus, Vespronius Candidus, Asper, and Pudens are named there by our Tertullian after Hilarianus and Saturninus in the same order in which each man was proconsul of Africa (Tertullian. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 4). For he mentions them because they treated the Christians with as much leniency as the others treated them with cruelty. Why, then, should he be said to have observed chronological order in naming the former rather than the latter? Moreover, there is room to doubt whether these men [Col. 0778B] were governors of Africa, for after Hilarianus and Saturninus, those most dreadful torturers of Christians, he added Claudius Herminianus, prefect of Cappadocia. If anyone should nevertheless prove that our author presents the true sequence of their African proconsulates, we shall readily assent to him.

In any case, he placed these examples of more merciful governors before Scapula’s eyes for no other reason than that, after the laws against Christians had been enacted by the emperor Severus, the governors of Africa were raging and reveling against them with greater bitterness (Ibid. cap. 3). For besides the aforementioned Saturninus and Hilarianus, Scapula himself, as our author testifies in the same passage, had not only condemned Mavillus of Hadrumetum to the beasts; but Christian Africa, because Scapula either did not restrain it or gave the order, had also, he says, [Col. 0778C] been made subject to extortions by soldiers and by each person’s enemies (Ibid. cap. 4). Although we cannot determine in what year the aforementioned proconsuls governed Africa, it can nevertheless be gathered from the preceding discussion that this book *To Scapula* was published by Tertullian after the emperor Severus’s persecution of Christians had begun.

It therefore remains to inquire whether it was published immediately after the fire of that persecution had been kindled or after it had been extinguished. Some think that it appeared publicly after the year 210 and the death of the emperor Severus. For Tertullian, they say, speaks there of a certain eclipse of the sun in this manner: [Col. 0778D] “At the assembly in Utica, the sun, with its light almost extinguished, was so portentous that it could not have suffered this from an ordinary eclipse, being situated in its own *hypsoma* and domicile” (Ibid. cap. 3). This eclipse, they say, occurred in the year of Christ 210, on the eleventh day of April, at the very hour of noon. Others, however, consider the entire foundation of this opinion altogether unstable. For those authors identify an ordinary eclipse of the sun, whereas Tertullian identifies one that was entirely extraordinary and that, as he says, had been a portent. For at that time the sun, he says, was in its own *hypsoma*, that is, at its summit, height, and highest point. This is what the Greek word *ὕψωμα* (*hypsōma*) means. No eclipse can occur while the sun is situated there; it suffers one only when it passes near the nodes that occur at the dragon’s head and tail. [Col. 0779A] Although our Tertullian may have been wholly unacquainted with the science of astrology, it can nevertheless be gathered from his words that this eclipse of the sun was not an ordinary one, but so extraordinary that the astrologers, to whom he refers us, could not deny it.

Scaliger therefore sets that argument aside and thinks this book was composed after the death of Caracalla, when Elagabalus was already reigning, having been made emperor in May of the year 218. For Tertullian speaks of Severus, as we have seen, in these words: “He kept the Christian Proculus . . . . . . in his palace until his death; Antoninus Caracalla knew him very well” (Tertull. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 4). But no sane person, Scaliger says, would think that this was said while Antoninus Caracalla was still alive (Scalig. [Col. 0779B] *animadv. in Chron. Euseb.* pag. 129).

Those whose minds are sound and unimpaired, however, do not recognize soundness in Scaliger’s reasoning here. For what prevents the young and adolescent Caracalla from having known Proculus and from still being alive when Tertullian committed these things to writing? Moreover, Tertullian employs the verb “had known” because Proculus, and not Caracalla, was then actually dead. No further hearing, therefore, should be given to those who, relying upon this invalid argument of Scaliger, yet departing slightly from his opinion, suspect that this book was published in the year 217, when Macrinus reigned. For once the foundation of this opinion has been overturned, the opinion itself also collapses.

Those who date this [Col. 0779C] book shortly after the year 211, when Severus is said to have laid down both his life and his imperial power at York in England in the month of February, therefore appear to come closer to the truth. For Tertullian undoubtedly speaks of him in this book when he says: “Severus himself also, the father of Antoninus Caracalla, was mindful of the Christians” (Tert. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 4). Why, they ask, is he called the father of Antoninus unless he had already departed this life and his sons Caracalla and Geta had succeeded in his place? Does not our author subsequently allude to them as already reigning when he asserts that Scapula had masters in his cruelty against the Christians? After Severus’s death, or certainly not long thereafter, when Tertullian saw that the fury of Scapula and others against Christians had abated, he appears to have presented this petition to him. [Col. 0779D] For he did not consider that it should be presented while Severus—whose extreme clemency toward Christians during the first years of his reign he had praised, as we have seen—was raging atrociously against them until his death. But once Severus had died, it is more probable that Tertullian immediately seized the opportunity offered to him to deter the proconsul Scapula from persecuting Christians.

### ARTICLE III. On the Manuscript and Printed Copies of This Book, the Observations of Various Scholars upon It, and Certain Variant Readings and Notes

Several codices of this little book are mentioned. The first is the Gorze codex, which Rhenanus used for his edition of it. Another is called the Angelicus, and three Vatican codices [Col. 0780A] are also mentioned. Gelenius revised and corrected earlier editions of this little book according to the authority of the former, and Pamelius according to that of the latter. Rigaltius further testifies that he had seen another in the possession of Pierre Belin, S.C. of Dijon, whose readings differing from those of the printed editions had come into the hands of Pierre Pithou.

This book is said never to have emerged alone from the printers’ workshops into public view, but always together with Tertullian’s other works. There have therefore been as many editions of this book as of those works. Since we have already discussed these editions at some length, there is no need to repeat that discussion now. Rigaltius followed Pamelius everywhere; Pamelius, as he says, corrected the old editions from three Vatican codices. For although Rigaltius lays claim to the Dijon codex, he noted only [Col. 0780B] a single variant reading from it, which he entirely rejected. It occurs where in all editions we read these words of Tertullian concerning Proculus: “Whom Antoninus Caracalla also knew very well, having been brought up on Christian milk” (Tertull. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 4). The Dijon codex, however, reads: “whom Antoninus knew to have been brought up among Christians.” This signifies that Proculus, rather than Antoninus Caracalla, had been brought up on Christian milk.

Rigaltius rejects this reading of the Dijon codex, however, because those words of Tertullian must refer to Antoninus. Spartianus relates that when Antoninus, as a seven-year-old boy, heard that a boy who was his playmate had been severely beaten on account of the Jewish religion, he for a long time refused to look upon either his own father, the boy’s father, or those responsible for the beating (Spart. [Col. 0780C] *in Vit. Carac.* pag. 331. col. 1). By the term “Jewish religion,” however, Spartianus means the Christian religion, since pagans confused the two. We may add, if one pleases, that Proculus had been called a Christian by Tertullian himself shortly before. There was therefore no need to repeat this with the words “brought up among Christians,” which appear sufficiently corrupt in the Dijon codex.

Furthermore, at the beginning of this book, in Pamelius’s and subsequent editions, we read: “We, indeed, are not frightened . . . . . since we have come to this sect after accepting the condition of its compact, so that, having pledged even our lives, we enter into these contests” (Tertull. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 1). But the earliest editions had read: [Col. 0780D] “We, indeed, are not frightened . . . since we come to this sect, having accepted its condition, having previously made a compact with our life and pledged ourselves, into these contests.” Laurentius de la Barre alone testifies that he corrected these words from the Gorze codex. But he should have transcribed the words of the old editions in full in his notes, so that everyone might more easily recognize whether they had been correctly amended, for they do not appear to be altogether devoid of meaning. In any event, Tertullian there uses the verb *auctorari*, as he does elsewhere: “Some,” he says, “have pledged themselves to the flames” (Tertull. lib. *ad Martyr.* cap. 5. p. 158). The verb *auctorare* means to bind oneself either for pay or by an oath. In the first words of Tertullian, this verb is taken in the latter sense; in the second, in the former sense. If, however, you require this to be proved to you by the testimony of certain authors, first [Col. 0781A] hear Cicero: “The very wage,” he says, “is the bond of servitude” (Cicer. lib. II. *de offic.* p. 274. lin. 4). Suetonius, moreover, relates that Tiberius Caesar gave a gladiatorial show “for an enlistment bounty of one hundred thousand” (Sueton. lib. III. § 7). Seneca also said: “I gave him over to the ghosts and ordered him to be placed among the newly enlisted gladiators” (Senec. *lud. de morte Claud.* p. 919). Soldiers bound by the military oath are frequently called *auctorati*, and those discharged from it *exauctorati*, by Livy and other authors. We shall note below the other variant readings of this book as occasion presents itself.

The same men who wrote notes and observations on the *Apologeticum* wrote upon this book as well, and in the same manner. The same judgment, therefore, that we expressed above must be passed upon all of them. Nothing further need be added, [Col. 0781B] except that, in connection with these words of Tertullian *To Scapula*: “When you had condemned Mavillus of Hadrumetum to the beasts, this affliction immediately followed” (Tertull. lib. *ad Scapul.* cap. 3), Rigaltius thinks that an attack of some extremely painful illness is denoted, with which Scapula himself was again afflicted on account of his repeated cruelty against Christians, as Rigaltius says. For Tertullian immediately added: “And now, from the same cause, there is an appeal of blood”; that is, as Rigaltius likewise believes, the blood of the Christians had cried out to God and interrupted the delays of his divine patience. But our author did not say a single word—not even the least—about a first or second illness of Scapula. Indeed, if he had once and then again suffered from an extremely painful illness, as Rigaltius would have it, Tertullian would have pressed him much more forcefully on that basis, [Col. 0781C] so that he might more easily be restored to a sounder mind. But passing this over, he insists and presses him with the examples of other governors and proconsuls who had paid the penalty for their cruelty against Christians. We shall therefore be more readily persuaded that those words of Tertullian must be referred to the destruction of Byzantium, which he mentions in the same passage, through which the pagans openly confessed that they had been punished by God for their most unjust cruelty against those same Christians.

Rigaltius seems to have departed less from the truth where, in Tertullian’s words “Proculus, procurator of Euhodia” (Ibid.), he approves Pithou’s emendation, who suspected that *Evodi* should be read. But these observations, whether those of Rigaltius or of others, must be discussed more fully below.

## CHAPTER VI. New Observations on Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, the Two Books *To the Nations*, and the Single Book *To Scapula*, in Which It Is First Shown That the Christian Religion Is Supported by the Sacred Scriptures of the Old Testament; How These Scriptures Came to the Christians, the Successors of the Jews; and How Great Their Authority Is

[Col. 0781D]

### ARTICLE I. How Tertullian Shows That the Christian Religion, Introduced into the World During the Reign of Tiberius, Is Supported by the Sacred Scriptures of the Jews; and That Christians, Although They Differ from Those Same Jews in Name, in the Use of Certain Foods, in Feast Days, and in Circumcision, Were Nevertheless Truly Substituted in Place of the Jews, Who Were Driven from Their Native Land on Account of Their Crimes

[Col. 0782A] Tertullian had no other purpose in the books with which we are concerned than, in pleading the cause of the Christians, to demonstrate that their religion was true and that of the pagans false, and that the pagans were in reality guilty of all the crimes of which they falsely accused those same Christians. But because he does not treat these matters with sufficient distinction and orderly arrangement, and because numerous difficult questions arise, we shall endeavor, to the best of our ability, to reduce them to a better order and to examine and explain them in our customary manner.

Tertullian acknowledges that the Christian religion is not especially ancient, since it was founded by Christ the Lord during the reign of Tiberius Caesar, before whose time the name of Christians had never been heard. “The registration,” he says—that is, the origin and beginning—“of this discipline, as we have already stated, dates from Tiberius” (Tertullian. [Col. 0782B] *Apologet.* cap. 7). Earlier he had said: “Tiberius, in whose time the Christian name entered the world” (Ibid. cap. 5). If anyone desires to know where and when it was first introduced, he will learn from the Acts of the Apostles that Paul and Barnabas taught a great multitude in the Church at Antioch, “so that the disciples were first called Christians at Antioch” (Act. cap. XI. 25). Hence our Tertullian says in another book: “Our era does not yet number two hundred and fifty years,” and subsequently: “Our era does not yet number three hundred years” (Tertull. lib. *ad Nat.* cap. 5 et cap. 9). Indeed, in the *Apologeticum* he says: “We are of yesterday,” that is, we were born not long ago, but, as it were, only yesterday in comparison with others. There he wished to warn us that it occurred not without a great miracle [Col. 0782C] that Christians, although they had arisen so few years before, had filled every place throughout the whole world, with the sole exception of the pagans’ temples (Idem, *Apolog.* cap. 37).

Yet although the Christian religion, by the admission even of its adherents, was, as Tertullian says, so new that it “belongs to the time of Tiberius”—this is how he speaks (Ibid. cap. 21)—that is, although it was introduced into this world, as we have just said, during the reign of Tiberius Caesar, it is nevertheless certain that it is supported, as he further says, by the most ancient “documents of the Jews,” that is, by the sacred Scriptures of the Old Testament. It is not only founded upon them, but those Scriptures now belong no less to the Christians than they formerly did to the Jews. [Col. 0782D] For a little earlier our author had written: “The archive of a single prophet,” that is, the books of Moses, “surpasses the books of all others by many centuries; in it appears to have been deposited the treasure of the entire Jewish mystery, and consequently of ours as well” (Ibid. cap. 19); in our manuscript codices the reading is, “and consequently now of ours as well.” By these words Tertullian plainly signifies that the divine treasure of our mystery, just as formerly that of the Jews, is truly deposited in this and the other books of the Old Testament.

The pagans objected, however, that this was an empty and absurd presumption and boast on the part of Christians, who wished to conceal themselves “under the shelter of a most renowned religion” (Idem, cap. 21) and to defend the impious worship that they rendered to a crucified man. For, they said, there was a great discrepancy between Christians and Jews, [Col. 0783A] not only in name but also in the actual facts. First, just as no one was unaware how great the antiquity of the Jews was, so absolutely no one was ignorant that Christ was a man whom the Jews themselves, not long ago, as we have just observed, condemned and subjected to the punishment of the cross. Second, they disagreed among themselves in “distinctions of diet,” that is, in certain kinds of food from which the one group abstained but which the other consumed. Third, they differed in their observance of feast days, which was entirely different among the two groups. Finally, they differed in the very “mark,” or, as Minucius Felix says, the “bodily sign,” which the Apostle calls “the sign of circumcision” and “the seal of the righteousness of the faith in uncircumcision” (Minut. Felix, pag. 376. Paul. *Epist. ad Rom.* IV. cap. 4). Yet all these things [Col. 0783B] ought to have been common to Christians and Jews if they were devoted to the same God.

This line of argument against Christians was not new, whether on the part of pagans or of Jews. For Justin Martyr, in his *Exhortation to the Greeks*, presses it in the name of the pagans as follows: *Εἰ δὲ τὶς φάσκοι τῶν προχείρως ἀντιλέγειν εἰθισμένων, μὴ ἡμῖν τὰς βίβλους ταύτας, ἄλλα Ἰουδαίοις προσήκειν, διὰ τὸ ἔτι νῦν ἐν ταῖς συναγωγαῖς αὐτῶν σώζεσθαι, καὶ ματὴν ἡμᾶς ἐκ τούτων φάσκειν τὴν θεοσέβειαν μεμαθηκέναι λέγοι* (Justin. martyr. *Cohort. ad Graec.* pag. 14): “But if one of those who are accustomed rashly to contradict us should object that these are not our books but those of the Jews, and should say that through vanity we boast of having drawn our religion from them.” In another book, moreover, he introduces a Jew making the same objections to Christians as Tertullian does, as follows: *Ἐκεῖνο δὲ ἀποροῦμεν μάλιστα, εἰ ὑμεῖς εὐσεβεῖν λέγοντες, καὶ τῶν ἄλλων ὀιόμενοι διαφέρειν, κατ` οὐδὲν αὐτῶν ἀπολείπεσθε, οὐδὲ διαλλάσσετε ἀπὸ τῶν ἐθνῶν τὸν ὑμέτερον βίον, ἐν τῷ μήτε τὰς ἑορτὰς, μήτε τὰ σάββατα τηρεῖν, μήτε τὴν περιτομὴν ἔχειν, καὶ ἔτι ἐπ` ἄνθρωπον σταυρωθέντα τὰς ἐλπίδας ποιούμενοι*. (Idem *Dialog. cum Tryph.* pag. 227). [Col. 0783C] “This certainly is what we find most astonishing when we consider the matter: that you, who boast of the true religion and wish to excel other human beings, differ from them in nothing and in your manner of life do not differ from the nations, since you neither observe feast days nor Sabbaths, nor possess circumcision, and, furthermore, place your hope in a crucified man.”

Tertullian therefore replies that the Jews once possessed very great favor with God because of the exceptional righteousness [Col. 0783D] and faith of their “original founders” (Tertullian. *Apologet.* cap. 21), that is, of the earliest patriarchs from whom they derived their origin. Hence they were then prosperous and flourished, as he says, both in the greatness of their race and in the loftiness of their kingdom. But their descendants, he continues, “puffed up by confidence in their fathers,” deviated from discipline in a profane manner, spurned God’s warnings, and, trampling upon his laws, rushed headlong into every kind of vice. But the God whom they despised in his goodness they experienced as a severe judge. This is most clearly proved by their present condition, in which, our author says, “scattered, wandering, and exiles from their own sky and soil, they roam throughout the world without man or God as king” (Ibid.). Cyprian excerpted this entire passage from his master and even borrowed some of his very words in the following manner: [Col. 0784A] “At first the Jews possessed favor with God. Thus were they righteous in former times; thus did their ancestors obey religious ordinances. Hence both the loftiness of their kingdom flourished and the greatness of their race arose. But afterward they became negligent, undisciplined, and proud, and, puffed up by confidence in their fathers, they lost the favor given to them when they despised the divine commands. How profane their life became and what offense they incurred by violating religion they themselves also testify: though silent in speech, they confess it by their fate. Scattered and wandering, they roam about, fugitives from their own soil and sky, and are driven from one alien lodging to another” (Cyprian. *de idolor. vanit.* pag. 15). But we have already quoted these things in our dissertation on the book of Minucius Felix, who thereby refutes the obstinacy of Caecilius [Col. 0784B] (*Dissertat. in Minut.* cap. 6. art. 3).
