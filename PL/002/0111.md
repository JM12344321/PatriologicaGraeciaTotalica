## First Appendix

# [Col. 1049] POEMS ATTRIBUTED TO TERTULLIAN

[Col. 1049D] Among scholars it is still universally accepted, with no one dissenting, that the poems which Pamelius judged should be inserted into the volume of Tertullian’s works are spurious and wrongly bear Tertullian’s name. Thus, says Lumper, the five books against Marcion composed in verse are not Tertullian’s.

1. Their style sufficiently proves them unworthy of a man of such outstanding genius: so often guilty of violated prosody, so often unequal to its subject matter, so often blunt when drawing weapons against the enemy.

2. In Book I *Against Marcion*, chapter 1, Tertullian himself states in his preface that he prepared his work against that heresiarch a second [Col. 1050D] and a third time, and that he then annulled it by a fuller composition. Certainly it was annulled in the same genre of discourse in which it had first been hurriedly written. Since, therefore, the work against Marcion by which Tertullian annulled the earlier composition is a disputation, not a poem, it follows that the earlier work too was a hastily written disputation, not poetic lucubration.

3. These verses were ascribed to Tertullian by Fabricius on the authority of a single, admittedly unknown and obscure manuscript.

4. None of the ancient writers numbered Tertullian among the ancient poets.

5. The author of these books openly contradicts Tertullian. For in *On the Soul*, chapter 57, Tertullian maintains that it was not Samuel himself but a phantom that had been raised by the Pythoness through evil arts. “Far be it from us,” he says, “to believe that the soul of any holy man, much less of a prophet, was drawn forth by a demon, since we have been taught that Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light, much more into a man of light, and will even affirm himself to be God,” etc. But the author of Book III of the poems against Marcion states the contrary in these verses:

> Wondrous Samuel, who first received the command  
> To anoint kings, to bestow chrism, and reveal men as anointed ones;  
> So praiseworthily did he act throughout the span of life  
> That even after death he retained prophetic rights.

In *On the Prescription of Heretics*, chapter 32, when describing the succession of Saint Peter’s successors in the Roman See, Tertullian teaches that Clement succeeded Peter immediately. But the author of Book III of the poems against Marcion states the contrary in these verses:

> [Col. 1051B] In this chair, in which Peter himself had sat,  
> Great Rome ordered Linus to be placed and sit first;  
> After him Cletus himself received the flock of the fold.  
> By lot Anacletus was appointed his successor,  
> And Clement follows him . . . . . . .

Who could believe that Tertullian was so forgetful of himself that he made statements so contrary and mutually contradictory? It is, moreover, uncertain who the author of that poem was. Nevertheless, this poem against Marcion deserves commendation for certain points, both historical and doctrinal. First, it affirms the eternity of the Word and the twofold nature in Christ Jesus; cites the Apocalypse under the name of John the disciple; relates that Jesus Christ suffered on the same day and in the same place in which Adam fell; attributes the name “Holy Spirit” to Jesus Christ; considers Hermas, surnamed the Shepherd, to have been the brother of Pope Pius; and finally holds that Ezra, filled with the divine Spirit and assisted by his own memory alone, dictated anew the lost books of the Prophets and restored them to the canon of Holy Scripture.

[Col. 1051C] The poems *On the Judgment of the Lord*, *On Genesis*, and *On Sodom* have likewise been falsely attributed to Tertullian. For it must not be believed that so great a man ever had Muses so hostile to him, as Rigault elegantly says. The poem *On Jonah and Nineveh*, which Margarinus de la Bigne published from the manuscript of the most distinguished P. Pithou at the end of volume VIII of the *Library of the Fathers*, must be placed in the same category. Finally, the poem *To a Senator*, which is read among Cyprian’s works in volume III and which Allix considers to have been composed before the age of Constantine, is wholly denied to Tertullian by learned men. As for Gennadius’s book *On Definitions of Faith and Ecclesiastical Dogmas*, formerly attributed to Tertullian, no scholar today assigns it to him, since many things are found there that postdate the third century and are not compatible with him.

On the spurious works of Tertullian, consult Natalis Alexander, *Ecclesiastical History*, second century; Dom Rémy Ceillier, *History of Ecclesiastical Writings*; Dupin, *Ecclesiastical Library*, vol. I, p. 252; Tillemont, *Ecclesiastical Memoirs*, vol. III, Life of Tertullian, art. 14; P. Allix, *Dissertation on the Life and Writings of Tertullian*, X.31; Walch, *Patristic Library*, chap. 4, §46; chap. 7, §70. In the dissertation just mentioned, P. Allix says that the poem *On the Judgment* is by Verecundus, an African bishop who flourished around the middle of the sixth century. The same scholar suspects that the books against Marcion were written after the time of Jerome.

Tertullian’s poetic works have appeared many times: namely, together with several Christian poets, in Georg Fabricius, *Works of the Ancient Christian Poets*, Basel, 1564; Michael Maittaire, *Works and Fragments of the Ancient Latin Poets*, London, 1715; in the Pesaro collection of all Latin poems, Pesaro, 1766, vol. V, pp. 1–16. The poem *On Jonah and Nineveh*, with corrections by François Juret, appears in the *Library of the Fathers*, vol. VIII, and at Leipzig, 1681, octavo, at the end of the Daum edition of Paulinus of Périgueux, 1681. Finally, Andreas Rivinus edited all Tertullian’s poetic works and, after Pamelius, Junius, and Barthius, added brief notes: Gotha, 1651. These poems did not appear important enough for us to furnish them with extensive commentaries; it was therefore sufficient to publish the annotations of Pamelius, slight and insubstantial though they are. # FIVE BOOKS AGAINST MARCION (C, G, S)

## TO THE READER

[Col. 1051D] It had been decided to omit the poems against Marcion that follow, not only because they appear scarcely worthy of Tertullian, but especially because Septimius himself testifies that they are by no means Tertullian’s. That they are plainly unworthy of a man of such excellent genius is sufficiently indicated by their style: so often guilty of violated prosody, so often unequal to its subject matter, so often blunt and inept when drawing weapons against the enemy.

Finally, at the beginning of his disputations against Marcion—from which I see that arguments for defending these verses are being drawn—Tertullian himself expressly states in his preface that he revised his work a second and a third time. He does not, however, relate that any of those revisions was poetic; indeed, he indicates that none was poetic when he says that he afterward annulled his first little work, which had been composed as though in haste, by a fuller composition. For if he had preluded against Marcion in verse, that work could indeed have been called hurried and preliminary; but if what he afterward annulled by a fuller composition was poetry, it ought to have been annulled by fuller poetry, not by a rhetorical dissertation or a dialectical [Col. 1052D] disputation. For if the genres of writing differed, the treatments of the same subject would have remained distinct: the poet would have played his part, the dialectician his, and the rhetorician his.

What he had published in Greek concerning the veiling of virgins, the crown, and spectacles he afterward published in Latin. He did not, however, annul the Greek works by the Latin ones, but added Latin works to the Greek. If he had wished to annul the Greek works, he would surely have revised and reshaped those earlier ones through later Greek works as well. Thus, whatever writing of his against Marcion he annulled was a disputation, not a poem. He annulled a hurried and insufficiently finished disputation through a later and fuller disputation, and submerged the meagerness of the earlier beneath the abundance of the later.

Let this suffice concerning the first and second products—unless it may also be useful to add that, when publishing the third, Septimius judged it necessary to preface it with an account of those three versions, lest the diversity of the work, found in scattered circulation, confuse anyone. Those words certainly confirm what we said above: namely, that none of the three revisions against Marcion was [Col. 1053A] poetic. For if any had been, it surely ought to have been excepted from the character of the others, since the difference in style would have protected it from all confusion.

One must also observe that Septimius says he annulled that first product; he does not complain that it had been made public. It is therefore no longer credible that anything of it survived except what he himself transferred into the fuller second version. But if we believe that he transferred poetic material, we must also admit that the poet was so thoroughly dismembered that none of his limbs can now be found. Indeed, if anything poetic is observed in those celebrated disputations—such as, perhaps, the description of Pontus that follows the preface—no trace of it appears anywhere in the five books of poems.

Lastly, to remove every doubt: if Septimius’s first product poured forth verses against [Col. 1053B] Marcion, then, when he annulled it, he himself destroyed his verses, and we seek them in vain. The third version, moreover, shows that the second was not poetic, since Septimius declares that the third was composed by correcting and enlarging the second.

We therefore conclude as follows: these verses, whatever their quality, were inscribed with Tertullian’s name by Fabricius, who was induced to do so solely by the authority of some manuscript or other. Experts in such matters know how slight and frivolous that authority must be considered, especially since Fabricius produced no sufficiently competent witness acquainted with that manuscript, while the ancient copies of this most diligently studied author that passed through their hands disclosed nothing poetic. Nor did any ancient writer number Tertullian among the Christian poets.

Nevertheless, although these poems have been badly combed by an uneven versifier, we do not despise them so greatly as to deny that there is anything good in them. Indeed, we affirm that in very many places they can usefully be compared and collated with Septimius’s works against Marcion. We therefore permit them to appear here as appendices. But because we do not know their author with certainty, we give them the name “Anonymous.” VENETIAN EDITOR.

## SYNOPSIS OF BOOK I, FROM TERTULLIAN HIMSELF, AT THE BEGINNING OF BOOK V

[Col. 1053C]

> The first book will recount in order the enemy’s words,  
> Which the mad renegade unlawfully contrived and uttered;  
> Then too the hope of the flesh and Christ’s victory,  
> And a brief description of the forms of false paths.

Georg Fabricius, who was the first to publish these five books among the Christian works of the ancient ecclesiastical poets, considers them—and on this point I do not disagree with him—to be the books mentioned by Tertullian at the beginning of Book I *Against Marcion* in these words:

“If we have previously composed anything **AGAINST MARCION**, let it henceforth fend for itself. We undertake a new work from an old one. I had afterward annulled my first little work, composed as though in haste, by a fuller composition. Before this second version had been adequately supplied with copies, I lost it through the fraud of a man who was then a brother and afterward an apostate; he happened to have transcribed certain portions with the utmost corruption and exhibited them to an audience.”

Together with Fabricius, I acknowledge that this statement is entirely true of the present work. Fabricius complains that there were almost as many errors as words and as many blemishes as verses. He adds that, although he removed very many errors from traces of the ancient text—you see that he possessed a manuscript communicated to him by Johann Herold—nevertheless, he says, “the reader will see from what remains how much still requires [Col. 1054C] correction.” The reader will perceive from our annotations that we too have attempted something in this regard; and we would have done somewhat more if any manuscript had been at hand.

The same scholar adds:

> “The subject itself shows this work of Tertullian to be unfinished. For, following his own impetus until time should permit correction, he did many things contrary to metrical rules. Some of these are excused by the circumstances of time; some can be excused as grammatical figures; and some must be attributed to the author’s haste, since he intended to bring his work, as though merely outlined, back to the anvil, as the saying goes. Such features include his willingness to employ caesura, his shortening of the ablative of the first declension, and his indulgence in unusual elisions. These poems are therefore presented not for imitation, but for recognition, because of the devotion—singular and weighty in this man—and the effort he devoted to sacred matters for the instruction of all posterity.”

Thus far Fabricius. If anyone wishes to know more precisely the licenses of the ancient Christian poets, and therefore of our author, let him read the Venerable Bede’s book *On the Metrical Art*. PAM.

# CHAPTER I

> The profound impiety of the Evil One [Note: [Col. 1053D] *The profound impiety of the Evil One, a mind envious of life, etc.* We have entitled this chapter: “On the Devil’s First Seduction of Man, and Then His Seduction into Idolatry”; for in Tertullian’s manner the poet calls the devil “the Evil One.” A little later we corrected *to be able to see* in place of *to seem*.], a mind envious of life,  
> After it captured deluded humankind with empty hope,  
> [Col. 1055A] Stripped them bare by persuading them wickedly to believe its falsehood—  
> Yet not with impunity; for immediately, as one guilty of the deed  
> And the reckless author of so great a crime,  
> He received his deserved curses. Then, still more insane,  
> Advancing further, a most desperate enemy,  
> He flooded human minds, now plunged in darkness.  
> Teaching them, forgetful of the Lord, to abandon certain hope,  
> To follow empty idols [Note: [Col. 1055C] *To follow empty idols, etc.* According to Bede in *On the Metrical Art*, the middle syllable of *idolum* is always short. Ecclesiastical poets, perhaps yielding to popular usage, employed it thus; consequently, the changes made by some scholars in several passages of Prudentius, Sedulius, and Arator in order to lengthen it run contrary to the trustworthiness of the ancient reading.], and fashion for themselves a throng of gods;  
> To follow lots, auguries, and the false names of stars;  
> To constrain the nativities of those being born [Note: [Col. 1055C] *To constrain the nativities of those being born, etc.* This is directed against astrologers who attempt to bind the *geneses* of those being born—that is, their births—to fixed outcomes. You have many passages on them above.],  
> To believe they could see through inspection of entrails and await events to come.  
> The dreadful magical audacity of the renegades [Note: [Col. 1055C] *Of renegades, etc.* Similarly below, chapter 6:  
>   
> “The dreadful renegade assigns savage gifts of death.”  
>   
> He uses *refuga* for a deserter, as does Prudentius in the *Peristephanon*. Again, at the beginning of Book II, “as the renegades breathe”; chapter 4, “the monstrous trophy of renegades”; and Book V, “which the mad renegade,” etc., “uttered.”],  
> Led them to admire a fabricated mass and despise life [Note: [Col. 1055D] *Led by audacity, etc.* Not otherwise a little later, “plunged by criminal madness”; and in the next chapter, “wisdom beneath veiled words”; likewise “by endurance of punishment”; finally, chapter 7, “full of magical madness,” and chapter 9, “with his own part abandoned,” etc.; “he did it by wisdom and art,” and “clothes him in his own glory”—and very frequently throughout these five books. In these instances he shortens ablatives in *-a*, which the ancients ordinarily lengthen, following the custom of Christian poets, as Bede relates in *On the Metrical Art*.],  
> And drove them headlong, plunged in criminal madness,  
> Rejoicing in blood, to threaten savage murders;  
> [Col. 1055B] Then to love, in another’s body, the wounding of flesh;  
> And, burning too, captivated by heated sweetness,  
> To transgress the covenants of nature and defile clean bodies,  
> Polluting the male sex with an unspeakable embrace;  
> To mingle feminine practices with the illicit pollution [Note: [Col. 1055D] *Pollution, etc.* He uses the same word below, Book IX, chapter 4: “the populace full of pollution,” for a polluted thing, or filth and disgrace.]  
> Of the common herd; and to regard chaste wombs, dedicated to offspring,  
> As instruments for obscene intercourse in the service of lust.  
>   
> Advancing in this way through times past, he accomplished such deeds  
> Through the hiding places of the soul, while the seps-serpent’s venom flowed [Note: [Col. 1055D] *While the seps-serpent’s venom flowed, etc.* The *seps*, says Epiphanius, *Heresies* 36, is a four-footed reptile resembling a newt. Its bite is regarded as causing no injury, but when its spittle is discharged into food or drink it immediately brings death upon those who consume it. Hence the comparison with the serpent-devil is most apt: through the eating of the forbidden tree he infected humankind with venom. Yet the following expression—“not because human beings might blame,” etc.—is obscure unless “the devil” is understood as the object of *blame*.].  
> It was not because human beings might blame him—for they followed willingly:  
> He persuaded through deceit; man accomplished it through freedom.

# CHAPTER II

> While the faithless one continually breathes out these things through the ages [Note: [Col. 1056C] *While the faithless one continually breathes out these things through the ages, etc.* We have given this chapter the title “On Christ, Redeemer of Fallen Man.” *Breathes out* is used here in the sense of infecting and corrupting with one’s breath. Yet the passage is defective down to the verse “A throng of prophets,” together with what follows. It is as though the author intended to say: Although the enemy of the human race hoped that he would retain forever among humankind the path of crime, he was first obstructed by the prophets’ words; and although he nevertheless continued to attempt secretly what he did not dare publicly, Christ the Redeemer came.],  
> [Col. 1055C] And implants his design in deluded hearts;  
> [Col. 1056A] Hoping—alas!—that he, the fool, possesses the path of crime,  
> Ignorant of what sentence awaits the deed.  
>   
> A throng of prophets, wisdom beneath veiled words,  
> Speaking with a foretelling voice from God’s Spirit [Note: [Col. 1056D] *From God’s Spirit, etc.* Not only here but frequently afterward in the books of poems, he shortens the letter *u* at the ends of verses. Such is the following in Book II, chapter 5: “and poured forth a pledge from his own Spirit,” and “life from the Spirit”; and Book IV, chapter 2: “no place confines within a circuit.” Bede observes that other Christian poets employed something similar. Of the same kind is the shortening of final *e* in the word *publice*, and of *i* a little later in “Son of God.” Yet the now-antiquated phrase “to curse the Lord” belongs to Tertullian’s diction, both here and below in chapter 6. Likewise, *fashioner* is presently used for one who bestowed form, as also below, chapter 4:  
>   
> “And he who fashioned living flesh by his breath.”  
>   
> And somewhere above.],  
> Caused him not to dare curse the Lord openly and plainly,  
> Though in his secret cunning he hoped he could remain hidden.  
>   
> At last the light of the soul is held captive in flesh;  
> The hope of the desperate strives, stronger than the enemy:  
> The Fashioner himself, himself the Renewer of the body;  
> The true Glory of the Father, Son of God, sole Author;  
> Judge and Lord, he comes, the glorious King of the world,  
> Ready to grant pardon to the oppressed and release the bound.  
> Through friendship with him as aid and endurance of punishment,  
> Both God and renewed man are mingled into one [Note: [Col. 1056D] *Both God and renewed man are mingled into one, etc.* The true doctrine concerning Christ’s incarnation and his two natures, divine and human, is found above in the book *Against Praxeas*, chapter 27.].  
>   
> A holy virgin gives birth; a new door of life has opened [Note: [Col. 1055C] *Is opened*, Pam., Obert.].  
> [Col. 1056B] The prophets’ words are proved fulfilled by deeds.  
> Priests leave the temples; guided also by a star,  
> They marvel at the Lord and distinguish so extraordinary a birth.  
>   
> Waters are turned into wine, a memorable sight;  
> Sight is restored to the blind, and at his command trembling  
> Demons are expelled; they cry out and confess Christ.  
> Limbs already wasting away are all healed by a word;  
> Now the lame man walks, the deaf man immediately hears hope,  
> The maimed extends his right hand, and the mute speaks mighty things.  
> At his command the sea grows calm and the winds fall still.  
> All things recognize the Lord; the enemy is confounded,  
> And now the savage foe, defeated in triumph, submits to hostile arms.
