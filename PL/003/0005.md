LII. On the Revenues of the Church and Their Use.—The Church had its own income and revenues, which it distributed each month among the clergy for their support. These distributions were called *mensurnae*, as though one were to say “monthly distributions.” Clerics who had fallen into some offense or had failed in their proper duty were deprived of them, as is gathered from the twenty-eighth Epistle, in which St. Cyprian speaks as follows [Col. XLII] [Note: [Col. XLII] Cyprian, Ep. XXVIII, vol. IV, col. 301 B. Cf. Ep. XXXIV, cols. 320 ff.]:

> You also requested that I write back concerning what I think should be done about Philumenus and Fortunatus, subdeacons, and Favorinus, an acolyte, who withdrew in the meantime and have now returned. . . . For the present, let them merely abstain from the monthly distribution, not so as to appear deprived of their ecclesiastical ministry, but so that, with all matters left intact, their cases may be deferred until we are present.

Another portion of these revenues was expended on alms for the poor. For in that age the Church both supported the poor, especially those “who, standing firm in the faith and fighting bravely, did not abandon the camp of Christ” [Note: [Col. XLII] The same, Ep. XXXVII, vol. IV, col. 329 A], and assisted destitute artisans by providing them with something so that they could practice their own trades. The Church’s charity toward the poor is indicated quite clearly in St. Cyprian’s Epistle to Euchratius. Speaking there about a certain actor whom he wished to recall from that infamous profession, he says [Note: [Col. XLII] The same, Ep. LXI, vol. IV, col. 363 A–B]:

> But if such a man pleads the want and necessity of poverty, his need too can be assisted among the others who are maintained by the provisions of the Church.

These alms, however, were moderate, and the poor were obliged to be content with more frugal food, as the same holy Doctor states in the same passage:

> Provided, however,

he says, speaking of the same actor,

> that he is content with more frugal but innocent food.

That the Church assisted poor artisans is proved by the authority of the same holy bishop, who in Epistle XLI addresses those to whom the Epistle was written as follows [Note: [Col. XLII] The same, Ep. XXXVIII, vol. IV, col. 330 A]:

> I sent you as my representatives, so that you might meet the needs of our brethren out of the funds, and, if any also wished to practice their trades, might assist their desires by an additional grant sufficient for the purpose.

For this reason a *corba*, that is, a box for receiving alms, was placed in the churches [Note: [Col. XLII] The same, *On Works and Almsgiving*, vol. IV, col. 612 B]. The faithful, even though poor, supplied “material for the sacrifice” [Note: [Col. XLII] The same, ibid.]; they also paid “tithes” [Note: [Col. XLII] The same, *On the Unity of the Church*, vol. IV, col. 518 B].

LIII. On the Care of the Sick.—Among the ministers of the Church were men entrusted with the duty of carrying the Eucharist to the poor and the sick and of baptizing catechumens who were mortally ill. The Roman clergy write [Note: [Col. XLII] Roman Clergy in Cyprian, Ep. II, vol. IV, col. 227 A–B]:

> We have also added the other matters incumbent upon you: that if any who have fallen into this trial begin to be overtaken by illness, repent of what they have done, and desire communion, assistance must certainly be given to them. Whether they are widows, or persons under affliction (*thlibomeni*) who cannot present themselves, or those who are in prison, or those driven from their homes, there must certainly be persons available to minister to them. Catechumens overtaken by illness must likewise not be disappointed, but assistance must be given to them.

LIV. *Sportulae*.—Besides the monthly distributions listed above [Col. XLIII], there were also others called *sportulae*, and the clerics who shared in them were called *sportulantes*. See what St. Cyprian says about these in his Epistle to Clelus of Furni. We must admit, however, that St. Cyprian did not express his meaning clearly concerning these *sportulae*; nor do the authors who discuss them explain themselves clearly enough for us to establish anything certain on the matter.

Rigaltius considers “to be held in the honor of the *sportulantes*,” as St. Cyprian writes, to mean the same thing as receiving a share in a particular distribution distinct from what the same holy Doctor calls the “monthly distribution” (*divisio mensurna*), which was the general distribution. For our part, we most readily add that only the poorer clergy shared in this particular distribution, which consisted of those alms of the faithful that were deposited in chests. Since these chests resembled a kind of basket, they were called *sportulae*; hence those who obtained from these alms part of what they needed to live were called *sportulantes*. I am of the opinion, however, that because of this special distribution the poor clergy were by no means excluded from the general monthly distribution, which consisted both of the Church’s revenues and of what each member of the faithful was obliged to contribute toward the support of the clergy.

LV. The Canonical Hours.—Besides the three hours of the day appointed for public prayer—namely, the third, sixth, and ninth—St. Cyprian likewise mentions morning prayer, saying [Note: [Col. XLIII] Cyprian, *On the Lord’s Prayer*, vol. IV, col. 543 B–C ff.] that one must pray in the morning so that the Lord’s resurrection may be celebrated by morning prayer. He likewise speaks of another prayer made at the end of the day; this corresponds to the prayer that we call Vespers.

Moreover, I do not know whether what the holy Doctor says in the same place about nocturnal prayers may be interpreted as referring to Vigils [Note: [Col. XLIII] “Let there be no loss of prayers during the hours of the night, no idle and slothful neglect of prayer.” Cyprian, loc. cit., col. 545 A. It is quite well known that Christians held assemblies at night. Tertullian calls them “nocturnal gatherings” in Book II *To His Wife*. Pliny calls them “assemblies before dawn” in his *Letter to Trajan*. But in addition, out of private devotion, the faithful tore themselves from bed and rose during the night to pray, as Tertullian says in the passage cited above. Thus in the *Apologeticum*: “They satisfy themselves in such a way as to remember that they must worship God even during the night.”]. On these premises, we have all the canonical hours except Prime and Compline.

Besides these public prayers, there were others performed in private homes, such as prayer before food, which St. Cyprian mentions in his treatise *To Donatus* [Note: [Col. XLIII] Cyprian, Epistle I, *To Donatus*, otherwise the book *To Donatus*, vol. IV, col. 222 B ff.], and which the same holy Doctor presents as a custom already extremely ancient in his own age.

LVI. On the Visions of the Faithful.—From several passages in the works of St. Cyprian it is clearly evident that in his age “many manifest visions occurred” [Note: [Col. XLIII] The same, Ep. LIV, vol. III, col. 855 A], and that “even the innocent age of children sees with its eyes in ecstasy, and hears and speaks those things by which the Lord deigns to admonish and instruct us” [Col. XLIV] [Note: [Col. XLIV] The same, Ep. IX, vol. IV, col. 253 A]. He reproves those, however, to whom these appeared to be “ridiculous dreams and foolish visions” [Note: [Col. XLIV] The same, Ep. LXIX, vol. IX, col. 407 A].

We may likewise observe from St. Cyprian that black garments were formerly used in times of mourning. For he writes in his treatise *On Mortality* [Note: [Col. XLIV] The same, treatise *On Mortality*, vol. IV, col. 596 A]:

> The dead should be missed, not mourned, nor should black garments be assumed here when they have already received white garments in heaven.

ARTICLE IV. LITERARY ANNALS

§ I. Literary Annals of the Lesser Fathers

NINTH CENTURY

Around the Year 850

THE UNIQUE ARNOBIUS–MINUCIUS MANUSCRIPT.—“Only a single manuscript has thus far been discovered. From it Arnobius first issued and was corrected again and again; it is now in the Royal Library at Paris. It would be desirable for some learned man who has access to this royal manuscript to describe it accurately; to prove with sound arguments in what century it appears to have been written; and, above all, to determine whether it was written in characters or scribal abbreviations so unsightly that distinguished scholars—whom we name in the appropriate place—could repeatedly work upon it, never without harvesting a new crop of variant readings.”

Thus writes the distinguished Schoenemann. Although for more than two centuries all who have read Minucius or Arnobius have unanimously requested this, nevertheless, unless I am mistaken, no one has yet been found to undertake the task energetically—not Arnobius’s latest editor, J. C. Orelli, nor the recent Lyon translator of Minucius, although it was fitting for them above all others to assume this responsibility. Since the others have failed, therefore, we shall say a few words, to the best of our ability, in description of this unique manuscript.

That manuscript, formerly in the Vatican and now preserved in the Royal Library at Paris as a gift from Pope Leo X, under no. 1661—formerly 1344 or 3975—is firmly bound in smooth, elegant, and intact parchment; in quarto format; almost two inches taller than it is wide; gilded and polished on the front, but ornamented on the back with three small golden lilies; enclosed between two wooden boards, with the clasps torn from each; covered on both sides with black calfskin sprinkled with various ornaments, among which the emblems of Henry II and the arms of Diana of Poitiers stand out. It bears no title other than the single name *ARNOBIVS*, affixed both to the cover and to the first folio. It consists of twenty-three gatherings of eight folios, in which sixty-two folios contain Arnobius and twenty-eight contain the *Octavius*.

These are the more noteworthy paleographical and grammatical features. The manuscript is written in an uncial or Carolingian minuscule hand, certainly elegant, pure, and thoroughly genuine, unmixed with any Merovingian or Lombard variety. It is divided at the beginnings of the individual chapters by red [Col. XLV] rustic capitals—the first alone excepted—without any pseudomorphic or decorated letters. The two elements of the diphthong *ae* are usually written separately; yet sometimes, in a practice that appears to derive from Lombard script, they are represented by a single letter *e* with a small hook beneath it. The upper strokes of projecting letters appear to swell at the top. In the letters *m*, *n*, and *u*, the initial strokes sometimes bend to the right, but more commonly to the left. The letter *a* is completed by a tighter lower loop, as in uncial script, and is nowhere enclosed from its apex, as in cursive script; *c*, meanwhile, curves around in a rounded form whenever it occurs.

As for the remaining orthographical rather than paleographical signs, we shall add a few observations. The dative and ablative plural endings are represented merely by a point, for example *nostris pedib.*; *esse* and *esset* appear respectively as *ee* and *eet*; *est* is sometimes written *ê*. The letter *m* is very frequently omitted; each omission is ordinarily indicated by a horizontal stroke. Few ligatures occur, almost none except in the syllables *re* and *ra*, in the conjunction *et*, and where the last letters of one word join the first letters of another. Otherwise, the writing abounds in errors; punctuation is placed at random; and the text is divided into inappropriate sections.

Nevertheless, this unique manuscript of two excellent writers is of the greatest value—indeed, beyond all valuation. Its date is not difficult to determine: from the characteristics described above, especially the forms of the letters *m*, *n*, and *u*, and with the support of many authorities of no mean standing, it may safely be assigned to the first part of the ninth century; it appears nearer the middle than the beginning of that century.

In the Same Year

HEIDELBERG EDITION, the first issued under the author’s proper name. Francis Baudouin was the first to restore the *Octavius*, which until then had been regarded as the eighth book of Arnobius, to Minucius. Yet Hadrianus Junius, a Dutch physician and the most celebrated philologist of his age, had detected the fraud before Baudouin. Indeed, that honor belongs not to Junius but to Antoine Morillon, secretary and librarian to the Most Eminent Cardinal Granvelle, to whom several letters from Junius are extant and to whom Junius himself assigns this credit in his *Critical Observations*, Book III, chapter 1, new edition, Rotterdam, 1709.

Otherwise, this edition of Baudouin [Col. XLVI] presents the bare text. A preface is added, in which both a defense of Minucius Felix as the true author is offered from the testimony of Jerome and Lactantius and from the difference between his style and form of writing and those of Arnobius, and an outline and analysis of the entire book is presented. Baudouin dedicated his Minucius to Frederick, Elector Palatine, as though he were consecrating with these first fruits of the Muses the University of Heidelberg, which that prince had recently established. From the workshop of Ludwig Lucius, printer, Heidelberg, in octavo.

SEVENTEENTH CENTURY

In the Year 1603

SECOND BASEL EDITION, from the Froben bookshop, in octavo. *The Octavius of Marcus Minucius Felix and Cyprian’s On the Vanity of Idols*. Johann von Wower edited it. He supplied no preface. He frequently cites readings that for the most part do not agree at all with the Royal manuscript; they have no credibility, since the good man allowed himself to be deceived by someone who falsely claimed that they were found thus in an excellent manuscript. He prepared his notes, learned though not always appropriately placed, in the space of ten days. In the more difficult passages, however, he had the assistance of the most learned Scaliger.

1603

FIRST HANOVER EDITION. Geverhart Elmenhorst, from Wechel’s press, in octavo, with Arnobius, appears to have published nothing but the bare text in this year. Later he added a vast crowd of authors and passages illustrating the same subject, and nothing besides. This nevertheless gave rise to dissensions, quarrels, and insults between two men; on this matter Scaliger’s Epistles CCLX–CCLXIII should be read.

FIRST HAMBURG EDITION, in folio. *The Octavius of Marcus Minucius Felix, according to the recension and with the commentaries of Geverhart Elmenhorst; with Francis Baudouin’s Prolegomena to the Octavius of Marcus Minucius Felix appended*. Rigaltius observed that Elmenhorst’s notes were interspersed with Wower’s commentaries and that Elmenhorst often cited a manuscript where they should merely have been called readings of the Royal manuscript. He was, of course, citing that manuscript on the authority of Wower, as may be learned from what we wrote above concerning Wower’s edition. Otherwise, in his customary manner, he appends entire pages from ancient authors dealing with a similar subject, believing that Minucius is splendidly illuminated by this cloud of witnesses.

1613

FOURTH PARIS EDITION, Paris, in quarto, printed by Pierre Mettayer. *The Octavius of Marcus Minucius Felix. Desiderius Heraldus emended it with the aid of the Royal manuscript and added notes.*—Compare what we have said concerning Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, edited by the same man in this year. He had already arranged for the text printed here to be published with Arnobius in 1605; now at last he adorned it with notes learned and refined after his fashion.

In sum, he professes to have followed the Roman edition of Fulvio Orsini under this rule: he would not depart from that edition, which he considered the most faithful of all, unless the Royal manuscript led the way. Thus he retained even readings that revealed their corruption at first glance, and did not follow the example of Sigismund Gelenius, who had not hesitated to alter all such readings—meaning the corrupt ones. Nevertheless, and I wish this to be said in praise of the man, he did not abandon several reasonably good readings of Gelenius but preferred them to those of the Roman edition. He had obtained the Royal manuscript through the kindness of de Thou. He dedicated this little book to Jean Bochard, counselor of the Sacred Consistory, etc.—Fabricius mentions another edition of the *Octavius* with Heraldus’s observations, published with Arnobius in 1615, in quarto; nevertheless, neither Fabricius himself in his treatment of Arnobius nor anyone besides him mentions it. (*Latin Library*, Book IV, chapter 3, Venice edition, vol. II, p. 282; and *Argentoratum Delights*, p. 216.)

[Col. XLIX]—The flood of notes produced by this most futile commentator far surpasses all the others in sheer volume. To every single word of Minucius he attached, with incredible dullness and indiscretion, everything relevant and irrelevant scraped together from every quarter. Nor did he draw from a store of his own reading, but from heaps amassed by others with an entirely different purpose.

As for the text, he boasts that he accomplished many excellent things in the separation and punctuation of the words; let the judgment concerning them rest with others. Ouzel dedicated his work to Christina of Sweden. Firmicus was edited according to Wower’s recension and with Wower’s observations alone.

1672

SECOND OUZEL EDITION. Leiden, from the Hackius press: a reprint of Ouzel’s edition, though not reproduced exactly. In an arrangement ingeniously devised by the printers, the whole mass of notes—which in the first Ouzel edition marched in procession after the text—here presses upon the text itself. Thus, because the observations of the individual authors, distinguished by numbers, are repeated six times or more alongside the same lines, one can scarcely discern the text amid the text.

They newly added, likewise broken into pieces, Meursius’s *Minucian Critique*. Finally, in the most amusing development of all, a separate heading, “Variant Readings,” appears among the observations of the other commentators. You will scarcely guess where these readings were brought from unless you have a thorough knowledge of this bookseller’s diligence in embellishing the edition. They are, in fact, the variant readings that Elmenhorst presented separately at the end of his edition. Just as he had gathered them from the individual annotations of previous editors, so the printers reproduced each in its proper place and then printed them again as a collection.

Halle, at the expense of Johann Friedrich Zeitler and Heinrich Mussel, in octavo. *The Octavius of Minucius and Cyprian; Both Revised and Illustrated by Christoph Cellarius*.—To serve the needs of schools—in keeping with the diligence of this man, who deserved exceedingly well of the young—he arranged for the text of Minucius to be printed in a form improved by the observations of earlier editors and also of Johann Friedrich Gronovius, to whom he deservedly gives first place. He explained it with notes that were neither numerous nor lengthy, but excellent and beneficial to readers at every point, whether they concerned the reading itself, history and mythology, or, finally, Latinity.

He added Cyprian’s little book following Wower’s text, and Baudouin’s dissertation as well. Finally, because they had been supplied only later, after the text had already been printed, certain observations by Jacob Perizonius were added at the end. Lastly—and this is no small merit of the book—he was the first to divide Minucius’s text appropriately into chapters and paragraphs.

EIGHTEENTH CENTURY

1703

THIRD ENGLISH TRANSLATION. *A Dialogue, by Marcus Minucius Felix*, translated into English by M. Combe. London, in octavo.

1707

DAVIES EDITION, THIRD SCHOOL EDITION. Cambridge, printed by the University Press at the expense of John Owen, in octavo.—*The Octavius of Minucius, with the Complete Observations of Nicolas Rigaltius and Selections from Others, Revised and Furnished with His Own Notes by John Davies*.—Francis Baudouin’s dissertation is placed first, and indexes of subject matter and Latinity are appended.

Since Davies considered Rigaltius the leader of the whole company of Minucius’s interpreters, although he conceded that Rigaltius was not safe from every danger of error, he took particular care with his work. Yet he did not neglect the others, nor did he allow observations on Minucius made here and there by learned men while treating other subjects to escape him, whenever any usefulness could be found in them. He adopted Cellarius’s division of the text. Otherwise, this most learned man judges his own work very modestly, although for educating and sharpening young minds it is more useful than the works of many others. [Col. LI]

1708

FOURTH ENGLISH TRANSLATION. *M. Minucius Felix’s Octavius and Tertullian’s Apology for the Primitive Christians*. London, in octavo.

1709

GRONOVIUS EDITION. Leiden, from Cornelis Boutesteyn and Samuel Luchtmans, in octavo.—*The Octavius of Minucius, with the Complete Notes of Wower, Elmenhorst, Heraldus, and Rigaltius, and Others Collected from Various Sources, According to the Recension of Jacob Gronovius, Who Added His Own Corrections and Explanations. Caecilius Cyprian and Julius Firmicus Maternus Are Appended.*—I suppose the booksellers had to be granted this much: that Gronovius should permit the complete notes of the men praised on the title page to be repeated, although he, if anyone, passed a sound judgment upon them.

As a second part of his plan, he carefully sought out and examined scholarly work on Minucius published elsewhere—that is, annotations written on passages of Minucius when occasion arose in commentaries on various authors, whether for illustration or correction. In this connection he especially values Price’s notes on Apuleius.

What study he himself devoted to Minucius will, I think, be best shown by his own words:

> The doctrine implanted in the Dialogue had supplied a cheerful abundance. Moreover, I carried the subject matter of the little book fixed in my memory, and reverence was present for the Latin diction employed so chastely by the writer; thus, having pursued various matters in the course of reading, I was ready for an immense comparison. Although, besides Sabaeus and Orsini and besides Heraldus and Rigaltius, Joseph Scaliger and Salmasius too had worked on collating the manuscript that is at Paris, nevertheless, for a long time I learned of no one journeying there whom I did not exhort and urge to inspect it once again. This very thing also provided the occasion for a sharpened keenness in detecting various possibilities and in restoring the ancient hand or bringing forward the writer’s intended meaning, from which others wandered even while judging themselves especially successful and acute.

At the passage in chapter XXI concerning the Ephesian Diana constructed with spits, he appended an entire dissertation by Lucas Holstenius—certainly a learned and indeed ingenious dissertation, but one whose substance alone might perhaps more suitably have been given [Note: [Col. LI] See above, where we thought that the same dissertation ought to be published. EDITORS.].

Do not, however, be offended that he devotes himself wholly to attacking and ridiculing Ouzel, who scarcely deserved even to be named by him: one must know Jacob Gronovius. That age delighted in placing coins and engraved figures in editions of ancient authors; they contribute nothing to their utility.

1724

SECOND ENGLISH EDITION OF NOVATIAN. Oxford, from the Sheldonian Theatre, in octavo. *All the Extant Works of Novatian, Published Far More Correctly than Ever Before and Illustrated with Notes by Edward Welchman.*

The little works preserved among those of Tertullian and another [text] appended to Cyprian’s Epistles are presented. Welchman, opposing Whiston, reproduced Gelenius’s text. He says that “with the greatest care he applied a healing hand to all the errors from which it suffered throughout, both in words and in the division of clauses.”

For these claims he endured Jackson’s sharp and not wholly undeserved criticism. Jackson objected that by following Gelenius he had reintroduced errors removed by Pamelius and had, on the whole, left more errors than he eliminated, although he could easily have cleansed away a large proportion of them if he had not neglected the English manuscript transcribed by Pamelius and had not remained entirely ignorant of Fulvio Orsini’s manuscript observations copied by Johann Wower, which he could have obtained.

Jackson adds a second criticism: Welchman had indeed restored dislocated chapters to their proper places, but in this had a predecessor in the author of the *Reply against Dr. Waterland’s Defense of Certain Questions Published by Him concerning the Controversy over the Trinity*, yet did not mention him; and as for the brief notes, he added merely one or two and a very imperfect index of passages of Holy Scripture. This will perhaps appear to many to have been stated by Jackson somewhat unfairly.

1728

THIRD ENGLISH EDITION OF NOVATIAN. London, printed by John Darby and T. Brown, at the expense of John Noon in the street called Cheapside, next to the Mercers’ Chapel.—*All the Surviving Works of Novatian, Presbyter of Rome, Corrected According to Earlier Editions after the Recension of Jacob Pamelius of Bruges and Purged of Many Errors; Also Illustrated with Very Copious Observations* [Col. LIII] *and Notes, in Which the Author’s Entire Argument concerning the Rule of Faith Is Discussed at Length from the Monuments of the Ancient Fathers. Preceded by a Dissertation on the Son of God as Homoousios (ὁμοούσιος), or Co-essential with the One God the Father. By John Jackson, Presbyter of the Church of England and Prebendary of Wherwell in the County of Hampshire.*

Besides the material contained in the preceding edition, this one also includes the second Epistle of the Roman clergy to Cyprian, of which we spoke above. As for the method he followed in reviewing the text, he declares that he never departed from the Pamelian copy unless the author’s meaning and reasoning plainly required correction, or he had other authoritative supporters or the testimony of manuscripts on his side.

In this matter he acknowledges that he owed something to Latino Latini and François du Jon, but that he had received the greatest assistance from the English manuscript and Fulvio Orsini’s manuscript notes. He relates that he expended a great deal of labor in comparing the different translations of Scripture and the citations of the ancient Fathers with the archetypal text, so that he might gather together and present to the reader all noteworthy variant readings deducible from any text cited by Novatian.

> I undertook this labor,

he says,

> not because I wished everyone to think that I had accomplished something, but because I wished, if possible, to move others by my example to follow this method in editing ancient books and to collect all the variant readings of every passage of Scripture cited in each writer; and I trust that this will contribute greatly to advancing the correction and knowledge of Holy Scripture.

Finally, the many careful notes and observations, the learned prolegomena, the very full indexes, and the evident care devoted even to lesser matters show that Jackson performed the editor’s office admirably.
