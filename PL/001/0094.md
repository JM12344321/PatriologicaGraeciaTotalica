Why then, someone will ask, was that [Col. 0869A] name so detestable, and why was it always taken in a bad sense not only by Christians but also by gentiles? Do not Augustine himself and other Latin writers, as we have observed elsewhere (*Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, art. 4), testify that the Greek term δαίμονες (*daimones*, “demons”) means the same thing, namely those knowledgeable and skilled in affairs? Moreover, Tertullian, in this book of the *Apology*, informs us that the name “demons” is the same as “genii”: “Do you not know,” he says, “that genii are called demons, and hence, in the diminutive form, *daimonia*?” And shortly afterward: “demons, that is, genii” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 32, col. 447). Elsewhere, moreover: “Genii are assigned to everyone, which is the name of demons” (Idem, *On the Soul*, ch. 39, col. 341). What, then, is so evil in that name, and so detestable to each and every person? It would indeed be well if they did not misuse their knowledge, skill, [Col. 0869B] and powers of intellect. But because, as we shall see later, they thoroughly abuse these things to torment and deceive human beings, it is surely no wonder if everyone detested this name and applied it to others in curses.

Although our Author has transmitted little about the good Angels in the *Apology*, he nevertheless distinguishes them from the evil ones, and the latter from demons, with sufficient clarity. Concerning the former, indeed, he says this, though very briefly: “How, from certain angels corrupted by their own will, a still more corrupt race of demons arose and was condemned by God together with the founders of the race and with the one whom we call its prince, is learned in due order from the Holy Scriptures” (Idem, *Apology*, ch. 22, col. 405). Yet in a few words he encompasses many things, which must not be reviewed so briefly or without certain [Col. 0869C] observations.

First, he declares that certain Angels were corrupted. He thus signifies that there were many others who were not corrupted, whom for that reason we call good, and whom we rightly and deservedly believe to be blessed because of their perseverance in the good. Second, those same Angels were endowed with free choice of the will, by which, through acting well or badly, they could merit reward or punishment. Third, some of them were corrupted and, through abuse of their free power, fell into certain sins for which they were condemned by God. Fourth, they had a prince, of whom he had said shortly before: “Satan, the prince of this evil race” (*Ibid.*). Elsewhere, moreover, he calls him an Angel “whom God, after the pattern of His good works, established as good and wisest of all; both by his original constitution, [Col. 0869D] according to Ezekiel’s prophecy, a good Angel, and by his own will corrupted” (Tertullian, bk. II, *Against Marcion*, ch. 10). Then further: “He was blameless from the day of his creation, created by God for good, as by a good Creator of blameless creations, adorned with all angelic glory, and placed in God’s presence as one good in the presence of the Good; but afterward he transferred himself into evil . . . . . . he himself no less having been established with free choice, as a spirit” (*Ibid.*, col. 462). Finally, he adds that this Satan fell from his exalted position through his own transgression, of which he himself was the author. We have treated him in our dissertation on the books of Lactantius (*Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, art. 3).

What, however, was the sin of this Angel and of the other wicked angels whom he dragged to [Col. 0870A] their destruction? Tertullian explained it with complete clarity neither there nor in the *Apology*. In another book, however, he appears to indicate two of their sins in these words: “Let nothing be imputed to those angels except [others read: beyond] their rejection of heaven and marriage with flesh” (Tertullian, *On the Apparel of Women*, ch. 4). Hence elsewhere he calls them “Angels who deserted God, lovers of women, and for that reason condemned by God” (Idem, *On Idolatry*, ch. 9). Does he not distinguish there a twofold sin of the angels: the first, whereby they rejected heaven and deserted God; and the second, their perverse love of women and marriage with flesh?

Concerning this second offense of theirs, moreover, he speaks as follows: “Those angels,” he says, “who rushed down from heaven to the daughters of men are condemned and appointed to the punishment of death, so that this disgrace too might attach to woman” [Col. 0870B] (Idem, *On the Apparel of Women*, ch. 2). But if disgrace attached to women because of this crime of the Angels, and if for that reason, as we have just seen stated by our same Author, the Angels were condemned, why then, someone will ask, does he call it simply “marriage with flesh,” rather than criminal fornication? To anyone who therefore asks this of us, we shall reply in these very words of Tertullian himself: “The sons of God, seeing that the daughters of men were beautiful, took wives for themselves from all whom they chose. Here the Greek term for women carries the sense of ‘wives,’ because marriage is mentioned. When, therefore, it says ‘daughters of men,’ it manifestly indicates virgins who were still reckoned as belonging to their parents—for married women [Col. 0870C] are designated as belonging to their husbands—although it could have said ‘wives of men.’ Likewise, it does not call the angels adulterers but husbands, since they took the unmarried daughters of men, whom it had called ‘born’ above, thereby also signifying virgins: above, daughters born; but here, women married to angels” (Idem, *On the Veiling of Virgins*, ch. 7). He therefore calls it the marriage of the transgressing Angels because they took daughters of men who were not married but virgins, and for that reason the Angels themselves are not called adulterers.

Nevertheless, he did not consider this marriage lawful, but altogether unlawful and criminal, because of which disgrace attached to women and condemnation to the angels. Hence in another book he says: “The Apostle also knew,” he says, “that the spiritual wickedness of the Angels who were led into sin by the daughters of men had been at work in the heavens” (Idem, bk. V, *Against* [Col. 0870D] *Marcion*, ch. 18). But we have discussed this criminal marriage of the wicked angels with the daughters of men—or rather, indeed, their fornication—not merely once, both in our dissertation on the works of Lactantius and in the other places noted there (*Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, arts. 3 and 4).

In the fifth and final place, it remains to be observed that Tertullian asserted in the *Apology*, without any hesitation, that from the intercourse of those corrupted Angels with the daughters of men there arose a still more corrupt race of demons. Yet no fewer difficulties arise in this opinion than in the other opinion concerning the marriage of Angels with women. These difficulties are increased by what he determines concerning the nature of those same demons and of [Col. 0871A] Angels, whether good or evil. These matters must first be investigated by us, and others afterward.

## ARTICLE II. Whether Tertullian believed the nature of all Angels and demons to be spiritual; how great a difficulty arises in this matter from the origin of the latter from wicked angels and the daughters of men; concerning the envy of those same demons, by which they conspire to destroy the human beings to whom they are subject

Tertullian begins his discussion of Angels and demons with these words in the *Apology*: “We say that there are certain spiritual substances” (Tertullian, *Apology*, [Col. 0871B] ch. 22, p. 404). In the preceding article, moreover, we saw it stated of Satan, the most eminent of the Angels and an Archangel, as Tertullian himself says, that he was established by God with free choice “as a spirit” (Idem, bk. II, *Against Marcion*, ch. 7). The Creator, moreover, as he further writes (*Ibid.*, bk. III, ch. 9), made all Angels as truly spirits as He made His ministers a blazing fire (Psalm 103, v. 4; Hebrews, v. 7). He therefore never doubted that all Angels are spirits and that their substance is spiritual.

But it is disputed what he understands by the term “spirit”: whether a substance wholly distinct from every corporeal thing, or some extremely fine and subtle body that escapes all human sight. Nor, indeed, can we deny that this [Col. 0871C] dispute cannot easily be resolved by anyone. Nevertheless, we shall attempt to ascertain what can be stated with greater certainty about this opinion of our Tertullian.

First, therefore, it must be noted how he argues against the heretics who denied that Christ assumed a true body, and how he refutes their error. Those heretics inferred from the example of Angels, who are said once to have appeared to the patriarchs clothed in human flesh, that Christ’s flesh was neither real nor born. Tertullian, however, contends that the flesh of the Angels was not born, whereas Christ’s was, because it was not they but Christ who was born in order to be crucified and to die: “For it is agreed,” he says, “that the Angels did not bear flesh properly their own, being natures [another reading: *of a nature*] of spiritual substance, although perhaps of some kind of body, yet one of their own kind; they could, however, be transformed [Col. 0871D] into flesh for a time, so that they might be seen and might associate with human beings” (Tertullian, *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 6). He therefore acknowledges that these Angels assumed human flesh for a time. Although that flesh was real, it was nevertheless not their own flesh, because, as he affirms without hesitation, their substance is spiritual. But because either he himself or others thought it possible to doubt whether, as we noted above (supra, ch. 7, art. 3), there was anything intermediate between body and nothingness, he therefore added there, so as to leave no opportunity for quibbling: “although perhaps of some kind of body, yet one of its own kind.” It is just as if he had said: If someone more obstinately contends that absolutely every substance [Col. 0872A] is some kind of body, I shall not argue the point now, provided it be granted to me that angelic substance, if it perhaps is a body, is a body of its own kind. By the term “body,” however, as we saw elsewhere (*loc. cit.*), he means nothing other than any existing thing that is opposed to a nonexistent thing and to nothingness. May it not therefore be inferred, without absurdity, that he considered the nature of Angels to be purely spiritual, though it can nevertheless be called a body of its own kind because it is not nothing and truly exists?

By precisely the same argument he attacks the same error of Marcion: “All the more,” he says, “the Angels, who were not appointed to die for us, ought not to have taken on their brief use of flesh through birth, because they were not going to lay it aside through death . . . If the Creator makes the Angels [Col. 0872B] spirits, and His ministers a blazing fire, He makes them as truly spirits as He also makes them truly fire” (Tertullian, bk. III, *Against Marcion*, ch. 9). Do you hear how openly he declares that the Angels who were seen by the patriarchs in human form were as truly spirits as the human flesh they had assumed was real? That flesh was not properly their own, but was given to them by God for a brief time so that they might associate and converse with human beings. Their substance is therefore different from that of human beings: namely, spiritual, by which, being superior to human beings, they truly are spirits.

If anyone desires more, let him consult Tertullian’s book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, where he will be able to read these very words: “The Angels were at one time like human beings in eating and drinking and extending their feet for washing; for they had put on a human [Col. 0872C] exterior, while their inner substance remained intact” (Idem, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 62). What, however, was that proper substance and nature of the angels, concealed beneath a human appearance? He himself explains it in the same passage as follows: “Having become like human beings, the Angels undertook fleshly activity while remaining in the same substance of spirit” (*Ibid.*). By what clearer words, I ask you, could it have been expressed that the Angels truly are spirits, and that their spiritual nature admits absolutely nothing of matter or body?

What we have related thus far concerns the nature in which all Angels were created, which he quite openly asserts to be spiritual—had he not obscured this view, as though it were covered by a certain black mist and dense [Col. 0872D] darkness, through what he taught concerning wicked Angels and demons. For he calls all of them spirits, no less than the good Angels. Nor is this difficult to prove. For it has already been observed that he wrote in general of all Angels, both good and evil: “We say that there are certain spiritual substances.” Afterward, even more explicitly: “Every spirit is winged; such are both Angels and demons” (Idem, *Apology*, ch. 22, p. 404). Again: “Angels and demons, in substance the vilest spirits” (*Ibid.*, ch. 39, col. 468). The prince of the wicked Angels too, of whom we spoke in the preceding article, is called by him “a spirit of demonic and angelic constitution” (*Ibid.*, ch. 27, col. 433), that is, a spirit possessing the [Col. 0873A] dispositions and qualities of demons and Angels. But more will be said about this term below, in the following article.

What, furthermore, of the fact that, as we saw above (preceding article), the same Tertullian expressly pronounces that both this Satan and all the other wicked angels were created in the same manner as the good? Indeed, the demons themselves, he says, cannot deny that they are unclean spirits (Tertullian, *ibid.*, ch. 23, col. 410). Finally, he says that their subtlety and fineness are exceedingly great: by their swiftness they assail both substances of the human being, are everywhere in a single moment, and the whole globe of the earth is a single place to them. Moreover, he says: “Invisible and imperceptible, they appear in their effect rather than in their action” (*Ibid.*, ch. 22, p. 406). Do not all these things demonstrate quite manifestly [Col. 0873B] that Tertullian was persuaded that evil angels and demons, no less than good angels, were spirits?

Yet this view seems to be contradicted by what we noted above that he taught: that wicked angels polluted themselves through criminal intercourse with women, by which they begot demons. But someone might reply that the wicked Angels, just like the good, assumed human flesh, as we were saying shortly before, through which they could have intercourse with women. Yet another will immediately argue in reply that it must be proved from what source the wicked Angels took human flesh for some period of time. For although Tertullian openly pronounces more than once, as we have noted, that this flesh was given to the good Angels by God, no one can prove that he similarly [Col. 0873C] assigned it to the wicked Angels so that they might violate women.

Grant, nevertheless, that in some way or other, whether by their deceptions or by other impious arts, they put on such flesh: a greater difficulty certainly remains concerning the demons. For of what kind can those demons be as spirits, whom Tertullian supposes to have arisen from an unlawful union with unmarried virgins? How could there be begotten from them the finest, most subtle, invisible substances, surpassing the speed of birds, which penetrate the bodies and souls of human beings and are everywhere in a single moment? It certainly cannot be denied that this matter is enveloped in the greatest obscurity. For if Tertullian believed the demons to have some body, he undoubtedly did not consider it either human or fashioned in human form. [Col. 0873D] What, then? Did he think that they were certain monsters, neither angels nor human beings? Yet is that very idea not itself a monstrosity of absurd opinion? Nevertheless, Lactantius, as we observed in its proper place (*Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, art. 4), appears to have drawn from this his opinion that the nature of demons is in some way intermediate between angels and human beings. Tertullian, however, at least in his *Apology*, defined the matter neither fully openly nor absolutely, but said that their nature was that which “is learned in due order from the Holy Scriptures” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 22, col. 403). It would indeed have been excellent if in his other books he had kept himself within these limits of true doctrine.

Besides, even if he held that the nature of demons was [Col. 0874A] equal neither to the angelic nor to the human, but something intermediate between the two, it must not therefore immediately be inferred that he denied Angels to be spirits. For if he attributed any body to demons, he thought it, as we have seen, to possess such fineness and subtlety that none greater could exist. He appears, moreover, to have been persuaded that the nature of good Angels was more excellent than that of demons. But in what manner, I ask you, can it be more excellent unless it is purely spiritual and possesses absolutely nothing corporeal? How much weight the arguments we have thus far presented have in proving this, we leave to others to judge. Yet no one, unless we are entirely mistaken, will deny that, if there is any error of Tertullian’s in all these matters, none is worse than that by which he attempts to explain the intercourse of the wicked angels with the daughters [Col. 0874B] of men and the generation of demons from it. But see, I ask, what was discussed on these matters in the preceding dissertation (*Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, art. 5), and whether his opinion can thereby be brought back to the rules of truth.

No one, however, will doubt the truth of what he afterward relates concerning Satan and the demons. “Our Satan,” he says, “on account of his separation”—namely, from God—“and on account of his own sin, is a rival,” or an adversary and enemy, “and, because of God’s grace granted to Christians, is envious.” He has always attempted, through hidden inspiration and by suborning the gentiles, to persuade them to turn Christians away from the worship of the true God by every kind of torture and to compel them to worship false gods (Tertullian, *ibid.*, ch. 27, p. 432). For although “the whole power of demons and of a spirit of that kind is subject to us” (*Ibid.*, [Col. 0874C] col. 433), that is, the power of Satan . . . . yet “after the manner of convicts in rebellious workhouses, mines, or some such form of penal servitude, they break out against us Christians”; that is, they break out in the manner of those who, confined in workhouses and prisons, or condemned to mine metals or to some other form of penal servitude, oppose and resist their masters. Those whom these wicked demons were unable to bring to the worship of themselves and of false gods by violence and armed force, they attempted to suborn by every other kind of device, deception, fraud, and evil art. But what our Author has taught about them must be explained at somewhat greater length.

## ARTICLE III. How Tertullian shows that certain things which the pagans attributed to their gods as true miracles were accomplished by the arts and deceptions of demons

[Col. 0874D]

Tertullian certainly devotes much effort, and deservedly so, to explaining and exposing the “operation,” as he himself says, of demons (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 22, col. 405), that is, the devices, deceptions, tricks, frauds, and other evil arts by which they appeared to produce false miracles, oracles, and many other things outside the usual order of nature. For the pagans attributed all these things, which they believed to be real, to the power of their gods, and from this concluded that their gods were true and their religion true. Tertullian therefore strikes and utterly overthrows this last stronghold of theirs with a twofold argument, [Col. 0875A] as though with a double battering ram. First, he shows that those extraordinary deeds were produced not by their gods, as they supposed, but by the perverse arts of demons. Then he shows that those same demons, when adjured by Christians, openly confess that these things were performed by none other than themselves, who were not gods but demons only.

To demonstrate the first proposition more clearly, he lays down as certain (*Ibid.*)—what no one can deny—that the substance of demons, as we saw in the preceding article, is not only exceedingly subtle and fine, but that their powers are also spiritual and their swiftness so great that in a single moment they can be everywhere and penetrate every individual thing, even the very substance of a human being. Since they are thus constituted, therefore, “their [Col. 0875B] spiritual malice,” says Tertullian, “began from the beginning of the world for the destruction of humanity” (*Ibid.*), namely, when it deceived Eve and Adam as they lived their happy life in the earthly Paradise. By the same malice, they strive to lead other human beings into error and every kind of crime, and especially into idolatry and the worship of themselves under the name of false gods.

To begin with the miracles which the pagans attributed to their gods, our Author demonstrates in the following way that they were performed by demons. Equipped, he says, with such powers, both spiritual and invisible, they afflict unwary human beings with certain illnesses and drive them away with remedies either miraculously novel or even contrary, so that they are believed to have healed those whom, after removing only their harmful operations, they have ceased to injure. Hence [Col. 0875C] he ridicules them and their pretended miracles as follows: “They are benefactors indeed, even in the treatment of illnesses: for first they injure, and then prescribe remedies, miraculously novel or contrary, after which they cease to injure and are believed to have cured” (*Ibid.*, col. 409). Nor do they conspire to destroy human beings only by these afflictions inflicted upon and healed in the human body. Through their subtlety they also penetrate the very soul, and by stirring up savage lusts, madness, and frenzy, they lead human beings into any kind of crime (*Ibid.*). We have discussed these impious arts of demons elsewhere (*Dissert. on Minucius*, ch. XVIII, art. 2).

By the same subtlety, the demons put on phantoms, or the appearance of the Castors (Tertullian, *ibid.*), that is, Castor and Pollux; and by their innate speed they announced at Rome on the very same day the celebrated [Col. 0875D] Roman victory over King Perseus on which it had been won. We have related this elsewhere (*Dissert. on Minucius*, ch. 18, art. 2).

To this also pertain these few words of our Author, though they will appear obscure to many: “And the beard made red by a touch” (Tertullian, *ibid.*). Suetonius, however, explains them more clearly where he discusses the origin of the family of the Ahenobarbi: “The Ahenobarbi,” he says, “have as the founder both of their line and of their surname Lucius Domitius. It is related that, as he was returning from the countryside, the twin youths Castor and Pollux met him in a form more majestic than human and commanded him to announce to the Senate and people the victory of which there was still uncertainty. As proof of their majesty—namely, their divine majesty—they stroked his cheeks [Col. 0876A] so that they changed his hair from black to reddish and like the color of bronze. This distinguishing feature remained among his descendants, and a great part of them had red beards” (Suetonius, *Life of Nero*, §1). This is also confirmed by Plutarch (Plutarch, *Life of Paulus Aemilius*, vol. I, p. 268). Tertullian therefore said that the beard was “made red” because it was changed from black to red. For the sake of brevity, however, he records only this sudden and marvelous transformation of the beard, by which the other miracles that the pagans vaunted were also indicated with sufficient clarity.

He asserts that these things were accomplished by the spiritual capacities, powers, and deceptions of the demons (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*), as was also, he further says, the ship “moved by a girdle,” namely by Claudia the Vestal Virgin, [Col. 0876B] as we have already observed elsewhere (*Dissert. on Minucius*, ch. 17, art. 1). The pagans furthermore vaunted, as he continues (*Ibid.*), that water was carried in a sieve by Tuccia, another Vestal Virgin. By this miracle, as they supposed, she completely cleared herself of the charge brought against her of violated chastity—or, as Dionysius of Halicarnassus says (Dionysius of Halicarnassus, bk. II, *Roman Antiquities*, p. 128 ff.), of having extinguished the sacred fire. But concerning this story, whatever its character, Valerius Maximus has more, and you may consult him, as also Pliny, Augustine, and others (Valerius Maximus, bk. VIII, ch. 1; Pliny, bk. XXVIII, *Natural History*, ch. 2, p. 157; Augustine, bk. X, *City of God*, ch. 16, p. 252).

Tertullian therefore asserts that these and other extraordinary deeds of this kind, which the pagans marveled at, were produced by the [Col. 0876C] art and deceptions of demons so that “stone divinities”—that is, images of the gods fashioned from stone—might not be believed to be false gods, and so that the true God might not be sought (Tertullian, *ibid.*). In our preceding dissertations, however, we have discussed these matters abundantly, and have there made clear by what reasoning Arnobius attempts to demonstrate their falsity (*Dissert. on Arnobius*, ch. 18, arts. 1 and 2). Minucius Felix and Lactantius, however, following Tertullian, show that these things were accomplished, as we have said, by the tricks and frauds of demons (*Dissert. on Minucius*, ch. 20, art. 2; *Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, art. 7, and ch. 32, art. 4). We shall attempt in the following articles to clarify those points which are peculiar to Tertullian himself and which he specifically related.

## ARTICLE IV. How the demons attempted to steal knowledge of future events and, in the name of false gods, gave ambiguous oracles to certain persons, especially Pyrrhus and Croesus

[Col. 0876D]

“Foreknowledge and knowledge of future events,” says Cicero, “is a certain magnificent and salutary thing, if indeed there is any such thing, and is the nearest approach to the power of the gods that can be attained by mortal nature” (Cicero, bk. I, *On Divination*, p. 254). Nor was this unknown to the demons. Hence they wished in some way or other to claim this knowledge for themselves. For, as our Tertullian says with complete aptness, “They rival divinity while stealing divination” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 22, col. 408). Indeed, if [Col. 0877A] you trust him, nothing was dearer or of greater importance to them than this rivalry: “And what,” he says, “is a more carefully tended pasture for them than to turn a human being away from reflection upon the true divinity by the deceptions of false divination?” (*Ibid.*, p. 407). This is the reading in Rigault’s edition, for he thought that the words “for them” and “to turn away” should be referred to the demons. But in our manuscripts and the old editions we read “for it” and “that it may turn away,” which can certainly be referred to the aspiration of the demons noted there by Tertullian. The term “pasture” is used by him, as by our sacred Writers, for nourishment and food. For, to omit many passages from them, it is written in the twenty-second Psalm: “In a place of pasture, there He has settled me” (Ps. XXII, v. 20).

Our Author explained in the following manner how the demons attempted to steal divination: [Col. 0877B] “Through the subtlety of their intelligence they intercepted the dispositions of God, both when the prophets were preaching in former times and now when the readings of Holy Scripture resound” (Tertullian, *ibid.*, p. 408). Moreover, they “take,” that is, discern, certain “lots of the times,” or the future conditions and events of the times. We have explained these matters in our dissertation on the books of Lactantius (*Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, art. 7).

In still another way they easily predict rain. For, Tertullian further says (*Ibid.*), “from their dwelling in the air, from the proximity of the stars, and from the interaction of the clouds, they possess knowledge of”—that is, they discern—“the preparations of the heavens, so that they also promise in advance the rains which they already perceive.” Pamelius wished to warn us here that he did not remember whether [Col. 0877C] the term “preparation” was found in other authors. But either he had not read, or did not remember having read, these words of Pontius in the *Life of Saint Cyprian*: “The second birth had not yet illumined the new man with all the splendor of the divine light, and already the mere preparation of light was overcoming the old and former darkness” (Pontius, *Life of St. Cyprian*, ch. 2). The term “preparation” is used there in Tertullian’s own sense, meaning arrangement, equipment, and preparation, as has already been observed (preceding article).

Our Author therefore says that the things being prepared in the higher region of heaven are discerned by the demons, from their dwelling in the air, through the interaction of the clouds. For he knew that Satan is called by the Apostle “the prince of the power of this air,” and demons “spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places” (Epistle to the Ephesians, ch. 2, v. 2, and ch. 6, v. 12). Finally, [Col. 0877D] something must be said below concerning the rain announced in advance by them.

In the same way he further shows that the oracles of their gods, which the pagans vaunted so greatly, also proceeded from those same demons. Who is so blind that he cannot detect in them the customary frauds and malignant subtlety of demons all the more easily the more ambiguous they were? If anyone is unwilling to believe Tertullian, however, let him listen and consider how manifestly this is proved by him: “In oracles,” he says, “Croesus knows, and Pyrrhus knows, with what ingenuity they moderate their ambiguities to suit the events” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 22, p. 408). He therefore presents two celebrated examples of oracles, one given to Pyrrhus and the other to Croesus. How ambiguous and deceptive the first was Augustine made clear in these words: [Col. 0878A] “For the soothsayer said: ‘I say that you, Pyrrhus, can conquer the Romans’; and thus, whether Pyrrhus was conquered by the Romans or the Romans by Pyrrhus, the secure soothsayer could await either outcome” (Augustine, bk. III, *City of God*, ch. 17, p. 75). Deceptions and fraudulent snares of this kind cannot proceed from the true God, but from malignant demons, who, concealed beneath images of false gods through the subtlety of their nature, deceived human beings by these responses. We have discussed this deceptive response of the Pythian Apollo to King Pyrrhus in our dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius (*Dissert. on Minucius*, ch. 17, art. 3). The other response, given to Croesus, is certainly of the same kind. For Eusebius relates (Eusebius, bk. V, *Preparation for the Gospel*, chs. 21 and 22, p. 211 ff.) that this king of Lydia, [Col. 0878B] when he was considering making war upon the Persians, consulted the Delphic Apollo—whose temple he had adorned with boundless wealth and gifts—about the future outcome of this war, and received this entirely ambiguous response:

Χροῖσος Ἅλιν διαβὰς, μεγάλην ἀρχὴν καταλύσει.

“Croesus, having crossed the Halys, will overthrow a great power” (Herodotus, bk. I, ch. 65).

Eusebius afterward confirms this by the testimony of Oenomaus. But if that is insufficient for you, you may consult Herodotus, as well as Cicero, Bulenger, and others, who treat both Pyrrhus and Croesus (Cicero, bk. II, *On Divination*, p. 296; Bulenger, *On Oracles and Prophets*, ch. 6).

The following words of Tertullian likewise concern this same Croesus: “Moreover, the Pythian announced that a tortoise was being cooked with the flesh of a beast in the manner we described above: in a moment he had been in Lydia” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 22, p. 409). [Col. 0878C] Herodotus, whom we have already cited, recorded that Croesus sent messengers to consult various oracles of the gods and inquire what he himself was doing in Lydia. After receiving the individual responses, he approved none except that of the Delphic Apollo, who declared what Croesus was actually doing in this response: Χελώνην καὶ ἄρνα κατακόψας ὀμοῦ ἕψεε αὐτὸς ἐν λέβητι χαλκέῳ, χάλκεον ἐπίθημα ἐπιθείς. “He cut up a tortoise and a lamb together and cooked them in a bronze cauldron with a bronze lid placed over it” (Herodotus, bk. I, §48). Yet that demon did not learn by divine inspiration what Croesus was doing; rather, through his innate speed, Tertullian says, he flew to Lydia in a single moment and, returning with the same [Col. 0878D] speed, delivered to Croesus’s messengers in the name of Apollo the response just mentioned.

We have treated these and similar oracles, the ambiguous and deceptive responses of demons, and their cessation, not only in the cited dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius but also in our other dissertations (*Dissert. on Minucius*, ch. 30, art. 3; *Dissert. on Lactantius*, ch. 20, arts. 7 and 8, and ch. 32, art. 1). We think, however, that no one fails to understand from what has been said how greatly a certain more recent writer, as we observed elsewhere, departs from the opinion of Tertullian and the other most ancient Fathers of the Church, when he wrongly asserts that the ancient oracles of the gentiles were delivered not through these tricks and most wicked arts of demons, but through the frauds and deceptions of priests serving idols. [Col. 0879A] For although it were established that some proceeded from those priests, they nevertheless could not have produced nearly all of them, just as they could not produce the true miracles which must now be discussed.

## ARTICLE V. How Tertullian shows that, by the power and frauds of demons, those things are accomplished which magicians perform through itinerant conjuring tricks, by which the souls of the dead are dishonored, children are forced into delivering an oracular utterance, dreams are sent, and goats and tables divine; and that no one can reply that at least those things performed within the temples of the gods must be attributed to them

Tertullian presses the pagans still more sharply and makes it clear that those things which they supposed to have been produced beyond the order of nature by itinerant magicians were accomplished by the frauds and deceptions of demons. [Col. 0879B] And surely, how could those magicians accomplish anything by their accursed arts unless they possessed, Tertullian says, “the assisting power of angels”—namely, evil angels—“and demons once they had been summoned” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 23, p. 410)? For they do not possess in themselves that power which exceeds the ordinary strength of a human being, nor do they receive it from the supremely good God, who cannot be the author of evil deeds. They therefore received it from demons, who, through their malice, as we have said, attempt to lead human beings into every kind of crime. But Tertullian demonstrates this more clearly and fully in another book (Idem, *On the Soul*, ch. 57).
