But, setting aside—or at least postponing until later—an investigation of the text we have presented, and excluding the excessive bulk of a laborious commentary, we have thought it best to proceed as quickly as possible to a brief exposition of the clearer and sounder meaning of Septimius in this preface. We consider it to be as follows:

“The Valentinians—whose association must plainly be the largest among the heretics, both because it has retained very many doctrines of the faith it renounced, because it readily inclines toward popular fables, and because it is not deterred by a harsher discipline—care about nothing more than concealing what they preach, if indeed those who conceal can be said to preach, or to be preaching at all. For among them the anxious duty of guarding a secret must rightly be regarded as the function, or the indication, of an unhealthy conscience. The disgracefulness of their morals is proclaimed while religion is either asserted or preserved by them, whether it is disclosed or kept silent. For even in those Eleusinian rites—which are themselves a heresy derived from the fountain of Attic superstition—what the *epoptai*, or initiated participants in the rites, keep silent is something shameful. For that reason they torment and almost kill those approaching the entrance—that is, those coming to them or learning from them—by circumlocutions and trials. Thus they spend longer initiating than sealing, and consume more time in preparing than in completing the initiation, since even before the gates they prescribe a five-year period to test those who desire to become *epoptai*. Their purpose is to build up an enormous reputation through the protracted suspense of secret knowledge, and thus to appear to display in the hidden [Col. 0538B] rites as much majesty and *semnotēs* (σεμνότητα, solemn dignity) as the desire they have first constructed, elevating the mind to expect great things. Then follows the law and duty of silence, for what is discovered slowly is guarded attentively. Yet the entire divinity hidden in the innermost sanctuaries, all the curtains fastened to the leaves of the doors, this whole seal of secrecy impressed upon the tongue, ends in obscenity; and once so wearisomely prolonged an initiation has been completed, nothing but a foul image of the male member is revealed—shameful to tell. But by putting forward the venerable name of nature and alleging that the arrangement of shameful myths contains empty allegorical inventions, under the patronage of a forced and foolish figure it obscures, diminishes, and repudiates the sacrilege, and excuses the manifest reproach of a false and foul fabrication by means of such counterfeit images.”

Having expounded these matters *paraphrastikōs* (παραφραστικῶς, by paraphrase), no small task might appear to lie before us: namely, to comment on those words of Tertullian one by one, either by defending the genuine authority of the text that we have ventured to publish after so many emendations, or by comparing this preface with other testimonies on the same subject and extracting from them many pieces of evidence useful for explaining ancient religions. From this could easily arise a vast dissertation upon the discipline of the Eleusinian mysteries—a subject worn threadbare again and again, although even now covered with dense [Col. 0538D] darkness—which it is better to omit, since it has been almost exhausted by many others far superior in learning. We shall cast some light upon only two or three points in this preface, while taking care to append here and there annotations from various scholars’ notes.

1. The words of Herodotus, book VIII, chap. 45, are cited against him. Herodotus introduces a certain Dicaeus discussing the celebration of the Eleusinian rites with King Demaratus and saying, among other things:

But what if an evident scribal error occurs there? For if Herodotus had wished to say that the rites were celebrated and revealed annually, he would surely have written ἀνὰ πᾶν ἔτος, “every year.” It is therefore intrinsically certain that πάντα, “all,” should there be read as πέντε, “five”—a reading that supports our Septimius’ position remarkably well.

2. Entering, not without praise, the academic arena from the Hyperborean region, he too wished a kind of summary of the Primitive Revelation of the Patriarchs, together with certain venerable prophecies about fallen humanity and its restoration, to have been safely entrusted to the Eleusinian discipline. These are the principal modern fabrications concerning the Eleusinian secrets.

But if one wishes to return to the ancients, their opinions vary so much that one must remain in uncertainty. I would not wish to deny that several of them—Pindar, Plato, Cicero, and Epictetus—received the Eleusinian rites of Ceres with great praises and commended them to posterity. Nor could anyone easily be brought to believe that so many and such distinguished men, conspicuous for nobility of mind and character no less than for intellectual excellence, mistook shameful things for honorable ones through blind delusion and deliberately conspired in unanimous collusion to foster that error. Consequently, there is some reason to suppose that, at least up to a certain period, the rites of Ceres were to some extent pious from their beginning.

Why, then, did all the most holy Fathers without exception, and especially the earliest Apologists, cry out with one voice against the unspeakable mysteries and think it right to vie with one another in assailing that religion in their writings? Justin, Athenagoras, and Tatian indeed affirm that nothing but Jupiter’s incest was displayed at Eleusis. Clement of Alexandria inveighs against the rites sharply and with the full force of his spirit. He reproaches Jupiter’s intercourse with Ceres and her daughter; unfolds the secrets of the mystic chests, poppies, parsley, and the rest of the rubbish; shudders as he reports that among the symbols of Themis a representation of the κτένος γυναικείου, “woman’s comb” (with an obscene implication), was carried in the phallic procession; and finally rebukes the hierophants and torchbearers. He judges that those who venerate a wretchedly dismembered boy, a wailing little woman, and the shameful parts of the body should be numbered among atheists. See St. Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation*, pp. 19, 30.

Consider, then, who the witnesses standing on either side are: on the one side the wise men, poets, and legislators of the nations; on the other, the Fathers, Doctors, and Pontiffs of the Church. Nor, in order to settle the dispute between them more easily, can you perhaps tell yourself that two periods of Eleusinian history must be distinguished: one blackened by hoary antiquity, in which the rites were observed with pious worship, and another foul with the recent dregs of superstition and deservedly chastised by the Fathers. Nor does the final refuge of a wavering mind remain: to think privately that the Fathers, carried away by harsher contempt for the nations’ rites and more fervent anger, overstepped the bounds of fair criticism.

For pagan witnesses are not lacking—either from the ancient period or from the Christian era and contemporary with the Fathers—from whom it becomes known that the same rites were infamous in every age. In Caecilius’ play *Titthe*, fragment 1, one reads: “By the mysteries he dishonorably made her pregnant through violation.” Plautus, *Aulularia*, prologue 36: “He violated her during the vigil of Ceres.” That prostitutes were frequently present at these rites is clear from Alciphron, fragmentary letter IV. In Athenaeus XIII, 591 F, a certain Themistius is punished with death because he ὕβρισε τοῖς ἐλευσινοῖς, “violated a Rhodian female lyre-player during the Eleusinian rites.” Dinarchus, *Against Demosthenes*, p. 93. Plutarch reports reproaches of this kind, *Table Talk* II, 2, 73: ἐν Ἐλευσῖνι μετὰ τὰ μυστήρια τῆς πανηγύρεως ἀκμαζούσης, “at Eleusis, after the mysteries, when the festal gathering was at its height.” Compare *On Isis and Osiris*, § 36. Nor did Lucian restrain his sarcasm on this subject, as may be seen in *Alexander, or the False Lover*, § 39, vol. V of the works, pp. 99–100, Bipont edition. For they no longer speak like those to whom the Lord said: ‘Freely you have received; freely give.’ Instead they impart isolated, monstrous, and profound mysteries, sought out with great labor, to the credulous.” RHEN.]

If you test them subtly, they affirm the common faith through double-tongued ambiguities. [Note: [Col. 0544C] “They affirm the common faith.” Irenaeus: “Speaking things similar to ours, but understanding dissimilar things.” RIG.] If you hint that you know, they deny whatever they recognize. [Note: [Col. 0544D] “They deny whatever they recognize”: that is, whatever they recognize that you know about their mysteries. RIG.] If you contend with them at close quarters, [Note: [Col. 0543B] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “certainly.”] they shatter your simplicity with their own blows. [Note: [Col. 0543B] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “your fixed simplicity”; Rigaltius and the Paris edition, with a distinguishing comma, “your foolish simplicity.”] [Note: [Col. 0544D] “They shatter your simplicity with their own blows.” Tertullian uses the noun *caedes*, “striking/slaughter,” and the verb *caedere*, “to strike,” in the same sense when he says in the book he wrote *To the Martyrs*, chap. 2: “You are not struck by the cries of the shows, by the cruelty or frenzy or immodesty of those celebrating them.” St. Jerome, imitating Tertullian in the *Life of Hilarion the Hermit*, said “the striking of the hand”: “Angry with himself, therefore, and beating his breast with his fists, as though he could drive thoughts out by the striking of his hand.” PAM.]

They do not entrust their teaching even to their own disciples [Col. 0543B] before they have made them their own. They possess an art by which they first persuade [Note: [Col. 0543B] The Paris edition reads “they persuade.”] and then instruct. Truth, however, persuades by teaching; it does not teach by persuasion.

## CHAPTER II

[Note: [Col. 0544D] See the final note on this book, col. 594.]

And so we are marked among them as “simple,” as though we were merely that and not also wise—as though wisdom were immediately compelled [Col. 0544A] [Note: [Col. 0543B] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “is compelled.”] to depart from simplicity, although the Lord joins the two together: “Be prudent as serpents and simple as doves” (Matthew 10:10).

Or if we are foolish because we are simple, are they therefore not simple because they are wise? Yet those who are not simple are the most harmful, just as those who are not wise are the most foolish. And yet I would rather be classed on that side under the better fault, if perhaps [Note: [Col. 0544B] Junius prefers: “I would prefer that, on that side, the better person should assume the fault, if perhaps.”] it is better to know too little than to know what is worse—to err rather than to deceive.

Moreover, the face of God looks upon [Note: [Col. 0544B] Semler and Oberthür read “awaits.”] those who seek him in simplicity (Wisdom 1), [Note: [Col. 0544B] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “of seeking.”] as Sophia herself teaches—[Note: [Col. 0544B] Others read “wisdom itself”; others prefer the Greek name.] not Valentinus’ Sophia, but Solomon’s.

Next, infants consecrated Christ’s testimony with their blood. [Note: [Col. 0544D] “Infants consecrated Christ’s testimony with their blood.” Augustine spoke in precisely the same sense in book X of *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, chap. XXIII: “That tender age too possesses the great weight of testimony, for it was the first to deserve to shed its blood for Christ.” PAM.] Shall I call those who cry aloud for the cross “children”? Yet they were neither children nor infants—that is, they were not simple.

The Apostle also commands us to become children again according to [Col. 0544B] God (1 Corinthians 14:20), so that, being infants in malice through simplicity, we may thus at last be wise in our understanding; [Note: [Col. 0544D] “Thus at last wise in our understanding; at the same time I have placed [the words] in the order of the wisdom that flows from simplicity.” We have emended this passage, most corrupt in every edition, from the Pithou manuscript. He says: “The Apostle also commands us to become children again according to God, so that, being infants in malice, at the same time I have placed [the words] in the order of the wisdom that flows from simplicity.” The manuscript reads *manandi* (“of flowing”), erroneously. RIG.] at the same time I have placed [the words] [Note: [Col. 0544B] The Franeker and Paris editions read: “let us be in the order of divine wisdom.”] in the order of the wisdom that flows from simplicity. [Note: [Col. 0544B] The Franeker and Paris editions and Junius retain “by loving”; Semler prefers “by flowing”; he says *minandi* is a verb used of those who drive beasts of burden.]

In short, the dove is accustomed to reveal Christ, whereas the serpent is accustomed to tempt him. From the beginning the former was the herald of divine peace; from the beginning the latter was the plunderer of the divine image. Thus simplicity by itself will more readily be able both to recognize and to reveal God; prudence by itself will rather shake and betray him.

## CHAPTER III

Let the serpent, then, hide himself as much as he can, and twist all his prudence through the winding paths of hiding places. Let him dwell in the depths, be thrust down into darkness, unwind his length through crooked passages, and advance tortuously, never appearing whole at once—a beast that flees the light.

The house of our dove [Note: [Col. 0545C] Semler and Oberthür omit “also.”] also is simple, always in elevated, open places and toward the light. The form [Note: [Col. 0545C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “the image.”] of the Holy Spirit loves the East, [Note: [Col. 0545C] “The East, a figure of Christ.” In Holy Scripture Christ is often understood by the word “East,” as in Zechariah: “Behold, I shall bring forth my servant, the East”; and a little later in the same prophet: “Behold the man whose name is East.” He is called East because in his days righteousness arose. From this you have the ancient Christians’ custom of praying while turned toward the east, which we still observe. RHEN.] [Note: [Col. 0545C] Semler and Oberthür read “the East, a figure of Christ.”] the figure of Christ.

Truth is ashamed of nothing except being concealed. For the person concerned will not be ashamed to lend his ears to it and to recognize as God the one whom nature has already commended to him, whom he perceives daily in all his works, and who is less known for this reason alone: that he did not regard him as one, named him among a multitude, [Col. 0545B] [Note: [Col. 0545C] Junius reads “named him among multitudes.”] and worshiped him in others.

Otherwise, to recommend still another crowd in addition to their multitude, to remove faith from a familiar sovereignty to an unknown one, to turn it away from what is manifest toward what is hidden, is to make faith stumble on the threshold. Now if he is also initiated into the whole fable, will he not be given something like what you heard from your nurse in infancy amid the troubles of sleep—the towers of Lamia [Note: [Col. 0545D] “The towers of Lamia and the combs of the Sun?” Children’s tales at Carthage in Tertullian’s time. Below we have written “so many offspring,” and a little later “and yet we simple people know everything,” from the Gorze collation. “We” means the orthodox. RIG.] and the combs of the Sun?

But if someone comes to the faith with a different conscience and immediately finds so many names of Aeons, so many marriages, so many offspring, [Note: [Col. 0545C] The first edition of Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “so many pairs.”] so many departures, so many events, successes and misfortunes belonging to a scattered and dismembered divinity, will he hesitate to declare on the spot [Note: [Col. 0545D] “That these are fables and interminable genealogies.” Irenaeus at the beginning of his book against the Valentinians: “Inasmuch as certain persons, rejecting the truth, introduce false words and endless genealogies,” etc. The Greek in Epiphanius is γενεαλογίας ματαίας, “vain genealogies.” The Apostle had said γενεαλογίας ἀπεράντους, “endless genealogies,” 1 Timothy 1. RIG.] that these are the fables and interminable genealogies which the Apostle’s Spirit twice condemned in anticipation even then, when the seeds of heresy were already sprouting?

They are therefore deservedly called “not simple,” deservedly “merely prudent”: people who neither readily bring such things forward nor openly defend them, and who do not even teach fully all those whom they instruct. This is certainly cunning if the things are shameful, but inhuman if they are honorable.

And yet we simple people [Note: [Col. 0546C] The first edition of Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür read “we all.”] know everything. Finally, we have armed this first wedge for the encounter, as the revealer and indicator of their entire conscience, and we take this as an omen of our first victory: because, when something is hidden at such great expense, merely to expose it is also to destroy it.
