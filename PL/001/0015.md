Next comes the most elegant *Apologeticus*, which I venture to call the most outstanding of Tertullian’s books [Col. 0118A] both in learning and in candor. Although its date has been discussed at length by everyone—even by those who did not address his other books—and especially by Mosheim, that most accomplished man, to whom first place in this field is owed, it is nevertheless permissible, after that harvest, to gather a few ears of grain and, passing over what he has said with the greatest learning, to set forth as briefly as we can, ascending as it were step by step, what we have to say on this subject.

First, then, it cannot be obscure to anyone who has treated this book with any care that at that time Severus’ law against the Christians had not yet been issued. For who could believe that, by presenting this book to the provincial governors, Tertullian could have expected them not only to mitigate the anger conceived against the Christians, [Col. 0118B] but to put it aside altogether and release them as innocent, if the governors had been obliged to act according to Severus’ laws, from which they were not permitted to deviate even a finger’s breadth—especially since it was well known how harshly he had formerly dealt with those whom he learned had not properly discharged their judicial office (see Spartianus, *Severus*, ch. 8)?

Nor should anyone find it surprising that, if the governors together with the common people alone were inflicting injuries upon the Christians, Tertullian did not appeal to Caesar himself, and that the Christians did not take refuge under his protection. For if, at the instigation of Plautianus, the Christians endured every kind of calamity on the occasion of the punishment of those implicated by friendship with Niger and Albinus, such an appeal to Caesar would have had no prospect of success because of the supreme authority of Plautianus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard; [Col. 0118C] nor would it have done so even if made to Plautianus himself, a man of utterly unrestrained power and cruelty. Besides, even if Plautianus was by no means the author of so great an outrage against the Christians, how, I ask, could assistance against the assaults of the frenzied populace have been obtained from Severus, who had an insatiable appetite both for gold and for the slaughter of innocent men and of those suspected of sedition? There was rather every reason to fear that, although he had not yet undertaken public hostilities against the Christians, once alerted by their petitions he would go so far as actually to increase their sufferings by using his authority to confirm the governors’ fury.

This point being established, it must certainly be said that the occasion for writing the *Apologeticus* was a disturbance against the Christians stirred up by the populace. The force of certain passages of Tertullian [Note: [Col. 0118D] *Apol.*, ch. 37: “How often does the hostile mob invade us on its own authority with stones and fires? In the very frenzy of the Bacchanals they do not spare even dead Christians, but tear them from the repose of burial, cut them apart, and rend them limb from limb.” And ch. 35: “No one clamors more loudly for the punishment of Christians than the mob. Clearly the other orders, in keeping with their authority, are religiously observant in good faith: nothing hostile breathes from the senate itself, from the equestrian order, from the camps, or from the palaces themselves.”] is so manifest that it is plainly absurd to seek anything else there than this very circumstance. Hence Tertullian cannot be said to have delivered this defense of the Christians to the governors before the year 197 had fully run its course, because we have assigned the origins of these disturbances to that year by suitable arguments.

Accordingly, there is no need to use the passage (*Apologeticus*, ch. 35) [Col. 0119A] in which Tertullian included Cassius, Niger, and Albinus among the enemies of the empire in order to establish that the *Apologeticus* was later than the death of Albinus. Nevertheless, that very passage contains certain not particularly obscure traces by following which we can determine more precisely the date that we assign to the *Apologeticus*. We shall now say nothing of distributions of money given by the emperor to the people, nothing of the solemn day of the prince celebrated with every apparatus of luxury, nothing of vows undertaken for the emperor’s safety; for these considerations are either insufficiently suited to settling the matter, or have already been discussed most elegantly by Mosheim.

Let us examine a single passage, namely the one already brought forward above (§ 15), both because it settles the matter most clearly and because it moves us to depart slightly [Col. 0119B] from the opinion of that most learned man. Tertullian says, then, that associates in or applauders of criminal factions—surviving gleanings after the harvest of the parricides—were being revealed daily; he also says that certain astrologers, augurs, and magicians had taken counsel concerning the life of the Caesars. Spartianus coupled these same matters together, and the resemblance between the cited passages of the two authors led me to say that both had precisely the same period in view. But since neither does Tertullian expressly place the consultation of the magicians in the very same period as the search for the remaining friends of Niger, nor does Spartianus, where he does not explicitly define the time, follow the order of events with perfect exactness, we now set aside what is said in both authors about consulting magicians.

Let us instead follow the other indication of the time when the *Apologeticus* was written, namely [Col. 0119C] Plautianus’ zeal in dragging forth all those whom he declared suspect of hostility toward Severus. Spartianus says that Plautianus persecuted them during the period when Severus had attacked the Parthians and had returned to Syria to prepare himself to wage war against them with greater force. It is indeed credible that Severus set out against the Parthians approximately after the middle of 198 [Note: [Col. 0119D] The emperor’s departure is associated by Mediobarbus, under A.D. 199, p. 172, and by Vaillant, *Numismata Imperatorum*, vol. I, p. 113, with the sixth tribunician power, which ended in the middle of 199. But another coin in the former author, p. 273, has Severus’ sixth tribunician power together with the surname *Par. Ar. Ad.* and other emblems of victory.]; after checking their attacks to some extent, he returned to Syria, perhaps to pass the winter there. While he was preparing there what seemed necessary for prosecuting the war against the Parthians, Plautianus tracked down those whom he knew to survive among Niger’s friends; this therefore occurred approximately at the beginning of 199.

Consequently, I have no doubt that Mosheim’s opinion that this book was written in the middle of 198 [Col. 0119D] must be abandoned on this point, because it was only then that Severus set out against the Parthians, while Tertullian speaks not of the eliminated friends of Albinus but of Niger’s friends, who were still surviving after the Albinian affair. Yet this search for Severus’ enemies does not appear to have occurred later than the time we have stated; for that would conflict with the fact that Tertullian had already joined the Montanist party in 200, and that before this secession from the Church he wrote the books *Ad Nationes* and *De Testimonio Animae*, [Col. 0120A] which do not give even the slightest grounds for suspicion of Montanism and are earlier than the *Apologeticus*. This is beyond doubt regarding the book *On the Testimony of the Soul*, and highly probable regarding the books *To the Nations*.

Nor do I think anyone will now suppose that Tertullian was already a Montanist when he composed the *Apologeticus*. For unless they prefer, with the distinguished Havercamp (preface to his edition of Tertullian’s *Apologeticus*), to invent the claim that Tertullian issued this *Apology* twice, slightly altered, they must yield to our arguments by which we have proved it earlier than Severus’ edict. I do not approve Havercamp’s opinion, since certain variant readings in the text of this book do not sufficiently establish the point. From Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, ch. 2, I indeed know that Tertullian’s *Apology* [Col. 0120B] was written in Latin and afterwards translated into Greek; but that it was issued twice, and altered at that, I do not know at all.

If, therefore, they concede that the *Apologeticus* predates Severus’ edict, then surely, if they can, they must contest our position with surer arguments than those with which Hoffmann attacked the opinion demonstrated by Mosheim—arguments which Mosheim excellently showed to possess no solidity in the Addenda to vol. I of his *Dissertations on Ecclesiastical History*, pp. 741–48. We ourselves shall answer Hoffmann’s other argument in its proper place; of the remaining one, however, we point out that it conflicts with Hoffmann’s own position, for I am pleased to add these observations to Mosheim’s reply. For even if what Hoffmann says about Severus’ restoration of the cult of Serapis at Rome is true—that it occurred only [Col. 0120C] when he had toured Egypt after defeating the Parthians, that is, in 202—then what he had previously determined, namely that the *Apologeticus* was written before Severus’ edict, collapses; for Severus did not return to Rome before 203, and yet no one at all can call into question that the edict against the Christians was promulgated in 202.

## § 17.—The Two Books *To the Nations* Written in the Year 199

Tertullian relates in his *Apologeticus* (ch. 16) that very recently a certain man had put on display an image clothed in a toga, furnished with ass’s ears, and having one hoofed foot, with the added inscription, “The Christians’ God, *onochoetes*.” He reports [Col. 0120D] in the first book *To the Nations*, ch. 14, that the same thing had happened not long before. This may serve as evidence that both books *To the Nations* were published at approximately the same time as the *Apologeticus*, and, as I think, in such a way that they appeared after it. I say both; for the words with which he begins the second book at once show that it followed the first after the *Apologeticus*. This is so, first, because “very recently” appears to designate a shorter interval of time than “not long before”; and secondly, because it is more likely that Tertullian first addressed the governors, whose authority was greater and whose disposition was more tractable, than the people.

Besides these, there are not a few indications from which it may be understood that their date was much earlier than Severus’ edict. I refrain from enumerating them now, but nevertheless employ two [Col. 0121A] arguments for this purpose. One is that these books were intended primarily for the people; the other, that they indicate the victory over Niger and Albinus as having been won not very long before. Nowhere does he address the governors, as he did in the *Apologeticus*, but solely the people, from whom I recall that Tertullian somewhere even expressly distinguished the governors (e.g., book I, ch. 3). Next, he says that those to whom he dedicated these books had accused the Christians (book I, ch. 20); while I see that this may indeed fit the governors, it refers far more naturally to the common people. Finally, it is evident that the designation “Nations,” which stands in the title of these books, was applied by Tertullian to those devoted to the worship of the gods; but here it doubtless designates, properly speaking, the superstitious populace, because he had already placed before the governors for their consideration in the *Apologeticus* almost the same matters [Col. 0121B] as were discussed in these two treatises.

But he introduces mention of the recent slaughter of Niger and Albinus in such a way as to say that Syria breathes with the odors of corpses and that the Gallic provinces have not yet washed themselves clean in their Rhône. What else can this mean than that Severus’ war with Niger and Albinus had scarcely been brought to an end? All these considerations clearly show that the books *To the Nations* were published not after Severus’ edict but before it, and can teach everyone that we have not without reason assigned these books as companions to Tertullian’s *Apologeticus*, since for this very reason they must have been written when half of 199 had already elapsed. From this it appears at the same time that their author had not yet gone over [Col. 0121C] to the Montanist position, since that happened only in 200, nor is anything found in these books that can raise any appreciable suspicion of Montanism.

Yet this one point in Hoffmann’s arguments seems to me worthy of notice. Relying on the same similarity of subject matter that exists between the *Apologeticus* and these books, he incorrectly pronounces in the opposite direction. For his opinion—that the books *To the Nations*, together with the treatise *On the Testimony of the Soul*, differ from the *Apologeticus* only in length and set forth somewhat more fully the same defense which Tertullian had compressed, as it were, into a fist in the *Apologeticus*—is neither altogether correct nor sufficiently adapted to proving the point. Nothing need be said about the conclusion of the argument itself, for this has been sufficiently discussed [Col. 0121D] by us, and will still be discussed in several places. And although in the *Apologeticus* Tertullian presents matters generally similar to those set forth in the books *To the Nations*, there are nevertheless many things in the former that do not occur at all in the latter, and conversely things in the latter that are sought in vain in the *Apologeticus*. Anyone wishing to perceive this more fully is referred to the whole of the second book, which is occupied with refuting the gods of the Gentiles; there, if one leaves aside the final sections of the book, one will find nothing that is treated in the *Apologeticus*.

## § 18.—The Book *On the Testimony of the Soul* Assigned to the Same Year

I hold the same opinion concerning the treatise that Tertullian composed *On the Testimony of the Soul*. I certainly do not deny that it is contemporary with the books *To the Nations*. [Col. 0122A] But I doubt whether Hoffmann is right to say, at the place cited, p. 34, that it is, as it were, the second part of a commentary that Tertullian issued on the *Apologeticus*, the books *To the Nations* having constituted the first. For the words of *Apologeticus*, ch. 17, “Do you wish us to prove it from the testimony of the soul itself?” which gave Hoffmann occasion to think so, do not seem to me framed in such a way that they can bring all readers to acknowledge the truth of this opinion, since in that same passage Tertullian also says that he can demonstrate the one God from his works; yet I do not recall him ever discussing that subject systematically, at least in a book of the kind written at the same time as the apologetic works.

That this book is contemporary with the *Apologeticus* is taught more securely by the words Tertullian uses in section five, p. 81: “The divine Scriptures, which are in our possession or that of the Jews, by far precede secular literature, not merely by some modest span of time, as we demonstrated in its proper place.” [Col. 0122B] But where did he demonstrate this, if not in the *Apologeticus*, ch. 18 ff.? Since he composed that work at the beginning of 199, but the books *To the Nations* somewhat later, he must be judged to have published our treatise when that year was nearly complete. Nor is this inconsistent with the task that Tertullian undertook to accomplish in the treatise: that the rivals and persecutors of the Christians should be convinced of the truth, and convicted of error in themselves and of injustice toward the Christians (ch. 1).

But I do not think this book should be assigned to another year, and one later than that which we have stated. For besides the fact that no indication of Montanism appears in this book, one might not inaptly say that it was added as a kind of appendix to the *Apologeticus* and the books *To the Nations*, because Christian doctrine is discussed in it somewhat more fully. Nor did Tertullian compose any book of this kind against the absurd worship of the gods [Col. 0122C] that could be assigned to another time than this, when the very violence against the Christians demanded a demonstration of the supreme injustice conspicuous in propagating superstition by sword and flame.

# ARTICLE III

## On the Books of Tertullian Whose Date and Montanism Are Established

### § 1.—The Book *On the Crown* Shown to Have Been Composed in the Year 201

In the preceding part of the discussion, we believe that enough has been set forth concerning those books whose date and purity of doctrine seemed capable of being established with a certain degree of certainty. [Col. 0122D] There now follows a second topic, which will treat those books whose date is no less established than the fact that they were published by Tertullian after he had abandoned his former discipline and gone over to that of Montanus. Among these the first place is occupied by the book *On the Crown*. It seems worthwhile to investigate its date somewhat more fully, all the more because this is, as it were, [non-Latin text omitted], the standard against which all the other books must be measured if it comes into dispute whether they were written before Tertullian’s defection or not.

Since we see learned men disagreeing among themselves in various opinions on this matter, by which they have hindered rather than facilitated the discovery of the truth, we shall state candidly what seems probable to us, and shall employ the first section of the book alone, from which both the writer’s actual doctrine and the time [Col. 0123A] when he brought this book before the public can be elicited. Anyone may recognize the former from that entire discussion concerning the avoidance of things that might arouse even the slightest suspicion of worshiping fictitious gods. There one will hear Tertullian speaking, not calmly, modestly, and yielding, but austerely and vehemently, inflamed with extraordinary anger against those who thought otherwise, as generally happens with those who, having only recently advanced into some discipline, imagine themselves to possess exceptional wisdom.

For when he says that the pastors of the other sect are lions in peace and stags in battle, and that they have rejected the prophecies of the Holy Spirit, it plainly appears that a disciple of Montanus is speaking here, not a member of the common Church. For by these words he both declares himself to belong to a different flock from those whose teachers he attacks, and clearly indicates what had provoked his mind against [Col. 0123B] them: namely, that they had not joined him in following the same decrees of the Montanists’ Paraclete.

He begins this same section of the book with words that are like a fountain from which all knowledge of its date must be drawn. Thus he writes:

> Very recently there took place a largess of the most excellent emperors; in the camp the soldiers were receiving their gratuities crowned with laurel. One man there, more truly a soldier of God—alone with his head uncovered, the crown held idly in his hand.—The murmuring is reported to the tribune. The tribune at once asks, “Why are you so different in your dress?” He denied that it was lawful for him to do as the others did. When pressed for his reasons, he replied, “I am a Christian.”

When the other Christians condemned this soldier’s deed, thinking that so good and long-lasting a peace was thereby endangered, Tertullian strove against them, extolling it with the highest praise. [Col. 0123C] In this passage we first see him commemorate a largess of the emperors made very recently, and I doubt whether anyone would say that these were any other than Severus and Caracalla, both Augusti. Now above we said that Caracalla was adorned with the rank of Augustus when Severus was preparing his eastern expedition against the Parthians, on which he departed approximately after half of the year 198 had passed; and later we shall show that the peace which some believed was here being put at risk was the peace enjoyed by the Christians before they were publicly harassed by Severus’ law, which all agree was issued in 202. This book, therefore, must have been written in the interval that elapsed from Severus’ sixth to his ninth [Col. 0123D] year.

Within this period I have observed three years marked by imperial largess: the first, shortly before Severus set out for the East in 198 [Note: [Col. 0123D] Spartianus, *Severus*, ch. 14: “He set out for the Parthian war after presenting a gladiatorial show and giving a distribution of money to the people.”]; the second, the year 201, when, because the soldiers had proclaimed Caracalla Augustus and Geta Caesar, he handed Ctesiphon over to them for plunder [Note: [Col. 0123D] The same author, ch. 16: “On account of these titles he gave the soldiers a most lavish donative, granting them all the booty of the Parthian city, which the soldiers demanded.”]; the third, 202, when Severus and Caracalla were planning their journey to Egypt [Note: [Col. 0123D] The same author, ibid.: “After this—when he had designated his elder son consul with himself—he gave the soldiers an increased stipend and proceeded to Alexandria.”].

[Col. 0124A] I maintain that the first of these years cannot be intended here. For it may be debated whether the gladiatorial show and distribution recorded by Spartianus are the same as those which Herodian reports (*Book III*, ch. 8, ed. Boecler, p. 145) were given by Severus to the people when he returned to Rome after defeating Albinus. Nor should it be objected that Spartianus elsewhere (*Severus*, ch. 12) commemorated the same thing that Severus provided immediately after his return. For, not to mention that Spartianus often repeats the same things as though they were different, in that passage he does not speak of a donative granted to the soldiers, but of Severus’ giving the soldiers an amount of pay greater than that given by any prince. If, therefore, each author had the same distribution in view, it is certainly understood that Tertullian did not mean that distribution, since only in 198 was the rank of Augustus conferred upon Caracalla by the Senate.

But even if it is granted that Herodian means one distribution and Spartianus another, the event cited from this first passage cannot be referred to Tertullian’s passage, because Spartianus speaks only of a distribution given to the people and of spectacles presented, whereas Tertullian speaks of an entirely different benefit granted to the soldiers in camp. [Col. 0124B] Nor should the last interpretation be admitted, namely that this largess took place in Syria in 202, after the war in the East had ended and both Augusti had entered upon the consulship. For Aelius Spartianus, the only writer who mentions that event, says that the soldiers were given an increased stipend, and I doubt whether this can be called a donative of the sort Tertullian appears to indicate. [Col. 0124C]

But even if there are those who prefer to understand a donative in this passage of Spartianus, I hope that they will not entirely scorn my opinion that the largess described by Tertullian refers to the one concerning which I said Spartianus spoke when he related the events that occurred after the capture of Ctesiphon. When we examine the circumstances in this way, we observe not only that all the details accord well, if a comparison is made, but also that one passage can be excellently explained by the other. First, no one can fail to see that at this time there were two Augusti; for Caracalla had already been made Augustus before the Parthian expedition in 198, and was now also declared a partner in the empire by the soldiers. Next, Spartianus commemorates a donative given to the soldiers, [Col. 0124D] which explains the emperors’ largess in the camp. Finally, at the beginning of this year 201 Severus had not yet promulgated a law barring everyone from the Christian religion, whose community will soon appear to have been disturbed by it.

But since the claim that this donative was distributed to the soldiers at the beginning of 201 admits of some doubt, we shall briefly explain how the reckoning should be made. To begin, we ask that it be observed that, as we argued elsewhere (above, § 16, col. 119, note), Severus set out for the East after the first part of 198 had elapsed. He brought this war undertaken against the Parthians approximately to an end with the capture of Ctesiphon. For although Dio Cassius reports that only afterwards did Severus twice attack Hatra in vain, Severus does not appear—if Dio is correct—to have spent much time on this, [Col. 0125A] since the second and most violent assault upon that city was completed in twenty days (Dio, book LXXV, ch. 13, p. 1265), and Severus’ journey into Palestine and Egypt immediately followed it.

However this may be understood, Spartianus confirms in *Severus*, ch. 16, that Ctesiphon was captured near the winter season. This cannot be said to have happened in 198. For even if we say that Severus left Rome immediately after Caracalla assumed the rank of Augustus, how, I ask, could he in so short a time complete so long a journey to what were almost the remotest regions, equip a fleet, traverse Armenia, capture Seleucia and Babylon, and devastate Arabia? Nor is it any more credible that this occurred in the following year. For after the capture of Ctesiphon and the two sieges of Hatra, [Col. 0125B] Severus came to Syria, where he afterwards assumed the consular fasces. But all who have consulted the consular lists know that he held them with his son in 202. It would therefore have to be said that for nearly two or three years Severus did nothing to prosecute the war against his remaining enemies, which is wholly implausible.

It remains, therefore, to say that Ctesiphon was captured by Severus approximately as the year 200 was drawing to its close. Since its capture was immediately followed by the conferral of the rank of Augustus upon Caracalla by the soldiers and by the granting of a donative to them for that reason, this certainly occurred at no other time than the end of 200 or the beginning of the following year. This conclusion is excellently confirmed by those monuments of antiquity that have preserved for us the memory of both events. For, to begin with the Parthian victory, not only Eusebius—whose judgment some may perhaps refuse to accept—assigns this victory over the Parthians, Adiabenians, and Arabs to the year 200 (*Chronicle*, book II, at this year, Scaliger’s edition, p. 172); [Col. 0125C] but the consular lists as well (in Pamelius, *Life of Tertullian*, p. 18.II) and coins (in Mediobarbus, under A.D. 201, p. 274) attribute the surname *Parthicus Maximus* to Severus while he held the tribunician power for the eighth time, which everyone knows extended from the middle of 200 to the middle of the following year.

Next, that the rank of Augustus was conferred by the soldiers upon Caracalla, and that of Caesar upon Geta, in 201 may in some measure be understood from Spartianus, who says that this occurred when Caracalla was in his thirteenth year (loc. cit., ch. 16); and he undoubtedly was of that age in that year, as appears from what we have argued elsewhere concerning his birthday [Col. 0125D] (§ 6). If perhaps Spartianus’ authority seems worthy of rejection because he is not sufficiently consistent with himself in determining Caracalla’s age, this scruple is certainly removed by a most elegant coin from the treasury formerly at Schwarzburg and now at Gotha, which cannot be doubted to have been intended to preserve the memory of this event.

[Note: [Col. 0125D] Polycarp Tentzel communicated it to the learned world in a dissertation held under the presidency of Johann Andreas Schmidt, in which he explains selected gold, silver, and large-module bronze coins from the Amstadt-Schwarzburg treasury, pp. 22 ff. Scaliger, in his *Annotations on Eusebius*, p. 228, has another inscription in which the names of those three are joined with the ninth year of Severus’ tribunician power and the fourth year of Caracalla’s.]

One side presents the head of Severus encircled with laurel, with the added inscription: “Severus Aug. Part. Max. P. M. Tr. P. IX.” [Col. 0126A] The other presents the bare head of Geta and the laureate head of Caracalla, indicating that the latter was Augustus and the former Caesar, with the legend: “Aeternit. Imperi.” For the coin not only confirms what Spartianus reported concerning the time when they assumed these ranks, but also supports what we said before, that this event occurred in the year 201, since Severus assumed his ninth tribunician power in that year.

But let us return to the book *On the Crown*. We say that the passage examined above can be understood best if the opinion that we believe ourselves to have sufficiently proved is admitted. For it makes it particularly intelligible why the soldiers marched crowned with laurel—a circumstance that should surprise no one who knows that soldiers likewise wore crowns in triumphs, and something of that kind can readily be discerned here. For by now almost all those against whom Severus had led his army had been subdued, especially the most warlike nation of the Parthians; [Col. 0126B] and Severus himself, since an ailment of the joints prevented him from celebrating a triumph over the Parthians, nevertheless assigned his son a triumph to celebrate over the Jews, and perhaps, to compensate for the Parthian triumph, granted the soldiers the insignia of triumphants.

If we hold to these points, it also becomes clear how a fear that peace would now be disturbed could arise among the Christians. For all the emperor’s enemies had now been laid low, and Severus’ harshness, now that he was free from other business, threatened the utmost severity, which, while he was still occupied by graver affairs, he had been unable to pursue. And the soldier’s example seemed especially well suited to inflaming that harshness, since the worshipers of the gods, in their customary manner, believed that by rejecting the crown he had committed parricide and declared himself a public enemy of Caesar. [Col. 0126C]

### § 2.—The Opinions of Those Who Think Otherwise Examined, Especially Those of Hoffmann and Orsi, Who Assigned the Book to 198, and of Pagi, Who Assigned It to 202
