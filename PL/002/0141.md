**LOCARE.** I, 626 B, “How many idle men does the affectation of arms assign to the sword?” This concerns those who, under Affectation as though under a mistress and teacher, are assigned to gladiatorial services, although no one can compel them; Junius’ conjectural reading *vocant* (“they call”) is therefore unnecessary.

**LOCULUS.** I, 1314 A, “A great patrimony is displayed in the smallest compartments.” The *loculi* are not cases and repositories in which that feminine finery is stored and preserved, but compartments in women’s dress—settings, slings, and anything else of that kind—in which those precious objects are displayed, that is, carried openly for all to behold in ostentatious display.

**LOCUS.** I, 1043 B, “Then of places,” that is, “then of times.” I, 446 B, “We are found in some place of accident,” that is, [Col. 1328A] some accident may strike us. II, 103 B, “We shall give place,” that is, there will be an opportunity to speak elsewhere.

**Λόγος [LOGOS].** I, 398 A, “Among your own sages too it is agreed that the *logos* [λόγος], that is, speech and reason, is regarded as the artificer of the universe.” Besides those whom he names, there are Plutarch, Numenius, Plotinus, and Plato himself. Theodoret quotes Plotinus’ words: “Thus, then, from the one Intellect and from the Logos proceeding from it, this universe arose and was differentiated” (*Οὕτω δὴ ἐξ ἑνὸς νοῦ καὶ τοῦ ἀπ’ αὐτοῦ λόγου, ἀνέστη τόδε τὸ πᾶν καὶ διέστη*). A statement by Numenius about Plato survives in Hesychius of Miletus, *On Illustrious Men*, who censured him with this remark: “As one who plundered from the books of Moses what he wrote about God and the cosmos” (*Ὡς ἐκ τῶν Μωσαϊκῶν βιβλίων τὰ περὶ Θεοῦ καὶ κόσμου ἀποσυλήσασαν*). For this reason he was called by the same author “Moses speaking Attic Greek”: “For what is Plato but Moses speaking Attic Greek?” (*Τί γάρ ἐστιν Πλάτων, ἢ Μώσης ἀττικίζων;*). I, 400 B, “The Logos of God,” that is, that primordial, first-begotten Word. II, 160 A, “The Greeks call this reason *logos* [λόγος], a word by which we also designate speech.”

**LONGINQUARE.** II, 987 B, “Nothing that is second becomes distant from the first.”

**LUCAR.** II, 137 B, “He is cut down in an insulting slaughter for the fee of a dancing girl.” *Lucar* was the pay of dancers [Col. 1328B] and actors. Plutarch renders it “that which is paid for sacred spectacles” (*τὸ τελούμενον εἰς τὰς θείας* [the transmitted Greek may be defective]). Charisius and the glossaries give *θεατρικόν*, “theatrical allowance,” and likewise *μισθὸς ἀπὸ φισκοῦ*, “payment from the treasury.” From that *lucar*, money was supplied for theatrical costs and expenses; hence grammarians render it *θεατρικόν*. Tacitus, *Annals* I, reports that limits were imposed upon these expenses and that actors’ *lucar* was curtailed in the time of Tiberius.

**LUCERNATUS.** I, 1298 A, “And he comes forth from a doorway adorned with laurel and lamps.” In antiquity, at every public and private celebration, it was customary to decorate the doorway with laurels and lamps. Lamps of this kind were pendant, being suspended while lit by little chains from the doors or windows of houses. See *Apology*, chap. 35.

**LUCIFUGUS.** II, 545 A. “A light-shunning beast,” said of the serpent.

**LUDERE.** II, 1038 A, “To play with one’s own hide.” A proverb concerning people who accommodate themselves most conveniently and suitably to every circumstance. See **CORIUM**.

**LUDUM.** I, 1262 A, “When at last they assign themselves to the training school and the camp”: he means the gladiatorial school. [Col. 1328C]

**LUMEN.** II, 1030 C, “By the assembly of light.” See **CONCILIUM**.

**LUNENSES.** II, 590 A, It denotes an ancient town of the Etruscans, from which comes Luna marble. See also Pliny, *Natural History*, book XI, chap. 42.

**LUSIO.** II, 550 A, “Regard it, reader, as a playful exercise before the engagement,” that is, a prelude. He alludes to the preliminary exercises of gladiators, which were conducted with wooden practice swords.

**LUSIUS.** I, 280 B, “How *lusius*.” Pamelius reads *qui Lucius* (“who, Lucius”). The manuscripts of Ursinus and Scriverius have *lascivus* (“wanton”). Havercamp thinks that Tertullian wrote *pusus*, or perhaps *pusius*, whence *pusio*, “a little child.”

**LYMPHATUS.** I, 1205 B. Festus: “*Lymphae* are said to have been named from the nymphs; it has commonly been handed down, moreover, that whoever saw a certain apparition from a spring—that is, the likeness of a nymph—would never cease raging. The Greeks call such people *nymph-possessed* (*νυμφολήπτους*), while the Latins call them *lymphati*.”

**MANTICULARIUS,** a thief who searches purses. According to Festus, *manticulari* means to handle little purses for the purpose of stealing. I, 496 B. [Col. 1329C]

**MANUS.** I, 468 A, “As though arrayed as a troop before God,” that is, with forces gathered before God in compact companies. **MANUALIS.** I, 477 A, “Water for the hands,” which was poured out for washing the hands, as distinguished from baths, which people used before dinner.

**MAPPA.** The term denoted a whitish cloth which the praetor or the sponsor of the games threw or raised when about to give the signal for the games to begin. Hence Quintilian too, book I, chap. 5, calls it a name adopted from the circus. From this came the proverbial expression “to throw the *mappa*,” meaning to give any sort of signal. I, 648 B, “They do not see what it is; they think the *mappa* has been thrown.” [Col. 1329D]

**MARGO,** the edge of a public road. II, 1048 A, “I am accustomed to prescribe medicine for morals from any roadside or altar.” Turnebus proposes *imagine* (“image”). Here, however, it is the philosopher in *The Pallium* who is speaking, not a Christian. A Christian could not have been induced to leap upon an altar for the sake of delivering a public address, since Christians at that time detested altars and sacrifices and blew upon them in abhorrence as they passed.

**MARTYRIUM.** II, 63 A, “He denies that martyrdoms should be performed,” that is, that martyrdom should be undergone for Christ’s name—evidently because he denies that Christ suffered for us. II, 125 A, “Having tasted martyrdom in prison, they hunger for it.” Rigault reads “having tasted martyrdoms,” in this sense: they desire to consummate the martyrdoms which they have only tasted, having been beaten with clubs or claws and, as it were, merely grazed on the outermost skin.

**MARTYR.** II, 970 B, “Uncertain martyrs,” that is, persons whose faith has been less thoroughly tested and not sufficiently ascertained.

**MARRUCINUS.** II, 515 A, “And here you are not a Marrucinian but a Pontic.” The Marrucinians were celebrated for thievish tricks and stratagems, as Latinius observes from Catullus’ twelfth epigram; the people of Pontus, however, for mental dullness. On the Marrucinians, see Strabo, book V.

**MASSALIS.** II, 224 B, “Of that massive bulk,” equivalent to Ovid’s “a rude and undigested mass.” II, 402 B, “Into the Lord, as into his own collective sum” (John), that is, the sum in its entirety.

**MASSALITER.** II, 570 A, “And thus consolidated into a mass.” II, 118 C, “They imposed upon themselves collectively the tribute of the whole Church,” that is, jointly and on behalf of all, not individually on behalf of this person or that.

**MATERIARIUS.** II, 219 C, “And the other materialist heretics,” that is, those who maintain that matter is eternal.

**MATHESIS.** I, 672 A, “Today, of course, the *mathesis* concerns Christ,” that is, today’s *mathesis*—discipline or doctrine—is the discipline of Christ. The star of Christ, that is, Christ’s nativity, and his examples and commandments, are to be observed and proclaimed by us. Le Prieur wishes these words to be understood ironically—not altogether aptly. [Col. 1330B]

**Μάθησις [LEARNING].** II, 687 A, “Acts of learning are acts of recollection” (*Μαθήσεις ἀναμνήσεις*), that is, learning consists in recollections. This passage was undoubtedly taken from Plato’s *Phaedo*, not from the *Timaeus*. For there it reads: “But also according to that argument, Cebes said, if it is true, Socrates, which you are often accustomed to advance: ‘Our learning proves to be nothing other than recollection’” (*Ὅτι ἡμῖν ἡ μάθησις οὐκ ἄλλο τι ἢ ἀνάμνησις τυγχάνει οὖσα*).

**MATRIX,** *σημεῖον τῶν ὀνομάτων*, “a register of names,” in the Glossary; in law, *matricula*, that is, a register of offices or a list. II, 118 C, “In the registers of *beneficiarii* and intelligence agents.” II, 599 B, “As though the matrix of all God’s commandments.” II, 211 B, “Matter will also appear to be the matrix of good.” II, 803 B, “More splendid and nobler from a more worn-out matrix.” II, 304 A, “And their very matrix, goodness.”

**MATRIMONIUM.** I, 532 A, “She gave herself to the funeral pyre instead of to a second marriage,” that is, she threw herself upon the pyre rather than allow herself to be joined in a second marriage to Hiarbas, king of the Moors, who was pressing his suit even with threats. Justin, book XVIII. [Col. 1330C]

**MATTHAEA.** I, 614 A, “When, after paying funeral honor to yourself with savory dishes and *matthaeae*, you withdraw to the tombs.” Junius reads *mattyis*. Athenaeus, book XIV: “*Mattye* is every costly dish” (*ματτύη πᾶν τὸ πολυτελὲς ἔδεσμα*); hence *ματτυάζειν*, “to prepare such dishes,” and *ματτυολοιχός*, “a gourmand for such dishes.” Here, in a somewhat distinctive sense, it appears to mean *τὰ ἔπαικλα*—dessert foods, sweetmeats, or the second course—since savory dishes have already been mentioned. In this class are the foods called *macteae* in Latin, from *mactare*, that is, “to honor”: delicacies, choice honorary dishes, and sweetmeats are names for the same things. Suetonius, *Caligula* XXXVIII: “He sent poisoned *macteae* to many”; hence also Arnobius’ *macteolae*, book VII. The other word must therefore be read either *mattyis* or *macteis*. Of these consisted the *silicernium*, which at Rome was customarily furnished to poorer people on the Esquiline or at the *Puticuli* outside the gate—namely, the banquet mentioned in what follows.

**MATURARE.** I, 1229 A, “When he had again hastened back to his mercy,” that is, had returned promptly and at the proper time. Thus Cyprian spoke of “grace that hastens.”

**MICA.** II, 655 B, “Or he inserted crumbs from Aristotle’s minute discourse.” By “crumbs” and “minute discourse” he means *λεπτολογία*, minute argumentation: a subtle, concise manner of writing such as Aristotle’s. Junius reads *intersit*. *Intergere*, however, is *ἀπομάσσειν*, “to wipe off,” in the Glossary, and properly describes nurses, who present porridge to little children by wiping it off, as though wiping their fingers against the children’s cheeks.

**MILESIAE.** II, 686 C, “Through which the histories and Milesian tales of their aeons.” Latinius reads “histories equally Milesian,” or “those histories, and Milesian ones at that.” In Latin, grandiose and extravagantly tragic tales (*ὑπερτραγωδούμεναι*) are called “Milesian.” Julius Capitolinus, in *Clodius Albinus*: “Some say that certain Milesian tales are his as well. Their reputation is regarded as not undistinguished, although they are only moderately well written.” They appear to have been called Milesian because of the Milesians’ empty boasting about the oracle of Didymaean Apollo at Branchidae. After Xerxes had long before destroyed its temple and the oracle had ceased, they wove together innumerable tales in a vain effort to affirm the restoration of their oracle in the temple which they had rebuilt as the greatest of all, as Strabo recounts in book XIV of his *Geography*. [Col. 1332B]

**MILITARE.** II, 1031 B, “Which serves as a soldier to shatter walls,” that is, whose military function is to shatter walls. Ciacconius, cited by Latinius, prefers *minitat* (“threatens”).

**MIMICE.** I, 504 A, “Philosophers affect truth in the manner of mimes,” that is, they act as mimes, since they appear one thing outwardly but are another inwardly, and so sometimes become ridiculous when their vices are uncovered.

**MINISTRARE,** to employ as a servant, to instigate. I, 276 A, “Which may employ you as its servants.” Heumann prefers “which you may serve.” II, 775 B, “Without this knowledge of itself, no soul could have ministered itself,” that is, could rationally have offered itself by itself as a minister.

**MINUTALIS.** II, 251 A, “If, so to speak, in minute kingdoms.” II, 703 A, “Gnats, ants, moths, and minute creatures of this kind.” I, 1310 B, “The minute particles of the same earth,” that is, crumbs or the smallest parts.

**MINUTILOQUIUM.** II, 655 B, “Or he inserted crumbs from Aristotle’s minute discourse.” Irenaeus, book II, where he had said against the Valentinians: “They also attempt to introduce into the faith minute argumentation and subtlety concerning questions, although this is Aristotelian.”

**MIRIONES,** *θαυμασταί*, people who marvel at everything and stand astonished at it; or, as Rhenanus thinks, those who, because of their excessive and unseemly readiness to marvel and to take offense, were a source of distress and astonishment to their steadfast brethren. II, 14 B, “Those wonderers, indeed, are accustomed . . .” Others read: “Those weaker people, indeed, are accustomed . . .”; in this sense, heresies derive what strength they have from the weaknesses of certain persons. Those weaknesses—that is, those weaker Christians—are accustomed to being built up into ruin, namely into heresy, by those whom heresy has already deceived.

**MISCERE.** I, 399 B, “A man is born mingled with God.”

**MISERABILIS.** II, 963 B, “Insofar as you are pitiable,” that is, how great a favor of compassion you have obtained.

**MITHRA.** *Μίθρα* is the Persian name for the sun. II, 54 A, “And if he still remembers Mithras,” that is, performs his distinctive rites. II, 261 A, “Just as the lions of Mithras philosophically interpret the sacraments of a dry and burning nature.” Images of lions were employed in the rites of Mithras, or of the Sun. They were *sacramenta*, that is, signs or mysteries of the sun’s dry and burning nature. For the heat intensifies and springs and rivers dry up when the sun is said to enter Leo; then, finally, the abduction of Ceres is celebrated—that is, the harvest is carried out—as the scholiast on Aratus teaches.

**MITRA,** a woman’s head ornament: a slender band, like a little wreath made of loosely woven thread and suited to holding the hair. II, 912 A, “For some women do not veil their heads with headbands and wool.”

[Col. 1333A]

**MNA.** Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXI, chap. 27: “The *mna*, which our people call the *mina*, weighs one hundred Attic drachmas.” Ten drachmas, however, equal one gold crown. A mina was therefore worth ten crowns or gold pieces. Fannius, *On Weights* V, 32, teaches that the *mina* itself is the Greek *μνᾶ*.

**MODESTIA.** I, 464 B, “From foresight for public restraint,” that is, provision and foresight are exercised for public peace.

**MODULARI.** I, 1170 A, n. (2), “By conforming ourselves to the Lord’s passion while praying, we confess Christ,” that is, by imitating the posture which Christ displayed with his hands spread upon the cross. Thus, while praying, we confess Christ, because in the very gesture of prayer we profess ourselves Christians, conforming our body to the figure of Christ crucified. Tertullian himself, *To the Nations* I: “If you set a man upright with hands outspread, you will have made an image of the cross.” I, 1208 A, “But shall God not be permitted, in his own instrument, through holy hands, to give form to spiritual sublimity?”—that is, to perform and display it in deed according to its proper measure. I, 433 A, “Regulated and equipped for every perversity,” [Col. 1333B] that is, adjusted and adapted.

**MODULUS,** arrangement. I, 399 B, “God produced another from God by measure, not by number; by degree, not by state.” That is, Father and Son differ by *modulus*—arrangement, distribution, or distinction—but not by diversity or numerical separation; by degree, in that the Son is the second person in the Godhead, but not by state, as though he were another God.

**MODUS.** II, 232 A, “Concerning the position of matter, I maintain the same thing as concerning its measure,” that is, the principle of position and measure is the same, and consequently so is the judgment concerning them. For *modus* is the quantity or bulk of any given thing, and the position corresponds to the thing’s measure.

**MOECHUS.** II, 1021 A, “With that apocryphal *Shepherd* of adulterers.” He calls the Psychics adulterers; they had accepted the book of *The Shepherd*.

**MOLIRI,** to prepare something for oneself. II, 1048 A, “Likewise, Sulla contrives platters weighing one hundred pounds.”

**MOLITIO.** II, 807 B, “The queen of his own construction,” that is, of creation. God created the human being as king [Col. 1333C] of everything on land and in the sea.

**MONARCHIA.** II, 158 A, “‘We uphold the monarchy,’ they say,” that is, we affirm one God. Jerome, in his book *On Ecclesiastical Writers*, states that Irenaeus composed a book with the title *On the Monarchy, or That God Is Not the Author of Evils*. Tertullian himself used the word “monarchy” to signify God. II, 164 B, “Thus the Trinity, descending from the Father through interwoven and connected degrees, neither makes any discord against the monarchy nor impairs the state of the *economy* (*οἰκονομία*, ordered dispensation).”

**MONELA.** II, 445 B, “Our God’s admonition from heaven commanded that not Moses and the Prophets, but Christ, be heard.” I, 1262 B, “The Lord’s admonition is at hand.”

**MONSTRUM.** II, 1041 A, “A monstrous instructor,” that is, a monstrous teacher—namely Chiron, composed of horse and human and thus a Centaur. Others read “an instructor of monsters.”

**MORA.** I, 1248 A, “A stag pierced by an arrow, in order to expel from the wound the iron and its barbs, which prevent withdrawal.” In surgery, a *mora* is part of a splint into which, after it has been bandaged, a broken leg is placed. [Col. 1333D] It is called *mora* because it restrains the leg, holds the thigh as it has been set, and does not allow it to slip from its proper position. Similar *morae* are found on arrows: they hold them fast, so that they cannot readily be withdrawn or torn out. See Celsus, book VIII, chap. 10; on the stag, Pliny, *Natural History*, book VIII, chap. 27. I, 434 A, “Meanwhile enjoying malice through the delay of punishment.” I, 468 B, “We pray for a delay of the end.” II, 711 A, “Unless the delay of resurrection is also paid as the penalty for a slight transgression.”

**MORATUS.** I, 237, “Something already endowed with qualities in common with the Creator.” He calls something *moratum* when it is endowed with certain dispositions such as the Creator possesses—namely hatred and benevolence, anger and mercy, justice and clemency. I, 1255 A, “How much more ought we to be found disposed according to—” (read: “disposed according to the Lord”). I, 1203 A, “The Spirit of God, who from the beginning was borne above the waters, destined to assume the character of baptizer.” II, 707 A, “Likewise those who, having lived uprightly, commend their life to the Judge.”

[Col. 1334A]

**MORI.** II, 810 A, “The day dies into night and is buried everywhere in darkness.”

**MOROSE,** carefully, accurately. II, 1045 A, “Although this very cloak has been arranged more carefully.”

**MOROSITAS.** II, 55 A, “That punctiliousness of the Jewish law,” that is, the scrupulosity of Jewish customs and rites. Rigault reads “of the Jewish law.”

**MORS.** II, 1034 B, “It lives death,” that is, that sea is still visible and alive, but it is called “dead”; therefore it does not live life, but death. II, 800 A, “And now the death of every designation”: the dead cease to be whatever they were called while they lived, and now are nothing. Pliny, *Natural History*, book II, where he discusses the nature of the earth. II, 810 A, “Destroying its own death,” said of the sun, which rises on the next day. I, 524 A, “Who fashioned this whole body of the world from that which had not existed, no less than from the death of vacancy and emptiness.”

**MOTATOR.** II, 666 A, “As those wish who even declare the soul to be the mover of the universe.”

**MOTIUNCULAE,** slight fevers. II, 577 B, “Meanwhile, I shall argue that it was shaken out by its slight agitations.” [Col. 1334B]

**MOTORIUM.** II, 608 B, “For these are not members of the animal substance, but faculties: the motive, the active, and the cognitive.”

**MUGIRE.** I, 1244 A, “And to bellow day and night to the Lord your God.”

**MULIERITAS.** II, 906 B–907 A, “Having openly professed womanhood,” that is, the female condition.

**MULLEOLUS,** a shoe, a crescent-bearing shoe. II, 1044 B, “He puts on a plain shoe or a *mulleolus*”: a plain shoe is one to which nothing is sewn; a *mulleolus* is one to which a little crescent is sewn. The *mulleus* was the shoe of the patricians.

**MULSA.** II, 655 B, “No one ever poured honeyed water from Plato’s discourse into a person hesitating about the soul’s departure.” *Aqua mulsa*, *ὑδρόμελι* or *μελίκρατον*, is water mixed with honey. He alludes not only to the abundance but also to the honeyed or mellifluous quality of Plato’s eloquence. In the book *On the Soul*, he says of the same Plato: “On account of the honey of his eloquence.”

**MULTINUBENTIA,** polygamy (*πολυγαμία*). II, 953 C.

[Col. 1334C]

**MULTIVORANTIA,** gluttony or eating much (*πολυφαγία*). II, 953 C.

**MULUS.** I, 574 A, “This is to paint a mule from an ass,” a proverbial expression meaning to produce, after a model of follies, an example of similar follies, or to fabricate new falsehoods after old ones. For he adds: “and to copy Ptolemy from Valentinus.” Valentinus first devised these monstrous trifles. Ptolemy added something of his own, differing somewhat from his master. Hence the name of ass belongs to Valentinus, the author of this heresy, and that of mule to his disciple Ptolemy.

**MUNDITENENS,** world-ruler (*κοσμοκράτωρ*). II, 518 B; 576 A.

**MURTIA.** I, 640 A, “An idol also made the turning-posts ‘Murtian,’” that is, even the *metae Murtiae* were named after an idol. The Murtian turning-posts were the last turning-posts in the Circus Maximus, near the shrine of Murtia. Behind them stood the herald, who watched the contestants’ performances from that side and proclaimed those who had performed well or badly. They hold Murtia to have been the goddess of love, that is, Venus, etc. This Venus was first called “Myrtle Venus” or “Murtian Venus” from two myrtles—one patrician and the other plebeian—planted before the shrine of Quirinus, and was subsequently called Murtia. [Col. 1334D] Pliny recounts the story in *Natural History*, book XV, chap. 29. Varro mentions it in book IV of *On the Latin Language*. So Junius; but Rigault reads “the god of languor.” Augustine, *City of God* IV, chap. 16: “They called her the goddess Murcia, who would not move beyond due measure and would make a person, as Pomponius says, *marcidus*, that is, excessively sluggish.”

**MUTARE.** II, 229 A, “To be changed is to perish with respect to one’s former state”; things lose their former state when they are changed.

**MYSTERIA ATTICA.** I, 474 A, The “Attic mysteries” are the Eleusinian rites.

**NASCIBILIS.** II, 349 A, “He denies that mine is mortal, although he acknowledges that it is capable of being born.”

**NASUS.** II, 984 B, “And one who is not mocked by the nose”: thus he rendered Paul’s expression in Galatians VI, *οὐ μυκτηρίζεται*, “is not mocked.” Hesychius: “*μυκτηρίζει*: he mocks, he ridicules, from making a contemptuous sound with the nostrils” (*μυκτηρίζει, χλευάζει, καταγελᾷ, ἀπὸ τοῦ μύζειν τοῖς μυκτῆρσι*).

[Col. 1335A]

**NATALES,** origin, beginning. I, 1256 B, “Therefore I detect the origin of impatience in the devil himself.” Thus, in book I *Against Marcion*, he spoke of “the origin of recognition,” and in *The Pallium*, of “the origin of the world.”

**NATATILIS.** II, 228 B, “So that swimming and flying creatures should arise from the waters.”

**NATURA.** I, 811 A, “And by the disposition of nature.” The Fulda manuscript has “of the divine nature”; and the person who supplied that collation—either Modius or Scioppius—made a note of it. This is certainly a weightier expression and more consonant with Christ’s words. I, 471 A, “By the right of nature, their one mother.”

**NATURALIS.** II, 894 B, “*Female* is a natural designation; *woman* is the general designation within the natural one.” “Natural designation” means one common by nature to all animals, which are distinguished by sex. “General” is that which distinguishes within the human race; beneath this comes the specific designation.

**NATURALITAS.** II, 722 B, “Who, then, will doubt the natural character of sleep?”

**NATURIFICATUS.** II, 584 B, “By kinship with the now naturalized spiritual condition.”

[Col. 1335B]

**NAUFRAGIUM.** I, 695 A, “Shipwreck is inescapable for those dashed against them”—Greek *ναυάγιον ἄφυκτον*, “a shipwreck that cannot be escaped.” Those who strike against reefs and wreck their ship cannot disentangle or extricate themselves from danger.

**NAVICULARIUS.** II, 374 B, “It would be another matter if he had deliberately chosen someone from the guild of shipowners.”

**NAVIGARE.** I, 695 A, “Among these reefs and bays, among these shallows and straits of idolatry, faith sails with the Spirit of God filling its sails.”

**NECESSARIUS.** I, 1310 A, “More numerous and more necessary services.” II, 768 A, “As though they brought something necessary to it,” in place of “something more necessary.” I, 1266 A, “Where the injury is greater, there the remedy for the injury is more necessary.” II, 1002 B, “Unless it is so that they may bind them perpetually to more necessary things.”

**NECESSITAS.** I, 273 A, “O judgment confounded by necessity,” that is, one which necessarily sins against justice in some respect. Or rather: Trajan, wishing to make allowance both for hatred against Christians and for the justice of their cause, properly satisfies neither. I, 275 A, The necessity [Col. 1335C] which unjust laws enacted against Christians impose upon judges. I, 345 A, “According as each has found domestic necessity a holier master.” The sense is: in truth they recognize that no good accrues to them from those gods who are themselves subject to a greater power—namely, domestic necessity. II, 385 C, “Since he adduces human necessity as though it were an advocate pleading in defense.” II, 93 A, “The condition of faith does not admit a plea of necessity.” II, 93 B, “Those for whom the advocacy of necessity is most familiar.”

**NEGARE.** II, 246 C, “Whose designation as ‘Euxine’ is belied by its nature,” that is, it deceives through the falsehood of its surname, since, because of the character of the place and the savagery of its nature, it ought to be called *Axenos* (*ἄξενος*, “inhospitable”). Ovid: “The Pontus which bears the lying surname Euxine.” II, 247 A, “All are denied,” that is, are restrained by ice and cannot flow. I, 324 A, “Otherwise they would have to be denied, if they had not tasted it,” that is, it would have to be denied that they were Christians if they did not taste the blood which they are so insistently said to crave.

**NEGATOR.** II, 95 A, “Thus, by surrendering my faith, I am found to be a denier.”

**NEGOTIUM.** II, 285 A, “But now God suffers trouble,” [Col. 1335D] that is, the rashness and audacity of the Marcionites cause trouble for God, since they fabricated for themselves another god distinct from the Omnipotent. II, 905 B, “For they send females into business at twelve years, but the male two years later,” that is, they establish that they are legally independent, as though releasing them from the restraints of another’s authority; they grant them the power to conduct business—a legal expression. II, 649 B, “From the business of inquisitiveness.” II, 888 B, “Now a concern peculiar to me, arising from the suffering occasioned by my opinion,” that is, I who was previously occupied in setting forth in Greek my opinion about the veiling of virgins. Junius explains it thus; but Rigault thinks that, in some disputation against the Greeks, Tertullian may have argued rather forcefully that virgins too ought to be veiled, and was met with Greek abuse and harassed as a heretic.

**NEOPHYTUS,** *νεόφυτος*, a person who has recently come to the Christian faith. II, 57 A; 268 B.

**NEPOTARI,** to live luxuriously and extravagantly. I, 513 A, “Beneath a grand façade of seriousness, he lives extravagantly.”

[Col. 1336A]

**NERVUS.** II, 786 B, “Through that umbilical cord,” that is, the joining of veins and arteries, insofar as their fibers converge and unite in the navel. I, 623 B, “The leg feels nothing in the *nervus*.” The *nervus* is a kind of wooden device by which separated and dislocated legs were broken or tortured. In the Law of the Twelve Tables: “Let him lead him away with him; let him bind him, either in a *nervus* or in fetters of fifteen pounds,” etc. An old glossary gives *nervus*: *ξυλοπέδη*, “wooden fetter.” I, 512, “Other sinews for incestuous lust.”

**NIDOR.** I, 407 A, “Their peculiar foods of smoke and blood,” through libations and sacrifices; but in this passage he may perhaps be speaking of sacrifices generally, not of necromantic sacrifices.

**NIMIETAS.** II, 236 A, “Incomprehensible because of the excessive magnitude of the conflict.”

**NOBILIS.** II, 150 C, “Are the Apostles’ writings, then, also well known?”—that is, are they not known to everyone, clear, and manifest? Ursinus’ copy has *mobiles* (“changeable”), that is, writings which mean something other than they sound, with one thing in the words and another in the meanings. Rigault nevertheless prefers the former reading. Junius reads *nubilae* (“clouded”), that is, covered by a cloud of obscurity and darkness so that they cannot be understood.

[Col. 1336B]

**NOEROS.** *Νοερός*, “mental,” “intellectual”; others read *noetos*, *νοητός*, “intelligible,” as in Irenaeus. II, 574 B.

**NOMEN,** the Christian religion. I, 278 A, “Let us be excluded from that name,” that is, let us cease to be called Christians and reject the Christian religion. I, 280 A, “Those who formerly, before this name,” that is, in earlier times, before they became Christians. I, 1252 A, “Persecuting the name together with its family,” that is, the Christian religion and its followers. I, 661 A, “Persecutors of the Lord’s name.” I, 525 A, “You, human being, so great a name,” that is, a thing of immense importance and great glory. II, 890 A, “And the whole scope of the name ‘tree’ is unfolded,” that is, everything signified by the word “tree.” II, 800 A, “And destined to perish from this designation too, thereafter into no designation.” After the corruption of our corpse, no designated name exists by which the matter that had constituted the human body may be marked. This he calls “the death of the designation.”
