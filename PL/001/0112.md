Tertullian presses the point: If some window into the human heart and mind lay open through which its innermost thoughts might be examined, we should then certainly recognize with what ardent wishes they all desired “the spectacle of a new Caesar, and yet another new Caesar”—that is, the frequent death of one Caesar and the choice of another, who would preside over the distribution of a largess [Col. 1058D] (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 35, p. 455). Some, however, would prefer the reading *praesiderantis* (“of one presiding”), but this rests on a slight conjecture and conflicts with the authority of the manuscripts. For Tertullian means that the pagans were always longing for the spectacle of a new Caesar who would preside over the distribution of a *congiarium*, or gift—usually one of money—to the populace or soldiers, or would order it distributed in his presence. It was indeed customary for newly elected Caesars, or those who sought election, to lavish gifts of this kind upon the people. Many examples of this largess may be found everywhere in the writers of Roman history. If anyone nevertheless desires some, we shall select a few of them.

On the day after Claudius had been carried off by the soldiers to imperial power—seized rather than elected—“he allowed the armed men,” Suetonius says, “to swear allegiance to his name before the assembly, [Col. 1059A] and promised fifteen thousand sesterces to each of them, being the first of the Caesars to pledge the loyalty of the soldier by means of a reward as well” (Suetonius, *Life of Claudius*, § 10). After Claudius died, “Nero was brought into the camp”—these are the words of Cornelius Tacitus—“and, after a preliminary address suited to the occasion, he was acclaimed emperor upon promising a donative modeled upon his father’s generosity” (Tacitus, at the end of Book XII of the *Annals*). Spartianus, moreover, attests that Hadrian gave the soldiers a double largess at the beginning of his reign (Spartianus, *Life of Hadrian*, shortly after the beginning); and Lampridius records other gifts bestowed by Macrinus when he proclaimed Antoninus Diadumenus emperor (Lampridius, beginning of the *Life of Antoninus Diadumenus*). Anyone for whom these examples are insufficient may consult Robertellus (Robertellus, *De Commodis Praemiis Donativisque Militum*), Pancirolus (Pancirolus, *Notitia Imperii Orientalis*, [Col. 1059B] chap. 74), and others. The pagans, therefore, always impelled by gain, desired the death of the reigning Caesar so that they might more frequently receive a donative, and longed for the spectacle of a new Caesar—though he too was soon to die.

Our Author presses the point still further: at the very time and in the very hour when they acclaimed:

> May Jupiter add to your years from our years,

they were all the more eagerly desiring this spectacle and the Caesar’s death. But in our manuscripts and the older printed editions we read, with the metrical form broken:

> From . . . . . may Jupiter add years to you.

(Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 35, p. 456.) However it may be read, Tertullian there intimates that this acclamation of the pagans was feigned and altogether mendacious, since at that very time they desired the Caesar’s death with their whole hearts. Nor does there seem to be any room for doubt that, [Col. 1059C] with a similar falsehood, Ovid acclaimed Caesar Augustus with a similar falsehood in this verse:

> May he increase the dominion of our Leader, and increase his years.

(Ovid, *Fasti*, Book I, v. 611.)

Yet if this acclamation of the pagans was entirely feigned, it is evident that Ambrose’s prayer for the emperor Valentinian, though already dead, was utterly sincere: “Nor do I deny that he died at an untimely age, whom we would have wished to sustain with portions of our own lives, so that he might live from our years, since he could not fulfill his own” (Ambrose, *On the Death of Valentinian*, § 43, p. 1186). We observed long ago that Ambrose was alluding here to the solemn formula of acclamation noted by Tertullian. Furthermore, we frequently read many similar acclamations, in which the pagans appeared to wish the Caesars a long life, in the books of those who wrote the lives of the emperors Commodus, Pertinax, [Col. 1059D] Probus, Balbinus, and others. One may also consult Brissonius (Brissonius, Book I, *De Formulis*, p. 95 ff.) and Bernardino Ferrari’s first book on acclamations and applause (Ferrari, Book II, *De Acclamationibus*, chaps. 19, 20 ff.). In any event, Tertullian rightly concludes from what has been said that the pagans who made wishes of this kind for the emperor’s imminent death were in fact public enemies both of him and of the commonwealth, as will be further apparent from what must be said in the following article.

## ARTICLE IV. That the greater enemies of the emperors were the pagan traitors—the Cassii, Nigri, Albini, and others—who beset Caesar between two laurels; who practiced wrestling in order to squeeze his throat; who broke into the palace, more daring than all the Sigerii and Parthenii; who were daily revealed as accomplices and applauders of criminal factions; and, finally, who consulted astrologers, haruspices, augurs, and magicians concerning the emperors’ lives, and offered prayers for their safety that were as false as those of the Christians were sincere

[Col. 1060A]

If the pagans who demanded the death of Caesars and emperors with such impious wishes were guilty of violating human majesty, other rebels and cowardly traitors undoubtedly sinned against them far more gravely—those who had either deserted their side or, after attempting to deprive them of life, sought to seize despotic power. Among their number undoubtedly belonged those mentioned by Tertullian [Col. 1060B] (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 35, p. 457): the Cassii, Nigri, and Albini—that is, Cassius Clemens, Pescennius Niger, and Albinus—who, as we saw above (above, chap. 2, art. 1), contrived to usurp the imperial power of the emperor Severus. They were therefore public enemies and guilty of violating human majesty.

Those too are plainly convicted of the same crime who, to continue in Tertullian’s words, “beset Caesar between two laurels” and “practice wrestling in order to squeeze his throat” (Tertullian, passage cited). He seems to be speaking with tolerable clarity about the emperor Commodus, against whose life plots were laid more than once. First, Pompeianus—or Quadratus, as others maintain—plotted against his life in a certain narrow entrance, as we read in the Epitome of Dio (Dio, *Epitome*, p. 284); or, [Col. 1060C] as Ammianus Marcellinus says, in the seating area of the amphitheater (Marcellinus, Book XXIX, p. 550); or, as Herodian relates, at the entrance to the amphitheater, in a dark place (Herodian, *History*, Book I, p. 472). Now, if laurels were planted at this entrance to the amphitheater, it is not difficult to understand why Commodus is said to have been beset between two laurels. But because stricter critics do not readily accept this, Rigaltius thought that these words of our Author designated the laurel grove which, according to Herodian (the same, ibid.), was planted with various trees and with beautiful, luxuriant laurel, and to which Commodus withdrew upon his physicians’ advice while a plague was raging at Rome. This interpretation might perhaps not be wholly inappropriate, had Herodian not said that the plot against Commodus was prepared not there, but at the entrance to the amphitheater. Some therefore suspect [Col. 1060D] that by “laurels” are meant two palaces of Commodus, between which the conspirator attacked him. For the laurel, as Pliny observes, was the doorkeeper of the Caesars (Pliny, *Natural History*, Book XV, chap. 30). Consider, I pray, which of these three explanations pleases you more, and we shall gladly hear your verdict. Whatever the explanation may be, it follows most evidently that this man who plotted against Caesar’s life was a public enemy.

The following words of Tertullian can undoubtedly be explained more easily: “Those who practice wrestling in order to squeeze his throat” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 35, p. 457). For a second plot was laid against the same Commodus, in which he was strangled by Narcissus, “an athlete,” as Xiphilinus says (Dio, *Epitome*, p. 384 ff.); or, [Col. 1061A] as Herodian writes (Herodian, *History*, Book I, p. 486 ff.), by Narcissus, a bold and vigorous young man; or, as Aurelius Victor relates (Aurelius Victor, *Life of Commodus*), by a very powerful athlete; or, as Lampridius attests, “by the athlete with whom he customarily trained” (Lampridius, *Life of Commodus*, p. 315), who compressed his throat.

Tertullian adds that other pagans too are public enemies and guilty of violating majesty: those “who break into the palace” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 35, p. 457)—namely, the rebellious assassins by whom the emperor Pertinax was cruelly killed, as Herodian (Herodian, *History*, Book II, p. 598), Capitolinus (Capitolinus, *Life of Pertinax*, p. 318), and Xiphilinus (Dio, *Epitome*, p. 390) committed to writing. But these men, says [Col. 1061B] our Author, were “more daring than all the Sigerii and Parthenii” (Tertullian, passage cited), by whom, that is, the emperor Domitian was killed, as we have shown elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lucius Caecilius*, chap. 6, art. 2). Accordingly, nothing remains for us to observe except that these words of Tertullian do not appear entirely uncorrupted. For our older manuscripts have *tot Sigeriis* (“so many Sigerii”), while some editions have *Stephanis* (“Stephani”), undoubtedly because Suetonius (Suetonius, Book VIII, *Life of Domitian*, § 17), without mentioning Sigerius at all, relates that Domitian was first wounded by Stephanus and was afterwards killed by Clodianus, Parthenius, and Saturius. But it is difficult to say why the name of Sigerius should have been omitted there, since he is expressly mentioned not only by Xiphilinus (Xiphilinus, Epitome of Dio, p. 335), but also by Martial in these two [Col. 1061C] verses:

> Ten times you retrace the palaces upon the sacred slope,  
> And sound the measures of Sigerius and Parthenius.

(Martial, Book IV, epigram 79.)

Let Heraldus’s conjecture therefore be dismissed: at first he suspected that *Sergiis* should be read in Tertullian’s text, and later, after better consideration, as he himself says, *Chaereis* or *Corneliis*. For these readings are opposed by the authority of our better-quality manuscripts, confirmed by the testimony of Martial and others.

Tertullian further asserts that those too are public enemies “who are now daily revealed as accomplices or applauders of criminal factions” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 35, p. 458). We have indeed already observed above (above, chap. 2, art. 2) that these words [Col. 1061D] designate the confidants and supporters of Albinus’s faction, who had conspired to destroy the emperor Severus. There too we more fully elucidated what our Author added: “Those also discharge the same duties who consult astrologers, haruspices, augurs, and magicians concerning the lives of the Caesars” (Tertullian, passage cited). For why did they consult them with such curiosity, unless because they were thinking about the Caesars’ future death, which they certainly wished would occur in a short time? For he immediately said: “Counsel is not sought concerning loved ones”—that is, blood relatives and parents—“in the same spirit as concerning masters” (Tertullian, passage cited, p. 459), whom we do not sincerely love. Regarding the former, he says, there is “the anxious concern of blood”—that is, concern for those to whom [Col. 1062A] we are joined by blood, and whom we desire to remain safe; but the concern arising from slavery is different, since every slave longs, once his master has been removed, to cast off its yoke as quickly as possible. Yet you must not suppose that Tertullian approves the first kind of consultation, concerning the welfare of relatives. His sole meaning is that the latter consultation, concerning the death of the Caesars, is far worse than the former. Indeed, he utterly and completely condemns every consultation of magicians, since such practices were, as he says (ibid.), handed down by deserting angels, as we saw above, and forbidden by God (above, art. 5).

Moreover, who would not shudder at the monstrous perfidy of all these men, who, even up to the very outbreak of their impiety, “performed sacred rites for the emperor’s safety . . . so that they might now declare their own wishes on the occasion of another’s solemnity”—namely, [Col. 1062B] that of the emperor—“and inaugurate the pattern and image of their own hope, changing the prince’s name in their hearts” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 33, p. 459)? That is, while uttering prayers wholly feigned for the emperor’s safety, but sincere prayers for his death and for his immediate successor, they named the former merely with their lips but the latter in their inmost hearts. What could be more detestable than such perfidy?

We therefore cannot sufficiently marvel at the audacity of the pagans, who, though so many examples showed them to be public enemies guilty of violating majesty, most shamelessly accused the wholly innocent Christians of this crime. For among so many rebels and public enemies no Christian was ever discovered. Hence our Tertullian wrote the following to Scapula, governor of Africa, in response to the charge: “We are defamed in matters concerning the emperor’s majesty. [Col. 1062C] Yet Christians could never be found among the Albinians, the Nigrians, or the Cassians. Rather, those very men who, up until the day before, had sworn by their guardian spirits, as we showed above, who had both offered and vowed sacrifices for their safety, and who had often condemned Christians, were discovered to be their enemies. A Christian is no one’s enemy, much less the emperor’s; knowing him to have been appointed by God, he must love, revere, and honor him, and wish him safe together with the whole Roman Empire” (the same, *To Scapula*, chap. 2). But we have sufficiently explained these matters above.

Thus the Christians’ prayers for the safety of the emperors were as sincere as those of the pagans were false, and the Christians treated the emperors with as much devotion and honor as the pagans pursued them with hatred. For, says our Author, “the dutifulness, religion, [Col. 1062D] and loyalty owed to emperors does not consist in observances of this kind, which hostility can employ rather as a veil for itself” (the same, *Apologeticum*, chap. 36, p. 460)—that is, observances beneath whose shadowy mask of false piety religion can conceal a perfidious and hostile disposition. Rather, “dutifulness and religion reside in those morals through which civility must be manifested as truly toward the emperor as toward everyone else.” This is the reading of the Wouwer manuscript and some more recent editions. In the older editions and in our manuscripts, however, the reading is: “in those morals which divinity commands, as truly . . . toward everyone else,” etc. (ibid.). Surely the authority of so many older and better-quality manuscripts carries greater weight than that of the single Wouwer manuscript? [Col. 1063A] Nor should you say that the term *civility*, by which the fellowship and concord of citizens is signified, is more suited to Tertullian’s purpose. For he is speaking of conduct commanded by God, through which religion and dutifulness must be displayed toward the emperor and all others. From this it is demonstrated still more evidently that Christians, who regarded it as impious not to obey the laws of their God, honored the emperor with sincere intent, and consequently were neither public enemies nor guilty of violating human majesty.

## ARTICLE V. What unlawful factions are, and how greatly the pious manner of life of Christians differs from them—Christians who were undeservedly called factious because they shunned the pleasures of spectacles and pursued pleasures as lawful as those in which Epicureans and others indulged, though other pleasures were forbidden

[Col. 1063B]

The implacable hatred of the pagans against the Christians could never be appeased by any arguments, however powerful; instead they cried out more and more that the Christians’ faction, being altogether opposed to both the emperors and the commonwealth, must be torn out by the roots. Tertullian intimates with tolerable clarity that the word *faction* may be understood in either a good or a bad sense (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 38, p. 464). Festus likewise says: “*Factio* and *factiosus* were originally honorable terms. Hence we also speak of the factions of actors and chariot teams. Nowadays, however, sedition and armed violence are called by the name of faction” (Festus, *On the Signification of Words*, chap. 2, s.v. *Factio*). The factions of the circus, which the pagans did not consider evil, are also mentioned frequently in the books of Suetonius (Suetonius, Book V, *Claudius*, § 5, [Col. 1063C] and Book VIII, *Domitian*, chap. 7), Cassiodorus (Cassiodorus, Book III of the *Variae*, epistle 41, p. 56, col. 2), and others, as well as in various inscriptions.

Tertullian therefore castigates the pagans in these most forceful words: “Accordingly, with no less leniency, this sect of the Christians should have been reckoned among lawful factions, since it commits nothing of the kind that is usually feared from unlawful factions” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 38, p. 464). Heraldus, however, conjectures, against the protest of all the manuscripts, printed and handwritten alike, that the reading should be “unlawful,” not “lawful factions.” Tertullian meant nothing else, he says, than that the opinion of certain pagans seemed more lenient because they believed the Christian sect to be prohibited by the laws promulgated against factions, though they erred [Col. 1063D] in placing this sect among the number of unlawful factions. But what can one venture against the unanimous agreement of all the manuscripts? What, moreover, if the particle *nec* has been transposed, or should be taken with the term “lawful,” so that the meaning of our Author here is: The pagans falsely cried out that Christians were public enemies. Nor did those treat them much more leniently who enrolled them among factions that were not lawful, with which they had nothing in common, and by which nothing of the kind is committed that is usually feared from unlawful factions (ibid.).

Let us now consider how he proved that the sect of the Christians was not unlawful. “The reason for prohibiting factions,” he says, “clearly consists in provision for public propriety, lest the community be split into parties” (ibid.). In the older [Col. 1064A] printed editions we read “in provision and public propriety.” But that reading is less satisfactory, since our Author declares the reason for prohibiting factions to be that provision should be made for and regard paid to public propriety. *Propriety* is defined by the Latin orator as follows: “The knowledge of assigning to their proper place those things which are to be done or said. And so it appears that order and arrangement will have the same force . . . since this propriety, as we interpret it, is, as I said, knowledge of the suitability of times appropriate for action” (Cicero, *On Duties*, Book I, p. 370, line 47 ff.). It seems that the term *propriety* was taken in this sense by our Tertullian, who accordingly asserts that evil factions were forbidden lest the community be split into parties through the disturbance of the order of things to be done or said at their proper place and time. For this division and [Col. 1064B] dissension “disquiets,” as he puts it—or disturbs—*comitia*, that is, general assemblies of all the orders, and *concilia* (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 38, p. 464), that is, an assembled part of them. For Laelius Felix, cited by Aulus Gellius, says: “One who commands not the entire people, but some part of it, to be present commands the holding not of *comitia*, but of a *concilium*” (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, Book XV, chap. 7, p. 847). It also disturbs the *curiae*, through which, as Varro observes (Varro, *On the Latin Language*, Book IV, p. 51), the priests formerly attended to divine affairs and the senate to human affairs; it disturbs *contiones*, whether understood as the public speeches of advocates or as the gatherings of people convened to hear them; finally, it disturbs public spectacles—the circus and other public games, as we were saying shortly before.

But all these things, Tertullian continues, were especially [Col. 1064C] to be feared at the time “when they had already begun to keep for hire the venal and mercenary service of their violence” (Tertullian, passage cited), in order to stir up disturbances and seditions against the emperor, as we have observed. For conspirators against him drew other desperate men into their faction with payment and wages.

Nothing of this kind, or even the smallest thing resembling it, was to be feared from Christians. First, nothing was done in their public assemblies except what was pious and holy; and there, as elsewhere, they prayed to God with all their hearts for the emperors, for the tranquil condition of the empire, for their enemies, and for everyone else (ibid., chap. 39, p. 468). We have already demonstrated all these matters abundantly (above, chap. 14, art. 3, and chap. 21, art. 1).

Second, since they utterly abhorred all ambition and desire for offices [Col. 1064D] and honors—or, to use our Author’s words, “since we are cold toward every ardor for glory and office, we have no need of assemblies” (Tertullian, ibid.)—that is, no need to attend the assemblies of the pagans just mentioned, where offices, magistracies, and the insignia of honors were customarily bestowed upon the covetous and ambitious. Moreover, “aloof from public affairs, we recognize only one commonwealth for all humankind—namely, the world,” which we desire to be tranquil and peaceful. Thus, inclined no more to one party than to another, we stand far removed from every turbulent, seditious, and unlawful faction whatsoever.

Furthermore, Christians [Col. 1065A] wholly and utterly shunned and detested the public spectacles of the pagans, which were extremely frequent incitements to faction. For these have no other origin than superstition, as he says—that is, the impious worship of false deities—as he himself demonstrates more fully and individually in another book. Hence in his *Apologeticum* he rightly draws this conclusion: “We have nothing to do, in speech, sight, or hearing, with the madness of the circus, the shamelessness of the theater, the savagery of the arena, or the vanity of the exercise-ground.” Thus all the manuscripts and ancient editions read; but Rigaltius and his followers removed the words “in speech, sight, or hearing.” Whether rightly, I appeal to you as judge. For those words are genuine and were added by him with the greatest deliberation, to show that Christians avoided these spectacles because, beyond the crime of idolatry, they regarded it as impious to take pleasures unlawful to speak of, see, or hear (Tertullian, [Col. 1065B] passage cited, and *On Spectacles*, chaps. 6, 7 ff.). How harmful and pestilential those pleasures were you may learn from these words of his, by which he elsewhere calls them “the solemn pleasures of the raging circus, the savage enclosure, and the lascivious stage” (the same, *Against Marcion*, Book I, chap. 27). Elsewhere: “Even a believer perishes when he slips away to a spectacle of charioteering frenzy, gladiatorial bloodshed, theatrical foulness, and gymnastic vanity” (the same, *On Modesty*, chap. 7). But more about these matters may be found in his book *On Spectacles*, and in this and other dissertations of ours.

The pagans nevertheless objected [Col. 1065C] that, by that very fact, the Christian sect ought to be prohibited and condemned: Christians spurned the assemblies of other people and avoided, as they would a public plague, the amusements of public spectacles, which the emperors and the older human laws favored. But Tertullian replies to them: Let these pleasures be as lawful as they are impious. “How do we offend you,” he says, “if we claim and pursue other pleasures? What, indeed, if we refuse to know how to be amused and reject every kind of pleasure? Surely the injury is ours, if perhaps it is not yours” (the same, *Apologeticum*, chap. 38, p. 467)—that is, we inflict loss only upon ourselves and by no means upon you, unless perhaps it is also to your loss that you devote yourselves entirely to such impious pleasures.

Nor should you press the objection that “we reject what pleases you” (ibid.). We do not deny it; indeed, we glory in it. But what concern is that of yours? Do not our pleasures likewise fail to delight you? If, then, you reject our lawful and chaste enjoyments, why should we not be permitted to reject yours, which are altogether lustful [Col. 1065D] and utterly unworthy of a human being endowed with reason?

Tertullian continues and confirms the point by the example of the Epicureans, which he sets forth in these exact words of our manuscripts: “The Epicureans were permitted to determine some true form of pleasure—that is, equanimity of mind—and extensive Christian occupations” (ibid.). The ancient editions have the same reading, except for the final word *christianae* (“Christian”), which no one of sound judgment will deny was added through the carelessness of scribes. Rigaltius, however, as we noted above (chap. 3, art. 1), retained only these words: “The Epicureans were permitted to determine some true form of pleasure”; moving them from their proper place, he placed them before the words: “How do we offend you?” He wished us to understand that he had learned of this emendation from those who had seen the Fulda manuscript. But even if matters stand thus, [Col. 1066A] should the authority of the single Fulda manuscript for that reason have been preferred to all the others, many of which are far older? If the term *christianae*, as we have said, is removed as superfluous, do not those words accord very well with our Author’s purpose and thought?

For his argument proceeds in this manner: Why should Christians not be permitted to pursue the pleasure they consider honorable, when the Epicureans were permitted to choose that which affords equanimity—or rather evenness and tranquility—of mind, disturbed by no unpleasant agitation and by no extensive occupations? For, Cicero said, “the highest [Col. 1066B] pleasure, as Epicurus holds, is to feel no pain” (Cicero, *On Ends*, Book I, p. 45, line 28); or, as Theodoret relates, it is that pleasure which excludes every kind of care and disgust, and consequently extensive occupations (Theodoret, sermon 12, *On the Blessed Life*, p. 675). If this is indeed Tertullian’s meaning, then his text should read *nec* (“nor”), not *et* (“and”), “extensive occupations,” unless by that expression he understood occupations which by their extensiveness cause no disturbance of mind. We have, moreover, discussed that opinion of Epicurus and his followers elsewhere, more than once. From all these considerations Tertullian rightly concludes that the sect of the Christians, who rejected the unlawful pleasures of the pagans, was not an unlawful faction that ought to be prohibited, but was indeed excellent and superior to all others. By contrast, the pagans were plainly factious and seditious, since, as we saw [Col. 1066C] in the preceding articles, they openly assailed their emperors with scurrilous wit, abuse, and insults, wished for their death, conspired against them, and laid violent hands upon them.

## CHAPTER XXIII. That Christians Are Not the Cause of Public Calamities

## ARTICLE I. What the public calamities were of which the pagans complained, as frequently as falsely, that Christians were the authors, and how Tertullian refutes them

We observed in our earlier dissertations (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 6, arts. 1 and 2) that no accusation was repeated more often than that by which the pagans complained that Christians were the cause, as [Col. 1066D] Tertullian says, “of every public calamity and every popular affliction.” We would indeed be weary of reheating once again cabbage so often reheated, by refuting anew this utterly foolish argument, if the pagans had ever repented of pressing it so ineptly. But these madmen, raging with monstrous hatred against the Christians, believed that they could devise scarcely anything more suitable than this calumny for destroying Christians (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 40, p. 479, and Book I *To the Nations*, chap. 9, p. 571). Let us therefore follow our Tertullian and attempt to elucidate whatever distinctive or somewhat obscure point he made in setting forth and overthrowing it.

He begins with the Romans as follows: “If the Tiber [Col. 1067A] rises to the walls” (ibid.); or, as he says in another book, “if the Tiber has overflowed”—that is, if by excessive flooding it has harmed the walls of the City and even the fertility of the lands. Conversely, “if the Nile does not rise to the fields” and inundate them with its customary flood—or, as Virgil sings:

> . . . does not fertilize them with black sand.

(Virgil, *Georgics*, Book IV, v. 290.)

Furthermore, our Author adds, “if the sky has stood still”; or, as he says later, “when summer and winter weather suspend the rains” (Tertullian, passage cited). If you ask what he denotes there by the word “sky,” we shall answer you in the words of Tullius: “This sky in which clouds, rains, and winds gather” (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations*, Book II, p. 122, line 14). If the rains do not flow down from this sky at the proper season of winter or summer and render the earth fertile—which [Col. 1067B] Cyprian, following Tertullian, expressed thus: “When you say that very many complain because prolonged clear weather suspends the rains and showers”; and elsewhere: “when clear weather suspends rain from the clouds” (Cyprian, treatise *To Demetrianus*; and *On Mortality*)—likewise “if the earth has moved,” and towns have collapsed from its unusual motion or people have been swallowed by its chasms, as we shall soon see; finally, “if famine and plague” (here “rage and spread” must be supplied); or, as he says more clearly elsewhere, “if famine has afflicted” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 40, p. 480, and Book I *To the Nations*, chap. 9, p. 571): amid all these public calamities, says Tertullian, the cry is raised with one voice by all the pagans, “The Christians to the lion!” (the same, *Apologeticum*, ibid.). The final word, however, which is found in our manuscripts [Col. 1067C] and ancient editions, was lopped off by Rigaltius. Yet even if it is absent, it must certainly be understood. That acclamation, which we discussed above, was customarily made chiefly in the circus. By that cry alone, however unjust it might be, Christians were seized and immediately exposed to be devoured by lions and other wild beasts. Tertullian derides and rejects it in only these three words: “So many to one!” (ibid.), just as if he had said: “Good heavens! Do you think that the almost infinite multitude of Christians, as we observed earlier, could have been exposed to a single lion and devoured by it?”

After this he demonstrates [Col. 1067D] by weighty arguments that nothing more false could have been devised than the claim that Christians were the authors of public or other calamities: “For before Tiberius,” he says—that is, before Christ’s advent—“what great calamities struck the world and its cities” (ibid.)? He did not say “before Tiberius” in the sense that Christ had not come into this world earlier, but that his advent was manifested to the world when, during Tiberius’s reign, he performed miracles and founded his Church. For in the book *To the Nations* he says: “Under the emperor Augustus this name arose; under Tiberius his teaching shone forth” (the same, Book I *To the Nations*, chap. 7, p. 567). Accordingly, he later said: “Our era is not yet three hundred years old; how many calamities, before that span, struck the whole world, individual cities, and provinces? How many foreign and civil wars? How many plagues, famines, fires, chasms, and earthquakes has the age set down? [Col. 1068A] Where then were the Christians, when the Roman state supplied so many histories of its sufferings” (ibid., chap. 9, p. 571)? Since we cannot pass over without some explanation the examples by which he demonstrated these claims, let us proceed, if you please, to expound them individually.

## ARTICLE II. How Tertullian demonstrates that Christians are not the authors of public calamities, because before Christ’s birth Hiera, Anaphe, Delos, Rhodes, and Cos were destroyed by numerous inundations and earthquakes, along with many thousands of people; likewise the greater part of Asia or Africa, and the Corinthian Sea was drained dry; Sicily was severed from Lucania and became detached from Italy; and all human beings perished in a cataclysm that affected not merely the plains but the whole world

[Col. 1068B]

No more powerful argument can certainly prove that Christians were not the authors of public calamities than the many examples of such events that occurred before Christ’s birth. And yet no one who has touched the histories of ancient times even with the tips of his fingers can fail to know how frequently almost all writers have mentioned them. It therefore seemed scarcely necessary for Tertullian to collect many examples of this kind, unless he judged that they had to be advanced in order to crush the pagans’ obstinacy. We must therefore examine them, both because of the variant readings of the manuscripts, by which proper names have been deformed, and because of certain difficulties that may cause trouble for some readers.

[Col. 1068C] The first example is presented in Rigaltius’s edition in these words: “We read that the islands Hierannape, Delos, Rhodes, and Cos perished together with many thousands of people” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 45, p. 36). In our copies, however, the reading is: *Hierananpeam, et Delon, et Rhodon, et Cho*; with the exception of one copy, in which *Hierant pean* appears; the Bongars manuscript has *Iherrapean*; one Vatican manuscript, *Hieramapeam*; another Vatican manuscript likewise has *Hiera Niepaeam*; the Fulda manuscript, *Hierearranda penes*; and the editions before Rigaltius, *Hierapolis*, but incorrectly. For that city is not reported to have fallen before the beginnings of the Christian religion. You can certainly see how greatly, and for how long a time, this passage has been corrupted. In the book *To the Nations*, however, [Col. 1068D] the reading is plainly: “The islands Hierennape, Delphi, Rhodes, and Crete perished together with many thousands of people” (the same, Book I *To the Nations*, chap. 9, p. 54, Rigaltius’s edition). But these words neither remove nor even lessen the difficulty. Nor are these words of the same Tertullian in another book any more sound: “Delos, Rhene, and Samos no longer exist” (the same, *On the Pallium*, chap. 2). For many wish *Harenae* to be read there in place of *Rhene*, although Pliny (Pliny, *Natural History*, Book IV, chap. 12, p. 456) and others mention the island of Rhene. Let us therefore see whether we can borrow from elsewhere some light for explaining that obscure and corrupt passage of Tertullian.
