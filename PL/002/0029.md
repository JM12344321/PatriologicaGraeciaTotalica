If God is good, foreknows the future, and is able to avert evil, why did He permit man—and indeed His own image and likeness, or rather His own substance, in respect, of course, to the derivation of the soul—to fall away from obedience to the law into death, after being deceived by the devil? For if He was both good, so that He would not wish such a thing to happen; and foreknowing, so that He would not be unaware that it would happen; and powerful, so that He would be able to prevent it—then under no circumstances would that have occurred which could not occur under these three conditions of divine majesty. But if it did occur, the contrary conclusion is established: God must be believed neither good, nor foreknowing, nor powerful. For just as no such thing would have happened if God were of that character—that is, good, foreknowing, and powerful—so it happened for this reason: because God is not of that character.

In reply to this, we must first defend in the Creator those attributes that are called into doubt: I mean goodness, foreknowledge, [Col. 0290A] and power. Nor shall I linger over this point, since even Christ’s own rule of determination leads the way (John X, 25): proofs must be undertaken from works. [Note: [Col. 0290D] “Proofs must be undertaken from works.” When he attributes this rule to Christ, he alludes to John X: “The works that I do in my Father’s name, these bear witness concerning me; and if you are unwilling to believe me, believe the works.” PAM.] [Note: [Col. 0289D] Semler and Oberthür read the singular, “proof.”]

The Creator’s works testify both to His goodness, by which they are good, as we have shown, and to His power, by which they are so great—and indeed made from nothing. For even if they were made from some matter, as certain people maintain, nevertheless in this very respect they were made from nothing, since they were not what they now are. Finally, even on that supposition they are great, because they are good; and even on that supposition God is powerful, because all things belong to Him—whence He is also almighty.

But what shall I say of His foreknowledge? It has as many witnesses as the prophets He made. Yet what special title can “foreknowledge” have in the Author of all things, who surely, by ordering all things, foreknew them, and by foreknowing them, ordered them—certainly including the transgression itself? For if He had not foreknown it, He would not have issued a warning against it under threat of death.

## CHAPTER VI

But since we are now understood from this to be constructing a case for man’s free power of choice, so that what happened to him must be reproached not to God but to man himself, you might now object that he ought not to have been established in this way if the freedom and power of choice were going to prove destructive. I shall therefore first defend the proposition that he ought to have been established in this way, so that I may commend all the more strongly both the fact that he was so established and that he was established worthily of God, once the superior reason that caused him to be so established has been demonstrated.

God’s goodness and His reason will support this arrangement also, since in our God they act in harmony in all things. For reason without goodness is not reason, nor is goodness without reason goodness—unless perhaps [Note: [Col. 0291D] “In the case of Marcion’s god,” etc. Marcion appeared ridiculous, since by denying to God the attributes with which he has just dealt, he makes God good by taking reason away from Him. LE PR.] in the case of Marcion’s god, who is irrationally good, as we have shown.

It was necessary that God should be known: this is surely good and rational. It was necessary that there should be something worthy to know God: what could be provided so worthy as [Note: [Col. 0291D] “The image and likeness of God,” etc. Here he interprets the image and likeness of God in which man was made very differently from his usual interpretation elsewhere, especially in *On Baptism*, chapter 5, where we annotated something concerning this matter, as also concerning how the soul is said there to be God’s breath. PAM.] God’s image and likeness? And this, without doubt, is both good and rational.

It was therefore necessary that God’s image [Col. 0291B] and likeness should be established with freedom of choice and power of its own, since precisely in these the image and likeness of God would be reckoned—namely, in freedom and power of choice. For this purpose there was adapted to man a substance suited to this condition: God’s breath, surely free and possessed of its own power.

Besides, what sense would it have made for man, possessing the entire world, not to reign first of all in possession of his own mind—lord of others, but slave of himself? You therefore have God’s goodness to recognize from the honor bestowed, and His reason from the arrangement. Let goodness alone now be taken into account: it granted so much to man, namely, freedom of choice. Let reason defend for itself another consideration in an institution of this kind.

For God alone is good by nature (Matt. XIX, 17). He who possesses what He is without a beginning possesses it not by institution but by nature. Man, however, who exists wholly by institution and has a beginning, received together with his beginning the form by which he was to exist. Thus he was disposed toward good not by nature, but by institution. His goodness was not his own, because he was disposed toward good not by nature but by institution, according to his good Institutor, who is, of course, the Creator of good things.

Therefore, so that man might now possess goodness as his own, emancipated to him by God, and so that goodness might now become man’s property and in some sense his nature, there was assigned to him by institution, like the official who holds the scales [Note: [Col. 0291C] Rhenanus and Oberthür read “as though by an official holding the scales.”] in the emancipation of the good by God, [Note: [Col. 0291D] “Like the official who holds the scales in the emancipation of the good by God.” The alienation of *res mancipi*—that is, property capable of being held and possessed by full legal title—was effected by bronze and scales. RIG.—“Like the official who holds the scales in the emancipation of the good by God.” This metaphor, which he employs from the law, is taken from emancipation, which was a fictitious sale and was effected by bronze and scales—that is, in the presence of five witnesses and a scale-holder. This would bring it about that good, as something properly his own, [Col. 0292A] should thereafter be rendered voluntarily by man, since reason also required that goodness should be exercised voluntarily: that is, from freedom of choice, not favoring institution [Note: [Col. 0291C] Pamelius reads “of institution”; Wouwer reads “choice favoring institution”; [Col. 0291D] Semler conjectures “choice; for by favoring institution.”] nor serving it slavishly.

Only thus, indeed, would man stand as good: if he were found good in accordance with his institution, certainly, but now by his own will, as though from the property of nature. Similarly, against evil also—for God, of course, foresaw that as well—man would present himself [Note: [Col. 0292C] Semler and Oberthür add “as.”] all the stronger [Note: [Col. 0292C] Junius reads “press onward.”], since he was free and possessed of his own power. [Note: [Col. 0292D] “Because, if he lacked this right.” There is an extraordinary variety among the correctors: they substitute “received,” “desired,” “departed,” “underwent,” and “longed”; Rigaltius reads better, “lacked.” LE PR.] For if he lacked this right, so that he performed even the good not by will but by necessity, he would also be liable to appropriation by evil through the weakness of servitude, and equally a slave to evil as to good.

The whole freedom of choice was therefore granted to him in both directions, so that, as his own master, he might meet both alternatives steadfastly: by preserving the good voluntarily [Col. 0292B] and avoiding evil voluntarily. For there was another reason too: since man had been placed under God’s judgment, it was necessary to make that judgment just [Note: [Col. 0292D] “It was necessary to make that judgment just.” This passage too argues for the merits of works, as does what follows: “Otherwise neither the reward of good nor of evil,” etc. PAM.] on the basis of the merits of his own choice, which was free. Otherwise, neither the reward of good nor of evil would justly have been weighed out to one who had been found either good or evil by necessity, rather than by will.

For this purpose the law too was established, not excluding freedom but proving it, through obedience voluntarily rendered or transgression voluntarily committed. Thus freedom of choice lay open toward either outcome.

Therefore, if both God’s goodness and His reason are found to be involved in the freedom of choice granted to man, we must not omit the primary determination concerning goodness and reason—which must be established before the whole discussion—and then judge retrospectively from subsequent events that God ought not to have established man in this way because the outcome differed from what was fitting for God. Rather, after discerning that He ought to have established him in this way, we must preserve what has been discerned and investigate the remaining matters.

Otherwise, it is easy for those who immediately stumble upon man’s fall, before examining his condition, to refer what happened to his Author, because the Author’s reason has not been examined. Finally, both God’s goodness, perceived from the beginning of His works, will persuade us [Note: [Col. 0292D] “That no evil could have come from God,” etc. This also, and everything discussed in the preceding chapter and the two following chapters, argues against Calvin and others who are not ashamed to make God the author of evils. PAM.] that no evil could have come from God, and man’s freedom, when reconsidered, will show [Note: [Col. 0292D] Pamelius and Semler read “shows.”] that it is rather itself guilty of what it committed.

## CHAPTER VII

By this determination, all God’s attributes remain intact: the nature of His goodness, the reason of His ordering, and the fullness of His foreknowledge and power. Yet you must also require of God [Note: [Col. 0293D] Junius reads “you have.”] the highest constancy and the preeminent fidelity in every institution of His, so that you may cease asking whether anything could have happened against God’s will.

For once you maintain the constancy and fidelity of the good God—while recognizing that they must be vindicated in His rational institutions—you will not be surprised that God did not intervene against the things He did not wish to happen, so that He might preserve the things He did wish [Note: [Col. 0293D] Paris reads “did not wish.”]. For if He had once allowed man freedom and power of choice, and had allowed it worthily, as we have shown, He had surely allowed them to be used by the very authority of His institution. So far as depended upon Himself, however, He allowed them to be used according to Himself—that is, according to God, that is, for good; for who will permit anything against himself? But so far as depended upon man, He allowed them to be used according to the movements of freedom itself. For who does not grant to one whom he once allows to use something that he may use it according to his own inclination and choice?

It followed, therefore, that God should withdraw from the freedom once granted to man—that is, should restrain within Himself both His foreknowledge and His supreme power, [Note: [Col. 0293D] “Through which He could have intervened.” Among the Romans, the tribunes interposed against decrees of the senate when they obstructed them so that they should not be enacted. So too shortly afterward: “For if He had intervened.” RHEN.] through which He could have intervened to prevent man, once he had undertaken to use his freedom badly, from falling into danger. For if He had intervened, He would have annulled the freedom of choice that He had granted on rational and good grounds.

Finally, suppose that He had intervened; suppose that He had annulled freedom of choice by calling man back from the tree and by keeping the serpent, the deceiver, [Note: [Col. 0293D] “The serpent, the deceiver.” That is, one who circumvents or cheats. Juvenal, *Satire* XV, speaks of one “summoning a guardian of a ward to court … as a cheat.” For *circumscribere* also means “to circumvent.” RHEN.] from meeting the woman. Would not Marcion exclaim: [Note: [Col. 0293D] “O worthless lord,” etc. For Marcion made God irrationally good, as may be seen in chapter 6 of this book and elsewhere. LE PR.] “O worthless lord—unstable, unfaithful, annulling what He instituted! Why did He permit free choice if He intervenes? Why does He intervene if He permitted it?” Let him choose where he would mark God Himself with error: in the institution, or in its annulment.

Would God not then have appeared all the more deceived through lack of foreknowledge of the future, precisely when He obstructed it? And who would not say that He had granted something as though ignorant of how it would turn out? But even if He had foreknown that man would use His institution badly, what could be so worthy of God as the constancy and fidelity of His institutions, whatever their character?

It was for man to see to it if he failed to discharge well what he had received in good condition, so that man himself [Note: [Col. 0294D] Rigaltius and Latinus read “they themselves”; Pamelius and Semler omit “so that.”] might be guilty before the law that he had refused to obey, rather than that the Lawgiver Himself should defraud His own law by not permitting its prescription to be fulfilled.

If the Creator had opposed man’s free choice through the providence and power you demand, you would have delivered these most worthy arguments against Him. Now whisper to yourself, on the Creator’s behalf, about His constancy, patience, and fidelity, since He faithfully discharged His own institutions as both rational and good.

## CHAPTER VIII

For He had not brought man forth merely to live, without also bringing him forth to live rightly—that is, with regard for God and His law. Accordingly, He Himself had granted man simply to live by making him into a living soul; but He had entrusted to him the task of living rightly, after admonishing him to obey the law.

Thus He who now desires man to be restored to life, preferring the sinner’s repentance to his death (Ezek. XVIII, 23), proves that man was not instituted for death. Accordingly, just as God introduced man into a state of life, so man brought upon himself a state of death. And he did this neither through weakness nor through ignorance, lest anything should be imputed to his Author.

For although it was an angel who seduced him, the one who was seduced was nevertheless free and possessed of his own power; he was the image and likeness of God, stronger than an angel; and God’s breath was nobler than the material spirit from which the angels were constituted. “He makes,” Scripture says (Ps. CIII, 5), “spirits His angels” (Heb. I, 7), [Note: [Col. 0293D] “And His attendants a flame of fire.” It matters little whether this comes from the Epistle to the Hebrews, chapter I, as Pamelius affirms, or from Psalm CIII. For although the Apostle calls it πυρὸς φλόγα, “a flame of fire,” he cites this from Psalm CIII, verse 5, where it reads, “and His ministers a burning fire.” In Greek it is πῦρ φλέγον, “burning fire.” In my judgment, the Vulgate version agrees better with the text in this passage than Tertullian’s version does. For He would not have subjected the universe to man’s dominion if man were weak and not superior to the angels, to whom He subjected no such thing. ## CHAPTER IX

Nevertheless, you say, by whatever means it occurred, a substance derived from the Creator is found capable of sin, since God’s breath—that is, the soul—sinned in man. And the corruption of a portion cannot fail to be referred back to its original source [Note: [Col. 0294D] The Venetian edition and Rigaltius read “origin.”].

In reply to this, the character of the soul must be interpreted. First of all, it must be maintained [Note: [Col. 0294D] “What the Greek Scripture designated,” etc. The Greek word πνοή (*pnoē*, “breath”) was correctly noted in the margin by Rhenanus, for the author alludes to it here. It is the word read in Genesis II, not πνεῦμα (*pneuma*, “spirit”). Accordingly, the Latin translator also correctly rendered it “breath of life,” which the author relates to breathing, not “spirit.” PAM.] that the Greek Scripture designated it by calling it “breath” (*πνοή, pnoē*), not “spirit” (*πνεῦμα, pneuma*). For certain translators from the Greek, failing to reflect on the distinction or to attend to the proper meaning of the words, put “spirit” in place of “breath.” They thereby give heretics an opportunity to stain God’s Spirit with sin—that is, God Himself—and the question has now been usurped on that basis.

Understand, therefore, that the breath is less than the spirit [Note: [Col. 0295D] Semler and Oberthür read the accusative, “spirit.”], although it proceeds from the spirit: it is, as it were, a little breeze from it, but is nevertheless not the spirit. For a breeze too is thinner than the wind; although a breeze comes from the wind, the breeze is nevertheless not the wind.

[Note: [Col. 0295D] “It is also possible to call the breath an image of the spirit,” etc. Here too you see how he uses the word *capit*: that is, the breath also signifies an image of the spirit, or can be called an image of the spirit. RHEN.] It is also possible to call the breath an image of the spirit. For this is also why man is the image of God—that is, of the Spirit; for God is Spirit. The image of the Spirit, therefore, is the breath. [Note: [Col. 0295D] Pamelius and Semler delete “breath.”]

Moreover, an image will not be altogether equal to the reality. For it is one thing to exist according to the reality, and another to be the reality itself. Thus the breath also, since it is an image of the Spirit, cannot make the image equal to God [Note: [Col. 0295D] Ciacconius reads “be made equal.”] in such a way that, because the reality—that is, the Spirit, that is, God—is without sin, the breath too—that is, the image—ought therefore to have been incapable of committing sin. In this respect the image will be less than the reality, and the breath inferior to the Spirit. [Col. 0295B]

It certainly possesses those lineaments of God: insofar as the soul is immortal; insofar as it is free and possessed of its own choice; insofar as it often has foreknowledge; and insofar as it is rational and capable of understanding and knowledge. Yet even in these respects it is an image [Note: [Col. 0295D] Junius reads “as.”], and it does not extend to the full power of divinity. So neither does it extend to complete immunity from sin, because this belongs to God alone—that is, to the reality—and this alone is not permitted to the image.

For just as an image, although it expresses all the lineaments of the reality, nevertheless lacks its actual force [Note: [Col. 0295D] Semler conjectures “life.”], since it has no motion, so also the soul, as an image of the Spirit, was unable to express His power alone—that is, the blessedness [Note: [Col. 0295D] Pamelius conjectures “capacity.”] of not sinning. Otherwise it would not be a soul but the Spirit; nor would he who received the soul be man, but God.

Besides, not everything that belongs to God will therefore be regarded as God. You cannot demand that the breath should also be God—that is, empty of sin—merely because it is God’s breath. For if you blow into a flute, you do not thereby make the flute a human being, although you have blown into it from your own soul, just as God breathed from His own Spirit.

Finally, since Scripture plainly says (Gen. II, 7) that God breathed into man’s face, and that man became a living soul—not a life-giving spirit—it separated the soul from the condition of its Maker. For the work must necessarily be something other than the craftsman—that is, inferior to the craftsman. A vessel made by a potter will not itself be the potter. So too a breath made by the Spirit will not for that reason be the Spirit. By the very fact that it is a soul, it was called “breath.”

See also whether, from the condition of being breathed forth, it passed into some lesser quality. “Then,” you say, “you have conceded to the soul the weakness you denied above.” Certainly, when you require it to be equal to God—that is, immune from sin—I call it weak. But when it is compared with an angel, [Col. 0295D] I must maintain that the lord of the universe is stronger—he whom the angels now serve (Heb. I, 14), and who will even judge angels [Col. 0296A] (1 Cor. VI, 2), if he stands firm in God’s law, which at the beginning he refused to do.

God’s breath, therefore, was able to admit this very thing. It was able, but it ought not to have done so. It possessed the ability through the slightness of its substance, by which it was breath and not Spirit; but it had the obligation not to do so through its power of choice, by which it was free and not a slave. In addition, the warning not to sin, under threat of death, stood beside it, so that the slightness of its substance might be supported and the freedom of its decision governed.

Accordingly, the soul can no longer be seen to have sinned through that which is akin to God in it—namely, through the breath—but through that which was added to its substance, namely free choice: rationally bestowed by God, but exercised by man in whatever way he willed.

If this is so, God’s entire dispensation is now cleared of the reproach of evil. For freedom [Col. 0296B] of choice will not cast off its guilt upon Him by whom it was given, but upon him by whom it was not administered as it ought to have been.

What evil, finally, will you ascribe [Note: [Col. 0296D] The Venetian edition and Rigaltius read “assign”; Pamelius and Semler read “describe.”] to the Creator? If it is man’s sin, it will not belong to God, because it belongs to man. Nor must the person found to have forbidden and even condemned the sin be regarded as identical with its author. If death is the evil, death will bring reproach not upon Him who threatened it, but upon him who despised it, as its author. For by despising it he brought it about; it certainly would not have occurred if he had not despised it.

## CHAPTER X

But even if [Note: [Col. 0296D] Semler inserts “not”; Junius reads “now.”] you transfer the indictment of evil from man to the devil, [Note: [Col. 0296D] “Transfer the indictment of evil.” That is, attribute all the properties of evil to the devil; for this is Tertullian’s meaning of the expression. He used it thus in Book I *Against Marcion*. LE PR.] as the instigator of the sin, so that you may in this manner also direct the blame against the Creator as the devil’s author—He who makes angels spirits—the answer is this: what was made by God, namely the angel, will belong to Him who made it; but what was not made by God, namely the devil—that is, the slanderer—must necessarily have been made by the being himself, by slandering God, and indeed falsely.

His first falsehood was that God had forbidden them to eat from every tree; the second, that they supposedly would not die if they ate; the third, that God supposedly envied them divinity. Whence, then, came the malice of lying and deception against human beings, and of defamation against God? Certainly not from God, who also established the angel as good, in accordance with the form of His good works. Indeed, before he became the devil, he is represented as the wisest of all—unless wisdom is an evil.

And if you unfold Ezekiel’s prophecy (Ezek. XXVIII, 12), you will readily perceive that the angel was good by institution and corrupted by his own will. For under the person [Note: [Col. 0296D] Semler and Oberthür read “person.”] of the prince of Tyre, judgment is pronounced against the devil:

> And the word of the Lord came [Col. 0296D] to me, saying: Son of man, take up a lamentation over the prince of Tyre, and you shall say to him [Note: [Col. 0296D] Latinus adds “to him.”]: Thus says the Lord: You are the seal of likeness—

meaning, of course, that you had unsealed the integrity [Col. 0297A] of the image and likeness—

> full of wisdom [Note: [Col. 0297D] “Full of wisdom” is absent from Semler and the manuscripts, which do not contain “he says.” Yet nothing similar is found in the Septuagint version. Junius thinks it was inserted from the Hebrew or the Vulgate. Editors.], a crown of beauty—

this was said to him as the most eminent of the angels, an archangel, and the wisest of all—

> you were born amid the delights of the paradise of your God—

for it was there that God had made the angels, in the second formation of the figures of living beings—

> You were clothed with every precious stone: sardius, topaz, emerald, carbuncle, sapphire, jasper, lynx-stone, agate, amethyst, chrysolite, beryl, and onyx [Note: [Col. 0297D] Paris and Fran. read “onyx” with a variant spelling]; and with gold you filled your storehouses and your treasuries. From the day on which you were created, when I placed you with the cherub [Note: [Col. 0297D] Semler reads “cherubim,” against which stand the Hebrew reading, the Greek version of the Seventy, and finally the other editors, including Pamelius, Rigaltius, the Venetian edition, and Fran.; all support “cherub,” and the Vulgate favors it.] upon God’s holy mountain, you were in the midst of fiery stones. You were blameless in your days from the day on which you were created, until injuries were found in you. [Note: [Col. 0297D] “Until injuries were found in you.” The “injuries” are sins that injure the soul, for the Greek has ἀδικήματα (*adikēmata*, “wrongdoings”). In *On Patience*, chapter 7, he calls diminishing resources “injuries,” showing that through the casting away of riches, their losses too ought not to be counted as injuries. LE PR.] From the abundance of your commerce you filled your inner chambers and sinned,

and so forth.

It is manifest that these words pertain to the censure of the angel, [Col. 0297B] and not properly to that prince, because no human being was born in God’s paradise—not even Adam himself, who was instead transferred there. Nor was any human being placed with the cherub upon God’s holy mountain—that is, in the heavenly height from which the Lord also testifies that Satan fell. Nor did any human being dwell among the fiery stones, amid the sparkling rays of the burning stars, from which Satan was also cast down like lightning.

Rather, the author of sin himself was denounced under the person of the sinful man. Formerly he had indeed been blameless from the day of his creation, created by God for good, as by the good Creator of blameless creations; adorned with every angelic glory; and established with God, as a good being with the Good. But afterward he was transferred by his own act into evil.

“For from the time,” Scripture says, “when your injuries appeared”—reckoning to him those injuries by which, of course, he injured man, who was cast out from obedience to God [Note: [Col. 0298D] Latinus reads “chosen for obedience to God”; Rhenanus, “chosen from God.”]. And he sinned from the time when he sowed sin; and thus from that point onward he practiced the abundance of his commerce—that is, of his malice—namely the abundance of sins and designs. He too, no less than man, had been established with free choice, since he was a spirit. For God would not have ordered anything close to Himself without freedom of this kind.

Yet by condemning him beforehand, God testified that he had deviated from the form of his institution through his own lust for a malice conceived voluntarily. And by allotting a limited scope to his operations, God fulfilled the rational purpose of His goodness, with the same design that man [Note: [Col. 0297D] “By the same freedom of choice might crush the enemy,” etc. This passage maintains, against the heretics, that freedom was not altogether lost after Adam’s sin. LE PR.] might crush his enemy through the same freedom of choice by which he had fallen before him. Thus man would prove that the fault was his own and not God’s; would worthily recover salvation through victory; and the devil, defeated by the one whom he had previously overthrown, would be punished more bitterly. And God would be found all the more good, as He awaited man, who was now to return from this life to a more glorious paradise, with permission to pluck from the tree of life.
