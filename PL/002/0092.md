## CHAPTER LII

Let us now see [Note: [Col. 0870C] Rhen. and Seml. read *we see*.] in what body he argues that the dead will come. [Note: [Col. 0870D] “Let us now see in what body they will come.” From this chapter onward he attacks chiefly the Valentinians, who conceded the resurrection not of this flesh but of another, as is evident from the book *On Prescription*. Others too had come to the same opinion because of the arguments set out in ch. 13 concerning corpses devoured by wild beasts and consumed by fire; it seemed to them that a body dispersed in so many ways could not return as the very same body. In what follows he adequately answers and dissolves this objection. Nyssenus does so from the analogy of the seed, which the Author examines and supports from St Paul in this chapter. LAG.] And it is well that he immediately broke out into an explanation, as though someone had asked precisely such a question. “Fool,” he says, “what you sow is not made alive [Note: [Col. 0870C] Rhen. reads *is it not*; Wouw.’s manuscript perhaps likewise.] unless it has died.” [Note: [Col. 0870C] Fran. and Ven. read *will be made alive*.] From the example of the seed, therefore, let it now be established that no flesh other than that which will have died is made alive; and the subsequent statements will thus become clear, for nothing may be understood contrary to the governing principle [Col. 0871A] of the example.

Nor, because [Note: [Col. 0871C] Pam., Ven., Fran., and Rig. read *ne quia*; Rhen. and Seml. read *qua*.] what follows is, “And what you sow, you do not sow the body that is to be,” should you therefore suppose that a body other than the one sown by dying will rise again; otherwise you have departed from the example. For when wheat has been sown and dissolved in the earth, barley never springs forth, but the same kind of grain, of the same nature, quality, [Note: [Col. 0871C] Pam. and Fran. read *in the same quality*.] and form. Indeed, from where would it come, if not from that very thing? Even the corruption is that very thing, since it belongs to that very thing. Does he not also indicate how it is that the body which is to be is not sown, [Note: [Col. 0871C] Pam., Fran., Rig., and Ven. read *is sown* rather than *is to be sown*.] when he says: “But a bare grain, perhaps of wheat, or [Note: [Col. 0871D] Rhen. and Seml. read *or* (*vel*) rather than *or* (*aut*).] of some such kind; but God gives it a body as he wills”? Surely he gives it to the grain which, Paul says, is sown bare. “Surely,” you say. Therefore that to which God is to give a body is preserved. But how is it preserved if it is nowhere, if it does not rise, if that very thing does not rise? It is not preserved. And if it is not preserved, it cannot receive a body from God. But it is certainly preserved in every respect.

Why, then, will God give it a body as he wills, when it already possesses its own bare body, unless so that it may rise no longer bare? What is built over the body will therefore be an addition; [Note: [Col. 0871D] Pam. and Fran. read *something added* (*addititium*).] that over which it is built is not destroyed, [Note: [Col. 0871D] Pam. and Rhen. read *is destroyed*.] but enlarged. What is enlarged is preserved. For only the grain is sown, without the garment of its husk, without the base of the ear, without the protection of the beard, without the proud stature of the stalk; but it rises enriched with abundance, constructed into a fabric, arranged in order, fortified with adornment, and clothed on every side. These constitute for it the other body given by God, into which it is changed not by abolition but by enlargement. And “to each of the seeds he has assigned its own body” [Note: [Col. 0871D] “Its own body.” The Latin translator renders the Greek word ἴδιον more accurately as *proprium*, “its own,” unless one says that *suum* means the same thing. LE PR.] [Col. 0871C]—not “its own” in the sense of its former body, so that at that time what it acquires outwardly from God may also be its own.

Adhere, therefore, to the example, and preserve its reflection in relation to the flesh, believing that the same flesh which was sown will bear fruit: the very same, though fuller; [Note: [Col. 0871D] Rhen. and Seml. read *it is fuller*.] not another, though returning in another condition. [Note: [Col. 0871D] Pam. and Par. incorrectly read *showing reverence* (*reverentem*).] For it too will receive provision and adornment [Note: [Col. 0871D] “And it too will receive provision and adornment.” *Suggestus* here is an ensemble or ornament. LE PR.], of whatever kind God wills to lay upon it according to its merits. Undoubtedly the statement “not all flesh is the same flesh” tends to this point [Note: [Col. 0871D] Rhen. and Seml. read *tended*.]: not to denying a shared substance but to differentiating privilege; it reduces the body to a distinction of honor, not of kind.

To this end he also adds figurative examples drawn from animals and the elements. “One flesh belongs to human beings,” that is, to God’s servant, who is truly human; [Col. 0872A] “another to beasts of burden,” that is, to the pagan, of whom the Prophet also says: “Man has been made like irrational beasts of burden”; [Note: [Col. 0871D] “Another to a beast of burden.” Beasts of burden are animals subdued for carrying on their backs, such as horses, mules, and asses, which for this reason are called *dossuaria jumenta*, back-bearing beasts of burden, by Varro. Elephants and camels, as well as cattle, sheep, goats, and every kind of herd animal, are also included under this name “beast of burden.” [Col. 0872D] LE PR.] “another flesh belongs to birds,” [Note: [Col. 0872C] Rhen. and Seml. read *flying creatures*.] that is, to martyrs who strive toward the heights; “another, moreover, to fish,” [Note: [Col. 0872C] *Moreover* is absent from Rhen. and Seml.] that is, to those for whom the water of baptism suffices. [Note: [Col. 0872D] “For whom the water of baptism suffices.” This passage also supports baptism with water, which certain people do not shrink from attacking; but it is treated more fully in *On Baptism*, ch. 1. PAM.]

In the same way he combines arguments drawn from bodies above the heavens. [Note: [Col. 0872C] Rhen. and Seml. read: *It is appropriate to argue*.] “One is the glory of the sun,” that is, of Christ; “another of the moon,” that is, of the Church; “another of the stars,” that is, of Abraham’s seed; “for star differs from star in glory”; “there are earthly and heavenly bodies,” namely the Jew and the Christian. [Note: [Col. 0872D] “Earthly and heavenly bodies—the Jew,” etc. The Jew is signified by “earth,” but the Christian by “heaven,” because God promised only earthly things to the Jews, whereas he promised heaven to Christians. LE PR.] Otherwise, if the statements are not figurative, he has quite pointlessly set [Note: [Col. 0872D] Pam. and Fran. read *set in opposition*.] the flesh of mules and kites and the bodies of the heavenly lights alongside human bodies, although they have no relevance to a comparison of their condition, just as they have none to the attainment of resurrection.

Finally, when by these examples he had established a difference of glory, not of substance, he says [Col. 0872B]: “So too is the resurrection of the dead.” How so? [Note: [Col. 0872D] Rhen. and Seml. read *because*.] Not differing in any other respect, but in glory alone. For once again he refers the resurrection back to the same substance and looks again to the grain: “It is sown,” he says, “in corruption; it rises in incorruption. It is sown in dishonor; it rises in glory. It is sown in weakness; it rises in power. An animate body is sown; a spiritual body rises.” Certainly nothing other than what is sown rises; nothing other than what is dissolved in the ground is sown; and nothing other than the flesh is dissolved in the ground. For God’s sentence struck the flesh (Gen. 3): “Earth you are, and into the earth you will go,” because it had also been taken from the earth.

From this the Apostle derived his statement that it is sown when it is returned to the earth, since the earth is also the custodian of seeds, in which they are deposited [Col. 0872C] and from which they are reclaimed. For this reason he stamps the point with a second seal, [Note: [Col. 0872D] Rhen. and Seml. read *seals*.] impressing upon it the words, “For so it is written,” [Note: [Col. 0872D] Others read *just as*.] lest you suppose “to be sown” to be anything other than “you will go into the earth from which you were taken,” or suppose that it refers to anything other than the flesh: “for so it is written.”

## CHAPTER LIII

They argue, however, that the “animate body” means the soul, so that they may divert the resurrection in question away from the flesh. [Note: [Col. 0872D] Par. and Rhen. read: “so that they may divert *it* away from the flesh.”] Yet since it is established and fixed that the body which was sown is the one that will rise, they will be summoned to produce the reality itself. Let them show that the soul is sown [Col. 0873A] after death—that is, that it is dead, struck down into the ground, cast apart, and dissolved, although no such fate was decreed for it by God. Let them exhibit its corruption, dishonor, and weakness, so that it may also be the soul itself that rises [Note: [Col. 0873C] Rhen. and Seml. read *rises together*.] into incorruption, glory, and power.

But in Lazarus, the preeminent example of resurrection, it was the flesh that lay in weakness; the flesh that nearly rotted away in dishonor; the flesh that meanwhile stank in corruption. [Note: [Col. 0873D] “But in Lazarus,” etc. Here he speaks of a different Lazarus from the one mentioned above in ch. 17, namely the man of John 11. What our translator renders *instita*, “bands,” the Author calls linen bonds. The Greek is κειρίας. Furthermore, *comparet* below means “is manifested.” PAM.] And yet Lazarus as flesh rose again, together indeed with the soul, but with a soul that was uncorrupted: no one had bound it with linen bands; no one had placed it in the tomb; no one had perceived it already beginning to stink; no one had seen it sown for four days. The flesh of everyone today undergoes [Note: [Col. 0873C] Fran. and Ven. read *undergoes*.] the entire condition and entire end of Lazarus, but no one’s soul does. Therefore the entity in which the Apostle’s pen is manifest, and of which it is clear that he speaks, will be both the animate body when it is sown and the spiritual body when [Col. 0873B] it is raised.

To make you understand it in this way, [Note: [Col. 0873C] Pam. and Fran. read *that he may understand*.] he extends his hand still further and, again on the authority of the same Scripture, retraces how the first man Adam was made “into a living soul.” [Note: [Col. 0873C] Pam. and Fran. read *a living soul* with a different Latin adjective.] If Adam is the first man, while the flesh is man before the soul, then undoubtedly the flesh was made into a living soul. [Note: [Col. 0873C] *Living* is absent from Rig. and Ven.; Pam. and Fran. use a different form for “living.” *Was made* is absent from Par. and Pam.] Moreover, since it was made into a soul [Note: [Col. 0873C] Seml. reads *inanimate*.] while it was already a body, it was certainly made an animate body. [Note: [Col. 0873C] Rhen. and Seml. insert *into*.] What do they want it to be called, other than that which it was made through the soul—other than that which did not exist before the soul, and which will not exist after the soul except when it rises? [Note: [Col. 0873D] “Other than that which did not exist before the soul?” There was formerly a controversy in the churches, as Nyssenus testifies in *On the Making of Man*, ch. 28, over whether the soul existed before the body or whether our body and flesh existed before the soul. The Origenists maintained the former; others, against them, gave precedence to the flesh over the soul because of its iniquity. Nyssenus fully attacks both positions: the former in ch. 28, against Origen; the latter in ch. 29, where he attempts to prove philosophically this most true opinion of Tertullian and considers this view true concerning the formation of all human beings in [Col. 0874D] the womb: neither a vegetative body nor thereafter a sentient or animate body precedes the infusion of the rational soul, as philosophers commonly claim when they suppose that an embryo first lives by the vegetative life of plants and then by a sentient life before the advent of the rational soul. Nyssenus instead thinks that the rational soul is present from the beginning of every animation of the fetus and before its organization, and that the soul itself effects this organization for itself. See the words of Nyssenus. LAC.]

For when the soul has been received back, it again becomes an animate body, so that it may become spiritual; for nothing rises unless it previously existed. Thus, on the same ground on which it is appropriate to call the flesh an animate body, it is in no way appropriate to call the soul one. For the flesh existed as a body before it was an animate body. Once animated, it was afterward made [Col. 0873C] an animate body. The soul, however, although itself a body, still cannot be called or become an animate body, because it is not itself an animated body but rather a body that animates: it cannot become what it makes. [Note: [Col. 0873D] Seml. reads *made*.] For that which is an accident to another makes that other animate; but since it is not an accident to itself, how will it make itself animate? [Note: [Col. 0874D] “For that which is an accident to another.” He does not take *accident* in the strict sense, but in a broader sense that is also appropriate to substantial forms—that is, as something “adjoining,” a term that philosophers do not deny to substantial realities. Or perhaps the Author wrote *accidens*, “an accident”; or *accidens* may mean the same as “contingent,” because this or that form falls to matter. LAC.]

Therefore, just as the flesh becomes an animate body by receiving the soul, so afterward it becomes spiritual by putting on the Spirit. [Note: [Col. 0874C] *Before* is absent from Rhen. and Seml.] [Col. 0874A] In arranging this order, the Apostle rightly distinguishes it in Adam and Christ as well, as in the representative heads of that very distinction. And when he calls Christ the last Adam, recognize from this that he has directed the entire force of his teaching toward the resurrection of the flesh, not of the soul. For if the first man Adam was flesh, not soul, since he was finally made into a living soul [Note: [Col. 0874C] Others incorrectly read: “Why should not she who was made into a soul?”]; and if the last Adam, Christ, is Adam precisely because he is man, and man precisely because he is flesh rather than because he is soul; then he accordingly adds: “Not first that which is spiritual, but that which is animate; afterward that which is spiritual,” with reference to both Adams.

Does it appear to you [Note: [Col. 0874C] Rhen. and Ven. read *And what*.] that he distinguishes the animate body and the spiritual body in the same flesh, whose distinction he had laid down beforehand in the two Adams—that is, in the two men? From what [Note: [Col. 0874C] Pam., Par., and Fran. read *from both*.] substance do Christ and Adam stand in mutual relation [Col. 0874B]? From flesh, of course, although also from soul; but both are called “man” from the name of the flesh. [Note: [Col. 0874C] Paris., Fran., and Pam. read: “Of course from both flesh and soul; but from the flesh … they are … man.” Jun. reads: “Of course from flesh and from soul (but from the flesh—are—man), from the latter.”] For the flesh is the first “man.” From it they could also enter into an order, so that one might be reckoned the first and the other the last man—that is, Adam. Different things, however, cannot be placed in an order, at least with respect to substance, although perhaps they can with respect to place, time, or condition. Here, however, they are called first and last with respect to the substance of the flesh; just as, again, “the first man is from the earth, and the second from heaven,” because although the second is from heaven according to the Spirit, he is man according to the flesh.

Accordingly, since order is applicable to the flesh in both Adams but not to the soul—so that the first man was distinguished as made into a living soul and the last as made into a life-giving Spirit [Note: [Col. 0874C] Fran. and Pam. add *Adam*.]—their distinction likewise established in advance a distinction in the flesh, so that it is of the flesh that it was said: “Not first that which is spiritual, but that which is animate; [Col. 0874C] afterward that which is spiritual.” The same flesh must accordingly be understood both above and here: it is sown as an animate body and rises as a spiritual body, because “not first that which is spiritual, but that which is animate”; because the first Adam was made into a soul, the last Adam into a Spirit. The whole discussion concerns man; the whole concerns flesh whenever it concerns man.

What, then, shall we say? Does the flesh not even now possess the Spirit through faith, [Col. 0875A] so that we must ask how it is said to be sown an animate body? Certainly the flesh has received the Spirit here too, [Note: [Col. 0875C] Seml. incorrectly repeats: “Through faith, so that we must ask how the animate body is said to be sown. Certainly the flesh has received the Spirit here too.”] but only as a pledge; of the soul, however, it has received not a pledge but the fullness. Accordingly, for this reason too the flesh has been called an animate body under the name of the greater substance within which it is sown; and likewise, through the fullness of the Spirit, it is destined to be spiritual in addition when it is raised. What wonder is it if it has been named more from that with which it is filled than from that with which it is merely sprinkled? [Note: [Col. 0875C] Rhen. and Seml. read *scattered*.]

## CHAPTER LIV

Thus questions very frequently arise from the particular usages of terms, just as they do from shared uses of words. For because the Apostle also has the statement, “So that the mortal may be swallowed up by life”—meaning the flesh—they seize upon “swallowing up” as signifying destruction, namely the destruction of the flesh, [Col. 0875B] as though we were not also said to swallow bile and pain, that is, to hide and cover them [Note: [Col. 0875C] Rhen. reads *cover over*.] and contain them within ourselves.

Finally, since it is also written, “This mortal must put on immortality,” it is shown how the mortal is swallowed up by life: while clothed with immortality it is hidden, covered, and contained within, rather than consumed and lost. “Then death too,” you say, “will be preserved when it has been swallowed up.” Therefore distinguish the common use of words according to their meanings, and you will understand completely. [Note: [Col. 0875D] “You will understand completely.” Cyprian, Epistle 28: “Completely and with discipline.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0875D] Rhen., Seml., and Venet. read *you understand*.]

For death is one thing and the mortal another. Death, therefore, will be swallowed up in one way, and the mortal in another. Death is not capable of receiving immortality, but the mortal is capable of it. Indeed, it is written that “this mortal must put on immortality.” How, then, does it receive it? By being swallowed up by life. How is it swallowed up by life? By being received, [Col. 0875C] incorporated, and enclosed within life itself. [Note: [Col. 0875D] “Enclosed within life itself.” He takes *to enclose* in the sense in which, in the book *On Repentance*, he said that certain people neglect “to enclose repentance.” His meaning, then, is that the mortal is enclosed within life; in Spanish it may be expressed, *engastose en la vida*, “set into life.” LAC.]

Death, however, is rightly swallowed up into destruction [Note: [Col. 0875D] Rhen. and Seml. read *in destruction*.], because it too swallows up to that same end. “Death,” he says, “swallowed up by growing strong,” and for that reason “it has been swallowed up in conflict.” [Note: [Col. 0875D] “‘Death,’ he says, ‘swallowed up by growing strong.’” I do not know what passage of Scripture the Author has in view, unless perhaps he means Rom. 5: “Death passed upon all people,” as Pamelius thinks. I believe that *invadendo*, “by invading,” should be read rather than *invalescendo*, “by growing strong.” [Col. 0876D] LAC.] “Where, O death, is your sting? Where, O death, is your conflict?” [Note: [Col. 0876D] “Where, O death, is your sting?” He has the same statement in ch. 47, where, as here, he writes, “Where, O death, is your conflict?” The Vulgate translator, however, better renders the Greek νίκη or νῖκος as “victory.” LE PR.] Accordingly, life too, as death’s rival, will by conflict swallow up unto salvation what death had swallowed up by conflict [Note: [Col. 0875D] Rhen. incorrectly adds *your*.] unto destruction.

## CHAPTER LV

Although, therefore, by proving that the flesh will rise, [Col. 0876A] we prove by that very fact that no other flesh will rise than the flesh now at issue, [Note: [Col. 0876D] “We prove that no other flesh will rise,” etc. Here he presses the Valentinians; they denied only the resurrection of this flesh, but not that of another. LE PR.] nevertheless the individual questions, and their causes, demand their own particular engagements [Note: [Col. 0875D] Others read *whomsoever* (*quosque*) instead of *also* (*quoque*).], even though they have already been cut down from another direction. We shall therefore interpret more fully both the force and the rationale of the change, which commonly supplies the presumption that another flesh will rise—as though to be changed meant wholly to cease to be and to perish from one’s former condition.

Change, however, must be distinguished from every conception of destruction. [Note: [Col. 0876D] “Change, however, must be distinguished,” etc. The Marcionites trifled with the claim that to be changed is the same as to perish, and that the flesh we possess will therefore not enjoy the glory of the resurrection, since it must be changed, as the Apostle teaches. [Col. 0877D] LE PR.] Change is one thing, destruction another. Yet they would not be different if the flesh were to be changed in such a way as to perish. It will perish through being changed if that very flesh which is presented in the resurrection does not remain within the change. [Note: [Col. 0875D] Rhen. and Seml. read *into the change*.] For just as it perishes if it does not rise, so too, even if it does rise but is withdrawn into a change, it likewise perishes. For it will equally not exist, [Col. 0876B] just as [Note: [Col. 0875D] *Just as* is absent from Rhen. and Seml.] if it had not risen. And how absurd it is if it rises in order not to exist! It could simply have failed to rise and failed to exist, since it had already begun not to exist.

Change and destruction—things wholly different and certainly different in their operations—will not be confused. [Note: [Col. 0876C] Franc. reads *only*.] The latter destroys; the former changes. Therefore, just as what has been destroyed has not been changed, so what has been changed has not been destroyed. For to have perished is wholly to cease being what one was; to have been changed is to exist otherwise. But [Note: [Col. 0876C] Rhen. and Seml. insert *but*.] while a thing exists otherwise, it can still be that very same thing, for that which does not perish retains existence. It has undergone change, not destruction.

Indeed, something can be changed and nonetheless remain the same thing. Thus the whole human being in this age remains the very same in substance, yet is changed in manifold ways—in appearance, bodily bulk, health, condition, [Col. 0876C] rank, age, occupation, business, craft, resources, residence, laws, and morals—and nevertheless loses nothing of being human. [Note: [Col. 0876C] Seml. reads *admits nothing of the human being*.] Nor does one so become another [Note: [Col. 0876C] Fran. and Pam. read *another thing*.] [Note: [Col. 0876D] Others read *becomes*.] as to cease being the same; indeed, one does not become another person at all [Note: [Col. 0876D] Rhen. and Seml. read *becomes*.], but something different.

Divine proofs also attest this form of change. Moses’ hand is changed, and indeed into the likeness of something dead: bloodless, white, [Note: [Col. 0876D] Fran. reads *and white*; Seml., *whitened*.] and cold. But when its heat is restored and its color returns, it is the same flesh and blood. The face of the same man is afterward changed with a brightness that cannot be gazed upon. [Note: [Col. 0876D] Paris. and Fran. read *with a brightness that can be gazed upon*.] But he was still Moses, [Note: [Col. 0876D] Venet. reads *they were*.] even though he could not be looked upon. So too Stephen had already put on the summit of angelic glory, yet no other knees gave way [Col. 0877A] beneath the stoning. [Note: [Col. 0877C] Paris. and Fran. read *during the stoning*.]

The Lord also, in the mountain retreat, had changed even his garments with light, but had preserved features recognizable to Peter. [Note: [Col. 0877D] “But features recognizable to Peter.” Christ’s transfiguration was the pattern of our resurrection; therefore each person will preserve the features and form by which that person can be recognized. Justin gives the reasons for this in Question 60. LAC.] There too Moses and Elijah had taught that the same bodily condition persists even in glory: the one in the image of flesh not yet recovered, and the other in the reality of flesh not yet deceased. [Note: [Col. 0877D] “The one in the image of flesh not yet recovered.” This opinion of Tertullian is approved by St Thomas in his commentary on Matthew and *Summa Theologiae*, III, q. 45, art. 3, ad 2; Lyra on Matt. 17; and Abulensis, Questions 54 and 55. Nevertheless, the more numerous testimonies of the Fathers and interpreters suggest that Moses too appeared in the reality of his own flesh, having risen from the dead for that occasion. Epiphanius approves this opinion on the authority of Proclus and Methodius; Augustine, *On the Miracles of Scripture*, bk. III, ch. 10; Jerome on Matt. 17. [Col. 0878D] LAC.]

Instructed by this example, Paul too says: “He will transfigure [Note: [Col. 0877C] Venet. reads *has transfigured*.] the body of our humiliation so as to conform it to the body of his glory.” But if you maintain [Note: [Col. 0877C] Rhen. and Seml. read *even if*.] that transfiguration and conversion involve the passing away of any substance, [Note: [Col. 0878D] “The passing away of substance.” Here the Author calls *passing away* what earlier in this chapter he repeatedly called *destruction*, just as we customarily call death a passing. This is confirmed by what he adds: “Therefore Saul too, when converted into another man, departed from his body.” PAM.] then Saul too, when converted into another man, departed from his body, [Note: [Col. 0877C] Rig. and Venet. delete *his*.] and Satan himself, when transfigured into an angel of light, loses his own quality. I think not. Thus, with a view to the event [Note: [Col. 0877C] Others read *in the event*.] of the resurrection, it will be possible to be changed, converted, and re-formed while the substance is preserved.

## CHAPTER LVI

For how absurd, how truly unjust [Note: [Col. 0877C] Others incorrectly read *pagan*.], and how unworthy of God in either respect, that one substance should perform the work and another be paid the reward [Note: [Col. 0877D] Rhen. and Seml. read *reward* in the accusative]: that this flesh should be torn apart through martyrdoms but another crowned; and conversely, that this flesh should wallow in filth but another be condemned! Is it not better to draw all faith away from the hope of resurrection once and for all than to make sport of God’s gravity and justice? It would be to raise Marcion instead of Valentinus!

For it is not credible that the mind, memory, or conscience of the present-day human being will be abolished by that changing garment of immortality and incorruption—an abolition that would, of course, empty the benefit [Col. 0877C] and fruit of the resurrection and the character of God’s judgment in either outcome. If I do not remember that I am the person who earned the reward, how shall I give glory to God? How shall I sing him a new song, if I do not know that I am the person who owes gratitude to God? [Note: [Col. 0878C] Rhen. and Seml. delete *to God*.]

Why, moreover, is the change of the flesh alone made an exception, and not simultaneously that of the soul, which has governed the flesh in everything? What sense does it make that [Note: [Col. 0878C] *That* is absent from Rhen.] the same soul which passed through the entire course of life in this flesh, which learned God [Col. 0878A] in this flesh, put on Christ, and sowed the hope of salvation, should reap its fruit in some other flesh, I know not what? Indeed, that would be a most highly favored flesh, for which life would cost nothing. But if the soul too will not be changed, there is no resurrection of the soul either; for the soul itself will not be believed to have risen unless a different soul has risen.
