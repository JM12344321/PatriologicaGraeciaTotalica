Such and so many are their futile arguments, by which they both flatter God and pander to themselves, enervating discipline rather than strengthening it. With how many equally weighty contrary texts can we strike back—texts which both press upon us the severity of God and summon up our steadfastness? For although God is good by nature, he is nevertheless also just (Ps. 7:12). As the occasion requires, he knows not only how to heal but also how to strike (Deut. 32:39); he makes peace, but also creates evils (Isa. 45:7); he prefers repentance (Ezek. 18:23, 32), yet he also commands Jeremiah not to intercede for a sinful people:

> “For even if they fast,” he says, “I will not hear their supplication”;

and again:

> “And as for you, do not pray for this people, and do not petition for them in prayer and [Col. 0984B] supplication, because I will not hear them when they call upon me in the time of their affliction” (Jer. 7:16; 11:14; 14:11–12).

And still earlier, the same God who gives mercy precedence over sacrifice says:

> “And as for you, do not pray for this people,”

and:

> “Do not ask that they obtain mercy,”

and:

> “Do not approach me on their behalf, because I certainly will not hear those who ask for mercy” (ibid.)—

those, assuredly, who weep and fast in repentance and offer their affliction to God. For God is jealous (Exod. 20:5; 34:14),

[Note: [Col. 0984D] “And who is not mocked by the nose.” In this way he rendered Paul’s expression in Gal. 6, *οὐ μυκτηρίζεται* (“is not mocked”). Hesychius: *Μυκτηρίζῃ, χλευάζῃ, καταγελᾷ ὑπὸ τοῦ μυζὴν τοῖς μυκτῆροις*—“You mock: you jeer, you ridicule; from making a contemptuous sound with the nostrils.” RIG.]

and one who is not mocked by a wrinkling of the nose (Gal. 6:7)—that is, by flattering his goodness. And although he is patient (Ps. 7:12), nevertheless through Isaiah he threatens an end to patience:

> “I have kept silent; surely I will not always keep silent and endure? I have been still like a woman in labor; I will arise and cause them to wither” (Isa. 42:14–15).

For fire will go [Col. 0984C] before his face and consume his enemies (Ps. 96:3), and he kills not only the body but also souls in Gehenna (Matt. 10:28).

Moreover, the Lord himself shows how he threatens those who judge:

> “For with whatever judgment you have judged, judgment will be passed upon you” (Matt. 7:2).

Thus he did not prohibit judging, but taught how to judge. Hence the Apostle too passes judgment—and indeed in a case of fornication—that such a man must be handed over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh (1 Cor. 5:5). He also reproaches them because disputes among brethren were not being judged before the saints (1 Cor. 6:1 [Col. 0984D] ff.). For he adds:

> “Why should I judge those who are outside?” (1 Cor. 5:12).

You forgive, moreover, so that you may be forgiven by God (Luke 6:37). The offenses that are wiped away are those which someone has committed against a brother, not against God. Indeed, in prayer we profess that we will forgive our debtors (Matt. 6:12).

But it is unfitting to continue stretching a contentious rope of this kind, drawn alternately [Col. 0985A] in opposite directions from the authority of the Scriptures, so that some texts appear to tighten the reins of discipline and others to loosen them, as though the Scriptures were uncertain, and as though some texts laid down the aid of repentance through leniency while others denied it through severity.

[Note: [Col. 0985C] “And” is absent from Rhen., Oberth., Pam.]

Rather, the authority of Scripture will remain within its own limits without mutual contradiction, provided that

[Note: [Col. 0985C] “If” is absent from Rhen., Oberth.]

the aid of repentance is defined by its own conditions, without indiscriminate concession, and that the grounds for repentance themselves are first distinguished, without a confused statement.

[Note: [Col. 0985C] *Praepositione* (“preposition”) Oberth., Seml.]

We stipulate that offenses are the ground of repentance. We divide these into two outcomes: some will be remissible, others irremissible; just as no one doubts that some deserve correction and others condemnation. Every offense is canceled either by pardon or by punishment: by pardon as a result of correction, by punishment as a result of condemnation. Concerning this distinction we have already set out certain opposing passages of Scripture, some retaining and others [Col. 0985B] remitting offenses.

But John too will teach us:

> “If anyone knows that his brother is committing an offense not unto death, he shall ask, and life will be given to him” (1 John 5:16),

because he does not sin unto death: this offense will be remissible.

> “There is an offense unto death; I do not say that anyone should ask concerning that” (ibid.):

this offense will be irremissible. Thus, where there is a condition or rationale for petition, there too is one for remission; where there is none for petition, there likewise is none for remission.

According to this distinction among offenses, the condition of repentance is likewise differentiated. One kind will be capable of obtaining pardon—namely, in the case of a remissible offense; another will be wholly incapable of obtaining it—namely, in the case of an irremissible offense. It remains, therefore, to examine specifically the status of adultery and fornication, and into which class of offenses they must be placed.

## CHAPTER V

What degree of wickedness, then, adultery—which is also a matter of fornication—must be judged to possess according to the nature of its act is shown at once by the foremost law of God.

[Note: [Col. 0988C] “The foremost law of God is at hand.” The first law of God was delivered through Moses, the second through Christ. ## CHAPTER VI

Certainly, if you show by what supporting examples and heavenly precepts you open wide the door of repentance to adultery alone, and in adultery to fornication also, our contest will now be fought on this line. Nevertheless, I must prescribe a rule for you: do not reach your hand toward the old things; do not look back.

For the old things have passed away, according to Isaiah (Isa. 43:18–19), and there is now a new renewal, according to Jeremiah (Jer. 4:3); and, forgetting what lies behind, we stretch forward toward what lies ahead, [Col. 0989C] according to the Apostle (Phil. 3:13); and the Law and the Prophets lasted until John, according to the Lord (Matt. 11:13). For although we have just now begun from the Law in demonstrating the character of adultery, we rightly began from that aspect of the Law which Christ did not abolish but fulfilled (Matt. 5:17).

The burdens of the Law lasted until John, not its remedies; the yokes of works were cast off, not those of discipline. Freedom in Christ has done no injury to innocence. The entire law of piety, holiness, humanity, truth, chastity, justice, mercy, benevolence, and sexual purity remains. Blessed is the man who will meditate upon that law day and night (Ps. 1:1). Concerning it the same David says again:

> “The law of the Lord is blameless, converting souls; the ordinances of the Lord are right, delighting hearts; the commandment of the Lord shines from afar, enlightening the eyes” [Col. 0989D] (Ps. 18:8 ff.).

So too the Apostle:

> “Therefore the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy and excellent” [Col. 0990A] (Rom. 7:12)—

certainly including:

> “You shall not commit adultery” (Exod. 20:14).

> “Do we therefore nullify the Law through faith? By no means; rather, we establish the Law” (Rom. 3:31),

> “You shall not commit adultery,”

> “Whoever looks with lust has already committed adultery in his heart” (Matt. 5:27–28);

> “You shall not kill,”

> “Whoever says to his brother, ‘Raca,’ will be liable to Gehenna” (ibid. 5:22).

[Note: [Col. 0989D] “Some” Pam., Rhen., Oberth.]
