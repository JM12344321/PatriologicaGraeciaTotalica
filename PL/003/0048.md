That edition of Ouzel was reprinted at the Hackius press in Leiden in 1672; but its author, and by whose care and labor it was produced, are left entirely unmentioned. [Col. 0393A] For in merely a brief notice, the printers declare that they had remained uncertain and anxious as to which writer’s work they should above all print in new type, and that at last, on the advice of learned men, they had published this *Octavius* of Minucius Felix—complete, as they say, in every respect, and illustrated with all the notes and observations of the various scholars that had appeared up to that time.

But Gronovius, as will be said a little later, took a very different view. Indeed, he thinks that there is no edition worse or less worthy of approval than this very one, precisely because of those same notes of Ouzel. Moreover, although it appears to have been printed in more elegant type than all the others, we have nevertheless detected numerous typographical errors, especially in the observations and notes where authors’ books are cited. [Col. 0393B] We have thought it necessary to give this warning so that no one in the future may be led into error by failing to examine them carefully enough.

In addition, Christoph Cellarius arranged for another edition of this work of Minucius to be produced at Halle in 1699 for the benefit of Christian youth. For this was his conviction—and would that it were everyone’s—that the best authors of Latin, Cicero and others who present nothing but what is modest and honorable, certainly ought to be read by Christian youths, but that with them should be joined the most ancient writers of the Christian Church who wrote in purer Latin. For he was persuaded, and not without reason, that through such reading young people could at once be imbued and formed by the splendor and elegance of the Latin language, by proofs of our faith, [Col. 0393C] by holiness of conduct, and by ecclesiastical history.

Finally, in 1709 this book was committed to print at Leiden “according to the recension of Jacob Gronovius.” The reasons that led him to publish it again are explained by him in these words at the very beginning of his preface:

> An edition of M. Minucius Felix had to be repeated in this city, when, through various conversations, the booksellers learned of my frequent recommendation of this little work and of my reading it both long ago and continually, so that it was impossible that they should not approach me.

The republic of letters is therefore undoubtedly indebted to him on many counts for not denying these booksellers—or rather printers—his assistance. Without it, indeed, there would have been no new edition of the *Octavius*, [Col. 0393D] or it would have appeared defiled rather than cleansed by less learned hands.

He censures all the earlier editions of this book, asserting that there is none in which much is not lacking. But he attempts above all to discredit those prepared by Rigaltius and Ouzel in the years 1645, 1652, and 1672. Yet he criticizes nothing except that the notes published in them “are,” as he says, “either excessively abundant and verbose, or too few.” For the longer notes not only obscure and cause the entire charm of Minucius’ style to vanish, but his text itself, submerged in them, can scarcely be read through by anyone without great weariness. He further believes that many passages still remain which, although requiring correction, [Col. 0394A] were nevertheless left entirely untouched and passed over by the authors of those observations.

Yet Gronovius certainly seems to exaggerate the faults of these and other editions for no other purpose than to persuade everyone that his own is superior to all the rest and complete in every respect. Nevertheless, those places in the text of our Minucius which he judges still to be disfigured by numerous errors or to be rather obscure, he endeavors to elucidate—or, more rarely, to correct—not on the authority of any manuscript codex, but solely by his own conjectures or those of others. How much weight these conjectures possess we shall show below in the appropriate place. And we shall presently make it plain that in his own notes he fell into the very faults that he dared to rebuke in others with such confidence.

[Col. 0394B] It must also be observed that some of the editions of this work of Minucius reviewed thus far were printed not merely once, but a second and third time, and even more often. But because nothing new was added to them, it seems to us of little use—or rather wholly superfluous—to enumerate and review them here.

## ARTICLE IV. On the Notes and Observations of Various Scholars upon This Book

Of all those through whose efforts, as we have said, this book was published in print, there are few who did not publish numerous notes and observations upon it. We must therefore now [Col. 0394C] explain what these are, what their quality is, and of what kind they are. First, Johannes Meursius produced several notes on this *Octavius*, which we possess in print in a certain little work entitled *Hypocriticus Minucianus, or A Critic in His Spare Hours*. In them he attempted to correct by his own conjectures certain passages of our author that seemed corrupt to him, but very often with little success or appropriateness. He also explains and interprets a few other passages by means of the words of other writers.

Franciscus Balduinus, in his *Prolegomena* to this book, after vindicating it for Minucius Felix, first makes some preliminary remarks about his period, family, native country, profession, advocates, and the Roman forum. He then discusses the style and subject of this book, the different methods of argument employed by Caecilius and Octavius, and briefly expounds several [Col. 0394D] topics in their debate. He confirms them from certain other writers, especially defenders of the Christian religion—Justin Martyr, Athenagoras, Theophilus of Antioch, Clement of Alexandria, Arnobius, Lactantius, Ambrose, and Augustine—but almost nowhere indicates in which books, or in what part of those books, the passages he cites are to be found. Finally, at the close of his preface, he exhorts Christians to the study of venerable ecclesiastical antiquity.

Wower, for his part, paid sufficient regard to brevity in his notes. In them he either attempts to correct errors in the text or to explain or [Col. 0395A] confirm the words and statements of Minucius Felix by certain testimonies of other writers. Yet one may certainly and rightly demand greater diligence from him in citing them. For in some places he mentions only the writer’s name but takes no care to indicate either his book or the place in the book where the words cited by him occur. We have noticed that he does this especially when he sends us to the works of Tertullian, and above all to his *Apologeticum*. At times he also wanders beyond the subject. An example is the passage where Octavius is insultingly called by Caecilius “the foremost of the bakers.” There Wower improperly accumulated much that contributes absolutely nothing to the author’s purpose.

Elmenhorst’s notes, however, are more extensive than Wower’s, and in them he records the passages of the authors he cites much more accurately than Wower does. [Col. 0395B] Yet in observations so lengthy you will search in vain for anything pertaining to the integrity or correction of Minucius’ text; for in them his only aim is to assert and establish many of the author’s statements by the testimony of others. Finally, he occasionally refers us to his own observations on Arnobius, or to Wower’s observations on this work of our Minucius. Gronovius, moreover, treats all those notes of Elmenhorst with his customary contempt and asserts that the judgment previously pronounced upon them by Scaliger was true (Scaliger, *Epistles* 260 and 261).

As for the notes originating with Heraldus, in most of them he discusses either the emendation of our author’s text on the authority of the Royal manuscript, or its correction by his own conjectures. In others, however, he refers us either to his own notes on the works of Arnobius or to Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, though he scarcely ever cites any of its chapters. [Col. 0395C] In the remainder of his notes, finally, he attempts to confirm the opinions of Minucius from other writers. But these pertain more to the first part of the book—namely, Caecilius’ speech and his calumnies against the Christians. Very few of this kind relate to Octavius’ reply and his rejection and denunciation of the impious rites of the pagans.

To some, Rigaltius’ observations on this book seem learned and quite curious. To omit others, Ouzel admits in his preface to the book that many excellent things are found in them and that Rigaltius restored the *Octavius* to soundness more than all the rest. [Col. 0395D] Yet he reproaches Rigaltius for indulging excessively in his own conjectures and, by intermingling them with Minucius’ words, making him say things that had never entered his mind. But if Gronovius is to be believed, this disputation of Minucius Felix deserved “to proceed more reverently, and to be more sharply polished and placed in a better light” than it was in Rigaltius’ edition.

Yet certainly whoever reads his observations will plainly discover that they sometimes provide a rationale for the emended text, explain certain rather obscure expressions, and support many passages of Minucius by the authority of other authors. Nevertheless, he will perceive no less clearly that often neither their books nor the chapters of those books are cited, but only the author’s name. He will also discover that Rigaltius entirely passed over [Col. 0396A] many things that appear to require explanation no less than the rest. Finally, from what will be said later, anyone will be able to recognize upon what slight conjectures he often wished to emend the text of Minucius.

Although Ouzel’s observations on this book are exceedingly lengthy, he nevertheless admits that he composed them “in his youthful years” and “while the press was running.” He further declares that in them he occasionally departed from the opinion of other men, though very learned, but without insult. Yet no one will deny that they are excessively long. For he often digresses entirely from the author’s text under discussion and accumulates to the point of nausea a great deal that contributes absolutely nothing to it. Would you like some examples of these digressions to be presented? [Col. 0396B] Consider, I ask, how irrelevant to the subject are the things he reported about the idols set up in the Turks’ temple at Mecca in order to explain Minucius’ discourse about stones that had been shaped and anointed. And how does Ouzel’s lengthy investigation contribute to Minucius’ purpose—his inquiry into the care devoted by Romans to dedicating shrines, or by certain others to observing auguries, and into the first inventor of augury and the etymology of *delubrum* (“shrine”)? Why, too, when merely presented with an opportunity to speak of the birthdays of the gods, does he range at very great length through explanations of the birthdays of men, both princes and private persons, and even of cities, and the various rites by which they were celebrated? No smaller are his digressions concerning Mercury’s name and offices, where Minucius simply says that he was depicted with [Col. 0396C] winged feet; concerning sneezing, where Socrates’ *daimonion* is mentioned; and—to pass over a great many others—concerning the dog and other animals worshiped by the Egyptians, of which Minucius had made no mention.

But perhaps these things will seem more readily pardonable in a young man who, since he wished to make a show of his empty learning, could not make his observations—published, as he says, “while the press was running”—any shorter. Gronovius, however, could grant him no pardon at all. For he calls him a wild and iron-hearted man, and an impious commentator who falsely peddles the claim that his observations were born “while the press was running.” They are, Gronovius says, a wicked repository in which he stored his thefts, which should rather have been consigned to curses, had he not derived some things from Gronovius’ own father, [Col. 0396D] from Boxhorn, Vossius, and Elias Schevius. But who will not admit that this is the judgment of a harsh and iron-hearted man rather than of an equitable judge?

Cellarius states that he accomplished three things in his notes. First, he gave an account of his emendation of the text. Second, obscure histories and fables, if there were any, were elucidated; and finally, Minucius’ Latin words, whenever less common or more difficult ones occurred, were explained more clearly. Yet in these observations he either repeats much that others had said or leaves unmentioned many things that required exposition no less than the rest.

Furthermore, Jacob Gronovius asserts that this entire collection of notes and observations with which we have dealt thus far is nothing but an undigested [Col. 0397A] mass, an infinite heap of filth, and insane digressions from Minucius’ text. Yet although he admits that the same text of Minucius is utterly and perversely overwhelmed and submerged beneath that absurd and useless profusion of observations, he nevertheless laments that many things were omitted from them—things by which Salmasius, Brummerus, Ernestius, and especially Johannes Pricaeus had attempted, in works on other subjects, to emend or elucidate certain obscure passages of our author.

For that reason Gronovius himself, in his edition of this book—the last of all its editions—added to those earlier observations, apart from certain ones of Ouzel, the notes of Salmasius and others, as well as his own. Yet he carelessly slips into precisely the same deviations that he condemns in others with such confidence. For Minucius’ text is far more thoroughly submerged than in the earlier editions beneath the lengthier notes [Col. 0397B] of Gronovius himself and of the others whom he transcribed. Indeed, one may see numerous pages on which not a single word of Minucius appears; on others there are only one, two, or three lines, and on the remainder very few lines of his text. Who, then, will deny that Ouzel himself—whose notes Gronovius thinks deserving of such disdain—was far more prudent, since in his edition he presents the whole and complete text of Minucius and relegates all his observations to its end?

If you wish to know how weighty and how much superior to the rest Gronovius’ notes are, this must be shown to you by several examples. First, upon these words of Minucius, “to return to things past,” [Col. 0397C] he notes that they present a most effective description of true pleasure derived from past joys. He attempts to prove this by the examples of Tullius and Vespasian, which are no more relevant to the subject than countless others would be. Then he immediately continues as follows:

> Certainly what the eyes accomplish here, reflection can accomplish in other matters; indeed, may I not live if this happiness does not accompany me throughout my years, so that domestic happiness may console me and I may live at Cambridge with John Pearson, at Paris with Henri Valois, and at Florence with Antonio Magliabechi, men blessed forever with every distinction of learning.

Good heavens, how excellent, how learned, how necessary this observation was! Who could ever have heard Minucius, who speaks perfectly clearly and plainly, if it had been omitted?

[Col. 0397D] Where Caecilius said that “mists always arise,” Gronovius heaps up numerous remarks about the Greek nouns ἀνεφέλη (*anephélē*, “cloudlessness” or “clear sky”) and νέφος (*néphos*, “cloud”), not in order to explain his author’s text, but to refute Pricaeus and the French translator of this book.

But he wanders still further afterward, where Caecilius objects to the Christians that no one had returned from the underworld, even with Protesilaus’ lot, “with leave granted for hours at least.” First Gronovius warns us that nothing in Latin is either more charming than this expression or more pleasing to himself. He then proves at great breadth that the single elegant genitive “of hours” signifies a short portion of time that did not extend beyond one day. Who, I ask you, could ever have understood this [Col. 0398A] without Gronovius’ warning? Finally, he sharply chastises Jan Broukhusius, who wished to write “of a few hours,” adding the word “few” before “hours,” and adds that he had been harassed by him with every kind of lie and calumny. Was it therefore permissible for Gronovius rather than for others not only to digress in lengthy notes from his author’s purpose, but also to avenge private injuries?

But come now: who in any edition of this book ever produced an observation longer than the one he wrote on these few words of Minucius concerning the statue of Diana: “constructed with many breasts and spits”? For this note fills six or more pages of his book, and an entire letter of Holstenius, which had already been published more than once, is transcribed there. Could he not, if this were necessary, either have relegated it to the end of the book or [Col. 0398B] selected from it—as he did from the observations of Wower, Elmenhorst, and others—certain matters more conducive to the explanation of our author?

But we ourselves would certainly become too lengthy if we wished to review individually Gronovius’ other notes resembling these, especially those in which he inserts the testimony of a certain more recent writer composed in French and everywhere points out Ouzel’s thefts and compilations—as though Minucius’ text could not have been understood if these things had been omitted.

Yet neither are we among those who, like Gronovius, scorn the notes and observations of everyone else or judge that they ought to be rejected entirely. For it must be admitted that in them one repeatedly finds varied learning, both that of Gronovius himself and of others, and that by them [Col. 0398C] certain of Minucius’ more obscure expressions and some words badly corrupted by the copyist are explained. Yet other passages still remain that call for a physician’s hand and undeniably require explanation. A new edition of this book, more accurate and complete than the rest, could therefore undoubtedly be prepared by some learned and sincere lover of truth who is not excessively devoted to his own party.

## ARTICLE V. On Certain Passages of This Book That Are Corrupt or Suspected of Corruption

Since only one manuscript of this book, the Royal codex, survives for us, it is certainly no wonder if various errors by an unskilled copyist ignorant of Latin occur in the author’s [Col. 0398D] text—errors which the editors of so many scholia and notes have either passed over untouched or attempted to correct by conjectures that were either insufficiently certain or false. Something must therefore be said here concerning these corrupt or suspect passages, apart from the matters to be discussed below when a more suitable opportunity presents itself; and, if possible, a physician’s hand must be applied to them.

First, then, Meursius suspected that in this passage of the *Octavius*, where Minucius said, “While walking by the sea at daybreak, etc.,” the name of Caecilius, the companion of the others, had been omitted. But this is mistaken. For Minucius soon afterward calls him by his own name, and a little later Octavius calls him “a man who at home and abroad clings to the side” [Col. 0399A] of Minucius. From this it is sufficiently clear that in the earlier passage everyone can easily supply Caecilius’ name.

Rigaltius observed that Minucius, when speaking of the sea’s waves, said: “delighted there by curling and swelling windings.” But in the Royal manuscript the first letter *C* of the first adjective *crispis* (“curling”) had been corrected, and another letter that preceded it appears to have been erased, as does one that stood in the middle of the word *torosis* (“swelling”). For this reason, some thought that *tortuosis* (“winding”) should be read there; others thought that the copyist had correctly changed it to *torosis*. Rigaltius approves this latter reading. Physicians, he says, spoke of the *tori* (“swellings”) of veins, and poets of the *tori* of manes. But he could have cited as more relevant to Minucius’ purpose this verse of Virgil:

> The swelling banks, and meadows fresh with streams,  
> We inhabit.  
> —Virgil, *Aeneid* VI.674

[Col. 0399B] Later, however, we read in the Royal codex: “It is easier to debate among messmates than to engage in the manner of wise men in those matters, etc.” These words appear corrupt to many. Accordingly, some thought that one should read “than to join hand with a wise man”; others, “than to engage in the manner of wise men,” that is, as wise men are accustomed to converse and debate among themselves. But because this verb *conserere* (“to join” or “engage”), standing alone and without the addition of some noun—such as “hand,” “battle,” “war,” or something similar—is scarcely ever used by good writers, nothing certain can undoubtedly be determined without the aid of another manuscript.

A little later Caecilius asks Minucius to hold the balance of a perfectly impartial judge in his debate with [Col. 0399C] Octavius. “And sit as judge for me,” he addresses him, “as though ignorant of either side.” But Ouzel, against the evidence of the Royal codex, thinks that the passage should be corrected, as in the Oxford edition, to “trust me,” since the expression “sit as judge for me” is said by grammarians to be unusual. Yet is it not often written in Virgil that one “sits upon” fields, tables, or benches? Why, then, was Minucius not permitted to write “sit as judge for me,” when Cicero and other highly polished writers likewise said, “he sits beside me,” and “he sits beside Jupiter”?

Moreover, what is written in the manuscript—“the things above us, suspended in heaven, have been raised up”—seems to some suspect of error. They therefore think that *sublimata* (“raised aloft”) should be restored in place of *sublata* (“raised up”). To confirm this, [Col. 0399D] Rigaltius cites these words from a certain book wrongly ascribed to Ambrose: “In what manner this entire heaven that we behold was suspended at a lofty height” (*On Solomon*, Appendix). Yet with better right we may cite the genuine Cicero in support of the Royal manuscript’s reading; speaking against Rullus in his speech on the Agrarian Law, he said: “Rome, situated among mountains and valleys, raised up and suspended upon upper stories” (Cicero, *Speech 2 on the Agrarian Law*, p. 309).

In some editions one may read these words: “although weights established the earth.” In others: “although weight established the earth.” But the manuscript’s reading should not have been altered on the basis of mere conjecture: “although it established the earth by weight.” For there the two words “its own matter” must be understood and supplied from the words that immediately precede: [Col. 0400A] “although it suspended the heaven by its own matter, although it established the earth by weight”; that is, it suspended heaven by its own matter and also established the earth by the weight of its own matter, which is dense and heavy.

This passage, however, is extremely corrupt; it is written as follows in the Royal manuscript: “But even κρῖani quantᾶ nrᾶ, what prodigies do they invent?” [The manuscript’s mixed Greek and abbreviated forms are materially uncertain.] For it is extremely difficult to say what these words mean. Rigaltius rendered them thus: “But even the Christians—where in so much that is ours—what prodigies do they invent?” He explained them as follows: “The Christians, insofar as they are dispersed through the cities of Italy, through the provinces of the Roman people, through so much territory of ours—what prodigies do they invent?” But no one followed this reading and conjecture. [Col. 0400B] Accordingly, all others wished to emend it to: “What monsters, what prodigies do they invent?” These words indeed accord better with what precedes and follows. But what can one dare to determine when there is only a single manuscript, and it plainly corrupt, unless a second and better one is discovered?

Where Minucius demonstrates from created things that God the Creator exists, he said: “the winter olive harvest is necessary.” “But how does this pertain to Minucius’ purpose?” asks Rigaltius. For olive trees do not grow in most regions. It would have been better, he continues, to write “winter moisture,” by which the soil rests in winter as though drenched with oil. Yet he did not sufficiently notice what we read in Columella: “The middle olive harvest generally falls at the beginning of December; for before that time bitter oil is produced” (Columella, book XII, chap. 50, beginning, p. 484). [Col. 0400C] Although this occurs only in certain regions, these words of Columella were nevertheless entirely sufficient to prove our author’s reading. For if the olive harvest is in December, could it not be called a winter harvest? Moreover, Cicero argues in precisely the same manner as Minucius from vines and olive groves, which do not grow in every part of the earth (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, p. 238, line 16). But Gronovius divines that Minucius wrote *hyberni*, “of winter,” that is, “the olive harvest of winter”; yet this is a wholly futile and empty conjecture, contrary to the evidence of the manuscript.

After this we read these words of Octavius in the printed editions: “Birds live without patrimony, and cattle are fed from day to day.” [Col. 0400D] But in the Royal codex it is written: “they feed upon pastures from day to day.” This reading of the author certainly should not have been altered with such ease. For *pascua* are places in which cattle graze. Why, then, could Octavius not have said “they feed upon pastures,” just as Virgil wrote:

> Indeed, they graze the forests and the heights of Lycaeus.  
> —Virgil, *Georgics* III.314

No less obscure than corrupt is this passage, which the manuscript presents as follows: “Some are raised up to commands and dominions to such a degree that their talent is freely put up for sale by the license of a ruined mind’s power.” Since these words seemed to have scarcely any meaning, they were emended in the printed editions as follows: “Some are raised up to commands and dominions [Col. 0401A] to such a degree that ruined minds may freely put their talent up for sale through the license of power.” Rigaltius thinks that “ruined minds” means the same as slanderers, flatterers, and other such plagues of the human race. He observes nothing more there. The words of Minucius as thus corrected yield this sense: flatterers or slanderers freely put the character of princes and kings up for sale by the license of the power that they possess over them.

Others, however, would prefer to read: “so that the character of their ruined mind may freely sell the license of power”; that is, they are raised up for the purpose that the character of their ruined mind may sell the license of unrestricted power. Finally, Gronovius, retaining the words “license” and “of unrestricted power,” explains the entire passage thus: “Dominions and commands are sold by their character of a ruined mind, through the license of unrestricted power.” [Col. 0401B] But who would dare assert that Minucius, who everywhere speaks with such clarity and elegance, fell into such obscurity of expression? It is therefore better to await the aid of a superior manuscript in healing this passage than to indulge in empty and frivolous conjectures.

Octavius then immediately continues: “Without knowledge of God,” he says, “what solid happiness can there be, when death is like a dream? Before it can be grasped, it slips away.” Some suspect that the word “death” is superfluous there. Others wish to substitute the noun “thing” for it. But prudently, nothing has been changed in the text. For our author’s meaning is sufficiently plain and clear: without knowledge of God there can be no solid happiness, [Col. 0401C] but only fleeting and empty happiness, since such happiness is death, which Cyrus in Xenophon said was like a dream or sleep: ἐγγύτερον μὲν τῷ ἀνθρωπίνῳ θανάτῳ οὐδέν ἐστιν ὕπνου, “Nothing is nearer to human death than sleep” (Xenophon, book VIII, *The Expedition of Cyrus*, p. 237).

Finally, some will perhaps suspect one of the following two passages. The earlier one, at the beginning of the book, reads: “And so, while proceeding along the shore at daybreak, etc.” By these words Minucius indicates that very early in the morning he proceeded with his two companions to the sea while walking, and that after a walk of no great length they all sat down in the same place to debate. The other passage, which appears to conflict with this one, occurs at the end of the book: “Yet even now some matters remain unsettled . . . concerning which we shall speak tomorrow, since the sun is now [Col. 0401D] declining toward its setting.” Did the walk and debate of Caecilius and Octavius, someone will ask, which are contained in this short book of Minucius, last throughout the entire day? Apart from this debate and a not very lengthy walk, did Minucius do nothing else with his companions, and did they all spend the entire day alike either walking or debating, without lunch and while fasting? Perhaps, then, the final words of the latter passage should be deleted, or the word “at daybreak” in the earlier one.

But this manner of speaking must not be taken altogether according to what are called the fine points of law. For Minucius did not commit to writing everything that was done that day by him and his companions. Indeed, since he testifies that he wrote down this debate at a time other than that at which it took place, [Col. 0402A] he thought that only the matters relevant to it should be narrated, with the rest omitted. Finally, Tertullian clearly declares, in the opening words of his book *Against the Jews*, that an entire day down to evening was consumed in a similar debate between a Christian and a Jewish proselyte:

> It happened recently that a debate was held between a Christian and a Jewish proselyte. Each in turn, tugging upon the contentious rope, drew the day into evening.  
> —Tertullian, *Against the Jews*, chap. 2

Yet no one has ever dared to argue from this either against the integrity of the text or against the truth of the debate. If there are other corrupt words of Minucius, or words suspected of corruption, we shall discuss them below when the occasion presents itself.

## ARTICLE VI. What Minucius Excerpted from Other Writers—Cicero, Seneca, and Tertullian—and, Conversely, What Cyprian and Others Excerpted from This Same Book of Minucius

[Col. 0402B]

Just as we observe that much in this book of Minucius was excerpted from other writers—namely Cicero, Seneca, and Tertullian—so too we observe that Cyprian transferred many passages from it, in precisely the same words, especially into his own book *On the Vanity of Idols*. We shall now select some of these; the rest, to avoid repeating the same material, we shall more conveniently reserve for their appropriate places, where the doctrines presented by Minucius will have to be examined.

First, then, Minucius proves that one God truly exists by this argument:

> What can be so manifest, so generally acknowledged, and so evident, when [Col. 0402C] you raise your eyes to heaven and survey the things below and around it, as that there exists some divine power of surpassing intelligence by which all nature is inspired, moved, nourished, and governed?

Cicero urges the same argument in precisely the same manner, but more briefly:

> What can be so manifest and so evident, when we look up at heaven and contemplate the heavenly things, as that there exists some divine power of surpassing intelligence by which these things are governed?  
> —Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, beginning, p. 214, line 10

Do you see how most of Cicero’s very words were expanded by Minucius?

Then Minucius says:

> A more extended discourse about the stars must be left to the astrologers . . . . Each individual thing required the supreme [Col. 0402D] craftsman and perfect reason not merely in order to be created, brought into being, and arranged; indeed, they cannot even be perceived, discerned, or understood without the highest skill and reason.

Tullius, however, says:

> This entire arrangement of the stars . . . . What other nature devoid of mind could have produced these things, which not only required reason in order to come into being, but cannot be understood for what they are without the highest reason?  
> —Cicero, in the same book, p. 231, line 41

Again Minucius:

> Why should I speak of the manifold protection that living creatures possess against one another? Some are armed with horns, others fenced about with teeth, supported by hooves and bristling with stings; some escape by speed of foot, others by raising their spines.

Cicero speaks more explicitly:

> We now also observe how each creature defends itself against fear and violence with its own weapons: bulls with horns, boars with tusks, lions with their bite; some protect themselves by flight, others by concealment.  
> —Cicero, ibid., p. 233, line 39

[Col. 0403A] Moreover, Minucius sometimes makes by compression a briefer statement of what Cicero had treated at greater length. Thus Minucius says:

> The Nile tempers the drought of Egypt; the Euphrates cultivates Mesopotamia; the Indus is said both to sow and to water the East.

Cicero, however, writes more fully:

> The Nile waters Egypt, and after it has kept the whole country buried and flooded throughout the summer, it then recedes and leaves the fields softened and covered with silt for sowing. The Euphrates makes Mesopotamia fertile, carrying into it each year what are, as it were, new fields. The Indus, which is the greatest of all rivers, not only enriches and softens the fields with water, but even sows them; for it is said to carry down with it a great quantity of seeds of grain and similar crops.  
> —Cicero, ibid., p. 234, line 20

Minucius then immediately continues:

> If, upon entering some house, you saw everything cultivated, arranged, and adorned, you would surely believe that a master presided over it and was much better than those good possessions. So too in this house of the world, when you behold heaven and earth, [Col. 0403B] its providence, order, and law, believe that there is a lord and parent of the universe, more beautiful than the stars themselves and all the parts of the world.

Cicero had anticipated these words:

> Just as, if someone entered a house, a gymnasium, or a forum, and saw the rational arrangement, measure, and discipline of everything, he could not judge that these things came about without a cause, but would understand that there was someone who presided and whom others obeyed; so, much more, amid such great movements and such great alternations, and amid the ordered courses of so many and such great things, in which immeasurable and infinite antiquity has never proved false, he must conclude that such great motions of nature are governed by some mind.  
> —Cicero, ibid., p. 216, line 3

It is also easy to prove that Minucius excerpted much from Seneca. For he introduces Caecilius assailing as follows the Christians who were unwilling for the dead bodies of human beings to be burned upon funeral pyres:

> As though it made any difference whether wild beasts tear them apart, seas consume them, earth covers them, or flame carries them away.

[Col. 0403C] Seneca, however, had said:

> When that divine spirit is about to depart from the human being, it judges the place to which its receptacle is consigned—whether fire burns it, wild beasts tear it apart, or earth covers it—to concern itself no more than the afterbirth concerns the child once it has been born.  
> —Seneca, *Epistle* 92, p. 381

Octavius, moreover, responds to the charge of Christian poverty:

> No one can be as poor as he was when born.

Seneca says in precisely the same manner:

> No one lives as poor as he was when born.  
> —Seneca, *Why Misfortunes Befall Good Men*, etc., or *On Providence*, chap. 6, p. 504
