Who the Salii were Livy will teach you, where he records the following concerning Numa Pompilius: “He chose twelve Salii for Mars Gradivus, gave them the distinctive dress of an embroidered tunic and, over the tunic, a bronze covering for the breast, and ordered them to carry the heavenly shields called *ancilia* and to proceed through the city, singing hymns with ritual stamping and solemn dancing” (Livy, book I, § 20). Servius, however, has far more concerning them, as do, among the Greeks, Plutarch and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, and others whom anyone may readily consult. We ourselves shall later have another occasion to discuss them further (Servius on book VII of the *Aeneid*, p. 503; Plutarch, *Life of Numa*, p. 66; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, book II, p. 29).

[Col. 0604B] The same writers have also committed to writing how that same Numa Pompilius instituted the Pontiffs and Vestal Virgins, and what sort of persons they were, as also the Augurs, who falsely claimed that they could forecast and foretell the future from food either taken into or rejected from the mouths of chickens, or falling to the ground, as Cicero says. But we must explain this more fully in the following chapter (Cicero, book II, *On Divination*, p. 289).

It is sufficiently agreed among all that this was the first institution of those Roman priests and pontiffs, and consequently of their religion and public worship of the gods. Minucius rightly concludes from this that it is false that the Romans merited dominion over the whole world through their devotion to these gods. For before they worshipped them, the Assyrians and the other nations named above [Col. 0604C] held dominion; they likewise venerated their own gods and appointed priests over their worship. Yet those peoples did not receive their kingdoms from these gods of theirs, since their kingdoms were taken away while they were still worshipping them. Therefore the Romans likewise did not acquire their empire through the favor or assistance either of their own gods or of foreign ones—an empire which, as we have shown, was founded and extended by the gravest crimes. But we shall have to treat these matters further in the dissertation.

# CHAPTER XIX

## The Arguments Are Examined by Which Caecilius Thought That the Existence of the Gods Could Be Proved from Miracles, Oracles, Auspices, Auguries, Dreams, and Other Predictions

### ARTICLE I

#### Whether Caecilius Proved the Existence of the Gods from the Miracles of Claudia Quinta, who was said to have drawn along, with her girdle alone, the ship in which the image of the Idaean Mother was being carried; of Castor and Pollux, who announced the Roman victory over King Perseus; from Latinius’s dream, by which the games were renewed; from the self-devotion of the Decii, by which the enemy was routed and defeated; and from the similar self-devotion of Curtius, by which a chasm in the earth is said to have been miraculously filled

[Col. 0604D]

Of all the arguments by which the pagans undertook to prove that their gods truly existed, the principal one certainly appears to be that which they attempted to derive from their gods’ miracles, oracles, auspices, auguries, and other predictions [Col. 0605A] of this kind. For no one could ever rightly deny that this line of argument would carry the greatest weight and importance if it rested on truth, and if all the things that the pagans used to proclaim had surpassed the powers of created nature. Therefore these matters must be carefully examined according to our custom, so that nothing whatever is concealed of what either the pagan Caecilius advances or the Christian Octavius replies, and so that each point, insofar as possible, is both developed and illuminated more fully.

Let us therefore begin with Caecilius’s miracles. By what greater miracle, he said, can the existence of the gods and their assistance to their people be proved than that by which the Idaean Mother “by her arrival both proved a matron’s chastity and freed the City from fear of the enemy”? In these few words, [Col. 0605B] which may be obscure to some, he indicates the celebrated deed of Claudia Quinta found in pagan writers. They relate that a stone image of the Idaean Mother, or Mother of the Gods—that is, Cybele or Ceres—was brought from Pessinus to Rome, on the advice of a certain seer, so that Hannibal might be driven out of Italy during the Punic War. But when the ship carrying the image reached the mouth of the Tiber, it remained so immovable that no human strength could shift it from its place. Then Claudia, whose reputation for chastity had hitherto been doubtful, proved it most clearly when, after pouring out prayers to the goddess, as they said, she alone, using only her own girdle, drew the goddess’s image, together with the ship itself, onto land at the place the Romans desired. Livy, Ovid, and, somewhat less clearly, Cicero handed this account down to posterity, as did the Christian writers Arnobius, Lactantius, Augustine, and others [Col. 0605C] (Livy, third decade, book IX, near the beginning; Ovid, book IV of the *Fasti*, § 3; Cicero, speech 30, *On the Response of the Haruspices*, p. 438; Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 22; Arnobius, book VII, *Against the Pagans*, p. 253 ff.; Lactantius, book II, ch. 7; Augustine, book II of *The City of God*, ch. 7; book III, ch. 12; and book X, ch. 16).

Arnobius, however, replies that, even if this is true, the Romans wrongly believed that through the agency or assistance of this image, or Pessinuntine stone—in which no divinity resided—the victory was won, as the pagans proclaimed, the honor of the City was restored, and the boundaries of the Roman Empire were extended. For afterward, when so many and such great Roman armies were slaughtered and routed, that truly illustrious Mother of the Gods neither checked nor repelled the greatest assaults of war. [Col. 0605D] We have already observed that Minucius similarly said that the tutelary gods of cities and nations brought no assistance to their own peoples, or to the Romans, against their enemies. Here, however, he replies that these miracles are not true but false, since they were accomplished not by any divine power but by the arts, power, and deceptions of demons. This, as we shall show shortly, he demonstrates fully and clearly.

By the same answer he also rejects the objection that Caecilius next makes as follows: “Witnesses are the consecrated statues of the Equestrian Brothers at the lake, just as they had appeared: breathless, with their horses foaming and steaming, they announced the victory over Perseus on the very day it was won.” By the “Equestrian Brothers” he means Castor and [Col. 0606A] Pollux, whom others call the Tyndarids (Cicero, book II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 214; and *Tusculan Disputations* I, p. 119, line 36). He boldly asserts that they performed two miracles: first, that through their protection King Perseus was defeated and captured by the Romans; second, that the victory was announced to the senate in Rome on the very day it was won; and finally, that their statues were erected in memory of so great a miracle. Valerius Maximus (book I, ch. 8, § 1, pp. 62 ff.), Florus (book I, ch. 11), Ammianus Marcellinus (book XXVIII, ch. 4, p. 528), Dionysius of Halicarnassus (book VI of *Roman Antiquities*, p. 350), Plutarch (*Life of Coriolanus*, p. 215), and many others relate the same story. Some of them, however, handed it down to us not as completely certain but as something popularly reported. Indeed, Cicero introduced two representative speakers: Balbus the Stoic, who attempts by this example to prove the presence of his gods, and then Cotta the Academic, who dismisses these things as old women’s tales [Col. 0606B] (Cicero, book II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 214, line 28; and book III, p. 241, line 30).

Caecilius presses another miracle: “A witness,” he says, “is the repetition, following a common man’s dream, of the games at which Jupiter had taken offense.” If anyone finds these words more obscure for their brevity, he will find them explained in the following words of Cicero:

> When the Great Votive Games were first being held during the Latin War, the citizenry was suddenly called to arms. Consequently, the games were interrupted, and renewed games were appointed. Before they were held, however, when the people had already taken their seats, a slave wearing a fork-shaped yoke [Col. 0606C] was led through the circus while being beaten with rods. Afterward, someone appeared to a Roman countryman in his sleep and said that the leading dancer at the games had displeased him, and ordered the man to announce this to the senate. The man did not dare to do so. He was ordered a second time and warned not to presume to test the speaker’s power, but not even then did he dare. After that, his son died. The same warning came a third time in his dreams; then he himself also became disabled. He reported the matter to his friends, on whose advice he was carried into the senate house on a litter. When he had related the dream to the senate, he returned home on his own feet. Thus, the dream having been confirmed by the senate, tradition records that those games were celebrated anew.

(Cicero, book I, *On Divination*, p. 264, line 49.)

Livy [Col. 0606D] describes the matter somewhat more fully (book II, ch. 36). The man whom the Orator and Arnobius (*Against the Pagans*, book VII, pp. 244 ff.) call merely a countryman is called T. Latinius by Livy and Augustine (*The City of God*, book IV, ch. 26, p. 207)—in one edition, Tib. Atinius—a commoner; by Dionysius of Halicarnassus (*Roman Antiquities*, book VII, p. 472) and Plutarch, he is called Τῖτος Λατῖνος (*Titus Latinus*; *Life of Coriolanus*, vol. I, p. 225) and is said to have been fairly wealthy; by Valerius Maximus (book I, ch. 7, § 4), unless there is an error in his printed editions and manuscripts, Ti. Atinius; by Lactantius, Tib. Attinius (book II, ch. 7, p. 176); and by Macrobius (book I of the *Saturnalia*, ch. 11), who writes that this occurred in the year [Col. 0607A] 473 from the founding of the City, Annius.

The Lactantius and Augustine whom we have cited, however, like Minucius, contend that these things should be attributed to the deceits and illusions of demons:

> Most people know all these things; some of you know that the demons themselves confess about themselves the things mentioned a little earlier: that Jupiter caused the games to be repeated by means of a dream; that the Castors appeared with their horses; and that the little ship followed the matron’s girdle. They confess all these things whenever, through the torments of our words and the fires of our prayer, they are driven by us from human bodies.

We shall explain these matters more clearly below and demonstrate how true they are.

What does Caecilius say next? “And the self-devotion of the Decii,” he says, “was duly fulfilled.” By these few words he refers to [Col. 0607B] the Decii, father and son—to whom some add a grandson—who, after devoting themselves for the Roman armies, rushed under arms into the midst of the enemy and by their glorious deaths secured a victory that had seemed hopeless. Various writers, especially Cicero, Valerius Maximus, Florus, Pliny, and Livy, have related these events.

When Balbus the Stoic raises these matters in Cicero, however, Cotta, as though he had wished to anticipate all the other pagans who would later fight against the Christians, replied in these words:

> You, however, think that the gods were appeased by the self-devotion of the Decii. What great injustice was theirs, that they could not be appeased toward the Roman people unless such men perished? That was the generals’ design: they had regard for their country and did not spare their lives. For [Col. 0607C] they thought that the army would follow its general as he spurred his horse and hurled himself against the enemy. And that is what happened.

(Cicero, book II, *On Ends*, p. 61; book I of the *Tusculan Disputations*, p. 130; book III, p. 146; *On Old Age*, p. 416; *Paradox* 2, p. 434; Valerius Maximus, book V, ch. 6, §§ 5–6; Florus, book II, chs. 14 and 17; Pliny, book XXII of the *Natural History*, p. 180; ch. 2, p. 557; Livy, first decade, book XVIII, p. 121; Cicero, book II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 215; and book III, p. 242.)

He was therefore so far from thinking that anything miraculous or any assistance of gods actually present played a part in this victory that, on the contrary, he quite rightly exposes the gods’ extreme injustice, if they had acted in this way. He thus teaches that this self-devotion of the Decii was nothing other than the generals’ στρατήγημα (*stratēgēma*, stratagem), [Col. 0607D] and the customary arts and devices by which they routed their enemies and gained victory over them. Zonaras appears to have subscribed to the opinion of Cotta—or, if one prefers, of Cicero—on this point (Zonaras, book VII of the *Annals*, § 26, p. 363).

Finally, Caecilius adds: “Curtius too is a witness, who by either the bulk or the distinction of his *eques* [here, ‘horse’] leveled the chasm of the deep abyss.” Some think that there is a double scribal error here, and that instead of “*equitis* … *honore*” (“of the *eques* … distinction”) one should read “*equi* … *onere*” (“of the horse … burden”). But this is pointless. For Livy writes that Curtius “mounted a horse adorned as splendidly as possible.” Servius, moreover, observes that in Virgil *eques* (“horseman”) sometimes signifies the same thing as *equus* (“horse”) (Livy, first decade, book VII, p. 106; Virgil, book III of the *Georgics*, v. 116).

[Col. 0608A] In that same Livy, moreover, we read that when a chasm suddenly opened in the middle of the city’s forum and could be filled by no one, M. Curtius, a warlike young man, devoted himself to the gods and, fully armed and mounted on horseback, plunged headlong into the cavern. Immediately, as Valerius Maximus puts it (book V, ch. 6, § 2), once produce had been thrown in as a mark of honor, the earth recovered its former appearance. This seemed no inconsiderable prodigy to Livy as well as to Caecilius. But Varro (book IV, *On the Latin Language*, pp. 36 ff.) reports that three different accounts of the matter were handed down by Procilius, Piso, and Aelius Stilo. What of the fact that Zonaras (book VII of the *Annals*, § 25, p. 360) declares that he will by no means contradict those who judge these things inconsistent with historical truth? [Col. 0608B] Orosius, moreover, rebukes whoever—whether gods or men—thought that the chasm in the earth could not be filled unless it swallowed a living human being (Orosius, book III of the *Histories*, ch. 5). Finally, so as not to pass over Jerome entirely, it is worth observing that he openly taught that even if the deeds of Curtius, the Decii, and others like them were proved true, the Christians’ faith was not only not diminished by them but made more certain:

> If we believe the histories of the pagans, that Codrus, Curtius, and the Decii Mures by their deaths checked the plagues, famines, and wars of cities, how much more must it be believed possible for the Son of God to cleanse by his blood not one city but the whole world?

(Jerome, on Ephesians 1:7.)

Augustine discusses at greater length these extraordinary effects which seemed miracles [Col. 0608C] to the pagans; you will readily be able to consult him (Augustine, book X of *The City of God*, p. 252; and book XXI, ch. 6 ff., pp. 624 ff.).

Besides, Minucius had already utterly overthrown all those examples or arguments raised against Christians by Caecilius or other pagans, by those invincible arguments through which he demonstrated that there is only one God, not many. But not content with this response, he makes it plain to everyone that all these things, as we said shortly before and shall show in the next chapter, were accomplished by the fraudulent arts and deceptions of demons.

### ARTICLE II

#### The Presence of the Gods Is Not Proved by Caecilius from Oracles and the Responses of Seers, Against Which Minucius Sets the Examples of Amphiaraus, Tiresias, and Pythian Apollo, as well as the Ambiguity and Cessation of Oracles

[Col. 0608D]

Although oracles in which future events are foretold in some manner surpassing the power of created things would be of no slight importance for proving the presence of the gods, Caecilius nevertheless says: “I pass over the destinies foretold by oracles.” He immediately gives the reason why he omits an argument of such great weight: “Lest antiquity seem excessively fabulous to you.” And rightly so. For the stronger the argument for asserting the truth of the Christian religion that is drawn, as we shall see elsewhere, from the true and sacred oracles of our Prophets which attained their fulfillment, the more trivial and feeble the argument is if the oracles are fabulous, [Col. 0609A] doubtful, and full of ambiguity. Events proved that the pagan oracles were of this kind so plainly that Caecilius could not deny it.

He therefore retreats to another stronghold, from which he thinks he will more successfully defend his gods and their superstitious worship against the Christians’ assaults. He thus presses the responses which seers were accustomed to give in temples. For he contends that in those very temples, where they believed the gods dwelt, the seers, filled with the gods’ divine inspiration and power, foretold the future, gave safeguards against dangers, remedies for diseases, hope to the afflicted, and consolation or relief to others. But how could those gods have provided these things when Minucius had most clearly demonstrated that they were wholly fictitious and did not exist?

[Col. 0609B] Nevertheless, lest Caecilius complain that the argument drawn from prophecies supposedly uttered under the gods’ inspiration was being evaded, although it had already been overthrown, Minucius therefore refutes several of the more celebrated responses of seers one by one and shows that they are wrongly raised against Christians:

> What, and how much, shall I pursue concerning oracles? After his death Amphiaraus gave responses concerning the future, though he did not know that his wife would betray him for a necklace.

Eriphyle was the name of this wife of Amphiaraus, of whom Cicero says: “We learn in the myths that Eriphyle was possessed by such cupidity that, when she saw a necklace, made, I believe, of gold and jewels,” offered by Polynices, “she was so captivated by its beauty that she betrayed her husband’s life” (Cicero, book IX, *Against Verres*, p. 167, line 3). But we have already discussed Amphiaraus [Col. 0609C] (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation 2, ch. 20, art. 2, p. 1117). To the writers cited there we shall add Jerome, who confirms the opinion of Cicero and our Minucius as follows: “Eriphyle is said to have betrayed Amphiaraus and to have preferred a golden necklace to her husband’s safety” (Jerome, book I, *Against Jovinian*, near the end). See also, if you wish, the scholia on book XI of Homer’s *Odyssey*, v. 325, and Servius’s annotations on book VI of Virgil’s *Aeneid*, v. 44.

Then our author says: “Tiresias, though blind, saw future things, when he did not see present ones.” Earlier, Cicero, from whom Minucius appears to have drawn his material, expressly affirms: “The poets never represent Tiresias the augur, whom they portray as wise, lamenting his blindness” (Cicero, book V of the *Tusculan Disputations*, p. 194, line [Col. 0609D] 17). If by “poets” he includes the Greeks, he undoubtedly means chiefly Homer, their foremost poet, who sang of Tiresias’s blindness in *Odyssey* X, v. 492. In the commentary on this passage, two causes of his blindness are given. Ovid supplies a third: because Tiresias had rendered a judgment concerning the pleasure of women and men contrary to Juno’s opinion, she is said to have blinded him (Ovid, book III of the *Metamorphoses*, § 4, pp. 106 ff.). Others supply a fourth: because he had seen Minerva bathing. We have discussed him elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation 2, ch. 20, art. 4, p. 1186). From this you will certainly understand by what right our Minucius ridicules a man who boasted that he foresaw future things, though he did not see present ones.

More serious, and no less true, is what he immediately added concerning Pyrrhus: “Ennius fabricated the responses of [Col. 0610A] Pythian Apollo, when Apollo had already ceased to compose verses.” These words were undoubtedly taken from the following passage of Cicero:

> Am I to consider Herodotus more truthful than Ennius? Was Herodotus any less able to fabricate concerning Croesus than Ennius concerning Pyrrhus? For who believes that this response came from Apollo’s oracle:
>
> **I say that you, descendant of Aeacus, can conquer the Romans [or: that the Romans can conquer you].**
>
> First, Apollo never spoke in Latin. Second, that lot was unknown to the Greeks. Furthermore, by the time of Pyrrhus Apollo had already ceased to compose verses. Finally, although, as Ennius says, “the race of the Aeacids was always foolish,” … Pyrrhus could nevertheless have understood the ambiguity of the verse, which could mean either “you can conquer the Romans” or “the Romans can conquer you,” and thus favored him no more than it favored the Romans…. [Col. 0610B] But the chief point is this: why are oracles no longer uttered in this manner at Delphi—not only in our own age, but already for a long time—so that nothing could now be more despised?

(Cicero, book II, *On Divination*, p. 296, line 21.)

You surely understand how greatly the argument drawn from pagan oracles in favor of their religion is weakened by Cicero himself. And certainly, besides the reasons he adduces, the cessation of oracles before the Orator’s age, and much more after it, as well as their ambiguity, manifestly demonstrates that nothing can be concluded from them in favor of pagan religion. Minucius therefore argued rightly from this against Caecilius and the other pagans, as did other very ancient writers of ours, as we have shown elsewhere. Consult also, if you wish, and read attentively, if you have leisure, what Eusebius reported concerning oracles and their ambiguity from the pagan writers themselves [Col. 0610C] (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 770; Eusebius, book V of the *Preparation for the Gospel*, chs. 18–19, pp. 208 ff.; book VI, ch. 1, pp. 256 ff.).

Permit us to add one point to these: what Minucius immediately said was also excerpted from that same Cicero: “Demosthenes, because he knew the responses were contrived, complained that the Pythia was φιλιππίζειν.” For in the Latin Orator we read the following recorded: “Indeed Demosthenes, who lived nearly three hundred years ago, said even then that the Pythia was φιλιππίζειν (*philippizein*, ‘taking Philip’s side’), that is, as though she were acting in concert with Philip. By this he meant that she had been corrupted by Philip” (Cicero, book II, *On Divination*, p. 296, line 38). What could be advanced that is clearer for explaining our author’s meaning or more forceful for refuting the pagans?

[Col. 0610D]

### ARTICLE III

#### Caecilius’s Argument Is Set Forth, by Which He Attempts to Prove That, Because Auspices and Auguries Were Disregarded by Sulpitius, Claudius, Junius, Flaminius, and Crassus, Roman Armies Were Slaughtered and Routed by the Avenging Gods, Who Therefore Existed

Caecilius presses on and, thinking that the argument drawn from auspices and auguries could evidently prove that the gods truly exist, endeavors to reinforce and establish it by various examples: “More frequently,” as he says, “than we wished, the presence of the gods has been attested when auspices were disregarded. Thus [Col. 0611A] the Allia became a name of ill omen.” Here he refers to the famous disaster at the Allia, in which the Roman army, commanded by the military tribune Sulpitius, was slaughtered and routed by the Gauls under their leader Brennus at the river Allia and the eleventh milestone, and the City was captured by them. For this reason they thereafter counted that day—although not all writers agree which day it was—among the unlucky, black, and ill-omened days. Cicero indeed wrote to Atticus: “Our ancestors held the day of the battle at the Allia to be more disastrous than the day on which the City was captured, because this latter evil arose from the former.” Anyone desiring more should consult Livy, first decade, book V, pp. 85 ff., and book VI, beginning, p. 92; Cornelius Tacitus, book XV of the *Annals*, p. 193, and book II of the *Histories*, ch. 230; Gellius, book V, ch. 17; Macrobius, book I of the *Saturnalia*, ch. 17; [Col. 0611B] Plutarch, vol. II, *Life of Camillus*, p. 137, and vol. II, *Roman Questions*, p. 269.

Caecilius next advances and presses other disastrous examples of auspices and auguries being scorned: “The battle of Claudius and Junius against the Carthaginians became instead a death-dealing shipwreck; and, so that Lake Trasimene might become greater and discolored with Roman blood, Flaminius spurned the auguries.” Cicero explains all these things somewhat more fully and clearly when he introduces Balbus the Stoic arguing for his gods in the same manner as Caecilius:

> Shall we be unmoved by the recklessness of P. Claudius in the First Punic War, who even ridiculed the gods in jest? When the chickens, released from their cage, would not feed, he ordered them to be plunged into the water so that, since they would not eat, they might drink. That jest, after his fleet was defeated, brought many tears upon him and a great disaster upon [Col. 0611C] the Roman people. What of his colleague Junius in the same war? Did he not lose his fleet in a storm because he had not obeyed the auspices? Accordingly, P. Claudius was condemned by the people; Junius took his own life. Caecilius writes that Gaius Flaminius, having neglected religion, fell at Trasimene, with a great wound to the commonwealth. From their destruction it can be understood that the commonwealth was enlarged under the commands of those who obeyed religious observances.

(Cicero, book II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 214, line 43.)

Surely, after hearing these words of Tullius, which encompass at once everything said by Caecilius, you will not wholly disagree with someone who suspects that Caecilius had them in view. Yet if you remain persuaded that he was looking to other passages of the same Cicero [Col. 0611D], where he again refers, though more briefly, to those three commanders, or where he separately and more fully relates the same things concerning Junius and Claudius, and elsewhere concerning Flaminius, we shall not altogether oppose you (same author, book II, *On Divination*, pp. 281 and 289, and book I, p. 268). Valerius Maximus also treats them, as Livy, Plutarch, Florus, and especially Ovid treat Flaminius; Ovid expressly sang on this subject:

> Let Flaminius and the shores of Trasimene be your witnesses  
> That the just gods give many warnings through birds.

(Ovid, book VI of the *Fasti*, p. 685.)

But we shall still have something to say about this history in the following dissertation.

Finally, Caecilius says: “And so that we might recover the standards from the Parthians, Crassus both incurred and [Col. 0612A] mocked the imprecations of the evil omens.” To grasp the meaning of these words, it should be observed that Suetonius handed down the following in his *Life of Octavian Augustus*: “The Parthians readily yielded Armenia when he claimed it, and, when he demanded back the military standards which they had taken from M. Crassus and M. Antonius, they returned them” (Suetonius, *Life of Octavian Augustus*, § 21). From these words it can be gathered that our author’s meaning is this: in order that Augustus might demand the military standards back from the Parthians, Crassus had lost them after disregarding the curses of the evil omens. Cicero had earlier recorded the same thing in fewer words: “We see what happened to M. Crassus when he disregarded the warning of the curses” (Cicero, book II, *On Divination*, p. 260, line 26). But Plutarch, *Life of Crassus*, vol. I, pp. 552 ff.; Dio Cassius, book XXXIX of the *Roman History*, pp. 121 ff.; [Col. 0612B] Florus, book IV, *Roman Deeds*, ch. 11; Eutropius, book VI of the *Roman History*, near the end; Velleius Paterculus, book II of the *History*, before the middle; and others describe this entire history at greater length. To them must be added Valerius Maximus, who concludes it, like Caecilius, with these words: “Thus the gods blaze with anger when scorned; thus human counsels are chastised when they prefer themselves to heavenly ones” (Valerius Maximus, book I, ch. [uncertain], § 11).

### ARTICLE IV

#### How Minucius Utterly Overthrows Caecilius’s Foregoing Argument by Other Examples—Those of Regulus, Mancinus, Paulus, and Julius Caesar—Including a Discussion of the *Tripudium Solistimum*

Before answering Caecilius’s foregoing argument, Minucius restates it as follows: “Claudius, Flaminius, and Junius therefore lost their armies because they did not think they should wait for the chickens’ [Col. 0612C] *tripudium solistimum*.” Anyone may learn what the *tripudium solistimum* was from Cicero, who rejects this kind of divination. The Orator speaks thus:

> The man who, from this very function, is called the *pullarius* brought the chickens in a cage. These, then, are the birds that carry messages between us and Jupiter. What difference does it make whether they feed or not? None at all for the auspices. But because, when they feed, something must fall from their mouths and strike the earth, this was first called *terripavium*, then *terripudium*, and is now called *tripudium*. Therefore, when a morsel falls from a chicken’s mouth, the *pullarius* announces the *tripudium solistimum* to the person taking the auspices.

(Cicero, book II, *On Divination*, p. 289, line 33.)

Some have therefore attempted in vain to correct *solistimum* to *solemnissimum* in our author’s text. [Col. 0612D] For that term properly signified what fell from the chicken’s mouth onto solid ground. Nor was it only there that Cicero said this; he had stated earlier:

> Our magistrates indeed employ compelled auspices, for when a morsel is thrown before a chicken while it feeds, a piece must fall from its mouth. What your books contain, however—that a *tripudium* occurs if something from it falls onto solid ground—this too, which I have called compelled, you call the *tripudium solistimum*.

(Ibid., p. 260, line 13.)

Brissonius has more on these matters, and you will doubtless not regret reading him (Brissonius, book I of the *Formulae*, p. 137).

After this, Minucius weakens and utterly destroys the examples advanced by Caecilius by setting against them examples directly to the contrary, which Cyprian, as was his custom, described in almost the same words (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 13). [Col. 0613A] By these he clearly demonstrates that whether auspices and auguries of this kind were observed or scorned and neglected, affairs turned out no less successfully or unsuccessfully for certain commanders. He therefore rightly argues from this that the presence of the gods cannot be demonstrated by them. First he says: “What of Regulus? Did he not observe the auguries and yet was captured?” We have already shown that M. Atilius Regulus was captured by the Carthaginians, whom he had defeated more than once, and put to death with the utmost cruelty. That he observed the auguries before this battle in the customary manner can be gathered from these words of Valerius Maximus, who openly declares that he does not know whether Regulus “surpassed everyone in matters pertaining to the observance of religion” (Valerius Maximus, book I, ch. 1, § 14). For this reason Augustine, together with our author, [Col. 0613B] concludes from it that Regulus taught by his example:

> That the gods are of no benefit to their worshippers for this temporal happiness, since he, though devoted to their worship, was both defeated and led away captive; and because he refused to act otherwise than he had sworn by them, he was tortured and killed by a new, previously unheard-of, and excessively horrible kind of punishment.

(Augustine, book I of *The City of God*, ch. 15, p. 15.)

Minucius sets forth a second example in Mancinus: “He,” as Minucius puts it, “observed religion, and yet was sent under the yoke and surrendered.” Yet the more certain it is that Mancinus, after his disastrous battles against the Numantines, was handed over into their power, the harder it is to say in what sense he is said to have observed religion. [Col. 0613C] For pagan writers testify quite clearly that he was afflicted by such great disasters precisely because he had undertaken the war despite the opposition and resistance of the auspices and of his gods. Listen, I ask you, to Titus Livy:

> While the consul Gaius Hostilius Mancinus was sacrificing, the chickens flew out of their cage; and when he was boarding the ship to depart for Spain, he happened to hear the words: “Stay, stay, Mancinus.” The outcome proved that these auspices had been ominous.

(Livy, epitome of book LV.)

Valerius Maximus relates these things somewhat more fully in the following words:

> The following prodigies happened to the consul Mancinus as he was about to depart for Spain. When he wished to perform a sacrifice at Lavinium, the chickens, released from their cage, fled into the nearest wood and, although sought with the utmost diligence, could not be found. When he boarded a ship at the harbor of Hercules, which he had reached on foot, [Col. 0613D] the following voice, though no speaker could be seen, reached his ears: “Mancinus, remain.” Terrified by it, he changed his route and made for Genoa; and when he had entered a skiff, a serpent of extraordinary size appeared and vanished from sight. Thus he matched the number of prodigies with the number of disasters: an unsuccessful battle, a disgraceful treaty, and a ruinous surrender.

(Valerius Maximus, book I, ch. 6, § 7.)

Thus, Valerius adds, he followed the headlong and mad persistence of Flaminius, whom we discussed shortly before.

How, then, did Mancinus observe religion when, “setting out against the Numantines,” as Aurelius Victor also reports, “though the birds forbade it and some mysterious voice called him back … he struck a treaty on the enemy’s terms, which the senate rejected, whereupon he was surrendered to the Numantines”? [Col. 0614A] (Aurelius Victor, *On Illustrious Men*, ch. 59.) For, as Paulus Orosius also testifies:

> The senate ordered that the treaty be annulled and Mancinus surrendered to the Numantines. His body was stripped, his hands bound behind his back, and he was exposed before the gates of Numantia. Remaining there until night, abandoned by his own people but not received by the enemy, he presented a pitiable spectacle to both.

(Orosius, book V of the *Histories*, ch. 4.)

Does not the example of so wretched a consul therefore favor Caecilius more, since from it he could have concluded that Mancinus was struck with such great calamities because he had scorned the gods’ auspices and religion?

[Col. 0614B] Yet Orosius warns us in the same place that Mancinus was one of those by whom, “so far as lay in them, the prodigies were dealt with according to custom” (ibid.), and yet he was deceived by the haruspices, those contrivers of frauds. Augustine therefore ridicules these very auguries and asks whether different chickens had marched against Numantia (Augustine, book III of *The City of God*, ch. 21, p. 80). Consider, then, whether Mancinus is said in this sense to have observed religion: because he had sacrificed according to custom and, deceived by the augurs, believed the auspices favorable to him.

More certain, therefore, is the example of Paulus next advanced by Minucius. He, as Minucius says, “had voracious chickens,” which therefore portended a successful outcome, “yet he was struck down together with the greater part of the commonwealth.” He is speaking of the battle of Cannae, which all writers of Roman history and others acknowledge was most disastrous both for Paulus and for the Romans, and which Livy declares equal in notoriety to the battle of the Allia [Col. 0614C] (Livy, book XXII, § 50). It is therefore unnecessary to produce several witnesses for a fact both certain and unknown to no one. But we should hear Cotta, who in Cicero argues from this that there is no providence of the gods: “For if,” he says, “they were caring for the human race, why did they destroy Paulus at Cannae? Why was the body of Regulus, of whom I spoke just now, delivered to the cruelty of the Carthaginians?” (Cicero, book III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 252, line 7 ff.) Indeed, Cicero himself concludes from this that there are no divine auspices, since Paulus obeyed them and nevertheless fell together with his army in the battle of Cannae (same author, book II, *On Divination*, p. 289, line 24).
