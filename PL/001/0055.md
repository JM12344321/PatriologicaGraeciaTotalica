## CHAPTER XLI

**SUMMARY.—Indeed, those very men who, scorning God, worship statues are the cause of the evils that occur every day. [Col. 0487B] For if their gods truly were Gods, they would not involve their own worshipers in the punishments of the Christians as well. But since he who truly is God sends evils upon both good and bad—upon the latter as punishment, upon the former as admonition—[Col. 0488A] he will not establish a distinction between the two before the severity of the eternal judgment. But if they persist in ascribing these evils to their own Gods because those Gods tolerate the existence of Christians, it follows that such Gods are unjust and ungrateful, since they wish their worshipers to be punished together with those who mock them.**

You, therefore, are a scourge [Note: [Col. 0488C] “A scourge to human affairs”: that is, calamitous and troublesome people, on whose account the world lacks public peace. Virgil, *Aeneid* XI.305:

> A ruinous war we citizens wage against the race of Gods  
> And unconquered men.

Here Servius explains “ruinous” as a place where there is no refuge because it lacks a harbor—that is, repose—where there is no haven. HAV.] to human affairs; you are the affliction of public interests, you are always the instigators of evils, among whom God is scorned and statues are worshiped. Surely it must be considered more credible that the one who is neglected should be angry [Note: [Col. 0488C] “That the one who is neglected should be angry.” This follows from the pagans’ own opinion. Accordingly, since the killing had been committed amid wine and feasting, the anger of the God was manifest. Arrian tells the same story, IV.9. There is also a sufficiently ridiculous tale in that witty writer of Samosata, Lucian, who relates something similar about a statue of Hippocrates in the dialogue entitled *The Lover of Lies*. There Antigonus [Col. 0488D] the physician says: Κἀμοὶ, ὦ Εὔκρατες, Ἱπποκράτης ἐστί χαλκοῦς (“I too, Eucrates, have a bronze Hippocrates”), etc.; that is: “I too, Eucrates,” he says, “have a bronze Hippocrates, approximately a cubit high, who, as soon as the lamp is extinguished, goes all around the house, making a clatter, overturning boxes, mixing medicines, and upsetting the door—especially if we defer the sacrifice that we offer them every year.” HAV.] than those who are worshiped—or else, assuredly, those gods are utterly unjust if, on account of the Christians, they injure even their own worshipers [Note: [Col. 0488B] Perhaps: “than the one who is worshiped.” Haverc.] [Note: [Col. 0488B] But “assuredly” is the reading of the Fulda manuscript.] [Note: [Col. 0488D] “They injure even their own worshipers.” This is utterly unjust, since, according to that well-worn maxim of the jurists, Digest V, *On Punishments*, it is better to let a guilty man go unpunished than to condemn an innocent one. Polybius too, Book V: Οὐ γάν ἑπ` ἀπωλεία δεῖ καὶ ἀφανισμῷ τοῖς ἀγνώμοσι πόλεμεν (“For one must not wage war against the unprincipled unto destruction and annihilation”; the recovered Greek is visibly corrupt), etc. “It belongs to good men, even when dealing with men of inferior character, not to wage a war of extermination, but only to the point that offenses are repaired and corrected; not to involve the innocent together with the guilty in a single punishment, but, for the sake of the innocent, even to spare the guilty.” Such too is Abraham’s argument addressed to God in Genesis 18:23. ## CHAPTER XLII

**SUMMARY.—It is no less wrong to call Christians unprofitable for commerce, since they live both with the pagans and from them, mingle their labor and crafts with theirs, and sail, serve as soldiers, and trade together with them. Although they differ in certain ceremonies, renounce the spectacles, do not make supplication with incense, and do not throw contributions into the temples, they are nevertheless both human beings and citizens. They readily compensate for that trifling loss through other benefits, and especially by paying the public revenues faithfully when due, instead of defrauding them as the pagans do.**

[Col. 0490B] 42. But we are prosecuted under yet another charge [Note: [Col. 0490B] “Still,” in the Fulda manuscript.] of injury: we are said to be unprofitable in business. [Note: [Col. 0490C] “We are said to be unprofitable in business.” Hence, without doubt, Suetonius Tranquillus—quite hostile to the Christian sect—calls the distinguished Flavius Clemens, son of Flavius Sabinus, the emperor Vespasian’s brother, a man of the most contemptible inactivity, where he relates that Clemens was killed by his cousin Domitian, *Domitian* XV. Torrentius rightly noted this there as well. To the pagan in Minucius, p. 8, Christians are “a secretive, light-shunning nation, silent in public and talkative in corners.” Rhenanus here and the Leiden manuscript read “unprofitable in business.” The Fulda manuscript, however, reads “unprofitable to business,” approximately as below in Chapter XLVI: “what has now become known by use and from commercial dealings.” Heraldus, Barræus, Aldus, Pamelius, La Cerda, and Rigault too read “in business.” Tacitus charges them with hatred of the human race, *Annals* XV.44. [Note: [Col. 0491A] Franciscus and the Paris editions read “the Lord God and Creator.”] We reject none of the fruit of his works; we merely exercise restraint, so that we do not use it immoderately or wrongly. [Note: [Col. 0491C] “And other commercial dealings.” Christians were therefore more tolerable even than the schools of philosophers, whose foolish wisdom the pagans not only tolerated but admired. We sail with you, serve as soldiers with you, till the soil, and trade. In the same way, we join our crafts with yours and make our labor available for your use. How we can seem unprofitable to your business, [Col. 0492A] when we live both with you and from you, I do not know.

Balsamon on Photius’s *Nomocanon*: Τοῦ αἰτοῦντος καὶ λαμβάνοντος ἐγγὺς ἵσταται ὁ Χριστὸς, συγκυλαίνων τούτῳ τὴν χεῖρα (“Christ stands near the one who asks and receives, cupping his hand together with him”); that is: “Christ stands nearest to the one who asks and receives, extending his hollowed hand together with him.” On this custom one should certainly consult the very learned Casaubon’s note on Suetonius XCI, where it is related that Augustus, lest he provoke Nemesis through excessive good fortune, following a nocturnal dream begged a contribution from the people every year on a fixed day, presenting his hollowed hand to those offering coins. Thus coins of Titus Vespasian, Domitian, and others attractively depict men receiving the donative or distribution in an outstretched cloak or garment rather than by hand. The same image appears on a coin of Hadrian bearing the inscription **ENRICHER OF THE WORLD**. Suetonius also relates an amusing story about Vespasian in his life, Chapter XXIII: “When ambassadors announced that a colossal statue of no moderate cost had been publicly decreed to him, he ordered them to erect it immediately, displaying his hollowed hand and saying that the base was ready.” Hence Seneca writes to his Lucilius in the preface to Book VI of the *Natural Questions*: “Add now a spirit unconquered by gifts, and a hand never held out to profit amid so great a contest of greed.” Tibullus censures his mistress’s greed:

> With hollowed hand she continually demands her price.

## CHAPTER XLIII
