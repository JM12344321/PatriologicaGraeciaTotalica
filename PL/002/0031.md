Thus in the other cases also you reproach him with contradictions in his commandments, as though he were changeable and unstable: he forbids work on the Sabbaths, yet commands the ark to be carried around for eight days—that is, even on the Sabbath—in the conquest of the city of Jericho. [Col. 0309C] For you do not examine the law of the Sabbath, which prohibits human works, not divine ones. For he says (Exod. 20:9–10): “Six days shall you labor and perform all your works, but the seventh day is a Sabbath [Note: [Col. 0309D] “Keep Sabbath,” Paris.] to the Lord your God; on it you shall perform no work.” What work? Surely your own. [Note: [Col. 0309D] “Surely your own”: Semler’s faulty correction.] For it follows that he excluded from the Sabbath those works which he had assigned to the six preceding days—your works, namely, that is, human and everyday works. But carrying the ark around can be regarded neither as an everyday work nor as a human one; it was both extraordinary and sacred, and, because it proceeded at that time from God’s own command, was certainly divine. I would also explain what this signified, were it not [Note: [Col. 0309D] “Unless it were too long,” Semler.] too lengthy a task to unfold all the Creator’s figurative arguments, which perhaps you do not even accept. But it is more effective if [Col. 0310A] you are refuted from things plain and absolute, by the simplicity of truth rather than by elaborate inquiry—as here too there is a definite distinction in the Sabbath’s prohibition of human works, not divine ones. Accordingly, the man who went to gather wood on the Sabbath was put to death, for he had performed his own work, which the law forbade. But those who carried the ark around on the Sabbaths did so with impunity, for they had administered not their own work but God’s, [Note: [Col. 0309D] “For—but God’s work,” Rigault, Venice.] in accordance, of course, with his own command.

## CHAPTER XXII

Likewise, when he forbids the making of a likeness of anything in heaven, on earth, or in the waters, he also discloses his reasons: namely, reasons restraining the substance of idolatry. [Note: [Col. 0310D] “Which restrain the substance,” Semler; Rhenanus: “which restrains the substance”; Ursinus’s codex. Semler conjectures *quasi* for *quae*. Rhenanus reads “restrain” for “restraining.”] For he adds: “You shall not worship them or serve them.” But the image of the bronze serpent, afterward commanded of Moses by the Lord, did not pertain to the category of idolatry, but to the healing of those who were being attacked by serpents. [Col. 0310B] And I pass over the symbolic meaning of the remedy. So too the golden Cherubim and Seraphim, fashioned as a representation upon the ark—certainly a simple ornament suited to its setting—had reasons far different from the condition of idolatry, on account of which likenesses are forbidden. They therefore do not appear to conflict with the law against forbidden likenesses, since they are not found in that condition of likeness on account of which likeness is forbidden.

We have already discussed the rational institution of sacrifices, which of course diverted toward God those acts of service formerly rendered to idols. If he afterward rejected them, saying (Isa. 1:11): “What need have I of the multitude of your sacrifices?” he intended precisely this to be understood: that he had not demanded them properly for himself. [Note: [Col. 0310D] “For I shall not drink,” he says, “the blood of bulls.” Here Tertullian departs from the reading of our Vulgate, in which Psalm 49 reads: “Shall I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?” But this is of no importance. God was indicating that he is appeased not by particular ceremonies or rites, but only by a sincere disposition of the heart. LE PRIEUR.] “For I shall not drink,” he says (Ps. 49:13), “the blood of bulls,” since elsewhere also he says (Isa. 40:28): “The eternal God will neither hunger nor thirst.” For even though he took notice of Abel’s offerings [Note: [Col. 0310D] Some read “you take notice.”], [Col. 0310C] and gladly smelled Noah’s burnt offerings [Note: [Col. 0310D] “Gladly” is absent from some copies.], what pleasure was there in the entrails of rams, or in the reek of burning victims? Rather, the sincere and God-fearing disposition of those offering what they possessed from God was accounted before God as nourishment and sweet fragrance, [Note: [Col. 0310D] “Of grace,” Junius, Semler.] for he demanded not the things that were done, but that for which they were done—namely, the honor of God.

If a client offers a wealthy man or king, who wants nothing, some gift, even of the most trifling kind, will the quantity and quality of the gift disgrace the wealthy man and king, or will the designation of the act of duty delight him? But if the client offers him gifts voluntarily, or even in accordance with the prescribed [Note: [Col. 0310D] “Prescribed,” Rigault, Venice; “or by prescribed order,” Latinus, Junius.] order of his rank, and observes the king’s solemnities, yet does so without faith, without a pure heart, and without complete obedience in all other matters as well, does it not follow that the king or [Col. 0311A] wealthy man should exclaim: “What need have I of the multitude of your gifts? I am full”; and: “My soul hates your solemnities, your feast days, [Note: [Col. 0311D] “And your Sabbaths my soul hates.” Not only the Jews are included in these words. Almost all other nations observed that day. Ovid sufficiently attests this concerning the Romans in Book I of *The Art of Love*:

> The seventh day, sacred to the Palestinian man, was observed as a festival.

And Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, Book I, chapter 7:

> The greater part of the Greeks—and Athens especially—  
> Perform rites for Saturn, which they repeatedly call the *Cronia*.

Since it was customary on those days to indulge one’s appetite, Sidonius Apollinaris, Book II, Letter 2, calls the luxury and banqueting of the Goths “Sabbath luxury.” Hence the Cynic Antisthenes calls a festival “an occasion for gluttony” (*ἑορτὴν γαστριμαργίας ἀφορμήν*); and in Isidore of Pelusium they call every festival a Sabbath (*σάββατον πᾶσαν ἑορτὴν καλοῦσιν*). LE PRIEUR.] and your Sabbaths”? [Note: [Col. 0311C] “My soul hates.” Semler omits “your.”] By saying “your,” he showed that his rejection of those things which he had prescribed should be performed was conditional and rational, because, by celebrating them according to their own desire and not according to the worship of God, [Note: [Col. 0311C] Some read “they celebrate for God.”] they had now made them their own rather than God’s.

## CHAPTER XXIII

If, moreover, you wish him to be understood as fickle [Note: [Col. 0311D] Others incorrectly read “of laws.”] even in his treatment of persons, because he sometimes rejects those whom he had approved, or as lacking foresight because he sometimes approves those whom he will later reject—as though he either condemned his past judgments or were ignorant of the future—nevertheless nothing is so fitting to one who is both good and a judge as to reject and choose according to present merits. Saul is chosen [Col. 0311B] [Note: [Col. 0311D] “Is selected,” Semler.], but before he has despised the prophet Samuel. Solomon is rejected, but only when he has been taken possession of by foreign women and enslaved to the idols of the Moabites and Sidonians.

What should the Creator have done to avoid criticism from the Marcionites? [Note: [Col. 0311D] “Should he have condemned beforehand those who were still acting well,” [Col. 0312D] etc.? This must certainly be read interrogatively, because he is speaking from the standpoint of the Marcionites. To this he aptly answers: “But it did not belong to a good God to condemn beforehand those who had not yet deserved it.” Correspondingly, should he not reject those who are sinning now because of their former good deeds? For foreknowledge of merits is not the cause of divine predestination, as Pelagius heretically supposed. To avoid excessive length, the reader may consult Sixtus of Siena, *Holy Library*, Book VI. PAMELIUS.] Should he have condemned beforehand those who were still acting well because of their future offenses? But it did not belong to a good God to condemn beforehand those who had not yet deserved it. Correspondingly, should he not reject those who are sinning now because of their former good deeds? But it did not belong to a just judge to pardon crimes after their former good deeds had already been annulled. [Note: [Col. 0311D] Others read “cut off.”]

Or what human being is without sin, so that God might always choose him [Note: [Col. 0311D] “Might have chosen,” Semler.] and never be able to reject him? Or, conversely, who is without some good work, so that God might always reject him and never be able to choose him? [Note: [Col. 0311D] “Select,” Semler.] Show yourself always good, and you will not be rejected; show yourself always evil, [Note: [Col. 0312C] Semler omits “always.”] and you will never be chosen. [Note: [Col. 0312C] “Is selected,” the same editor.] [Col. 0311C] But if the same person is each in turn at different times, God, both good and a judge, will settle his account in each respect. [Note: [Col. 0312D] “His account will be settled in each respect.” That is, satisfaction will be made to him in each, and in each he will receive his reward. RHENANUS.] God changes his sentences not through fickleness or lack of foresight, but by a most weighty and provident assessment dispenses according to the merits of each particular time.

## CHAPTER XXIV

Thus [Note: [Col. 0312D] “Thus also repentance,” etc. We supplied the missing word, without which the sense was badly impaired, and wrote “with him,” that is, with the Creator. RHENANUS.] you also misinterpret repentance in him, [Note: [Col. 0312D] “God,” Semler.] as though it likewise arose from changeability or lack of foresight, or indeed as though he now repented from recollection of a fault, [Col. 0312A] because he had said (1 Sam. 15:11): “I repent that I made Saul king.” You take it as established, of course, that repentance conveys a confession of some evil deed or error. Yet this is not always so. For a declaration of repentance also occurs in connection with good deeds, to the reproach and rebuke of one who has proved ungrateful for a benefit. So too it is then proclaimed by the Creator with reference to the honoring of Saul.

The Creator had not sinned when he took Saul up into the kingdom and increased him with the Holy Spirit, since Saul was still excellent: “There was none like him,” he says, “among the children of Israel.” He had chosen him most worthily. Nor had he been ignorant that events would turn out as they did, for no one will tolerate your ascribing lack of foresight to God when, since you do not deny that he is God, you acknowledge him also to be provident. Divinity is admittedly his proper attribute. Rather, as I have said, he was burdening Saul’s evil deed through his own declaration of repentance. [Col. 0312B] Since there was no fault in Saul’s selection, it follows that this repentance must be understood as reproachful rather than self-accusing.

“Look,” you say, “I perceive it to be self-accusing in the case of the Ninevites, when the Scripture of Jonah says (Jonah 3:10): ‘And the Lord repented of the evil which he had said he would do to them, and he did not do it.’” So too Jonah himself says to the Lord: [Note: [Col. 0312D] “God,” Semler.] “For this reason I hastened to flee to Tarshish, because I knew that you are merciful and compassionate, patient and abundant [Note: [Col. 0312D] “Of abundant,” Latinus.] in mercy, repenting of evils” (Jonah 4:2).

It is well, then, that he first placed the designation of a supremely good God: namely, one most patient toward the wicked, and most abundant [Note: [Col. 0312D] Others read “of most abundant.”] in mercy and compassion toward those who acknowledge and lament their sins, as the Ninevites did then. For if one who is such is supremely good, you will first have to concede concerning him that the conjunction of evil is incompatible [Col. 0312C] with such a one—that is, with one supremely good. And since [Note: [Col. 0312D] Semler and Junius insert “and.”] Marcion maintains that a good tree cannot also produce evil fruits (Matt. 7:18), yet Scripture has named “evil,” which the supremely good cannot contain, may there not be some interpretation by which even those “evils” can be understood which might have proceeded from one supremely good? There is indeed.

We say, then, that “evil” here signifies not something attributable to the Creator’s nature, as though he were evil, but something attributable to his authority [Col. 0313A] as judge. In accordance with this he declared (Isa. 45:7): “I am he who creates evils”; and: [Note: [Col. 0313D] “Behold, I send evils upon you,” etc. There are many passages of this kind in Scripture in which we read: “I shall bring evil,” or “evils,” upon them—for example, 1 Kings 9:4; 1 Kings 2:21; 2 Chronicles 7. PAMELIUS.] “Behold, I send evils upon you” (Jer. 18:11)—not sinful evils, but avenging ones. We have sufficiently cleared these of ill repute by showing them to be fitting for a judge. Although they are called “evils,” they are not condemned in a judge, nor do they show him to be an evil judge merely by bearing that name. In the same way, the “evil” here must be understood as one of those judicial evils and therefore as fitting for the judge.

For among the Greeks also “evils” are sometimes used for afflictions and injuries, not for acts of malice, [Note: [Col. 0313D] Formerly read “they use.”] as in this passage. Thus, if the Creator repented of this evil, it was the evil, of course, whereby the creature was to be rejected and punished with destruction. [Note: [Col. 0313D] Rhenanus: “on account of the offense”; Paris: *delitus*; others: “for destruction.”] Yet no criminal deed will be imputed to the Creator, who had worthily and deservedly [Col. 0313B] decreed that a most wicked city should be destroyed. Thus, what he had justly determined, without determining it wickedly, he had determined from justice, not from malice. Rather, he called the punishment itself “evil” because of the evil and painful nature [Note: [Col. 0313D] Semler inserts “and.”] of the suffering itself.

“Therefore,” you will say, “if you excuse the evil under the designation of justice, because he had justly determined destruction against the Ninevites, he is nevertheless blameworthy for repenting of justice, of which one certainly should not repent.” On the contrary, I say, God will never [Note: [Col. 0313D] Rhenanus reads “ever.”] repent of justice; it remains for us now to recognize what God’s repentance is. For although a human being repents, most often [Note: [Col. 0313D] “Most often from recollection of a fault.” *Plurimum* is adverbial, meaning “for the most part.” Terence in the *Phormio*: “I shall proceed home, where he is for the most part.” [Col. 0314D] Our author has spoken in this way above as well. The Latins likewise use *minimum* in this fashion. RHENANUS.] from recollection of a fault, and sometimes also from resentment at ingratitude for some good deed, it does not follow that God repents in the same manner. Insofar as God neither commits evil nor condemns good, there is no place with him for repentance over either good or evil.

The same Scripture [Col. 0313C] also establishes this for you when Samuel says to Saul (1 Sam. 15:28): “The Lord has torn the kingdom of Israel from your hand today, and will give it to your neighbor, one better than you; and Israel will be divided into two parts, and he will not turn back or repent, because he is not like a man, that he should repent.” This definition therefore establishes in every case a different form of divine repentance, one which must not be attributed, as human repentance is, either to lack of foresight or to the condemnation of any good or evil work.

What, then, will be the character of divine repentance? It is already clear, if you do not refer it to human conditions. It will be understood as nothing other than a simple alteration of a previous sentence, an alteration which can be admitted without condemnation of that sentence even in a human being, [Col. 0314A] much more in God, whose every sentence is free from fault. [Note: [Col. 0314D] “For even in its Greek sound,” etc. The word *μετάνοια* (*metanoia*), from which “repentance” is translated out of Greek, is derived from *μετανοεῖν* (*metanoein*), which signifies not confession of an offense but a change of mind, as is clear from Xenophon at the beginning of Book I of the *Education of Cyrus* and from other Greek writers. PAMELIUS.] For even in its Greek sound, the word for repentance is composed not from confession of a fault but from a change of mind; [Note: [Col. 0313D] Others read “prepared.”] and we have shown that this occurs in God as changing circumstances arise. [Note: [Col. 0313D] Venice and Rigault read “to be ruled”; the Pithou codex, “of a thing”; Pamelius and Franeker, “we have shown would occur.”]

## CHAPTER XXV

Now, to dispose of all such objections, I shall proceed to interpret and clear away the other instances of pettiness, weakness, and inconsistency—as you regard them. God calls out (Gen. 3): “Adam, where are you?” Was he, of course, ignorant of where Adam was? And when Adam alleges shame at his nakedness, God asks whether he had tasted from the tree: was he, of course, uncertain? On the contrary, he was neither uncertain of the deed nor ignorant of the place. Rather, it was fitting that the man who was hiding because of his consciousness of sin should be called forth and come out [Note: [Col. 0313D] “Betray,” Rigault, Venice.] into the Lord’s sight, not merely by the calling of his name [Col. 0314B] but with some rebuke, even then, of the deed.

For the words “Adam, where are you?” [Note: [Col. 0313D] Others read “Where are you, Adam?”] must not be read in a simple manner, that is, in an interrogative tone, but with emphasis and force, as an accusation: “Adam, where are you?”—that is, “You are in perdition”; that is, “You are no longer here.” Thus the utterance issues at once in rebuke and grief. Besides, he who holds the whole world in his hand as though it were a nest, whose throne is heaven and whose footstool is earth—could some portion of paradise really have escaped his eyes, so that Adam, wherever he was, would not have been seen by him before he was called forth, both while hiding and while taking the forbidden fruit? A wolf or petty thief does not escape the watchman of your vineyard or garden. I think that the more exalted sight of God cannot fail to observe anything below it. Fool, you who turn up your nose at so great an argument of divine majesty and [Col. 0314C] human instruction!

God was asking [Note: [Col. 0314D] “Asks,” Semler.] as though uncertain, so that [Note: [Col. 0314D] “So that” is absent from Pamelius.] by this means also, in testing [Note: [Col. 0314D] Rigault, Venice, and Semler read “condemning.”] man’s free will, he might give him opportunity, either for denial or confession, voluntarily to confess his offense and thereby lighten it, [Note: [Col. 0314D] Paris reads “reveal.”] just as he inquires of Cain where his brother is—as though he had not already heard Abel’s blood crying aloud from the earth. But this was so that Cain too might have the power, from that same faculty of free will, voluntarily to deny his offense and thereby aggravate it. In this way examples were established for us of confessing offenses rather than denying them, so that even then the evangelical teaching might be inaugurated (Matt. 12:37): “From your mouth you will be justified, [Note: [Col. 0314D] Rhenanus reads “justified together.”] and from your mouth you will be condemned.” [Note: [Col. 0314D] Others read “condemned together,” following the Vulgate.]

[Col. 0315A] For although Adam, because of the condition of the law, was consigned to death, hope remained safe for him, since the Lord said (Gen. 3:22): “Behold, Adam has become like one of us”—that is, with reference to man’s future selection for divinity. Finally, what follows? “And now, lest he at some time stretch out his hand and take from the tree of life, and live forever.” By interposing “and now,” an expression of the present time, he showed that he had imposed only a temporary and present postponement of life. Accordingly, he cursed neither Adam himself nor Eve, since they were candidates [Note: [Col. 0315D] “Candidates for restoration.” He calls the first parents “candidates for restoration” because they were to recover the happiness they had lost. Elsewhere he employs a similar expression when he speaks of “candidates for eternity.” *Candidates* were petitioners and seekers of public office at Rome; but there were also other “candidates” among the soldiers, who served as something like praetorians, *triarii*, or defenders of the emperor. Ammianus Marcellinus, Book XXV: “While Julian was engaged amid the hazards of war, the candidates, whom terror had scattered, cried to him from every side that he should avoid the fleeing mass as though it were the collapse of a badly constructed roof.” LE PRIEUR.] for restoration, [Note: [Col. 0315D] “For restoration,” Rhenanus.] having been relieved through confession. Cain, however, he both cursed and, though Cain desired to pay for his offense by death, forbade for the time being to die, since Cain was burdened not only with the deed but also with its denial. [Note: [Col. 0315D] Franeker reads “by the example.”]

Such will be the “ignorance” of our God: it is simulated so that sinful man may not be ignorant of what he must do. [Col. 0315B] But when he descends to Sodom and Gomorrah, he says (Gen. 18:21): “I shall see whether they have acted entirely according to the outcry that has reached me; and if not, that I may know.” [Note: [Col. 0315D] Pamelius, Venice, and Semler read “consume.”] Here too, presumably, was he uncertain from ignorance and eager to learn? Or was this a necessary tone of declaration, expressing not a doubtful but a threatening sense under the guise of inquiry?

And if you also ridicule God’s descent, as though he could not otherwise have carried out judgment unless he descended, take care that you do not strike your own god equally. For he too descended in order to accomplish what he wished.

## CHAPTER XXVI

But God also swears. Perhaps by Marcion’s god? “No,” you say—what is much more absurd [Note: [Col. 0315D] Semler and Pamelius read, uncertainly, “many things—foolish, because.”]—“by himself.” What would you have wished [Note: [Col. 0315D] Semler reads “what would he have wished [that he]…?”] him to do [Note: [Col. 0315D] Pamelius and Paris read “to do.”], if there was no other god [Col. 0315C] within his consciousness, especially when in the very act of swearing [Note: [Col. 0316D] “Swearing,” Wouwer.] that there was absolutely no other besides himself? Do you therefore catch him perjuring himself, or swearing vainly? Yet he cannot be thought to have perjured himself, since, as you say, he did not know that another existed. For, swearing truly to what he knows, he committed no perjury. Nor does he swear vainly that there is no other God. He would have sworn vainly only if there had been no people who believed in other gods—then, indeed, the worshipers of images; now, the heretics as well.

He therefore swears by himself so that you may at least believe God when he swears that absolutely no other God exists. You too, Marcion, compelled God to do this, for even then you were foreseen. [Col. 0316A] Likewise, if he swears in promises or threats, compelling a faith that was difficult in those earliest times, nothing is unworthy of God if it brings about belief in God.

God was, you claim, sufficiently petty even then in his very ferocity, when, enraged against the people because of the consecration of the calf, he asks his servant Moses (Exod. 32:10): “Let me be, and, angered in wrath, [Note: [Col. 0316D] Rigault and Venice write “in wrath” in place of “angered in wrath.”] I shall destroy them and make you into a great nation.” From this you are accustomed to affirm that Moses was better than his God, because he entreated him—indeed, restrained his wrath. “You shall not do this,” he says, “or else destroy me together with them.”

You too must be pitied [Note: [Col. 0316D] Junius reads “you too must be pitied”; Rhenanus, “by pitying.”] along with the people, because you do not recognize that in the person of Moses Christ was prefigured as the intercessor before the Father and the one who offered his own life for the salvation of the people. But it is enough that the people was, in the immediate situation, granted to Moses personally. The Lord asked of himself what his servant needed to be able to request from his Lord. For this purpose he said to his servant, “Let me be, and I shall destroy them”: so that Moses, by requesting and offering himself, would not let him, and thus you might learn how much influence a faithful man and prophet has with God. [Note: [Col. 0316D] “And thus you might learn how much influence a faithful man,” etc. This passage is similarly interpreted to mean that the prayer of the saints has great weight with God by St. Jerome, on Ezekiel, chapter 28, in his treatment of Ezekiel 13, and in his letter to Gaudentius; and by Cyril of Alexandria, Book V *Against Julian*. “But if the Apostles and martyrs,” says St. Jerome against Vigilantius, where he adduces this same example of Moses, “while still established in the body can pray for others, at a time when they must still be anxious for themselves, how much more after their crowns, victories, and triumphs?” This passage therefore supports the prayers of the saints for us even after this life. We have treated these, as well as the invocation of the saints themselves, at length in our notes [Col. 0317D] on Letter 57 of St. Cyprian to Cornelius. PAMELIUS.]

## CHAPTER XXVII

Now, to dispose briefly of the remaining matters as well, whatever you still collect as petty, weak, and unworthy for the destruction of the Creator, I shall put forward one simple and certain explanation: God could not enter into dealings with human beings unless he assumed human sensations and affections, through which, by means of humility, he might temper the force of his majesty—a force certainly intolerable to human frailty. This humility was indeed unworthy of himself but necessary for man, [Col. 0316C] and thus already worthy of God, because nothing is so worthy of God as the salvation of man.

I would discuss this at greater length if I were dealing with pagans, although the contest with heretics does not stand on a very different footing. [Note: [Col. 0316D] Wouwer reads “nor with heretics by much”; Semler omits “different.”] Insofar as you yourselves have already believed that God sojourned [Note: [Col. 0316D] Rigault and Venice read “diversified himself.”] in visible form and in the rest of the ordering of the human condition, you will certainly not require prolonged persuasion that God conformed himself to humanity; rather, you are refuted from your own faith. For if God—and indeed a more exalted god—laid low the summit of his majesty in such humility that he subjected himself [Note: [Col. 0316D] Others read “was subjected.”] even to death, and to death on a cross, why do you not suppose that certain petty characteristics were also fitting for our God, though more tolerable than Jewish insults, crosses, and tombs? [Col. 0317A]

Or are these the petty characteristics which ought already to furnish a presumption that Christ, exposed to human sufferings, belongs to that God whose human characteristics you reproach? For we also profess that Christ always acted in the name of God the Father; that it was he who associated with men from the beginning; he who entered into dealings with the patriarchs and prophets—the Creator’s Son, his Word, whom the Creator made his Son by bringing him forth from himself, and whom [Note: [Col. 0317D] Pamelius and Semler read “so that.”] he thereafter placed over his entire dispensation and will. He made him a little lower than the angels, as it is written in David (Ps. 8:6). By this diminution the Father also appointed him for those things which you condemn as human, so that from the very beginning he was already learning to be what he was to become at the end: man. [Col. 0317B]

He is the one who descends; the one who questions; the one who asks; the one who swears. Moreover, [Note: [Col. 0317D] Semler inserts “because”; Junius reads “who.”] the common Gospel will also testify that the Father has been seen by no one, when Christ says (Matt. 11:27): “No one has known the Father except the Son.” For he himself had also declared in the Old Testament (Exod. 33:20): “No one shall see God and live,” thereby defining the Father as invisible. Under the Father’s authority and in his name, the one who was seen—the Son of God—was himself God.

But among us also Christ is understood in the person of Christ, [Note: [Col. 0317D] “But among us also Christ is understood in the person of Christ.” Thus the copies read. Septimius means: No man has seen God and lived—in the person of the Father, that is. Yet men have seen the Father in the Son, because the Son always acted in the name of God the Father. The Father and the Son, however, are one—that is, one God. Thus the Son himself was the God whom men saw. Thus we [Col. 0318D] Christians, unlike the Marcionites, receive or understand Christ as God in the person of Christ, because in this manner also he is our God. For Christ is also man. RIGAULT.] because in this manner also he is ours. Therefore, whatever attributes you demand as worthy of God will be found in the Father, who is invisible, inaccessible to encounter, [Note: [Col. 0317D] Others read “knowable.”] and tranquil—the philosophers’ God, so to speak. Whatever you condemn as unworthy will be assigned to the Son, who was seen, heard, and encountered, the Father’s agent [Col. 0317C] and minister, mingling in himself man and God: God in his mighty works, man in his petty characteristics, so that he conferred as much upon man as he detracted from God.

Indeed, the whole dishonor of my God in your eyes is the mystery of human salvation. God associated with man so that man might be taught to act divinely. God dealt with man as an equal so that man might be able to deal with God as an equal. God was found petty so that man might become supremely great. You who disdain such a God—I do not know whether you sincerely believe in a crucified God.

How great, then, is your perversity toward both aspects of the Creator! You designate him a judge, and reproach as savagery the severity of a judge proportionate to the merits of the cases. [Note: [Col. 0317D] Wouwer reads “of judgment.”] You demand a supremely good God, yet disparage as pettiness the gentleness proportionate to his goodness, when it lowers itself in its dealings to the capacity of human frailty. [Col. 0318A] Neither the great nor the modest pleases you; neither the judge nor the friend. What if the same characteristics are now [Note: [Col. 0318D] Semler and Pamelius read “not”; Latinus: “For what if?”; Wouwer: “What of the fact that now?”] found in your god as well? We have already shown in his own little book that he too is a judge; and because he is a judge, necessarily severe; and because severe, correspondingly [Note: [Col. 0318D] Latinus reads “of course.”] savage—if indeed he is savage.

## CHAPTER XXVIII

Now, concerning his petty characteristics, acts of malice, and other marks, I too shall construct against Marcion rival *Antitheses*. If my God was ignorant that there was another above him, your god was equally altogether unaware that there was another below him. For, as that obscure Heraclitus says, [Note: [Col. 0318D] “That obscure Heraclitus.” That philosopher so affected obscurity that he was consequently called “the obscure” or *σκοτεινός* (*skoteinos*). Even when death was imminent he retained that style of speech. For when he suffered from water under the skin and dropsy, he asked the physicians whether by the skill of their art they could produce drought from rain. Laertius reports this in Book IX. LE PRIEUR.] [Col. 0318B] “The way upward and downward is the same.” In short, if your god had not been ignorant, he would have confronted mine from the beginning.

Sin and death, the devil himself as the author of sin, and every evil which [Note: [Col. 0318D] Latinus, Junius, and Semler read “every evil whom.”] my God permitted to exist—your god likewise permitted them, since he permitted my God to permit them.

Our God changed his sentences. So did yours. For he who took notice of the human race so late changed the sentence by which he had not taken notice of it for so long an age.

Our God repented of something. So did yours. For by at last turning his attention to man’s salvation, he repented of his previous disregard, a repentance owed to an evil deed. Neglect of human salvation will indeed be accounted an evil deed, corrected in your god only through repentance.

Our God commanded fraud—but fraud involving gold and silver. Yet as a human being is more precious than gold and [Col. 0318C] silver, so your god is the greater defrauder, since he snatches man from his Lord and Maker.

Our God demands an eye for an eye. But yours, by forbidding retaliation, makes the injury more likely to be repeated. For who will not strike again when he has not been struck back?

Our God did not know [Note: [Col. 0318D] Semler and Pamelius read “does not know.”] what sort of men he was choosing? Then neither would yours have chosen Judas the traitor [Note: [Col. 0318D] Semler and Pamelius insert “not,” and punctuate after “yours”: “Neither would yours. He would not have chosen Judas…”] if he had foreknown him.

Thus you also say that the Creator lied somewhere. A far greater lie is found in your Christ, whose body was not real.

Did my God’s savagery destroy many? Your god also certainly consigns to destruction those whom he does not save.

Did my God [Note: [Col. 0318D] “My” is absent from Franeker.] command someone to be killed? Yours wished himself to be killed, being no less a murderer against himself [Col. 0319A] than against the man [Note: [Col. 0319B] Semler reads “in him.”] by whom he wished to be killed.

But I shall prove to Marcion that his god has killed many. For he made a murderer, who will certainly perish—unless, perhaps, the people committed no offense against Christ. But the ready power of truth prefers few words. Falsehood will require many.

## CHAPTER XXIX

Moreover, I would also have cut down Marcion’s *Antitheses* themselves at close quarters, had their destruction by a more elaborate treatment been required for the defense of the Creator as both good and a judge, in accordance with the examples fitting God in each respect, as we have shown. But if both aspects—goodness and justice—constitute the worthy fullness of an omnipotent divinity, I can meanwhile, by a shorter method, have blunted those *Antitheses* which eagerly attempt, from differences in dispositions, laws, or mighty works, to distinguish [Col. 0319B] and thus estrange Christ from the Creator—as the supremely good from the judge, the gentle from the fierce, and the saving from the destructive.

For they unite the two all the more by placing them within those differences which are fitting to God. Remove Marcion’s title [Note: [Col. 0319B] “Remove Marcion’s title.” That man of Pontus had written the *Antitheses*, or a book under this title. [Col. 0319C] Since our author now believes that he has sufficiently refuted them, he says that they should be removed when he writes that the title is to be removed. LE PRIEUR.] and the aim and purpose of the work itself, and it supplies [Note: [Col. 0319B] Semler reads “to supply”; perhaps “would supply.” “It supplies” is Rhenanus’s conjecture, afterward adopted by Pamelius, Rigault, Franeker, Paris, and Venice. Junius reads “it will supply.”] nothing other than a demonstration of one and the same God, supremely good and a judge, because these two attributes belong together in a single God. For the very effort in those examples to set Christ against the Creator points rather toward their unity. So completely was there one and the same substance in their divinities [Note: [Col. 0320B] Rhenanus conjectures “of divinity.”]—both good and severe, in the same examples and in similar arguments—that he wished to display his goodness in the same matters in which he had previously manifested severity.

Nor will a temporal difference be surprising, [Note: [Col. 0320B] Semler and Pamelius read “was.”] if God was afterward gentler because circumstances had been tamed, whereas formerly he was more austere because they were untamed. Thus, through the *Antitheses*, it can more readily be shown that the Creator’s order was reformed, reflected anew, and restored by Christ rather than abolished—especially since [Note: [Col. 0320B] Pamelius and Semler omit “since.”] you separate your god from every harsher movement, and therefore, of course, from rivalry with the Creator.

For if this is so, how do the *Antitheses* demonstrate that he rivaled every individual manifestation of the Creator? I shall therefore recognize through them, even in this respect, my God as jealous: by his own right, with good and rational rivalry, he took advance care to bring to maturity those things of his which were more luxuriant in their beginnings. [Note: [Col. 0319C] “Who took advance care to bring his more tree-like things in their beginnings to maturity by good and rational rivalry.” “Who took advance care of his more tree-like things” means “his more robust things.” That is, he did not plant mere shoots, but trees already grown—and indeed more fully tree-like, that is, already large—which produce fruit much earlier or more quickly than shoots. RIGAULT.—An elegant metaphor. *Arbustiores*—as Rhenanus correctly noted in the margin of his first edition—appears to be used for “more luxuriant” or “more vigorous,” the metaphor being taken from luxuriantly growing trees. Pliny also uses the verb *arbustare*. Otherwise one might suppose, though incorrectly, that the reading should be *austeriores*, “more austere.” He is comparing the maturity of the New Testament with the harsh immaturity of the Old. PAMELIUS.]

Even his world will recognize its Creator’s *Antitheses* in the opposition of its elements, though it is regulated with the highest reason. Therefore, most thoughtless Marcion, you should have presented one god of light and another of darkness, so that you might more readily persuade us that there is one god of goodness and another of severity. But the antithesis will belong to him whose antithesis is also present in the world.
