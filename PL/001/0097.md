Whatever may be the true and genuine reading of our author, he teaches with the utmost certainty that, in Christ’s final judgment, the merits of each individual will be weighed and carefully examined. [Col. 0900D] For Christ himself will come with all the glory and majesty of divinity, as Tertullian says, and also with a convulsion of the whole world, by which it will indeed be overthrown; with the terror of the world; and with the lamentation of all unbelievers and criminals, though not of Christians (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 23, col. 25)—that is, of the faithful who have lived piously and virtuously. We have already discussed this final judgment elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 47, p. 42).

“But we are ridiculed by the pagans,” Tertullian continues, “for proclaiming that God”—whom he had previously called Christ—“will judge” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 47, col. 42). He judged, however, that this feeble mockery could be refuted in no more fitting way [Col. 0901A] than by citing their own doctrines: “For in like manner,” he says, “both poets and philosophers place a tribunal in the underworld” (*ibid.*). What that tribunal was he had explained in these words addressed to the pagans: “You laugh; let those demons also laugh with you. . . . Let them say this before the tribunal, if perhaps, in agreement with Plato and the poets, they have been allotted Minos and Rhadamanthus” (*ibid.*, ch. 23, col. 25). Since the tribunal of Minos and Rhadamanthus was very well known to everyone, he cites the poets collectively as witnesses, very many of whom, both Greek and Latin, had celebrated it in verse. Among the philosophers, however, he expressly names Plato, because he was readily acknowledged as the foremost of them all. Justin Martyr had argued in a similar way against the same pagans: [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0901B] [non-Latin text omitted]. “Plato likewise taught that Rhadamanthus and Minos would punish the unjust who came before them. We say that this very thing will indeed take place, but at the hands of Christ, when the same bodies are reunited with their souls and subjected to eternal torments” (Justin, *First Apology to Antoninus*, p. 57). Athenagoras later disputed against the pagans in the same way. The passages from Plato cited by him will be found in the *Gorgias* and the *Axiochus* (Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, p. 12; Plato, *Gorgias*, vol. I, p. 124, and *Axiochus*, vol. III, p. 371).

But if the pagans, as Tertullian rightly argues, believed these poets and philosophers, far greater credence ought to be given to our sacred writers, who, filled with the divine Spirit, consistently proclaim that Christ himself, when this age has run its course—that is, when it has been completed and brought to an end—will judge his worshippers for the recompense of eternal life and the profane for fire equally perpetual and unceasing, [Col. 0902A] after all who have died from the beginning have been raised, restored, and mustered for the settling of the account of either kind of merit, whether good or evil. He now says “for the settling of the account,” doubtless in the same sense in which we saw him say previously “for settling the account.” For we have seen him write elsewhere: “We, whose account is settled under God, the observer of all things”—that is, who are examined and judged by God, who knows all things—“make provision against eternal punishment from him” (*ibid.*, ch. 45, col. 39); that is, we take precautions against it, or foresee that the wicked will be afflicted with eternal punishment. Therefore, by innocence and integrity of conduct, we prepare to meet him, lest we suffer the eternal punishments and everlasting torments of the impious. Accordingly, in Christ’s final judgment, everyone will render an account of his good or evil merits; and when these have then been examined and the final [Col. 0902B] sentence pronounced, the wicked will be punished with eternal fire, while the righteous will be granted the reward of everlasting felicity. We must now investigate what our author teaches about this eternal fire and reward.

## ARTICLE IV. On the eternal fire by which wicked and impious people will be tormented; how Tertullian asserts its eternity and confirms it by examples of fiery rivers, perpetually burning mountains, and a person struck by lightning; and why that fire is called eternal

What Christians had learned from the sacred Scriptures they openly proclaimed: that impious and wicked people would be tormented with eternal fire after their death. “But if,” says Tertullian, “we threaten Gehenna, which is [Col. 0902C] an underground treasury of eternal fire reserved for punishment, we are likewise laughed to scorn” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 47, col. 42). So indeed our manuscripts, the edition of Rigault, and other later editions read; the older editions, however, have the masculine *subterraneus*, as though it must refer to the noun “treasury.” Yet it can also refer to the noun “Gehenna,” always with the same meaning. Our author therefore teaches that Gehenna is an inexhaustible treasury of eternal fire, by which criminal people will be tormented after this life. The pagans, however, rejected this doctrine just as they would an old woman’s tale, with laughter and unrestrained mockery.

Yet this Christian doctrine was true; otherwise the pagans themselves, by whom the Pyriphlegethon, as our author says (*ibid.*), was proclaimed to be a river [Col. 0902D] among the dead, were equally deserving of ridicule. Thus he, and Arnobius after him, turns back against them the weapon they had cast (Arnobius, *Against the Pagans*, book III, p. 52). These are Arnobius’ words: “Do you dare to ridicule us when we speak of Gehennas and certain inextinguishable fires into which we have learned that souls are cast by their enemies and foes? Does Plato, in the volume that he composed on the immortality of the soul, not name Acheron, Styx, Cocytus, and Pyriphlegethon, in which he asserts that souls are whirled about, submerged, and burned?” Plato is rightly cited by him, for in the *Phaedo* he described those four rivers of the underworld, sufficiently well known to everyone, which the pagans had invented (Plato, *Phaedo*, vol. I, p. 112 ff.). Pyriphlegethon, however, is called Phlegethon by Virgil and other Latin poets. [Col. 0903A] Since the matter is sufficiently well known, it will be enough to cite some of Virgil’s verses:

> Gods who hold dominion over souls, and silent shades,  
> And Chaos, and Phlegethon, places lying far and wide in the silence of night.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, book VI, v. 264 ff.)

And again afterward:

> Aeneas suddenly looks back, and beneath the cliff on his left  
> Sees broad walls, surrounded by a triple rampart;  
> Around them the Tartarean Phlegethon, a rushing river,  
> Rolls in torrents of flame and tumbles resounding stones.

(*Ibid.*, v. 543 ff.)

You may consult Prudentius and others, if you wish (Prudentius, *Hamartigenia*, near the end). But however the pagans may have invented or believed these things, they certainly could not ridicule Christians, who, instructed by their divine writers, asserted that profane people and those [Col. 0903B] stained with crimes would, after this life, be punished by the torment of unceasing fire (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 45, p. 497). In the first dissertation of this volume we heard Minucius Felix argue against the pagans in the same way (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 7, art. 3).

But the pagans asked how fire could possibly be eternal and never be extinguished. Tertullian therefore replies that, by divine power, this fire possesses “a supply of incorruptibility” (Tertullian, *ibid.*). Nor could the pagan philosophers deny this, since they recognized two kinds of fire that were plainly different. The first, they said, was ordinary and public terrestrial fire, which people use every day. The other [Col. 0903C] was mysterious and far less common, “drawing lightning bolts from heaven,” as our author says, or rising from the earth, by which the mountains—namely Etna and Vesuvius—are burned yet endure and are not consumed. For this fire, as he says, “does not consume what it burns, but restores while it expends”; hence the perpetually burning mountains remain. We have discussed this fire elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 7, art. 3, and *on Lactantius*, ch. 29, art. 7).

Only two observations therefore remain for us to make. First, these examples of lightning and burning mountains are not employed by Tertullian and our other writers as though their fire were identical with the eternal fire of the underworld, but “as testimony,” he says, “to eternal fire, and as an example of the perpetual judgment that sustains punishment” (Tertullian, passage cited, p. 497). [Col. 0903D] Thus he adduces these things merely as testimony to and an example of another fire, which, kindled by divine power and preserved forever, will burn the impious throughout eternity without ever consuming them.

Second, in the same passage he proposes another, though more obscure, example in these words: “He who is touched from heaven”—namely, by lightning—“is preserved, so that he can no longer be reduced to ashes by any fire” (*ibid.*). There is considerable variation and disagreement among interpreters in explaining these admittedly difficult words. Some think that they allude to an ancient pagan law that prohibited anyone struck by lightning from afterward being subjected to any punishment. For Jupiter was said to have punished such a person sufficiently, however impious he might have been. Others think that our [Col. 0904A] author had in view what Pliny records in these words: “It is unlawful for a person killed in this manner to be cremated; religious custom has handed down that he should be buried in the earth” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book II, ch. 54, p. 209). But what, I ask, does this have to do with Tertullian’s purpose, when he is attempting by this example to establish that the bodies of certain persons struck by lightning remain so preserved that they can no longer be reduced to ashes by any fire?

Consider, therefore, whether he may not rather be alluding to what Plutarch committed to writing: that almost everyone knew that bodies touched from heaven were neither cremated nor buried by many people, but were left surrounded by an enclosure, so that, being free from decay, they might remain perpetually visible (Plutarch, *Table Talk*, book IV, question 2, p. 665). Later he says: [non-Latin text omitted], [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0904B] [non-Latin text omitted]. “You know that the fire of lightning is called sacred and divine, because we see that the bodies of those touched from heaven resist decay for a long time” (*ibid.*, book VI, question 10, p. 685). Tertullian could have adduced this example, although it is not entirely perfect, in order to show and confirm that human bodies can be burned by eternal fire without ever being consumed by it.

If anyone wishes to know why Tertullian calls this fire “mysterious,” we shall answer that it was because he thought it hidden and concealed in the bowels of the earth. For in another book he explains both Plato’s view and his own concerning the place where the underworld is situated: “In the *Phaedo*, Plato describes the underworld as a kind of bosom of the earth, into which all the impurities of worldly filth flow together and, settling there, exhale, and, as though with the mire of their impurities, make the air breathed there denser and confined. We believe that the underworld is neither a bare cavity nor some open-air sewer of the world, but a vast space in a pit deep within the earth, and a depth hidden in its very bowels” (Tertullian, *On the Soul*, ch. 54). [Col. 0904C] If anyone should further inquire whence he derived this opinion, he will quickly hear Tertullian’s own answer in the same place: “For we read that Christ spent the three days of his death in the heart of the earth”—that is, “in an inward and interior recess, covered over within the earth itself and enclosed inside it, and itself lying above still lower abysses . . . so that there he might make the patriarchs and prophets partakers of himself. You have reason, therefore, to believe that the region of the underworld lies beneath the earth” (*ibid.*, ch. 53). There [Col. 0904D] he again commends Plato, in the *Phaedo* already cited by us, so as further to refute the pagans by the authority of the foremost of their own philosophers.

## ARTICLE V. On the eternal blessedness of the saints; where they reside; whether they enjoy it immediately after death, or await it until the final day of judgment in certain abodes; and how Tertullian proves that the doctrines of the Christian religion recounted thus far cannot be rejected by the pagans, and that Christians ought not to be harassed and slaughtered for defending them

In the same way that eternal punishment by fire awaits evil and wicked [Col. 0905A] people, says Tertullian, so an everlasting reward for good works awaits the good and righteous. For after our dead body has been raised to life again, “We shall be the same persons that we are now, and not others afterward,” he says (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, p. 520), because, as was stated above, this very same body of ours will rise again. Then, he continues, “the worshippers of God will remain with God forever, clothed over with the proper substance of eternity” (*ibid.*); that is, they will be clothed over with the gift of immortality, which will subsist and endure eternally. He spoke of “the proper substance of eternity” (*ibid.*, ch. 49, p. 528), by which righteous people will enjoy not only immortality but perpetual felicity—or, as he himself puts it, “eternal refreshment and divine delight” (*ibid.*, ch. 47, p. 515).

[Col. 0905B] Yet however certainly and truly he asserts this, we just as readily discover his doubt and wavering judgment concerning the place where their souls, released from their bodies, will be received until the final judgment. First, in his *Apologeticum*, he says: “We call Paradise a place of divine delight, destined to receive the spirits of the saints, separated from the knowledge of the common world by a kind of wall of that fiery Zone” (*ibid.*). Some think that he was there referring to these words of Genesis: “And he cast out Adam, and placed before the paradise of pleasure cherubim and a flaming, revolving sword to guard the way to the tree of life” (Genesis III, last verse). Others deny this. For they cannot persuade themselves that “a wall of the fiery Zone” signifies the same thing as “a flaming sword.” They therefore [Col. 0905C] think that another fire is denoted, serving as the boundary of that more delightful place. Yet this opinion ought to have been supported by at least some reason. Until they produce one, therefore, we do not see what prevents Tertullian from having called that place “a place of divine delight” which in the cited words of Genesis is called “the paradise of pleasure.” For what inconsistency is there if he thought that the fiery sword signified the fiery Zone? But we have anticipated some discussion of this passage of Tertullian in the preceding dissertation (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 22, art. 6).

A more certain judgment could be rendered on this matter if Tertullian’s short work *On Paradise*, which he testifies that he composed, were extant. For he himself referred us to it as follows: “You also have our short work *On Paradise*, in which we establish that every soul is sequestered in the underworld [Col. 0905D] until the day of the Lord” (Tertullian, *On the Soul*, ch. 55), that is, until the general and final judgment. But from these very few words certainly arises no small difficulty. For even though he might be said to have distinguished Paradise from the underworld, he nevertheless teaches most explicitly that every soul—of the righteous, of course, as well as of the impious—is sequestered and reserved in that underworld until the final judgment. These things, however, will doubtless seem obscure and difficult to understand to many. We must therefore investigate what he determined about them in his other surviving works.

In his book *On the Soul* he draws the following from the Gospel: “In the underworld the soul of a certain person”—namely, the notorious rich man—“suffers pain and is punished in flame, is tormented in [Col. 0906A] its tongue, and implores assistance from the finger of a happier soul” (*ibid.*, ch. 7), namely, that of the poor man Lazarus. Nor does he permit the objection that this is a parable. For the added name of Lazarus indicates a real event; and even if it were a parable, it is itself testimony to the truth. Then he added: “What, then, is that which, after separation from the body, is transferred to the lower regions; which is detained; which is reserved until the day of judgment; to which Christ also descended in dying? I suppose it was to the souls of the patriarchs. But why, if the soul is nothing beneath the earth? . . . in the prison, the temporary lodging of the underworld, in the fire, or in Abraham’s bosom” (*ibid.*). He therefore teaches that the underworld is a subterranean place, and that Abraham’s bosom is there; that the soul of the wicked rich man was tormented there; that Christ descended there after his death; and that the patriarchs were awaiting him there. [Col. 0906B] Later he says: “Thus the rich man in the underworld has a tongue, and the poor man a finger, and Abraham a bosom. By these outlines the souls of the martyrs beneath the altar are also understood” (*ibid.*, ch. 9). Do the souls of the martyrs therefore also dwell there, beneath the altar and in the same underworld? This will be explained somewhat more distinctly below.

Meanwhile, let us continue to hear him speak. He says that Christ descended into the lower parts of the earth “so that there he might make the patriarchs and prophets partakers of himself. You have reason, therefore, to believe that the region of the underworld lies beneath the earth, and to repel with an elbow those who quite arrogantly consider the souls of the faithful unworthy of the underworld—servants above their Lord and disciples above their Master—disdaining, perhaps, to receive in Abraham’s bosom the consolation of awaiting the resurrection” (*ibid.*, ch. 55). Do you see that the same underworld is still [Col. 0906C] called Abraham’s bosom and the habitation of the patriarchs and prophets, where souls receive consolation from the hope of resurrection?

Finally, toward the end of the same book he introduces someone objecting to him: “Then all souls are in the underworld.” He immediately answers: “Whether you wish it or not, both punishments and refreshments are already there. You have the poor man and the rich man” (*ibid.*, ch. 58). Since he says “every soul,” therefore, he makes no exception.

Nor does he profess this belief only in that book; in another he confirms it quite clearly with these words: “For the example of Lazarus proved that even now souls are tormented and comforted in the underworld, though naked and still exiles from the flesh” (the same author, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 17). Moreover, concerning the martyrs themselves, he did not hesitate to write the following [Col. 0906D] in the *Scorpiace*: “Who are those sons of Zebedee, such blessed victors, if not martyrs properly so called? For the victories belong to those whose struggles they are, and the struggles to those whose blood they are. Meanwhile, however, the souls of the martyrs rest peacefully beneath the altar, sustain their patience by confidence in vengeance, and, clothed in robes, assume the white brightness of glory, until others also complete the company of their glory” (the same author, *Scorpiace*, ch. 12). Was it not, therefore, Tertullian’s opinion that the souls of all dead people whatsoever, stripped of their bodies, are reserved until the final day of judgment in the underworld, in Paradise, in Abraham’s bosom, and beneath the altar—different names by which he doubtless appears to have designated the same place?

[Col. 0907A] But wait, I ask, a little longer, and restrain yourself somewhat. For presently you will hear him distinguish three places corresponding to the three classes of souls, or of dead people—namely, the impious, the righteous, and the martyrs—and distinguish the underworld from Abraham’s bosom. For he refutes Marcion’s error concerning the receptacles of those souls by the authority of sacred Scripture, “which,” he says, “distinguishes the poor man’s Abraham’s bosom from the underworld. For the underworld is one thing, as I think, and Abraham’s bosom another. Indeed, it says that a great gulf lies between those regions and prevents passage in either direction. Nor would the rich man have raised his eyes—and that from afar—unless he were looking upward, and from far below across that immense distance of height and depth. . . . Accordingly, I call that region Abraham’s bosom, even though [Col. 0907B] it is not heavenly; nevertheless it is higher than the underworld and will meanwhile provide refreshment to the souls of the righteous, until the consummation of all things settles the account of the resurrection of all with the fullness of reward. . . . Why may it not be understood that Abraham’s bosom is a certain temporary receptacle for the souls of the faithful, in which an image of the future is already sketched and a certain bright prospect of both judgments is discerned?” (the same author, *Against Marcion*, book IV, ch. 34).

Do you not now see how clearly he separates the underworld, where the souls of wicked people dwell and are tormented, from Abraham’s bosom, or the habitation of the righteous, where they, tasting beforehand the reward of eternal felicity, enjoy a temporary and preliminary delight and blessedness—or, as he calls it, refreshment? He asserts that this region, although it is not heavenly, is nevertheless far removed [Col. 0907C] from the underworld and situated much higher than it.

As for the souls of the martyrs, he appears to assign them a third place, namely Paradise itself. For these are his exact words concerning the souls of those martyrs: “How is it that the region of Paradise revealed to John in the Spirit, which lies beneath the altar, shows no souls with it other than those of martyrs? How is it that Perpetua, the most courageous martyr, in a revelation of Paradise on the day before her passion saw there only her fellow martyrs, unless it is because the sword that guards the gate of Paradise yields to none except those who have died in Christ, not in Adam? A new death for God, and an extraordinary one for Christ, is received in another and private lodging. Recognize, then, the difference between the pagan and the faithful person in death. If you fall for God, as the Paraclete admonishes, [Col. 0907D] not in soft fevers and beds, but in martyrdoms; if you take up your cross and follow the Lord, as he himself commanded, your blood is the whole key to Paradise” (the same author, *On the Soul*, ch. 55). Thus he indicates a third place, separate from both those already mentioned by us, which he calls the private lodging of the martyrs, of whom we shall speak below (*below*, ch. 15, art. 4).

Irenaeus had doubtless preceded him, and similarly explains his opinion concerning martyrs: “The Church,” he says, “in every place, because of the love it bears toward God, sends forward to the Father a multitude of martyrs in every age” (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, book IV, ch. 33, § 9, new ed., p. 272). But why, I ask you, to [Col. 0908A] the Father, unless so that they may obtain the reward of eternal felicity immediately after death?

Cyprian, Tertullian’s disciple, later subscribed to the opinion of both in these very words: “Blessed indeed are those among you who, journeying along the paths of glory, have already departed from the world and, having completed the course of courage and faith, have come to the embrace and kiss of the Lord, while the Lord himself rejoices” (Cyprian, letter 37, p. 73). Elsewhere he expressly affirms that martyrdom is a second baptism, which, as we depart from the world, immediately joins us to God. He then concludes his book with these words: “If persecution finds such a soldier of God, courage ready for battle cannot be overcome. Or if a summons comes first, the faith that was prepared for martyrdom will not be without reward. With no loss of time, the reward is rendered, with God as judge: in persecution the service, in peace the conscience, is crowned” (the same author, *Exhortation to Martyrdom*, p. 168; *ibid.*, p. 184). [Col. 0908B] He therefore did not think that the eternal glory and felicity of martyrs was postponed. Had he not also derived these things from his teacher and our author Tertullian, who similarly said that martyrdom is a second baptism by which martyrs are led directly to the divine throne? For he asserted that by unconquered patience and courage they are “brought forward for the experience of felicity, for the opportunity of a second immersion, for the ascent to the divine throne itself” (Tertullian, *On Patience*, p. 13). Elsewhere, as we shall see, he calls this second immersion a “second washing, by which all sins are forgiven and the fullness of God’s grace is purchased” (*below*, ch. 16, art. 3).

[Col. 0908C] But Tertullian’s opinion, like that of Irenaeus, appears not to have been the same concerning the souls of other righteous people. For Irenaeus handed down the following concerning them: After the death of the body, “They will depart,” he says, “to an invisible place appointed for them by God, and there they will remain until the resurrection, awaiting the resurrection, after which they will come into the sight of God” (Irenaeus, book V, ch. 31, p. 331). And do not be surprised, I ask you, if our Tertullian, like Irenaeus, remains uncertain and wavering in assigning dwelling places to souls released from the body, and does not always speak in the same way. For, as we have noted elsewhere, the opinions of other ancient Fathers of the Church were no more definite or consistent (*Admonition on Ambrose’s On the Good of Death*, vol. I, p. 385).

[Col. 0908D] Whatever our author’s opinion about these matters may have been, he rightly contends that it ought not to have been rejected by the pagans, whose belief, he says, “the Elysian Fields had preoccupied” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 47, p. 515), where they believed that the souls of the dead enjoyed delights. But since their opinion was not unlike the one he proposes, he says that theirs doubtless derived its origin from the Christian teaching. For that doctrine, and the other teachings of Christians, as was proved above (*above*, ch. 4, art. 4), are far older than the doctrines of the pagans. The pagans, therefore, had drawn their doctrines from the Christians, not the Christians from the pagans, and consequently ought to place no less faith in Christian teachings than in their own.

Moreover, even if that doctrine and all the other doctrines of the Christian religion that we have [Col. 0909A] reviewed thus far were entirely unlike and contrary to the opinions of pagan philosophers, among those philosophers, says Tertullian, they were called “products of the highest learning and distinguished intellects” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 49, p. 528)—or ought it rather to read “distinguishing marks of intellect,” that is, conspicuous signs and indications of intelligence? In any case, the sense will be exactly the same. Who, therefore, will not marvel that these most true teachings of Christians were called false by pagans who were not lacking in reason or intelligence, and, as our author further says, “foolish presumptions” (*On the Testimony of the Soul*, ch. 4)—that is, altogether vain and absurd divinations and conjectures concerning a doubtful matter?

Yet suppose, though it is utterly false, that they were wholly false: they were certainly harmful to no one; indeed, [Col. 0909B] they were useful and necessary to everyone. For all people are compelled to become better through fear of eternal punishment and hope of eternal life (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 49, p. 529). For whoever, as Athenagoras rightly teaches, is persuaded that the soul will perish together with the body, [non-Latin text omitted], dares every crime, [non-Latin text omitted] (Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, p. 39). But one who believes that, after being raised to life, he will render an account of all his sins at God’s final judgment is afraid to offend even in the smallest matters.

The pagans were therefore most unjust when they condemned Christians, the defenders of these doctrines, as though they were the most criminal of people and guilty of the gravest offenses. Our author therefore presses and assails them as follows: “But if in matters of this kind” [some editions add *error*, perhaps better as *in error*] “anything must indeed be judged worthy of laughter” or ridicule, Christians were not on that account to be most cruelly slaughtered [Col. 0909C] “with swords, fires, crosses, and beasts” (Tertullian, passage cited). Yet so far is there from being anything false underlying their doctrine that their altogether astonishing endurance amid so many and such terrible torments most invincibly demonstrated the truth of that doctrine and of the whole Christian religion, as has more than once been observed. But this is also plainly confirmed by the holy conduct, laws, and institutions of those same Christians, which must now be discussed.

## CHAPTER XII. It is shown who and what sort of people the Christians were, how they were distinguished from the pagans, and how much purer their conduct and how much better and holier their laws were than those of the pagans

[Col. 0909D]

### ARTICLE I. Who and what sort of people the Christians were in Tertullian’s time, how greatly they had multiplied, and how they were then distinguished from the pagans among whom they lived

Having explained the teachings of the Christian religion asserted by Tertullian, we must now investigate with him who and what sort of people their defenders, the Christians, were, and how they lived among the pagans. [Col. 0910A] It is therefore helpful to hear him speak about himself and the other followers of Christ: “We are a body,” he says (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 39, p. 431), just as the Apostle had previously written: “We, though many, are one body in Christ” (Epistle to the Romans XII, 5); that is, one society, congregation, and community composed of many people assembled together. If you ask how or on what basis they were assembled, he will answer you: “Through consciousness of religion” (Tertullian, passage cited); that is, because they were inwardly and most certainly convinced that the religion they all professed was true. In addition, they were joined “by unity of discipline” (*ibid.*), that is, by the one and the same, but most holy, body of teaching handed down by Christ and by the discipline of conduct. Finally, they were united “by a covenant of hope” (*ibid.*), that is, bound by covenant and associated [Col. 0910B] through the most certain hope of eternal life, which the true, sole, and omnipotent God promised them after this mortal life.

How greatly that Christian body had multiplied by the time of our Tertullian he himself certainly informs us (*ibid.*, ch. 1, p. 262). For at that time, as he relates (*ibid.*, ch. 37, p. 462), they had filled every place in the world: cities, islands, forts, towns, meeting places, military camps, tribes, municipal divisions, the forum, the senate, and the palace; and they had left the pagans nothing as their exclusive possession except their temples alone (*ibid.*, ch. 42, p. 491). They therefore mingled with them everywhere—in the forum, the provision market, the baths, shops, workshops, inns, fairs, and commercial dealings, which must be discussed below. Thus, dwelling together in mutual intermixture, they served as soldiers, worked the land, [Col. 0910C] engaged in commerce, and shared crafts and services through mutual dealings.

For this reason, in another book he addresses Scapula as follows: “What will you do with so many thousands”—the older editions read “ten thousand”—“people, so many men and women, of every sex, every age, and every rank, offering themselves to you? How many fires, how many swords will be needed? What will Carthage itself suffer when it is decimated by you, when each person recognizes among them his housemates; when he perhaps sees there men even of your own rank, matrons, all the foremost persons, and the relatives or friends of your friends?” (the same author, *To Scapula*, ch. IV). What more need be said? So great at that time was the number of Christians, not only in Carthage but everywhere throughout the world, that, “If so great a mass of people”—these are again [Col. 0910D] the words of Tertullian himself—“had broken away from you to some remote corner of the world, the loss of so many citizens, whatever their condition, would certainly have covered your dominion with shame; indeed, it would have punished it by the very abandonment. Without doubt you would have been terrified at your solitude, at the silence of affairs, and at a certain stupor as of a dead world; you would have asked whom you were to command. More enemies than citizens would have remained to you” (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 37, p. 463).

But we seem to hear someone whispering in our ears that there is some exaggeration by Tertullian here. For who, he will say, could ever be persuaded that the multitude of Christians was then so great? Yet with what effrontery, I ask, could this same Tertullian have asserted it so boldly in public books addressed not merely to Scapula alone, [Col. 0911A] the governor of Carthage, but to the Roman—or, if you prefer, African—governors, and even to the nations, that is, to all pagans, the bitterest enemies of Christians, unless it were entirely true? Did he not have to fear that, if convicted of falsehood, his case would collapse amid laughter and hisses? Yet if some obstinate person refuses to believe him, he surely will not refuse to credit Pliny the Younger, who had earlier written the following about those same Christians to Emperor Trajan: “Many people of every age, every rank, and both sexes are being summoned into danger, and will continue to be summoned. For the contagion of this superstition has spread not only through the cities, but also through villages and the countryside” (Pliny, letter 101, p. 376).

For this very reason, Tertullian further says, the pagans grieved that their number was so great and [Col. 0911B] publicly complained of it: “They cry out,” he says, “that the city is besieged; that Christians are in the countryside, the forts, and the islands; that people of every sex, age, condition, and now even rank are passing over to this name, and they grieve as though at a loss” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 1, p. 263). Will you still say, then, that this complaint of the pagans was invented or exaggerated by him? But in another book, addressing the pagans themselves, he repeats it in almost exactly the same words (the same author, *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 1).
