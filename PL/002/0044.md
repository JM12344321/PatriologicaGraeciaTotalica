Who would not wish us to take no thought for the soul’s food and the body’s clothing, except the one who had already provided these things for humankind [Col. 0432C] and, by thereafter supplying them, rightly forbids anxiety about them as though it rivaled his generosity—the one who furnished the very substance of the soul, which is more valuable than food, and fashioned the material of the body itself, which is more valuable than a tunic; whose ravens neither sow nor reap nor gather into storehouses, and yet are fed by him; whose lilies and grass neither weave nor spin, and yet are clothed by him; and in comparison with whose flowers even Solomon, most glorious though he was, was not more splendidly arrayed? Besides, nothing is so incoherent as that one should provide, while another commands people to feel secure [Note: [Col. 0432D] “Unconcerned,” Oberthür.] about his provision [Note: [Col. 0432D] *De praestantia.* Here *praestantia* means the same as *praestatio*, “provision” or “supply.” SEMLER.]—and that other, moreover, his detractor. Finally, if, as a detractor of the Creator, he does not wish people to concern themselves with such trifles, over which neither ravens nor lilies labor, because they are naturally beneath his notice owing to their worthlessness, the truth will become apparent shortly. Meanwhile, why does he accuse them of having little faith [Col. 0433A]—that is, little faith in whom? Is it the faith which they could not yet exhibit perfectly toward God, since they were only then learning about him under a veil [Note: [Col. 0433D] “Revealed,” Lat.], or the faith which under this very title they owed to the Creator, namely, that they should believe that he freely supplies these things to the human race and therefore should not [Note: [Col. 0433D] “Not,” Latinius.] be anxious about them?

For when he adds, “For the nations of the world seek after these things”—because, of course, they do not believe in God, the founder and provider [Note: [Col. 0433D] “Approver,” Semler.] of all things—he did not wish his hearers to be like the Gentiles, and so rebuked them for little faith in the same God in relation to whom he censured the Gentiles for unbelief [Note: [Col. 0433D] “Unbelievers,” Semler.]. Moreover, when he also adds, “But the Father knows that you need these things,” I shall first ask which father Christ wishes to be understood. If he indicates their Creator, he also confirms that he is good, since he knows [Note: [Col. 0433D] “May know,” codex Wouwer.] what his children need. But if he means another God, how does that God know that food and clothing are necessary for man [Col. 0433B], when he has supplied neither? For if he had known, he would have supplied them. But if he knows what things are necessary for man and nevertheless did not supply them, he failed to supply them either through malice or through weakness. Yet by declaring that these things are necessary for man, he has surely confirmed that they are good, for nothing evil is necessary. And he will no longer be a disparager of the Creator’s works and indulgences; thus I have settled the point I deferred above.

Moreover, if another both foresaw and supplies the things which he knows to be necessary for man, how does he himself promise them [Note: [Col. 0433D] “Promises,” Pamelius.]? Is he good at another’s expense? “For seek,” he says, “the kingdom of God, and these things will be added to you.” Surely by him. But if by him, what sort of person is he who will supply another’s property? If they are supplied by the Creator, to whom they also belong, who is this that promises another’s property? If these things are to be added [Note: [Col. 0433D] “Are added,” Oberthür.] to the kingdom, as goods to be bestowed in the second rank, the second rank belongs to him to whom the first also belongs [Col. 0433C]; the food and clothing belong to him whose kingdom it is. Thus the whole promise belongs to the Creator.

The coherence of parables and the correspondence of similitudes hold only if they refer [Note: [Col. 0433D] “To” is absent from Rigault and the Venetian edition.] to no other than the one whom they resemble in every respect [Note: [Col. 0434D] “Have come as equals,” another reading.]. We are servants [Note: [Col. 0434D] *Id* absent from Rigault and the Venetian edition.], for we have God as our Lord. We must gird up our loins, that is, be unencumbered by the hindrances of a trailing and entangled manner of life; likewise, we must have our lamps burning, that is, minds kindled by faith and shining with works of truth; and so await the Lord, that is, Christ. Returning from where? If from a wedding, he belongs to the Creator, to whom marriage belongs. If he does not belong to the Creator, not even Marcion himself would have gone to a wedding if invited, since he regards [Note: [Col. 0434D] “Fears,” Latinius.] his God as one who detests marriage. The parable therefore fails in the person of the Lord unless he is one to whom marriage is appropriate.

In the following parable too, the man is seriously mistaken who assigns to the person of the Creator that thief whose hour, if the householder knew it, he would not allow his house to be broken into [Col. 0434A] [Note: [Col. 0434D] “Dug out,” Paris edition.]. For how can the Creator be regarded as a thief when he is Lord of the whole man? No one steals or breaks into what is his own; rather, the thief is the one who descends upon another’s property and alienates man from his Lord. Moreover, since he shows us that the thief is the devil [Note: [Col. 0434D] “Will have shown,” another reading.], whose hour, even in the beginning, if man had known it, he would never have been broken into by him, he therefore commands us to be ready, because “at an hour when we do not suppose, the Son of Man will come”—not because he himself is the thief, but because he is the judge, namely, of those who have not prepared themselves or guarded against the thief. Therefore, if he himself is the Son of Man, I apprehend him as judge, and in the judge I uphold the Creator.

But if in this passage he presents the Creator’s Christ under the name “Son of Man” in order to foreshadow as a thief the one whose time of coming we do not know, you have it written above [Col. 0434B] that no one is a thief of his own property. There remains also this consideration: insofar as he impresses upon us that the Creator is to be feared, to that same extent, by advancing the Creator’s cause, he belongs to the Creator.

Accordingly, when Peter asked whether he had spoken the parable for them or also for everyone, he set forth for them and for all who would preside over the churches the analogy of stewards [Note: [Col. 0433D] The analogy of stewards. *Actor* means “agent” or “manager.” SEMLER.]. The one among them who treats his fellow servants well in the Lord’s absence [Note: [Col. 0433D] “The one among them who treats his fellow servants well in the Lord’s absence,” etc. Here the Author has translated in a general sense “treats well,” in place of Luke’s τοῦ διδόναι ἐν καιρῷ τὸ σιτομέτριον, that is, as the common Latin translator renders it [Col. 0434D], “that he may give them their measure of wheat at the proper time.” Although this applies to every kind of food, Clarius has nevertheless emended it much too freely here also to “allowance.” He ought therefore to have called it his own translation rather than an emendation of the common translator. PAMELIUS.] will, when the Lord returns, be placed over all his goods. But the one who acts otherwise will, when the Lord returns on a day he did not expect and at an hour he did not know—that Lord being, of course, the Son of Man, the Creator’s Christ, not a thief but a judge—be separated, and his portion will be assigned with the unbelievers.

Accordingly, either here too he confronts us with the Lord as judge and instructs us in submission to him; or, if he confronts us with the supremely good God, he now affirms that this God too is a judge, however unwilling the heretic may be. For they try to soften the meaning of this passage [Col. 0434C] when it is claimed for his God, as if it were an act of serenity and gentleness merely to separate a man and assign his portion with the unbelievers—as though the man had not been summoned so that, being returned to his own condition, this very thing should be done by a judicial sentence. Folly! What will be the fate of those separated? Will it not be the loss of salvation, since they will be separated from those who obtain salvation? What [Note: [Col. 0434D] “What thing,” Pamelius.] then will be the condition of the unbelievers? Will it not be damnation? Or, if the separated and unbelieving suffer nothing, then conversely the retained and faithful will equally obtain nothing. But if the retained and faithful obtain salvation, the separated and unbelieving must conversely lose it. This will be a judgment; and he who threatens it belongs to the Creator.

Whom else am I to understand as the one who beats servants with few or many stripes, and who demands from them in proportion to what he entrusted to them, except God the requiter? Whom is it fitting [Note: [Col. 0434D] “Teaches,” François and Pamelius.] for me to obey except the rewarder? [Col. 0435A] Your Christ proclaims, “I came to cast fire upon the earth”—that supremely good one, lord of no Gehenna, who shortly before had restrained his disciples from calling down fire upon a most inhospitable village. When did this Christ burn Sodom and Gomorrah with a fiery storm? When was it sung, “Fire will go [Note: [Col. 0435C] “Proceed,” Pamelius.] before him and burn up his enemies” (Ps. XCIII, 3)? When did he also threaten through Hosea, “I will send fire upon the cities of Judah” (Hos. VIII, 14) [Note: [Col. 0435C] “Judaea,” Semler.]? Or through Isaiah [Note: [Col. 0435C] Read “Jeremiah,” Junius; the explicit words occur at Jeremiah XV, 14.], “A fire has blazed forth from my indignation” (Isa. XXX, 27)?

Let him not lie. If he is not the one who also sent forth his voice from the burning bush, let him see [Note: [Col. 0435D] “You see,” Semler.] what fire he insists must be understood [Note: [Col. 0435D] “You insist,” Pamelius.]. Even if it is a figure, by the very fact that he takes from my element the imagery for his meaning, he is mine, since he uses what is mine. The likeness of fire will belong to him to whom its reality also belongs.

He himself will better interpret the nature of this fire [Col. 0435B] by adding, “Do you suppose that I came to send peace upon the earth? No, I tell you, but division.” [Note: [Col. 0435D] “A sword,” indeed, is what was written—in Marcion’s Gospel, that is, Luke’s Gospel adulterated by Marcion. Tertullian says at the beginning of this book that he appeals to this kind of Gospel in order to convict Marcion of madness from that very text. RIGAULT.] “A sword” indeed is what was written; [Note: [Col. 0435D] But Marcion emends it. For Luke said διαμερισμόν, “division.” RIGAULT.] but Marcion emends it [Note: [Col. 0435D] As though division were not also the work of a sword. It was in this sense that Matthew used μάχαιρον, “sword,” for what Luke called διαμερισμόν, “division,” so that Marcion’s emendation of Luke is now pointless. Epiphanius, however, does not mention it. RIGAULT.], as though division were not also the work of a sword. Therefore, the one who denied that he brought peace also threatens the fire of overthrow. As the battle is, so is the conflagration; as the sword is, so is the flame—neither being consistent with such a Lord.

Finally, he says: “Father will be divided against son, and son against father; mother against daughter, and daughter against mother; mother-in-law against daughter-in-law, and daughter-in-law against mother-in-law.” If the prophet’s trumpet sounded this conflict between relatives in these very words, I fear that Micah (Mic. VII, 6) may have proclaimed Marcion’s Christ. And for this reason he called them hypocrites: they knew how to discern the appearance of heaven and earth, but did not recognize [Col. 0435C] that time—the time, namely, in which he was fulfilling everything that had been foretold concerning them and teaching nothing different, and therefore ought to have been recognized. Besides, who could know the times of one who possessed no evidence by which he could be identified?

Rightly, too, does he reproach them for not judging what is just of themselves. Long ago he commanded this through Zechariah (Zech. VIII, 16): “Judge a just and peace-making judgment.” Through Jeremiah (Jer. XXII, 3): “Execute judgment and justice.” Through Isaiah (Isa. I, 17; V, 6): “Judge for the orphan and vindicate the widow”; he also charged the vineyard of Sorech [Col. 0436A] with having produced not judgment but an outcry. He, therefore, who had taught them to act according to a commandment was also demanding that they act according to their own judgment. He who had sown the commandment was also pressing for its abundant fruit.

And indeed, how absurd it would be for the one who was destroying the just God as judge to command people to judge justly! For they also interpret the judge who sends a man to prison and does not release him until even the last farthing has been paid as a figure of the Creator, for the purpose of disparaging him. I must answer this on the same footing: as often as the Creator’s severity is adduced, so often Christ belongs to him whose obedience he compels by fear.

## CHAPTER XXX

How did he settle once again the question concerning a cure performed on the Sabbath? [Note: [Col. 0435D] “Does not every one of you on the Sabbaths loose his ass,” etc. Epiphanius did not press this point—in which alone the Author differs from the others by reading “on the Sabbaths” instead of “on the Sabbath”—but rather, in Refutation 39, the words of Christ that follow: “But this daughter of Abraham, whom Satan bound.” From this he proves that Christ belongs to the same God to whom Abraham belongs. This is all the more so because, as he continues in Refutation 40, Marcion had also excised the statement near the end of this chapter of Luke, “When you see Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and all the prophets in the kingdom of God,” reading only, “When you see all the righteous in the kingdom of God.” Thus what the Author omitted, Epiphanius particularly presses. *Against Marcion.* PAMELIUS.] “Does not every one of you on the Sabbaths loose [Col. 0436B] his ass or his ox from the manger and lead it to water?” Therefore, by acting in accordance with the terms of the Law, he confirmed the Law rather than abolished it—the Law that commands no work to be done except what is done for every living being [Note: [Col. 0436D] “No work to be done except what is done for every living being.” We have already observed that the translation of the Septuagint followed by Tertullian can be understood in another way, one more consistent with the idiom of the Greek language and the meaning of the Hebrew text. See Chapter 12 of this book. EDITORS.], and how much more for a human being!

I insist that the coherence of the parables be recognized everywhere [Note: [Col. 0436C] “Recognize,” Latinius.]. “The kingdom of God,” he says, “is like a grain of mustard seed, which a man took and sowed in his garden.” Who is to be understood in the person of the man? Surely Christ, because although he is Marcion’s Christ, he is called the Son of Man—the one who received from the Father the seed of the kingdom, namely, the word of the Gospel, and sowed it in his garden, surely in the world, or suppose, for the present, in man. But since he said “in his own garden,” while neither the world nor man belongs to him but to the Creator, the one who sowed in his own property is therefore shown to be none other than [Note: [Col. 0436C] “Nor” omitted by Pamelius.] the Creator.

Or if, in order to escape this snare, they transfer the person of the man [Col. 0436C] from Christ to a human being who receives the seed of the kingdom and sows it in the garden of his own heart, the subject matter itself will still suit no one but the Creator. For how can the kingdom of the most gentle God be signified by a seed [Note: [Col. 0436C] “Be signified by a seed” is absent from Pamelius, but is present in the Wouwer codex.] which is followed even by the heat of judgment with its tearful harshness [Note: [Col. 0436D] “The judgment of heat and tearful severities follow,” codex Wouwer.]?

As for the next similitude, I certainly fear that perhaps it portends the kingdom of another God. For he compared it to leaven, not to unleavened bread, which is more familiar to the Creator. This conjecture too is fitting for those who beg for arguments. Accordingly, I too shall repel vanity with vanity [Col. 0437A], saying that fermentation also suits the Creator’s kingdom, because after it comes the oven or furnace of Gehenna.

How often does he still show himself to be judge, and in the judge the Creator! How often, surely, has he cast people out, and condemned them by rejecting them! As here too: “When the householder [Note: [Col. 0437D] “Head of the household,” Pamelius.] rises,” he says. Rises to what, unless to what Isaiah said (Isa. II, 19): “When he rises to shatter the earth”? “And shuts the door”—surely for the exclusion of the wicked. When they knock, he will answer them, “I do not know where you are from.” And when they in turn recount that they ate and drank in his presence and that he taught in their streets, he will add, “Depart from me, all you workers of iniquity; there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.” Where? Outside, of course, where they will be after being excluded by him and the door shut. Therefore punishment will come from the one by whom exclusion is effected as punishment, when they see the righteous entering the kingdom of God while they themselves [Col. 0437B] are kept outside.

Kept outside by whom? If by the Creator, who then will be inside, receiving the righteous into the kingdom? The good God. What business, then, has the Creator there, detaining outside for punishment those whom his adversary has excluded, when instead he ought to receive them himself, all the more to spite his adversary? Yet the one who will exclude the wicked must either know that the Creator will detain them for punishment, or not know it. Therefore, either they will be detained against his will, in which case he is inferior to the one who detains them and yields to him unwillingly; or, if he wills it to happen so, he himself has judged that it ought to be done so, and he will be no better than the Creator, since he himself is the author of the Creator’s ill repute.

Since these claims cannot stand on any rational basis—that one God is believed to punish and another to deliver—both judgment and kingdom will belong to one God; and since both belong to one, the one who also judges belongs to the Creator.

## CHAPTER XXXI

[Col. 0437C] What sort of people does he command to be invited to luncheon or dinner? Those whom he had indicated through Isaiah (Isa. LVIII, 7): [Note: [Col. 0437D] “Break your bread for the hungry, and the beggars,” etc. “Beggars,” where the Vulgate has “the needy,” or πτωχούς, “the poor.” PAMELIUS.] “Break your bread for the hungry, and bring the beggars and those without a roof into your house”—those, namely, who cannot repay the service of this humanity. If Christ forbids such repayment to be sought, while promising it in the resurrection, this is the pattern of the Creator, who is not pleased with those who love gifts and pursue repayment.

Consider [Note: [Col. 0437D] “I consider,” Pamelius.] also to which side the parable of the host is more applicable: “A certain man made a dinner and invited many.” Surely the preparation of the dinner represents the fullness of eternal life. I say first that strangers and those connected by no legal bond are not ordinarily invited to dinner; household members and friends certainly are invited more readily. It therefore belongs to the Creator to have invited those who belonged to the one who invited them—through Adam insofar as they were human beings, and [Col. 0437D] through the Fathers insofar as they were Jews. It does not belong to one to whom they belonged neither by nature nor by privilege.

Next, if the one who prepared the dinner sends to the guests, then on this ground too the dinner belongs to the Creator, who sent to remind the guests—those already invited beforehand through the Fathers, but now reminded through the prophets. It does not belong to one who sent no one to remind them and had done nothing beforehand to invite them, but who himself descended [Col. 0438A] suddenly: no sooner becoming known than inviting, no sooner inviting than compelling people to the banquet, making the hour of dining the same as the hour of invitation.

The invited guests excuse themselves. If the invitation came from another God, they do so deservedly, because they were invited suddenly. If not deservedly, then they were not invited suddenly. But if they were not invited suddenly, then they were invited by the Creator, by whom they had been invited long ago. For it was his invitation they had originally declined when they said to Aaron (Exod. XXXII, 1), “Make us gods to go before us,” and from that time onward (Isa. VI, 10), “Hearing with the ear and not hearing”—namely, the call of God. Most appositely to this parable he says through Jeremiah (Jer. XI, 40): “Hear my voice, and I will be your Lord and you will be my people; and you shall walk in all my ways, whatever I command you.” Behold God’s invitation. “And they did not hear,” he says [Col. 0438B], “and did not incline their ear.” Behold the people’s refusal. “But they departed after the things their evil heart desired.” “I bought a field, I purchased oxen, and I married a wife.”

And he further presses the point (Jer. VII, 24): “And I sent to you all my servants the prophets.” Here the Holy Spirit will be the one who reminds the guests, by day and before dawn. “And my people did not hear and did not incline their ears, but hardened their neck.” When this was reported to the householder, he became angry—well indeed that he became angry, for Marcion denies that his God is moved; by this too, then, he is mine—and commanded that a selection be made from the squares and streets of the city.

Let us see whether this is in the same sense as what he says again through Jeremiah (Jer. II, 31): “Have I become a wilderness to the house of Israel, or a land abandoned without cultivation?” That is: “Do I not have people whom I may choose, or a place from which [Col. 0438C] I may choose them?” “Because my people said, ‘We will not come to you.’” Accordingly, he sent to summon others, still from the same city.

Then, since there was still room, he commanded that people be gathered even from the roads and hedges—that is, us, now from the foreign nations—through that rivalry of which he speaks in Deuteronomy (Deut. XXXII, 20): “I will turn my face away from them and show what will happen to them in the last days,” that is, that others will possess their place; “because they are a perverse generation, children in whom there is no faith. They have provoked me to jealousy by what is no god and have provoked me to anger with their idols; and I will provoke them to jealousy by what is no nation; by a foolish nation I will provoke them to anger.” This refers, of course, to us, whose hope [Note: [Col. 0438D] “Appearance,” Latinius.] the Jews bear. [Note: [Col. 0437D] Concerning this dinner the Lord denies that they will taste it. This interpretation teaches that the Jews were rejected because of their obstinacy, while Christians were called to the dinner—or, as he explains it, to the fullness of heavenly life. LE PRIEUR.] The Lord denies that they will taste of that dinner. Zion was abandoned (Isa. I, 8) like a watchtower in a vineyard and a hut in a cucumber field, after she [Col. 0438D] had also refused the last invitation in Christ [Note: [Col. 0438D] “Christ,” accusative, Pamelius, François, Rigault, Paris edition.].

What feature of this sequence, when reviewed according to the Creator’s arrangements and prophecies, can suit one who possesses neither the sequence nor any arrangement harmonizing with the parable, but performed the whole work all at once? Or what will be his first call, and what his reminder in a second action? Some must first make excuses, and afterward others must have assembled. [Col. 0439A] But as matters now stand, he came to invite both groups simultaneously—those from the city and those from the hedges—contrary to the parable’s model. Nor can he now judge as disdainful those whom he never invited before and whom he is only now approaching.

Or if he judges that in the future they will despise the invitation, then he likewise foreshadows as future the selection of Gentiles in their place. Certainly he will come a second time for this purpose, to preach to the Gentiles! Yet if he is to come, I think it will not be to invite the guests still, but to seat them at the feast.

Meanwhile, you who interpret the invitation to this dinner [Note: [Col. 0439D] “To” is absent, Chacón would have it so.] as an invitation to a heavenly banquet of spiritual fullness and delight, remember that the earthly promises [Note: [Col. 0439D] Earthly promises of wine, oil, and grain, such as occur very frequently in the Old Testament. LE PRIEUR.] of wine, oil, grain, and the city itself are likewise interpreted spiritually by the Creator.

## CHAPTER XXXII

[Col. 0439B] Who searches for the lost sheep and the lost drachma? Is it not the one who lost them? And who lost them? Is it not the one who possessed them? Who, moreover, possessed them? Is it not the one to whom they belonged? If, therefore, man is the property of no one other than the Creator, he possessed man because man belonged to him. He who possessed him lost him. He who lost him sought him. He who sought him found him. He who found him rejoiced.

Thus the argument of both parables is inapplicable to one to whom neither the sheep nor the drachma—that is, man—belongs. For he did not lose because he did not possess; nor did he seek because he did not lose; nor did he find because he did not seek; nor did he rejoice because he did not find. Indeed [Note: [Col. 0439D] Rejoicing over a sinner’s repentance belongs to him. I do not see why Pamelius tortures himself so greatly in explaining this passage. For when he says from the Gospel context that the rejoicing belongs to the angels, and that therefore what was said of the angels is here attributed to God, he should take careful heed not to disrupt and divide the sequence of the discourse. For its order and meaning are straightforward: rejoicing over a sinner’s repentance belongs to the one who said—namely God—“I prefer the sinner’s repentance to his death.” LE PRIEUR.], rejoicing over the repentance of a sinner—that is, over the recovery of one who was lost—belongs to the one who long ago declared that he preferred the sinner’s repentance to his death.

## CHAPTER XXXIII

[Col. 0439C]

He himself makes clear which two masters he says cannot be served, because one must necessarily be offended and the other defended: he sets forth God and Mammon. Then, if you have no interpreter to tell you what he means by Mammon [Note: [Col. 0440D] What he means by Mammon: what the Punic speakers call *mammon* is called *lucrum*, “gain,” in Latin. Augustine, Sermon XXXV *On the Words of the Lord*. RIGAULT.], you can learn from Christ himself. For in accordance with the example of that servant who, when removed from his stewardship, provides relief for his master’s debtors by reducing [Note: [Col. 0439D] “He reduced,” Pamelius.] their promissory notes so that they may in turn support him, Christ admonishes us to secure for ourselves the assistance of worldly friendships. “And I,” he says, “tell you: make friends for yourselves from the Mammon of injustice”—that is, from money, the very thing with which that servant was dealing. For we all know that money is the author and ruler of the injustice of the whole age.

Seeing that the Pharisees’ greed was enslaved to money, he launched this saying like a javelin [Note: [Col. 0440D] “He launched this saying like a javelin”: he hurled it as a missile. The manuscripts variously spell the noun *amentum*, *admentum*, and *ammentum*. It derives from the Greek ἄμμα, “knot” or “fastening.” RIGAULT.—That is, he hurled it, sent it forth: the verb *amentare*, “to launch,” derives from *amentum*, meaning a strap. RHENANUS.]: “You cannot serve [Col. 0439D] God and Mammon.” Finally, the money-loving Pharisees mocked him because they understood, of course, that “Mammon” meant money [Col. 0440A]—lest anyone suppose that by Mammon the Creator is meant, and that Christ was recalling them from service to the Creator.

What then? Learn rather from this that Christ revealed one God. For he named two masters: God and Mammon, the Creator and money. “You cannot serve God”—surely the God whom they appeared to serve—“and Mammon,” to whom they were more truly devoted. But if he were presenting himself as the representative of another God, he would have disclosed not two masters but three. For the Creator too is Lord because he is God—indeed, more truly lord than Mammon, and more deserving of obedience because he is more truly lord. Why, then, after calling Mammon a master and coupling it with God, would he have kept silent about their true Lord, namely, the Creator? Or did he, by keeping silent about him, concede that the Creator ought to be served, since he denied only [Col. 0440B] that his own God and Mammon could both be served?

Thus, when he presents one God while intending to name the Creator—if he himself belonged to another—he has in fact named the Creator, whom he did not set forth as Lord alongside another God. And so this statement too will become clear: “If you have not proved faithful in unjust Mammon, who will entrust to you what is true?” He means unjust money, of course, not the Creator, whom even Marcion makes just. “And if you have not been found faithful in what is another’s, who will give you what is mine [Note: [Col. 0440D] “You are mine; who,” Semler.]?” What is unjust ought indeed to be alien to God’s servants. But how [Note: [Col. 0440D] “In another way,” Rhenanus.] was the Creator alien to the Pharisees, when he was the proper God of the Jewish nation?

If, therefore, these statements do not apply to the Creator but to Mammon, “who will entrust to you what is truer, and who will give you what is mine?” cannot have been spoken by one God about the grace of another [Col. 0440C]. It would have appeared to have been spoken in that way only if, by censuring them for unfaithfulness toward the Creator rather than toward Mammon, he had made, through mention of the Creator, a distinction involving another God—one who would not entrust his own truth to those unfaithful to the Creator. How, then, can he be regarded as belonging to another God if that other God is not introduced precisely so that he may be distinguished from the God under discussion by you?

Moreover, if the Pharisees, by justifying themselves before men, placed their hope of reward in man, he rebuked them in the same sense as the prophet Jeremiah (Jer. XVII, 5): [Note: [Col. 0440D] “Wretched is the man who places his hope,” etc. He should have said “cursed,” for the Greek is ἐπικατάρατος, “accursed.” LE PRIEUR.] “Wretched is the man who places his hope in man.” If he also adds, “But God knows your hearts,” he was recalling the power of that God who declared himself to be a lamp searching the kidneys and hearts. If he touches upon pride—“What is exalted among men is hateful to God”—he sets Isaiah before their eyes (Isa. II, 12): “For the day [Col. 0440D] of the Lord Sabaoth is against everyone who is insolent and proud, against everyone lofty and exalted; and they shall be humbled.”

I can now infer [Col. 0441A] why Marcion’s God remained hidden for so long an age. He was waiting, I suppose, until he had learned all these things from the Creator. He therefore continued learning until the time of John, and from then onward came forth to announce the kingdom of God, saying: [Note: [Col. 0441D] “The Law and the Prophets until John,” etc. The Author very frequently cited this passage against Marcion in this very book, because Marcion cited it on his own behalf. But Tertullian elegantly deduces that John’s advent too was foretold by the Creator, both here and elsewhere. What of the fact that Epiphanius, in Refutation 43, proves from it that the Creator God and the God of Christ are the same, since Christ names the Law and the Prophets, attributes no injustice to them, and thereby bears testimony to the same God? Epiphanius, moreover, translates “from that time” more clearly, for Luke’s Greek is ἀπὸ τότε, “from then,” whereas the Author more obscurely has [Col. 0442D] “from which time.” PAMELIUS.] “The Law and the Prophets were until John; from that time the kingdom of God is announced.”

This is as though we too did not recognize that John was established as a kind of boundary between the old and the new, at whom Judaism would cease and from whom Christianity would begin—not, however, so that the cessation of the Law and the Prophets and the inauguration of the Gospel, in which is the kingdom of God, namely Christ himself, were effected by a different power. For we have proved that the Creator foretold both that the old things would pass away and that new things would succeed them (Isa. XLIII, 19). If John is also shown to be the forerunner and preparer of the Lord’s ways—the Lord who would bring in the Gospel [Col. 0441B] and proclaim the kingdom of God—and if, from the very fact that John came, this will be the Christ who was to follow John as his forerunner; and if the old things ceased and the new began with John standing between them, it will be no wonder that the kingdom of God, which exists by the Creator’s arrangement, should be proved from some other source [Note: [Col. 0441D] “Whence,” Pamelius; “from whatever source,” Latinius.] rather than from the setting of the Law and the Prophets at John and the kingdom’s rising thereafter.

Let heaven and earth, then, pass away sooner—as also the Law and the Prophets—than one apex of the Lord’s words. “For the word,” says Isaiah (Isa. XL, 8), “of our God abides forever.” For since in Isaiah Christ himself—the Word, that is, and Spirit of the Creator—had already at that time proclaimed John as “the voice of one crying in the wilderness” to prepare the way of the Lord, and had foretold that he would come for this purpose, that from then onward the order of the Law and the Prophets should cease through fulfillment [Col. 0441C], not through destruction, and that the kingdom of God should be announced by Christ, he therefore added that the elements would pass away more readily than his words. In this way he also confirmed that what he had said concerning John had not passed away.
