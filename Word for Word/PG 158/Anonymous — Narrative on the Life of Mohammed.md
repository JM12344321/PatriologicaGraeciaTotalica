## PG 158.574

The thrice-accursed and most godless Mohammed—who among the Hagarenes subject to him is also called Machoumet, and is, among them, a great prophet and honoured equally with God—appeared in the days of Emperor Heraclius, born in some part of Ethiopia of Ethiopian parents. He grew up and advanced to this point, having progressed, in truth, toward the worse, in the following manner.

A certain monk called Gerasimos, an extreme Arian and Pneumatomachian [the OCR has a defective ending], as they say, originating from Isauria, was brought up both in Constantine’s [city, apparently Constantinople] and in the city of the Romans. Though often pressed by the pious to renounce Arianism and the other heresies by which he was held, he was not persuaded. Therefore, lest, though otherwise clever, he should be a stumbling-block of destruction to many, he was exiled by the Orthodox into the regions of Coele Syria.

There, living in a certain cave beside the imperial highway so that he might beg for his livelihood—for he was hated—and pursuing especially astronomy and the method of prognostication, he watched those passing by. On one day, among the other travellers, he saw also a young man, namely the aforesaid Machoumet, driving a few camels and coming down from Mesopotamia to trade at Jerusalem.

For this aforesaid Mohammed, having been orphaned while still very young [OCR: `νόος`, probably `νέος`], wandering from door to door, and nourished on scraps, was seen by a very wealthy widow named Fatouma. Taking him into her own house and bringing him up, she sent him each day to pasture her own camels. When he had become a man, the woman, having fallen in love with him, had sexual relations with him illicitly. When she was discovered, however, and wishing to escape the shame before the many, she openly made him her husband. Giving him some portion of her possessions, she sent him out for trade.

But she was troubled in another respect: because, being continually moon-struck [i.e., described as suffering seizures], he caused her no small disgrace. Hence she instructed him, wherever he might come, to seek and ask whether perhaps he could find some physician-healer able to free him from the affliction.

Accordingly, the aforesaid monk was sitting beside the road and, seeing Mohammed travelling, asked him from what city he came. After Mohammed in turn had asked him many things, including why he waited by the road—each simply questioning the other—Mohammed heard, along with the rest, that he also practised medical knowledge. He became as if greatly delighted and, having reported his own “moon-struck” condition, when he heard that the monk could free him from such an affliction, gave him some money for expenses and left, saying: “When I return from Jerusalem, I shall take you up with me to my own house in Mesopotamia, both for my own treatment and for much treatment of others, since the country is wholly lacking in physicians, so that you may collect money and live a relaxed and painless life.”

Having gone down to Jerusalem and conversed with some of the heretics—especially Jews and Nestorians, because he was very fond of learning—and indeed having encountered learned Jews,

he was taught by Jews monarchy [belief in one ruler/God], circumcision, the name of the prophets, and Abraham’s noble lineage; by the Pneumatomachians, warfare against the Spirit; by the Hemerobaptists, washing on every day, ostensibly for forgiveness of one’s own sins, as they wrongly interpreted Isaiah’s saying, “Wash yourselves and become clean”; and by Nestorians and Arians, that Christ is not true God but a great prophet who came for the salvation of the world. Having gathered these and other seeds of the impious from there, while still young and still a fire-worshipper, on his return he met two Jews who practised medicine and who said they were travelling with him to Mesopotamia. Becoming very glad, as about to be cured by them too, as they asserted, he continued the journey with them.

When they reached the place where he had found the aforesaid monk living beside the road, he took him up according to the agreement made with him, set him on the camel, and they continued their journey. On the road the monk instructed him thoroughly in Arianism, while the Hebrews, making circumcision a compact with him, induced Mohammed to choose their religion, provided only that he should be healed by them.

When they had arrived safely in his own homeland, Mesopotamia, Mohammed took the monk and the Jews and lodged them beside his own house, giving the monk separately a very small house near that same house in which to remain. But after many contentions arose among them—the monk with the Jews, and they again against him—each side insisting that Mohammed had been taught its own religion, the monk wished to part from Mohammed.

When, however, he was begged to stay and to do whatever he himself should say, the monk made a calculation [*thematíon*: possibly a horoscope/cast] concerning Mohammed; and, finding in it that Mohammed would become great and increase if he undertook some heresy and religion, he summoned Mohammed and said to him: “If you listen to my counsel, you will very soon become great.” When Mohammed agreed to obey, he said to him: “Whenever, falling to the ground, you are moon-struck, when you get up say: ‘Seeing the angel Gabriel coming toward me and speaking with me, unable to look upon the brightness of his face, I fall to the ground, as you see.’”

And the monk, seeing that the local people were altogether barbarous and superstitious and ready to receive whatever foolish things he had devised to say to them, said to those present and seeing the possessed man: “Observe what is happening: Mohammed, seeing God and the angels, becomes inspired.” When this man got up, he said, according to the monk’s instruction, that he fell because he saw an angel. The monk called him a prophet, deceiving those who heard.

When Mohammed’s wife also was distressed because he might consume all her wealth, the monk, making his defense to her, said: “Do not be troubled, because Machoumet is a great prophet.” Hearing and believing this, as a woman [the author’s misogynistic aside], she admonished and proclaimed it to the other women: that Mohammed was a great prophet. The deception then passed from the women to the men; and, because they were barbarians and very ignorant, they believed that the aforesaid man was a prophet of God.

When the monk saw his design making progress, he thought it necessary to give them also a faith and a law, supposedly for the establishment of Arianism and the other heresies for which he had been exiled. Sitting down, he wrote a book, called the Korran [Qur’an], which is a law of God, sowing into it all his own impiety: teaching that God has neither Word nor Spirit, and that Christ is not God but a great prophet; and he composed very many other foolish things. Giving it to his own disciple Mohammed, he declared to those fools that this book had come down from heaven to Machoumet, from the angel Gabriel, while he was asleep. They believed that it was so, as the monk had secured matters concerning the new law.

But the aforesaid Jews, displeased with Mohammed and unwilling to heal him because he had followed the monk’s prescriptions rather than their religion, [Mohammed], wishing to satisfy them too, adopted from Jewish customs both circumcision and calling only the prophets by name [the wording is corrupt/ambiguous]; and concerning what had been written by Moses and the other prophets, he did what seemed good to the monk.

When these things had happened and the local people had believed Mohammed’s new law, they were without a governing authority; at the monk’s suggestion they made Mohammed their ruler, so that he might be at once lawgiver, prophet, and king. Then, having formed a military force, he plundered the surrounding towns.

When this was reported to Emperor Heraclius, he sent an army against him; and when the Romans came there, they killed Mohammed’s attendants wholesale. He himself fled with a few persons and sent an embassy to the emperor, calling himself the emperor’s slave and asking that the emperor permit him to rest in one region of Ethiopia, while paying tribute to the emperor. Persuaded, the emperor permitted him.

After Heraclius’s death, however, when not a few disturbances had occurred in the Roman empire, Mohammed found freedom and plundered Syria. Then, rising from little—or from nothing—to much, through our sins, he destroyed the greater part of the Roman empire. After him his successors completely ruined, destroyed, and devastated the remainder. This, in summary, is all that has been said concerning him—the whole truth [the author’s polemical claim]. End.
