> Hear, house [Col. 0617A] of David: [Note: [Col. 0617D] “No small contest for you.” The passage of Isaiah is chapter 7; but for this Latin interpretation to agree with the Greek and also with the text of the Vulgate, it must be read: “Is a contest with men a small thing for you?” and then there should follow: “Because God presents a contest”; that is, because you contend even against God, you are troublesome even to God. Symmachus said: “Is it not enough for you to cause trouble to men? Therefore God himself will give you a sign: [Note: [Col. 0617D] “Behold, a virgin shall conceive.” In the corrupted Greek version it is νεᾶνις, “young woman,” not παρθένος, “virgin,” as in the version of the Seventy. LE PR.—Tertullian consistently reads it thus everywhere, both a little below and in the cited passage, and also in the book *On the Flesh of Christ*. We have therefore added “in her womb,” especially since it is also read in both passages cited immediately below from St. Cyprian. We discuss this more fully in our notes there on Epistle 6, no. 16, and in the book *Against the Jews*. PAM.] Behold, a virgin shall conceive in her womb and bear a son, and you shall call his name Emmanuel, which is interpreted: God with us. He shall eat butter and honey. For before the infant knows how to call father or mother, he shall receive the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against the king of the Assyrians. (Isa. 7:13–16)

And so the Jews say: “Let us challenge this proclamation of Isaiah and make a comparison, to see whether the Christ who has now come fits, first, the name that Isaiah proclaimed and, secondly, the distinguishing marks that he announced concerning him. Isaiah indeed proclaims that he must be called Emmanuel, and thereafter that he will take the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against the king of the Assyrians. But,” they say, “this man who came [Col. 0617B] was neither brought forth under such a name nor engaged in warfare.”

We, on the contrary, have thought that they should be admonished to examine the connected statements of this chapter as well. For the interpretation of “Emmanuel”—“God with us”—is also appended, so that you may consider not merely the sound of the name [Note: [Col. 0617C] “Expect,” Rhen., Oberth.], but also its meaning. For its Hebrew sound, which is *Emmanuel*, belongs to its own nation; but its meaning, which is “God with us,” is made common to all through translation. [Note: [Col. 0618C] “But its meaning, which is ‘God with us,’ etc.” According to Semler, these words are written in the margin of the Paterniacensis codex, but in such a way that they appeared to belong in the text. Semler nevertheless struck them out, although he found them in Pam., Rhen., and Rigalt. EDD.]

I ask, [Note: [Col. 0617C] “Therefore,” Seml.] then, whether this expression, “God with us,” which is *Emmanuel*, has been current in reference to Christ from the time when Christ shone forth. And I think you will by no means deny it. For those from Judaism who believe in Christ, whenever they wish to say “Emmanuel” after coming to believe in him, signify that God is with us. And [Col. 0617C] thus it is established that the one who was proclaimed as Emmanuel has already come, because what “Emmanuel” signifies has come—that is, “God with us.”

They are likewise misled by the sound of the name [Col. 0618A] when they understand “the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against the king of the Assyrians” as though these words foretold Christ as a warrior, failing to notice what Scripture places first:

> For before the boy knows how to call father or mother, he shall receive the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against the king of the Assyrians.

You must first examine [Note: [Col. 0618C] “For. Let us examine,” Jun.] the indication of his age: could this age present Christ even as a grown man, much less as a commander? The infant, of course, would summon men to arms by wailing and give the signal for battle not with a trumpet [Note: [Col. 0618D] “But with a rattle.” A *crepitacillum*, “little rattle,” is one of children’s toys; it is a diminutive form derived from *crepitaculum*, just as *specillum* is derived from *speculum*. He aptly uses the diminutive when speaking of a tiny infant. RHEN.], but with a baby-rattle; he would mark out the enemy not from horseback or from a wall, but from the back or neck of his nurse and bearer; and thus he would subdue Damascus and Samaria in place of breasts. It would be another matter if among you infants burst forth into battle—after first being anointed, I suppose, at the sun [Col. 0618B] [Note: [Col. 0618C] “At the seat,” Jun., Seml., Oberth.] [Note: [Col. 0618D] “At the sun.” *Solium* (“seat” or “bath”) is more pleasing to Semler, who interprets it as πυέλος, “a vessel in which people were anointed while seated.” EDD.], then armed with swaddling clothes and paid their wages in butter—infants who know how to wield a lance before they know how to lacerate. [Note: [Col. 0618D] “To wield a lance before they know how to lacerate.” Infants made irritable by teething lacerate breasts while sucking. RIG.]

But indeed, if nature nowhere permits this—[Note: [Col. 0618D] “To serve as a soldier before making a man.” Before puberty one is scarcely ever described as a soldier; and nature makes a man when it shows him to be mature. Tertullian expresses the same thought in book III *Against Marcion* as follows: “Nature nowhere permits this: to serve as a soldier before living.” For life makes the infant a man. RIG.] to serve as a soldier before becoming a man, to receive the might of Damascus before knowing one’s father—it follows that the declaration must be understood figuratively.

“But,” they say, “nature does not allow a virgin to give birth either, and yet the prophet must be believed.” Quite rightly. For he laid the foundation for belief in an incredible event by saying that it would be a sign:

> Therefore, he says, a sign shall be given to you: Behold, a virgin shall conceive in her womb and bear a son.

But a sign from God would not have appeared to be a sign unless it had been some extraordinary novelty. [Note: [Col. 0618D] “Finally, if at any time, in order to cast some people down.” More briefly and readily in *Against Marcion* III: “Finally, even the Jews, if at any time they dare to lie in order to cast us down, saying that it was not a virgin,” etc. RIG.] Finally, if at any time you are eager to draw some people away from this divine proclamation, or to turn aside individual simple folk, you dare to lie, claiming that Scripture says not that a virgin but that a young woman would conceive and give birth. Here too you are refuted, because an everyday occurrence—the pregnancy [Col. 0619A] and childbirth of a young woman—could not appear to be any sign at all.

A virgin mother, therefore, is rightly believed to have been set before us as a sign, but not likewise an infant warrior. For the rationale of the sign does not apply here also [Note: [Col. 0619C] “Not likewise? And here,” Rhen., Oberth.]; [Note: [Col. 0619C] “Applies. But the same.”] rather, once the sign of the new birth has been recorded, a different sequence concerning the infant is thereafter set forth: he is to eat honey and butter. Nor, certainly, is this a sign [Note: [Col. 0619C] “And this certainly is a sign of infancy,” Par. But that he would receive the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against the king of the Assyrians—this is the wondrous sign.

Observe the measure of his age and seek the meaning of the proclamation; rather, yield to the truth which you refuse to believe. Then the prophecy is understood just as clearly as it is declared fulfilled. [Note: [Col. 0619C] *Renuntiatur expuncta*, “is declared fulfilled”—that is, fulfilled by the event. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0619C] “The prophecy was declared, just as it is believed to have been fulfilled,” Oberth., Rhen. “Then … when,” Jun.]

> And Judah shall stretch toward Jerusalem [Note: [Col. 0620C] “Israel stretched forward,” Rhen., Oberth.], and shall gather all the strength of the surrounding peoples, gold and silver. (Zech. 14:14)

For David also spoke of this gift of gold:

> And there shall be given to him from the gold of Arabia. (Ps. 71:15)

And again:

> The kings of the Arabs and of Sheba shall bring him gifts. (Ibid., 10)

[Note: [Col. 0619D] “For the East also generally had Magi as kings.” It cannot be inferred from this passage that the Persian kings were Magi, but rather that they generally had Magi; for since they were instructed in the teachings of the Magi, they retained something from them. Those called Magi among the Persians were the tutors of kings, as Cicero testifies [Col. 0620C] in book III *On the Nature of the Gods*, and Apuleius in the *Apology*; and according to the same Apuleius, *Magus* is the same as “priest” in the Persian language. For the East also generally had Magi as kings, and Damascus was formerly reckoned part of Arabia, before it was transferred to Syrophoenicia when the Syrian regions were distinguished. Christ then received its might [Col. 0619C] by receiving its insignia, namely gold and perfumes.

The spoils of Samaria, moreover, were the Magi themselves. After recognizing him, honoring him with gifts, and worshiping him on bended knee as Lord [Note: [Col. 0620C] “God,” Fran.] and king, under the testimony of the star as indicator and guide [Note: [Col. 0620C] “Of the star that judged and indicated,” Rhen., Oberth.], they were made the spoils of Samaria—that is, of idolatry—by coming to believe in Christ. For he designated idolatry by the name “Samaria,” since Samaria was infamous for the idolatry by which it had then revolted from God under King Jeroboam.

[Note: [Col. 0620D] “For neither is this new to the divine Scriptures.” In book III *Against Marcion*: “For neither is it new for the Creator to employ a metaphor figuratively.” RIG.]

For neither is it new to the divine Scriptures to use the transference of names figuratively, by comparing crimes. Thus he calls your rulers “rulers of Sodom” (Isa. 1:10), and calls your people “the people of Gomorrah” (ibid.), although those cities had long since been destroyed. Elsewhere, too, he says through the prophet to the people Israel:

> Your father was an Amorite, and your mother a Hittite. (Ezek. 16:3)

They had not been born from the stock of those peoples, but were so named because of similar impiety—although through the prophet Isaiah he had at another time called them his own sons:

> I have begotten sons and exalted them. (Isa. 1:2)

[Col. 0620B]

[Note: [Col. 0620D] “Thus Egypt too is sometimes understood as the whole world.” He perhaps alludes to Isaiah chapter 19, in which, although most statements concern Egypt, some are nevertheless interpreted not only by the ancients but also by the Jews as referring to the world enveloped in Egyptian darkness, and specifically the statement concerning the Church dispersed throughout the whole world: “On that day there shall be an altar of the Lord in the midst of the land of Egypt.” PAM.]

Thus “Egypt” too (Isa. 19:1) is sometimes understood in his writings as the whole world, under the designation of superstition and a curse.

[Note: [Col. 0620D] “Thus Babylon too.” The marginal annotation *Babylon Rome* should be struck out. It was undoubtedly added by enemies of the Roman Church, since those who are not ashamed to twist this passage against it fail to see that it concerns not the Church but the city of Rome, and Rome at the time when, as the vanquisher of the saints, it was stirring up persecution against Christians through its high priests—that is, the pontiffs of its idols. PAM.]

Thus “Babylon” too (Rev. 17:5), in our John, is a figure of the city of Rome, which is correspondingly great, proud in its dominion, and a vanquisher of the saints. In this way, therefore, he also gave the Magi the designation “Samaritans”; and they were despoiled of what they had shared with the Samaritans—idolatry, as we have said.

“Against the king of the Assyrians” means against the devil, who thinks that he reigns for this purpose: to cast the saints down from the religion of God.

This interpretation of ours will also be assisted by the fact that elsewhere the Scriptures designate Christ as a warrior because of the names of certain weapons and words of this kind. But the Jews will be refuted [Col. 0620C] by comparison with the remaining senses. David says:

> Gird your sword upon your thigh. (Ps. 44:4)

But what do you read immediately before this concerning [Col. 0621A] Christ [Note: [Col. 0621C] Oberth. and Seml. add Ὡραῖος τῷ κάλλει, “Beautiful in comeliness.”]?

> Comely in beauty above the sons of men; grace has been poured upon your lips. (Ibid., 3)

It would be exceedingly absurd if, while girding someone with a sword for war, he flattered him concerning the comeliness of his beauty and the grace of his lips. Continuing about him, he said:

> Advance, prosper, and reign. [Note: [Col. 0621C] “And prosper, hasten and reign,” Fran. “And prosper and reign,” Jun.] (Ibid., 5)

And he added:

> Because of your gentleness and justice. (Ibid.)

[Note: [Col. 0621C] “For who will work with the sword and not practice things contrary to gentleness and justice?” Tertullian’s writings scarcely permit military service to Christians. In chapter 3 of *On Patience*, he says that the Lord’s patience was wounded in Malchus, and that the Lord thereby cursed the works of the sword for the future. Likewise in *On the Crown*, he says that the sword was not necessary even for the Lord’s defense. And generally, in *On Idolatry*: “How,” he asks, “will he serve as a soldier even in peacetime without the sword that the Lord took away?” RIG.—The author everywhere forbids military service to all Christians, as was observed before me. St. Jerome writes to Gerontia: “At that time it was said to warriors, ‘Gird your sword upon your thigh, most mighty one’; now Peter hears, ‘Put your sword into its sheath.’” Yet although many Christians and many martyrs were commanders of armies, that fact is nevertheless condemned by this most ancient teacher and also by others. In the letter that Emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote from Germany to the Senate on behalf of the Christians, he says that warfare was forbidden to them: διὰ τὸ ἐχθρὸν εἶναι τὸ τοιοῦτο αὐτοῖς, διὰ τὸν θεόν, ὃν φοροῦσι κατὰ συνείδησιν, “because such a thing was opposed to them on account of the God whom they bear within according to conscience.” This letter is found in Justin Martyr after *Apology* II. I can offer no other explanation than that military service at that time was for the most part bound up with pagan rites and impieties and was a workshop of crimes. The martyrs and Christians who distinguished themselves under arms, however, emerged uncontaminated by that mire. [Col. 0622C] LE PR.]

For who will work with the sword [Note: [Col. 0621C] “Who will gird on the sword?” Rhen., Oberth.] and not practice things contrary to gentleness and justice—that is, deceit, harshness, and injustice, the proper business of warfare?

Let us therefore see whether that sword is another sword, whose operation is also another: [Note: [Col. 0622C] “That is, the divine word of God.” In the passage very frequently cited, he inserts the actual text of the Apocalypse of the apostle [Col. 0622D] John, to which he alludes here (Rev. 1). In accordance with it, omitting *ut* as superfluous, we also read: “two-edged through the two Testaments.” LE PR.] namely, the divine word of God, two-edged [Note: [Col. 0621C] “It,” Jun.] [Note: [Col. 0621C] Rhen. and Oberth. add “as.”] (Heb. 4:12) through the two Testaments, the old law and the new law, sharpened by the equity of its wisdom and repaying each person according to his deed.

It was therefore permissible [Note: [Col. 0621C] “It is pleasing,” the same editors.] [Col. 0621B] for the Christ of God in the Psalms to be figuratively girded with the sword of God’s word, without reference to warfare. The aforesaid comeliness together with the grace of his lips appropriately belongs to him. He was then being girded upon his thigh in David when he was announced as destined to come to earth by the decree of God the Father:

> And the greatness of your right hand, he says, shall lead you forth (Ps. 44:5),

that is, the power of spiritual grace, through which the knowledge of Christ is led forth.

> Your arrows, he says, are sharp (ibid., 6),

meaning, certainly, the flying [Note: [Col. 0621C] “Everywhere,” the same editors.] precepts of God, threatening the arraignment of every person’s heart [Note: [Col. 0622D] “The arraignment of the heart.” This signifies the force of spiritual grace, which pierces the conscience of every believer with the arrows of the divine precepts. RIG.], pricking and piercing every conscience. [Note: [Col. 0621C] “Both of the one pricking and of the one piercing,” Rhen.] [Note: [Col. 0621C] “The conscience. For the peoples,” Rhen., Oberth.]

> Peoples shall fall beneath you,

certainly by worshiping you.

Thus Christ is mighty in war and armed; thus he shall receive [Note: [Col. 0621C] “Shall take,” Pam., Rhen., Oberth.] the spoils not only of Samaria but of all nations. Recognize [Col. 0621C] the figurative spoils of the one whose allegorical weapons you have learned. [Col. 0622A] And thus the Christ [Note: [Col. 0622C] Jun. adds “was.”] who came was so far from being [Note: [Col. 0622C] “Insofar as,” Lat.] a warrior, precisely because he was not proclaimed as such by Isaiah.

“But,” they say, “if the Christ who is believed to be coming is not called Jesus [Note: [Col. 0622C] “Is,” Oberth., Seml. “Nor indeed is he believed by the Jews to be Jesus,” Seml.], why is the one who came called Jesus Christ?”

Both names will be established in the Christ of God, in whom the name Jesus is also found. Learn the manner of your error. When Auses son of Nave was appointed Moses’ successor, he was certainly transferred [Note: [Col. 0622C] “The manner, when … Auses son of Nare was appointed, he was certainly transferred,” Oberth., Seml.] from his former name and began to be called Jesus. [Note: [Col. 0622D] “And began to be called Jesus.” Thus from the manuscripts.… He alludes to what is found in Num. 13:17: “And Moses called Osee, the son of Nun, Josue.” … St. Augustine translated this from the Greek version in book XVI *Against Faustus*, chapter 19: “And Moses surnamed Ausen, son of Nave, Jesus.” PAM.]

“Certainly,” you say. [Note: [Col. 0622C] “You say, this,” Oberth.]

We declare that this was first a figure of the one to come. For Jesus Christ was going to lead a second people—namely us, the nations who formerly dwelt in the wilderness of the world [Note: [Col. 0622C] “In the world, in the wilderness,” Oberth., Seml. “In the wilderness-world,” Par.]—into the land of promise, flowing with milk and honey, that is, into possession of eternal life [Note: [Col. 0622C] “Of the promise,” Seml.], than which nothing [Col. 0622B] is sweeter. And this [Note: [Col. 0622C] “Into which,” Rhen., Oberth. “Which,” Wouw codex.] was to come about not through Moses—that is, not through the discipline of the law—but through Jesus, that is, through the grace of the new law, after we had been circumcised by a blade of stone, that is, by Christ’s precepts. For Christ is the rock (1 Cor. 10:4), proclaimed in many manners and figures.

Therefore the man who was being prepared as an image of this mystery was also inaugurated with a figure of the Lord’s name, so that he might be called Jesus. For the one who spoke to Moses was himself the Son of God, who was also always seen. [Note: [Col. 0622D] “Who was also always seen.” This too appears to belong among Tertullian’s errors, as though he held the same view as the Arian Maximinus—that the Son of God had always been seen by men—against whom St. Augustine argues at length in book III, chapter 26. Moreover, what follows, “For no one has ever seen God the Father and lived,” occurs in entirely the same way in the book *Against Praxeas*. PAM.] For no one has ever seen God the Father and lived (Exod. 33:20). It is therefore established that the Son of God himself spoke to Moses and said to the people:

> [Note: [Col. 0622D] “Behold, I send an angel.” We have added “I” from the manuscripts, [Col. 0623D] here and later, for it is read in most Greek, Hebrew, and Latin codices. PAM.] Behold, I send my angel before your face—that is, before the people—to guard you on the way and lead you [Col. 0622C] into the land which I have prepared for you. For Jesus, not Moses, was to lead the people into the land of promise. > Behold, I send my angel before your face [Note: [Col. 0623D] “Behold, I send my angel before your face.” In the prophet Malachi it is “before my face,” πρὸ προσώπου μου. Nor is it new for the Holy Spirit to call those whom God has set over the ministry of his power “angels.” For the same [Note: [Col. 0623C] “He says,” Seml. “For, he says,” Pam., Oberth.] John was called not only the angel of Christ [Col. 0623B] but also a lamp shining before Christ. David proclaims:

> For I have prepared a lamp for my Christ. (Ps. 131:17)

Therefore Christ himself, coming to fulfill the prophets, says to the Jews:

> He was, he says, a burning and shining lamp. (John 5:35)

For he not only prepared his ways in the wilderness (John 1:23), but, by pointing out the Lamb of God (ibid., 29), he also illuminated the minds of men through his proclamation, so that they might understand him to be the lamb whom Moses had announced [Note: [Col. 0623C] “Had named,” Pam., Rhen.] would suffer (Exod. 12).

So too Jesus, because of the future mystery of his name—for he confirmed as his own the name that he himself had given him, since he had commanded him thereafter to be called not “angel” or “Ausen,” but “Jesus.” Thus, therefore, both names fit the Christ of God, so that he might also be called Jesus.

And because the virgin from whom Christ had to be born was to derive her lineage from David’s seed, as we recalled above, the prophet Isaiah says plainly in the following words:

> And there shall be born, he says, a rod from the root of Jesse—which is Mary—and a flower shall ascend from his root. And upon him [Note: [Col. 0623C] “From the root. Shall ascend upon him,” Rhen., Oberth.] shall rest the Spirit of God: the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might [Note: [Col. 0623C] “Truth,” Par.], the spirit of knowledge and piety; the spirit of the fear of God shall fill him. (Isa. 11:1–2)

For the totality [Col. 0624A] of spiritual gifts belonged to no human being except Christ, who was indeed likened to a flower because of grace [Note: [Col. 0623D] “Except to Christ, flourishing indeed because of glory, because of grace,” [Col. 0624C] Oberth., Seml.], but reckoned from the stock of Jesse [Note: [Col. 0623D] “Namely, to be reckoned thence through Mary.” Shortly afterward: “Just as Mary was registered in the Roman census.” He mentions this census in many places; I therefore refer the reader to our notes on chapter 7 of book IV *Against Marcion*. I remark in passing that there is no small difficulty in explaining that registration: whether it took place under Sentius Saturninus or under Quirinius, as St. Luke has it, or whether a double census was undertaken, as some assert. LE PR.], namely through Mary, who was to be reckoned from that stock. For Mary was from the native town of Bethlehem and the house of David, as she was registered in the Roman census (Luke 2:1 ff.); from her Christ was born.

I further ask: since it was proclaimed by the prophets that he would come from Jesse’s lineage and [Note: [Col. 0623D] “All humility and patience.” In chapter 3 of *On Patience*, he has almost the same: “But when about to depart, he wished to be feasted upon the delight of patience.” Not long before, he had called Patience the mother [Col. 0624D] of mercy. Thus the man who is shown to be such will himself be the Christ who has come.

For concerning him the prophet says:

> A man placed under affliction and knowing how to bear weakness (Isa. 53:4), who was led like a sheep to slaughter and, like a lamb before its shearer, did not open his mouth. (Ibid., 7)

If he neither contended nor [Col. 0624B] cried out, nor was his voice heard outside; [Note: [Col. 0624D] “Who the bruised reed.” In chapter 3 of *On Patience*, he says nearly the same: “He did not break the battered reed.” Here it is “bruised,” there “battered,” which is the same, just as in the prophet Isaiah 42 it is συγκεθλασμένον, “crushed,” and in Matthew 12 συντετριμμένον, “broken.” Here I shall use St. Hilary’s explanation: in commenting upon this passage of Matthew, he understands by the battered reed the Gentiles, who were indeed not broken by God but preserved for salvation. LE PR.] if he did not break the bruised reed—that is, Israel’s battered [Note: [Col. 0624C] “That is, battered” is absent from Rhen. and Oberth.] faith; if he did not extinguish the burning flax—that is, the momentary ardor of the nations (Isa. 42:2–3)—but rather caused it to shine more brightly [Note: [Col. 0624D] “By the rising of his light.” In accordance with: “The people who walked in darkness saw a great light,” a passage he used earlier.] through the rising of his own light, he can be no other than the one who was proclaimed. [Note: [Col. 0624C] “Was being foretold,” Pam.]

The conduct of the Christ who has come must therefore be examined according to the rule of the Scriptures. For, unless I am mistaken, we read that he was distinguished by a twofold activity: proclamation and miraculous power. I shall arrange the discussion under both headings. [Note: [Col. 0624C] “Thus arranged for the people,” Rhen., Oberth. “Thus arranged under a heading,” Jun.] Let us therefore examine in detail the sequence we have begun, showing that Christ was announced as a preacher, as through Isaiah:

## CHAPTER X

You do indeed raise doubts about the manner of his Passion, denying that the suffering of the cross was proclaimed concerning Christ, and further arguing that one must not believe that God exposed his Son to that kind of death, because he himself said:

> Cursed is every man who hangs upon wood. (Deut. 21:23)

But an examination of the circumstances must precede the interpretation of this curse. For he says in Deuteronomy:

> If there is in anyone an offense such that he is under a judgment of death, and he dies, you shall hang him upon wood. But you shall surely bury [Col. 0625B] him on that same day, because everyone who has been suspended upon wood is cursed by God; and you shall not defile the land that the Lord your God will give you as an inheritance. He did not, therefore, curse Christ in respect to this suffering. Rather, he made a distinction: the person who had incurred a judgment of death for some offense, died, and was suspended upon wood—this person would be cursed by God, because [Note: [Col. 0625C] “Who,” Rhen., Oberth.] he was suspended upon wood for what his offenses deserved.
