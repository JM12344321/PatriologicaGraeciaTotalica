Here are some ancient and modern judgments on Tertullian. According to Jerome—who learned it from a presbyter, who had learned it from Cyprian’s secretary—Cyprian customarily read something of Tertullian every day and, when asking for one of his books, said: “Give me my master” (*Ecclesiastical Writers* 53). He truly imitated him and drew much from him. He also composed most of his works on Tertullian’s plan: his *On Idolatry* after the *Apology*, and his books *On Discipline and Women’s Dress* [Col. 0090D], *On Jealousy and Envy*, *On the Lord’s Prayer*, and *On Works and Alms* in imitation of Tertullian. Eusebius says Tertullian was among the most learned Latin writers and renowned in every church (*Ecclesiastical History* II.2). Lactantius’s judgment, quoted above, does him little honor. Hilary writes in his commentary on Matthew that the error into which Tertullian fell diminished the authority of books otherwise deserving some approval. Jerome sometimes speaks honorably of him: in the *Catalogue* he calls him a man of vigorous and vehement genius; in his letter to Magnus he says every author is inferior to him in learning and subtlety. Elsewhere, however, he rebukes his errors and faults. In the *Apology against Rufinus* [Col. 0091A] he says: “I praise his genius; I condemn his heresies”; and against Helvidius, who cited Tertullian’s authority: “Of Tertullian I say that he is not a man of the Church.” Augustine praises his style and everywhere condemns his errors; in *Literal Commentary on Genesis* he says Tertullian’s acute genius sometimes perceived truth and could not avoid setting it down occasionally even against his own mind.

Vincent of Lérins paints him thus: “The same principle applies to Tertullian. As Origen among the Greeks, so he among the Latins must easily be judged chief of all our writers. What man was more learned, more practiced in divine and human matters? With wonderful mental capacity he embraced all philosophy, all philosophical schools, the founders and opponents of schools, all their disciplines, and every variety of history and study. Did he not excel in a genius so weighty and vehement that he proposed almost nothing to overthrow which he did not penetrate by acuteness or crush by weight? Who can recount the praises of his speech? It is packed with some compelling necessity of reasons, so that it drives to assent those it cannot persuade; almost every word is a thought, every perception a victory. Marcion, Apelles, Praxeas, Hermogenes, Jews, Gentiles, Gnostics, and the rest know this; their blasphemies he overthrew with the many great masses of his volumes, like thunderbolts. Yet after [Col. 0091C] all this, Tertullian, insufficiently tenacious of Catholic dogma—that is, universal and ancient faith—and far more eloquent than fortunate, changed his opinion and finally did what the blessed confessor Hilary writes: ‘By subsequent error he deprived his worthy writings of authority’” [Note: [Col. 0091D] Vincent of Lérins, *Commonitorium* 18].

I pass over the judgments of Trithemius, Rhenanus, Politian, Pamelius, Rigault, and other moderns, whom readers may consult, and end with two French authors who painted Tertullian differently. The first is the famous Balzac, Letter 2, book V, to Rigault: “I await the Tertullian you are publishing, that he may teach me the patience he transmits.… Your preface would have reconciled me to that author had I hated him [Col. 0091D] and had his harsh diction and the faults of his age deterred me from reading him. But I have long esteemed him; though covered with thorns and somber, he does not seem unpleasant. In his writings I encounter that black light of which the old poet sings, and his obscurity delights me like polished ebony. I have always thought African forms, though unlike ours, do not lack their own charm; and as Sophonisba eclipsed many Italian women, so minds of that region please by their foreign eloquence, and I rank him above many imitators of Cicero. Let us concede to the fastidious that his style is iron; let them concede that from this iron he forged excellent weapons [Col. 0092A], defended the honor and innocence of Christianity, fiercely pursued the Valentinians, and utterly pierced Marcion.”

The second is Father Malebranche, author of *The Search after Truth* II.3: “Tertullian was undoubtedly cultivated in every learning, but stronger in memory than judgment; keener and vaster in genius than in mind. There can be no doubt that he was a fanatic in the sense explained above and possessed nearly all fanatic qualities. His reverence for the visions of Montanus and his prophetesses proves weak judgment. That sharp genius, impulses inspired by trifles, and unbridled mind plainly reveal it. How many irregular movements in his hyperboles and figures! How many brilliant, magnificent reasons which prove only by brilliance and persuade merely by stunning and dazzling the mind” [Col. 0092B]. Malebranche then tries to prove this portrait by excerpts from *On the Pallium*, which he thought especially suitable.

If we consider this portrait drawn from learned men, one may ask whether Tertullian benefited or harmed religion and the Church more; whether he brought greater losses or advantages; whether he deserves more blame or praise. Given scholars’ varied judgments, a decision is difficult and delicate. Yet according to the elegant Schröckh [Note: [Col. 0092D] Johann Matthias Schröckh, *Ecclesiastical History Written Theologically*, vol. III, p. 321 in my copy], if we question Tertullian’s writings, the answer may generally favor him. The Montanist errors he championed did not greatly attack the Church, much less overthrow Christianity’s foundations, but arose rather from excessive religious fervor that commonly rejected rational examination. Even in defending them he departed little, if at all, from Catholic faith and the Apostles’ Creed. Why should the name Montanist harm his reputation more than other ancient Fathers’ errors harmed theirs? He spent the better part of his life among sectarians, yet the Church of that age has no man comparable in learning and merit. I think even Tertullian the Montanist benefited Catholics considerably, both because his invectives sharpened their prudence [Col. 0092D] in action and because, by commending principles of stricter ethics so ardently, he called them away from allegorical games in explaining Scripture and ingenious questions about faith toward more useful matters.

His faults—unavoidable in one so merciless in exposing and refuting others’—do not obscure his conspicuous virtues. His writings abound in signs of a fervent and profound mind distinguished by its own genius: now it rises vehemently, now sinks as though ill and crawls anxiously; rich in its own inventions and thoughts, rarely calm, somewhat sad even in exultation. An extensive apparatus of learning, abundantly and aptly scattered in its places; a manifold imaginative power enjoyable and profitable to readers; and the resulting fertility of genius spread everywhere through refined thoughts, acute maxims, vivid images, merry wit, biting jests, and every kind of diction. When these powers are repeatedly driven rapidly and turbulently, judgment stumbles; hence his arguments often have much brilliance and show, but little weight and solidity. He flares even over trifles. Besides readily believing any little tale, he often views or invents things greater than truth and, himself swept away, carries the reader with him instead of inviting him by reason. More often than the bare teaching or proof of a matter requires, he delights in ornaments. He says many things more harshly and emphatically than he intended them understood. He knew Greek philosophy exceedingly well, yet abstained from using it both from hatred, condemning it among Christians as the mother of heresies, and from a temperament that abhorred slow thought freed from beloved digressions. Thus he philosophized less often and successfully than his capacity allowed.

[Col. 0093B] This did not prevent his becoming an outstanding apologist for Christianity against the Gentiles. Excepting a few peculiar opinions of no great moment, especially Montanus’s familiar dogmas, his writings everywhere present pure Catholic faith gravely, without artificial explanations and formulas of comparison attempted by some doctors. In moral teaching, where he so often turns to rigor, the stricter he is, the more excellent he often becomes. His zeal for advancing religion kindles readers and inflames them to noble actions. He treats moral questions and assertions with such force and grace that one readily forgives him when he errs. He uses Scripture frequently and interprets it learnedly, though not impartially, being over-eager to elicit his own opinions from it. He is a most vigorous athlete in restraining false teachers and corrupters of Christian doctrine, pursuing them with innate impetuosity, deadly hatred, taunts, and reproaches scarcely worthy of a gentleman. To defeat them strongly on every side he uses unfamiliar argumentative weapons, most of which strike hard rather than miss.

His style smells of hardness more than African—or, precisely, Tertullianic. Everywhere appears his singular effort to find words matching the magnitude and force of his thoughts. Ideas crowd ideas and words crowd words, so that clarity only rarely flashes from darkness. Obsolete, newly coined, or affected words; harsh constructions; varied abuses; uneven flow; compressed brevity; bold metaphors; frigid maxims, antitheses [Col. 0094A], and empty wordplay everywhere reveal Tertullian. He expresses what thought sits in his mind, but in words so obscure it is extracted by conjecture rather than clearly seen. His chosen phrases are rarely penetrated fully, and he is rightly blamed for deliberately wrapping his thoughts in darkness and verbal coverings. Yet the Oedipal labors of one who skillfully searches these stylistic riddles will not be empty. The author compensates for the doubtful light scattered through his works by the abundance of truths he reveals to the careful searcher. How delightful for a student of antiquity to labor through the carefully heaped mass of words to deeper knowledge and often uncover splendid truths by investigating almost a single little word! How unlike the many writers whose swollen method pours mere floods of words and offers empty nuts to nauseated readers. As many difficulties as our author contains, making reading hard for his devotees, so many charms fill his books; their varied, choice thoughts and truths make them more useful and pleasant. Besides treasures of recondite learning, his writings give an account of Christian faith, ecclesiastical discipline, ceremonies used in worship, attacks by heretical sects against Catholic doctrine, and contemporary heretical opinions so candid, clear, and pleasant as one would vainly expect from others of that age.

[Col. 0094C] There is no need to fear a confusion of Montanism—his private opinion—and Catholicism, as many scruple to do. He professed it in few and lighter matters, and we have means to distinguish his opinions from the common view. Some say his account of Gnostic errors is inaccurate because he was ignorant of Eastern philosophy, but that is not fairly believed. Should one who studied their writings with such diligence and acuteness, and discussed their errors more abundantly than anyone, be thought ignorant of their origin? He derives their absurd ideas solely from Greek philosophy; one must rather concede, then, that Eastern philosophy was not their sole source.

Moreover, Tertullian founded ecclesiastical Latin, the speech used in public Church affairs, joining words and thoughts of ancient Latium to new notions drawn from Christian dogmas, persons, and customs. Though the orthodox did not number him in their communion, they everywhere praised his genius and style and had no equal among Westerners. Soon afterward Cyprian, most celebrated among bishops, wholly fashioned himself and his speech after Tertullian’s diction; others attempted it less openly. As Montanus’s faction gradually failed, danger from reading him also disappeared, especially because the advantages drawn from him were altogether singular. His commentaries and interpretations of Scripture are illustrious monuments for the history of biblical theology and exegesis, though it is difficult to decide how much he used a Latin translation already existing then.

The number and variety of Tertullian’s works require ordering into classes. By subject they fall into three. The first contains writings against the Gentiles and Jews, sworn enemies of religion: the *Apology*, two books *To the Nations*, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, *To Scapula*, and *Against the Jews*. [Col. 0095B] The second contains writings against heretics: *Prescription against Heretics*, *Against Praxeas*, *Hermogenes*, *Valentinians*, and *Marcion*; *On the Flesh of Christ* against Apelles, Marcion, and Valentinus; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* against Sadducees and resurrection-denying heretics; *Scorpiace* against Gnostics; and *On the Soul* against philosophers’ and heretics’ opinions. The third concerns discipline and morals: first *On Baptism*, whose former part proves against the Cainites baptism’s necessity and efficacy and whose latter describes discipline; *On Repentance*, first among works concerning ecclesiastical discipline alone; *On Prayer*, concerning morals and discipline; and *On Idolatry*, *Crown*, *Flight*, *Pallium*, *Shows*, women’s dress and adornment, *Veiling of Virgins*, *Patience*, *To the Martyrs*, and *To His Wife*. By critical and chronological considerations, five classes will be established in the next chapter. Finally note the four works written for the Montanists against the Church: *Modesty*, *Monogamy*, *Exhortation to Chastity*, and *Fasting*.

Learned men dispute much about the time and order of Tertullian’s works, and the controversy cannot be resolved with complete precision [Col. 0095D]. Among Protestants, Samuel Basnage (*Political-Ecclesiastical Annals* II, p. 215) and Pierre Allix (*Dissertation on Tertullian’s Life and Writings* IV ff., p. 28) investigated their dates; William Cave judges Allix’s chronology nearest truth (*Literary History* I, p. 92) and chiefly follows it, though differing sometimes. Among Catholics, Sébastien Le Nain de Tillemont (*Mémoires* III, p. 300 ff., Brussels 1732), Ellies Dupin (*Bibliothèque des auteurs* I), and Rémy Ceillier (*General History of Ecclesiastical Authors* [Col. 0096A] II.28) devoted their powers to the question. But, in Schröckh’s judgment, none performed the task more successfully than Johann August Noesselt, doctor and public ordinary professor of theology at Halle, in his 1757 dissertation *On the True Date and Doctrine of the Surviving Writings of Q. Septimius Tertullian*, more correctly reprinted at Halle-Magdeburg in 1768. Because of both its excellence and rarity—for almost a year I wrote booksellers everywhere urging them to procure it, and at last obtained a copy with great effort—I decided to append it here, not without scholars’ gratitude.

## Article II. On Tertullian’s Books Whose Date and Orthodoxy Are Established

[Col. 0096B]

### §1. Rationale of the undertaking

After the darkness of the preceding barbarism was dispelled, the ancient elegance of the liberal arts and disciplines restored, and every kind of literature increasingly illuminated, men of great genius appeared who, deterred neither by lengthy art nor immense difficulties, devoted every effort to illustrating Christian history. Holding that neither literary elegance, the art of thinking well and accurately, nor sacred disciplines could be adequately served without rigorous reading of ancient writers [Col. 0096C], they believed those authors especially should be examined whose reading greatly aided understanding of Christian doctrine and discipline. But opinions, like times, change. This manner of learning from antiquity gradually fell into contempt among us, especially when men appeared who believed common sense alone, inherited from the Fathers and constrained by geometrical subtlety, made a scholar. They treated theological preliminaries as theology itself and, content with meager knowledge, despised studies lacking novelty or requiring intense, laborious thought. Hence most now disdain anything marked by antiquity and think matters concerning Scripture, early Christian teachers, and Christian history worthier of cloistered pedants than acute minds.

Reflecting often on this and lamenting our age with lovers of sound judgment, we chose to emulate the diligence of the former rather than the obscure wisdom of the latter. Once we began considering how to dedicate something to others’ use, we deemed it wrong to follow the popular road, which gradually leads our people back to ancient barbarism, and resolved to resume studies of antiquity closely joined to diligent cultivation of divine letters. Having applied myself continually, as opportunity allowed, to early doctors, Tertullian seemed especially worthy of accurate reading. I doubt whether all antiquity contains another from whom ancient Christian doctrine and morals may be learned who joined greater divine and human knowledge and subtlety in writing to so great a genius [Note: [Col. 0097D] Lest we seem partisan, add Jean Daillé’s testimony, unsuspected here because of his excessive severity toward early doctors. He calls Tertullian “a writer marvelously learned, acute, diligent, copious, and an exceedingly rich witness to his age’s ecclesiastical affairs” (*On the Religious Worship of the Latins* I.11, p. 50)].

Yet using him to learn ancient Christian institutions is chiefly hindered by uncertainty whether each book was written before or after his defection to the Montanists. This question greatly exercised scholars, who sought the books’ dates so that Tertullian could reliably show what ancient Christians thought. Unfortunately, some excellent men treated only one or two works, others treated the task lightly, and others were led astray by preconceptions, though they might have attained truth had they followed it unencumbered. After repeatedly reading his books and observing matters concerning their dates and doctrine profitably disputable but unmentioned by others, and that most could be dated more accurately, I resolved to submit to learned judgment evidence drawn from Tertullian’s books, compared with ancient writers and monuments. We shall proceed by first premising general points; then treating books whose date and doctrine are established; next those lacking definite dates but demonstrably Montanist or free from that suspicion; and finally candidly examining those whose date is no clearer than their doctrine.

### §2. Certain general questions proposed

The whole discussion concerns two matters, date and Montanism, and therefore has two considerable difficulties: neither doctrinal character nor reliable chronological signs can always be found [Col. 0098A]. Whether a work preceded or followed his defection may be understood from its date or its Montanist teachings. The first method has three uncertainties: some books show no chronological traces; some permit only conjecture; some suggest a date but leave uncertain whether Tertullian had then separated from the Church or returned. The second method, besides not applying everywhere, leaves doubt whether he had fully embraced Montanus or merely shared one or two errors without yet renouncing the orthodox Church. Where evidence is absent or doubtful, we shall sometimes state the reason and enter the ancients’ *non liquet* (“not clear”), and elsewhere give probable grounds for conjecture. In the remaining cases we shall briefly discuss three general points: the year of his defection, which dates books known chronologically but not doctrinally [Col. 0098C]; whether he ever returned to a sounder mind or remained permanently Montanist; and signs distinguishing books by an orthodox Tertullian leaning toward Montanism from those by one wholly alienated.

### §3. In what year did Tertullian defect to the Montanists? Did he afterward return to the orthodox?

Neither Tertullian nor another writer tells the year. Jerome’s statement (*Illustrious Men* 53, in Fabricius’s *Ecclesiastical Library*, p. 125) that Tertullian remained a presbyter of the Church until middle age provides no sound route, since his birth, death, and attained age are uncertain. Some basis is found in chronologically successive books, some without Montanist traces and others with many. We shall show that no work before *On the Soldier’s Crown*—the latest being *On the Testimony of the Soul*—shows Montanism, while its first traces appear in *Crown*. We shall establish that *Crown* was written in AD 201 and *Testimony* near the end of 199; hence Tertullian probably joined the Montanists about AD 200.

The second question, whether he ever returned after embracing their errors, is harder, but there is no adequate basis for affirming it. His austere, excessively rigid weakness made him love stricter moral demands more and regard them as more pleasing to God and consonant with Christian perfection. As those afflicted by melancholy cling more firmly to what feeds sadness and their opinions strengthen with years, it is unlikely that he abandoned these opinions and the arena where he could practice them. Add the silence of all who wrote about him concerning a return, Augustine’s express testimony (*On Heresies* 86), and the consensus of those refusing his doctrinal authority because he joined the perversely minded. Nor do the books we shall show he wrote last oppose our judgment; almost all are stained with Montanist errors. Let this therefore be the rule: all books evidently composed after works undoubtedly convicted of those errors should be considered written after his separation.
