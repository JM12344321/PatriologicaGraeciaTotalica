# The Book *On the Shows*

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, *ON THE SHOWS* (C)

## NOTE

[Col. 0627B] The distinguished Noesselt has demonstrated that Tertullian wrote the books *On the Shows* and *On Idolatry* on the occasion when shows were being produced not only at Rome but also throughout the provinces because of Severus’s return and the victory he had won over Albinus. [Col. 0627C] Some indeed conjecture that Tertullian composed the book *On the Shows* during his Montanist period, because in it he appears to support those who sought revelations (Tertullian, *On the Shows*, ch. 29). But who does not know that the martyrs of the catholic Church themselves sought revelations from God, as may be seen in the acts of Saints Perpetua and Felicity (XXXV, Ruinart, *Acta sincera*, p. 87)? These were read publicly in the assemblies in the time of St. Augustine (Augustine, Sermon 280). Add that St. Cyprian expressly asserts that confessors were often warned in a vision of their approaching martyrdom (Cyprian, Epistles XI and XVI).

However that may be, Tertullian’s purpose in this treatise was certainly to call Christians, including catechumens themselves, away from the shows [Col. 0627D] by demonstrating how shameless such entertainments are, how contrary to true piety and the sincere worship that we owe to God, and how hostile even to those who have renounced the pomp of the world, its pleasures, and idolatry. First, he removes two plausible arguments of the pagans. The first is that so great an outward gratification of the eyes or ears presents no obstacle to religion in the mind and conscience, and that God is not offended by a human being’s enjoyment. The second is that Christians, a race ready for death, are trained for this obstinacy by the renunciation of pleasures, so that they may despise life more easily once its bonds have, as it were, been cut away. He then rejects another ridiculous pagan objection: that everything serving the theater—earth, stones, lions, bodily strength, and the charms of the voice [Col. 0628B]—was created by God and therefore ought not to be rejected.

Against the objection of certain Christians who asserted that attendance at the shows is nowhere expressly forbidden in Holy Scripture, he sets those words of the royal Prophet: [Col. 0628C] “Blessed is the man who has not gone into the council of the impious, nor stood in the way of sinners, nor sat in the seat of pestilence.” He then adds that believers are bound to flee from them because, when they entered the water, they professed the faith and attested with their own mouths that they had renounced the devil and his pomp and angels. He affirms that games and shows belong to idolatry, from which he demonstrates that they derived their origin; and he reviews the names of the games, their idolatrous apparatus, their superstitious venues, and their arts. In discussing the circus, he says that although it was filled with idols and superstitious emblems, Christians could nevertheless enter it without fault outside the time of the shows, just as they might enter the temples themselves if their reason for entering were free from blame.

From the circus he passes to the theater, particularly consecrated to Venus and Bacchus; from there to [Col. 0628D] athletic contests, each dedicated to its own deity; and finally to the gladiators, who owed their origin to funeral processions. These last shows belonged to the amphitheater. But besides idolatry, the principal reason, he also recalls still other reasons why Christians must avoid the shows: namely, the danger of losing the Holy Spirit amid the agitation of our passions; the loss of time, so precious to a Christian; and insults, curses, slanders, and abuse. For there everything shameful that was concealed elsewhere with the greatest care was publicly brought forth. He shows how repugnant it is to sound reason eagerly to seek at the shows what shame and horror forbid us to see or commit in the rest of life’s intercourse.

Then [Col. 0629A] he proceeds: it is not expedient to see an image of those things that one is not permitted to do; yet in tragedy you present dreadful crimes to the spectators’ eyes, and in comedy, wantonness. It is not fitting for a human being endowed with reason to be cruel and savage; therefore it is likewise not fitting to delight in a dreadful sight in which people are slaughtered, even if those killed deserved death for their crimes. It is insane to esteem highly an art whose practitioners are excluded from the society of respectable people because of the mark of infamy. He next rebukes the insolence of masked performers and recalls God’s curse directed against men who would assume women’s clothing, for men in feminine dress played women’s roles on the stage.

Afterward he shows once again that the use of the shows is contrary to baptism and that the gentiles themselves customarily distinguished Christians by their rejection of the shows. [Col. 0629B] Finally, he adduces two examples of Christians punished for frequenting the shows. He then exhorts Christians to pass their lives here amid groaning while the gentiles immerse themselves in pleasures, and to desire nothing more than to depart from this world in order to come to God. Wishing to show what pleasures and entertainments befit Christians, Tertullian writes: “What greater pleasure is there than loathing pleasure itself, than contempt for the whole world, than true liberty, than an unblemished conscience . . . than that you trample upon the gods of the nations, that you expel demons, that you perform cures, that you seek revelations, [Col. 0630A] that you live for God?” These are the pleasures, these the shows of Christians: holy, perpetual, and free of charge. Finally, he exhorts them diligently to contemplate the contests of the martyrs, the Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ, his approaching advent, the Last Judgment, the eternal punishments of the impious, and the rewards of the good. [Note: [Col. 0632C] “Offense,” Rigault.] What is not God’s that offends God? But when it gives offense, it ceases to belong to God; and when it has ceased to belong to him, it gives offense.

The human being himself, the perpetrator [Note: [Col. 0632C] “Author,” Paris., Rig.] of every crime, is not merely God’s work but also his image; [Note: [Col. 0632D] “Is the image of God.” Understand the writer’s meaning: he says that the human being is both God’s work and God’s image—his work because of the body, his image because of the spirit. Therefore he immediately adds that the human being defected in both body and spirit. The words must therefore not be confused, for the human being is called God’s image only because of the spirit. Zeno of Verona, Sermon 2 on Genesis. LAC.] and yet in both body and spirit he has defected [Note: [Col. 0632C] “And yet in body and spirit has ceased,” Rig.] from his founder. He spoke similarly in *On Fasting*: “Adam sold salvation for gluttony,” and in book I *Against Marcion* of the companions of gluttony, namely lust and luxury. PAM.] a belly for satiety in gluttony, [Note: [Col. 0632D] “Association,” Jun., from an MS., Rig.] genitals for the excesses of shamelessness, hands for violence, or feet for a wandering life. Nor, I think, was the spirit implanted [Col. 0633A] in the body so that it might become a workshop of plots, frauds, and iniquities.

For if God, the exactor of innocence, hates all wickedness and so much [Note: [Col. 0633B] “So much,” Wouw., MS., Rig.] deliberately contrived evil, he certainly did not create the things he fashioned for the outcome of the works he condemns, [Note: [Col. 0633C] “Did not create them for the outcome of the works.” This passage should be noted against Calvin, who in the *Institutes*, book III, chs. 22, 23, and 24, maintains that God created the greater part of the world for damnation and destruction by a simple decree of his will, as the Swiss themselves objected to him. PAM.—“For the outcome of the works.” I do not approve Pamelius, who here took *exitum* to mean destruction. The sense is entirely complete: the word must be related to the perverse use of things, and thus everything coheres; it must not be related to ruin, for Tertullian is not discussing that. LAC.] although those same works are carried out by means of the things he created, since this is the whole ground of condemnation: the perverse use of creation by created beings. [Note: [Col. 0633B] “By created beings” is absent from Rig.]

We, therefore, who have come to know the Lord [Note: [Col. 0633B] “God,” Rig.] and also discern his rival— [Note: [Col. 0633C] “We, therefore, who, having come to know God, have also discerned his rival.” We have changed nothing in this sentence, because [Col. 0633D] we found it so constituted in the Codex Agobardinus.
