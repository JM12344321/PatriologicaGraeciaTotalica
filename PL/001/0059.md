God’s powers will, I suppose, be doubted—God who composed this vast body of the world from what had not existed, no less than from the death-like vacancy and emptiness, and imposed order upon it [Note: [Col. 0523B] Wouwer thus emended the manuscripts’ reading “imposed the text,” with the assent of [Col. 0524A] Rigaltius and Havercamp.]; who animated it with the spirit that animates all souls; and marked it [Note: [Col. 0524C] **And marked it through the world itself.** He not only created the world from nothing and animated it, but also marked out within it many examples of the resurrection of living beings, so that these might serve as testimony to the nations. There is also a notable passage in Epiphanius, who does not permit me to be [non-Latin text omitted]. In the *Ancoratus*, p. 595 of my Greek edition, he writes: [non-Latin text omitted], etc. That is:

“And the whole creation openly refutes them, displaying every day the likeness of resurrection. For the day dies, and through sleep we represent the condition of the dead. The day rises, awakening us and demonstrating the sign of resurrection. Fruits are plucked, and the condition of things is cut down, so that in this way an image of our departure is set forth beforehand. The earth is sown and germinates, because things broken after the reaping rise again. The locust dies when it buries in the earth a certain cast-off product of itself; and after a time the earth restores what was buried. The seeds of fruits are sown and first die, then reach perfection; for unless they have died, they are not brought to life.”

He then continues in this fashion: [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0525B] [non-Latin text omitted], etc. That is:

“God has made tokens in us through our ten fingernails and ten toenails, which bear witness to the hope of our resurrection. He has also declared our resurrection, as it were, through the crown of our hair. For what appears to be a dead body in us—namely, our hair—sprouts again whenever it is cut. Countless further examples could be given to assure unbelievers. Yet neither the wood pigeon, a little bird, nor the dormouse, a little animal, has persuaded them. The latter dies for six months, and the wood pigeon for forty days, and each comes to life again after its appointed time. Scarab beetles, when near death, hide themselves in a ball of dung, burying and interring such a ball in the earth; and thus they are found to come to life again from their own moisture.” HAV.], and darkness likewise, in its turn, succeeds by withdrawing; the stars, though dead, come to life again [Note: [Col. 0525B] “Come to life,” Fran.]; [Col. 0525A] the seasons begin when they end; fruits are consumed [Note: [Col. 0525B] “Are brought to completion,” Fran.] and return; seeds assuredly rise more fruitfully only after they have decayed and dissolved. All things are preserved by perishing; all things are formed anew from destruction.
