First of all, whether there is in the soul some highest vital and cognitive faculty, which they call the *hēgemonikon* (ἡγεμονικόν), that is, the ruling faculty: if this is denied, the whole constitution of the soul is endangered. Indeed, those who deny a ruling faculty first judged the soul itself to be nothing.

[Note: [Col. 0670B] “Something” (*aliquid*), Venetian edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0669C] A certain Dicaearchus of Messene. Dicaearchus, Cicero says (*Tusculan Disputations*, book 1), in the discourse which he sets out in three books as having been conducted at Corinth by learned men engaged in debate, makes many persons speak in the first book; in the other two he introduces a certain Pherecrates, an aged Phthiotian whom he says was descended from Deucalion, arguing that mind is absolutely nothing, that the whole word is an empty name, that living beings and animate beings are so called to no purpose, and that neither a mind nor a soul exists in a human being or an animal. And shortly afterward Cicero indicates in the same place that this was indeed Dicaearchus’ own opinion: “What am I to say of Dicaearchus, who says that mind is absolutely nothing?” Suidas also attests that this man was a Messenian and a disciple of Aristotle. Plutarch and Quintilian cite his *Music*; Gellius, his *Pythagoras*; Athenaeus, several books; St Jerome, book 2 *Against Jovinian*, a description of Greece taken from his books on antiquities; and Cicero elsewhere, finally, a book on divination and dreams. PAM.]

A certain Dicaearchus of Messene, and among the physicians Andreas and Asclepiades, abolished the ruling faculty in this way: they wished the senses themselves to reside in the soul, whose ruling faculty is thereby vindicated. Asclepiades even presses the argument that many animals, after those bodily parts in which the ruling faculty is chiefly believed to reside have been removed, nevertheless both remain alive for some time and retain awareness: flies, wasps, and locusts, if you cut off their heads; goats, tortoises, and eels, if you remove their hearts. [Col. 0670B] Therefore, he says, there is no ruling faculty; for if there were, once it and its attendants had been lost, the soul’s vigor would not persist.

[Note: [Col. 0670B] “Certain persons” (*aliqui*), Junius.]

[Note: [Col. 0669D] “Once it and its attendants had been lost.” That is, once that ruling faculty and its attendants—namely, the head and heart—had been lost. “Its attendants”: as though with its entire household, retinue, and train. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0670B] Rigault reads: “Once it had been lost together with its seats.”]

But a greater number of philosophers opposed Dicaearchus—Plato, Strato, Epicurus, Democritus, Empedocles, Socrates, and Aristotle—and the physicians Herophilus, Erasistratus, Diocles, Hippocrates, and Soranus himself opposed Andreas and Asclepiades.

[Note: [Col. 0669D] Against Andreas. The physician Andreas is frequently cited by Pliny, not only among the names of the authors from whom he drew much material, but also in book XXII, chapter 23, and book XXXII, chapter 5. In certain copies, however, *Andrias* is incorrectly read for *Andreas*; for his book *On Plants* is also cited by Dioscorides and Galen. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0670B] And Asclepiades. Besides attesting, among the names of his authorities, that he drew much material from Asclepiades, the physician of Prusa, Pliny treats him specifically in book XXVI, chapter 3. Certain writings of his are also cited by Aëtius and Galen, although Galen mentions two men of that name. A little later the Author ridicules him in these words: “So that Asclepiades, confuted even by these men, may go looking for his goats bleating without hearts and chase away his flies flying without heads.” He tacitly signifies the absurdity of one who wished, from their example, to deprive even a human being of the ruling faculty. The Author mentions Asclepiades again below, chapter 25. Apuleius also relates much about the same Asclepiades in book IV of *The Golden Ass*. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0670C] Erasistratus. In the place cited above, Pliny ranks Erasistratus fifth among physicians, before Herophilus and Asclepiades. Likewise, in book XIV, chapter 7, he writes that Erasistratus lived in the 450th year of the City of Rome, and he mentions his school in book XX, chapter 9. Finally, in book XXIX, chapter 2, he makes him a disciple of Chrysippus, born of Aristotle’s daughter, who, after curing King Antiochus, was rewarded with one hundred talents by Ptolemy, the king’s son. Suidas also mentions this event, and Galen cites several of Erasistratus’ books in the work already cited. The Author cites him again in chapter 25. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0670D] Diocles. Diocles of Carystus, a physician second only to Hippocrates in age and reputation, is celebrated by Pliny in book XXVI, chapter 2. He is also cited by Pliny in book XX, chapters 3 and 5, and in book XXI, as well as in the catalogue of physicians whose works he used; likewise by Galen, Plutarch, the scholiast on Nicander, Athenaeus, and Paul of Aegina. PAM.]

And now there are more Christians than all of them, who are guided by God regarding both points: that there is a ruling faculty in the soul and that it is consecrated in a definite recess of the body.

[Note: [Col. 0670D] “Who are guided by God regarding both points.” So reads the Agobard manuscript. “And now,” he says, “there are more Christians than all the philosophers and physicians,” Christians who, when investigating what judgment should be made regarding both points—that is, regarding that ruling faculty of the soul and the place and position of that faculty—are guided by God and learn from the sacred pages. Thus the ancient Christians, even when disputing with pagans, adduced the authority of Holy Scripture. The pagans rejected divine utterances unless they were proved by human, that is, purely philosophical, arguments. In this blindness Galen inveighs against Archigenes with the following prohibition: “He will not listen to unproved laws” (*nomous anapodeiktous*, νόμους ἀναποδείκτους), “such as those who have reached the school of Moses and Christ commonly employ” (*Mōyseōs kai Christou diatribēn aphigmenoi*, Μωυσέως καὶ Χριστοῦ διατριβὴν ἀφιγμένοι). These are Galen’s words in the second book *On Differences of the Pulse*. RIG.]

[Col. 0671A]

[Note: [Col. 0671B] “Are led” (*deducimur*), Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür, and Pamelius; “we infer” (*deducimus*), Junius.]

[Note: [Col. 0671C] The ruling faculty in the soul. Jerome to Fabiola, letter 128: “The question is where the soul’s ruling faculty resides. Plato places it in the brain; Christ shows that it is in the heart: ‘Blessed are the pure in heart,’” etc. RIG.]

For if we read that God is the searcher and inspector of the heart (Wisdom 1; Proverbs 24); if his prophet is also approved by exposing the heart’s hidden things (Psalm 138); if God himself anticipates the thoughts of the heart among the people (Matthew 9): “Why do you think evil things in your hearts?”; if David too says (Psalm 50), “Create a clean heart in me, O God”; and Paul says (Romans 10) that belief unto righteousness takes place in the heart; and John says (1 John 3) that each person is reproved by his own heart; if, finally, whoever has looked upon a woman in order to desire her has already committed adultery in his heart (Matthew 5)—then both points are simultaneously made clear: that there is in the soul a ruling faculty upon which the divine intention bears, namely the cognitive and vital power—for whatever understands is alive—and that this faculty is contained in that treasury of the body toward which God looks.

[Note: [Col. 0671B] So reads the Paris edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0671B] “Which” (*quod*), Pamelius and Rhenanus.]

You are therefore not to suppose that this ruling faculty is agitated from without, according to Heraclitus; nor wafted throughout the entire body, according to Moschion; nor enclosed in the head, according to Plato; nor seated chiefly at the crown of the head, according to Xenocrates; nor lying in the brain, according to Hippocrates; nor around the base of the brain, as Herophilus held; nor in its little membranes, as Strato and Erasistratus held; nor in the middle of the eyebrows, as Strato the Natural Philosopher held; nor throughout the whole breastplate of the chest, as Epicurus held. Rather, it is where the Egyptians also assigned it, and where those who seemed to be recorders of divine matters placed it, as does that verse of Orpheus or Empedocles:

> For in man the blood surrounding the heart is sensation.

[Note: [Col. 0671C] Junius reads: “in its membrane.”]

[Note: [Col. 0671C] Strato the Natural Philosopher. This also comes from Plutarch. He is called Strato “the Natural Philosopher” both to distinguish him from the preceding Strato the physician and because, according to Suidas, he devoted himself to the study of nature more than others. Suidas makes him a native of Lampsacus, a son of Theophrastus, and reports that he wrote many books. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0671C] “Nor throughout the whole breastplate of the chest.” The corresponding Greek in Plutarch reads: *en holō tō thōraki* (ἐν ὅλῳ τῷ θώρακι), “in the whole chest.” PAM.]

[Col. 0672A]

[Note: [Col. 0672B] “And where” (*et quod*), Rhenanus, Pamelius, and Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0671C] “Assigned.” The Author appears here to take *renuntiare* in the sense of “attribute,” the same meaning which he discusses more fully below in *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 1. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0672B] “To it” (*ei*), Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler, and two manuscripts of Wouwer.]

[Note: [Col. 0671D] Recorders of divine matters. He seems to mean Hermes Trismegistus, Anebo, and Amabo, whom Plotinus, Iamblichus, and Plato himself generally followed. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0670D] “Who are guided by God regarding both points.” So reads the Agobard manuscript. “And now,” he says, “there are more Christians than all the philosophers and physicians,” Christians who, when investigating what judgment should be made regarding both points—that is, regarding that ruling faculty of the soul and the place and position of that faculty—are guided by God and learn from the sacred pages. Thus the ancient Christians, even when disputing with pagans, adduced the authority of Holy Scripture. The pagans rejected divine utterances unless they were proved by human, that is, purely philosophical, arguments. In this blindness Galen inveighs against Archigenes with the following prohibition: “He will not listen to unproved laws” (*nomous anapodeiktous*, νόμους ἀναποδείκτους), “such as those who have reached the school of Moses and Christ commonly employ” (*Mōyseōs kai Christou diatribēn aphigmenoi*, Μωυσέως καὶ Χριστοῦ διατριβὴν ἀφιγμένοι). These are Galen’s words in the second book *On Differences of the Pulse*. RIG.]

Protagoras, Apollodorus, and Chrysippus likewise hold these views; so that Asclepiades, confuted even by these men, may go looking for his goats bleating without hearts and chase away his flies flying without heads; and so that everyone may now know that those who have prejudged the organization of the human soul from the condition of animals are themselves living without heart and brain instead.

[Note: [Col. 0671D] Protagoras. Although Diogenes Laërtius mentions Protagoras, the philosopher of Abdera and pupil of Democritus, nevertheless, because no book *On the Soul* is listed among his works, and because the Author here appears once again to oppose other physicians to Asclepiades, I think that *Praxagoras* should be read. Yet I was unwilling to alter the text without the aid of a manuscript. Certainly the physician Praxagoras is celebrated by Pliny in the third place in the aforementioned book XXVI, chapter 2, etc. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0672C] Likewise Apollodorus. Here too, although Diogenes Laërtius lists Apollodorus of Athens, an Epicurean philosopher, I nevertheless think that the Author is speaking instead of another Apollodorus, one of those physicians whom Pliny discusses in book XX, chapter 4. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0672C] And Chrysippus. Here too I think that the Author is speaking of the physician Chrysippus rather than the philosopher mentioned above in chapter 5, for that physician is frequently cited by Pliny. PAM.]

## CHAPTER XVI

There is also a point relevant to the conclusion, namely that Plato divides the soul in two, into the rational and the irrational.

[Note: [Col. 0672B] “Faith” (*fidem*), Pamelius and Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0672C] “That Plato divides the soul in two,” etc. Similarly he said above, chapter 14: “It is divided into parts, but now into two by Plato.” Plato has this in dialogue 4 of the *Republic*: “It is therefore not unreasonable,” he says, “that these two parts are distinct and different from one another, so that we call the part by which the soul reasons [since it governs reason like a kindly and attentive mother, showing the right path of life] the rational part of the soul; but the part by which it loves, hungers, thirsts, and is affected as though distraught by other desires, the irrational part.” PAM.]

We too indeed applaud this definition, but not in such a way that both parts are attributed to nature. For the rational must be believed natural, since it was implanted in the soul from the beginning by a rational Author. For what would not be rational if God produced it by his command, much less that which he sent forth specifically by his own breath?

The irrational, however, must be understood as later, since it occurred through the serpent’s prompting—the very commission of their transgression—and thereafter took root and grew together with the soul, [Col. 0672B] so as by now to assume the likeness of a natural quality, because it occurred immediately at the beginning of nature.

[Note: [Col. 0672C] “The very thing” (*ipsum*), Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür.]

[Note: [Col. 0672D] “So as by now to assume the likeness of a natural quality.” Through the corruption and deformation of nature. This passage supports original sin. The Author deliberately said “after the likeness of a natural quality,” because neither corruption nor original sin is itself our nature, substance, or form, as Illyricus thought, but is something that has happened to nature. For, as Tertullian very properly intimates, if sin were our substance or nature, since every substance was created by God, it would follow that sin was created by God alone and implanted in Adam and his descendants. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0672D] “It occurred at the beginning.” As the illustrious Bossuet observes (*Defense of Tradition and the Holy Fathers*, vol. II, book VIII, chapter XXIX, p. 148), in Tertullian’s expression *primordium* one must understand not only a beginning in temporal sequence but also a beginning in principle and origin. By this is understood that immense change in our body and soul which occurred at the beginning and origin of the human race and which St Augustine undertook to defend against the Pelagians. Nor can the “in whom” of the Epistle to the Romans be better explained, nor can the fact that we all sinned in Adam be more expressly stated, than by saying that Adam’s sin passed into our nature. Tertullian acknowledges the natural consequence of this principle when he admits that even the infants of the faithful are born impure. Therefore Christ said: “Unless one is reborn of water and the Holy Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of heaven.” Thus every soul is reckoned in Adam until it is reckoned anew in Christ: impure for as long as it is not reckoned anew. Now, to be reckoned in Adam is not merely to be in misery and punishment, but also under curse, condemnation, perdition, and sin. He therefore adds: every soul is sinful because it is impure, and it remains impure until regenerated through baptism. This sacrament does not remove death or the root of concupiscence. Therefore, if baptism removes a stain from the soul, it surely removes the stain of sin, which, according to Tertullian, the soul contracted through its union with the flesh, owing to the disorderly concupiscence by which the flesh lusts against the spirit and which makes the flesh sinful before it can even exist. Behold the entire theology of original sin most clearly expounded! Behold the first sin transmitted into the nature of all human beings! Behold its propagation through the concupiscence of the flesh! Behold its remission in baptism! What more can be added? LUMP.]

But since Plato describes only the rational as present in the soul of God himself, if we also ascribe the irrational to the nature which our soul received from God, the irrational will equally belong to God as something natural, because God is the author of nature.

[Col. 0673A]

[Note: [Col. 0673C] “If we should ascribe” (*adscripserimus*), Pamelius, Rhenanus, and Semler.]

But the implantation of sin came from the devil, and every sin is irrational; therefore the irrational came from the devil, from whom sin also came. It is foreign to God, to whom the irrational is alien. Consequently, the difference between these two derives from the remoteness of their authors.

Accordingly, when Plato, reserving the rational for God alone, subdivides two kinds from the rational—the irascible, which they call *thymikon* (θυμικόν), and the appetitive, which they call *epithymētikon* (ἐπιθυμητικόν)—so that the former is common to us and lions, the latter to us and flies, while the rational is common to us and God, I see that I must reconsider this point as well because of what is found in Christ.

For behold, this entire triad is also present in the Lord: the rational, by which he teaches, discusses, and lays out the paths of salvation; the irascible, by which he inveighs against the scribes and Pharisees; and the appetitive, by which he desires to eat the Passover with his disciples.

[Col. 0673B]

[Note: [Col. 0674C] This entire triad. That is, the threefold function of the reasoning, angry, and desiring soul is indicated in the Gospel: the rational and teaching function in Matthew 7 and 12; the indignant or angry function in Matthew 23; and the desiring or appetitive function in Luke 22. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0673C] “By which” (*quae*), François and Pamelius.]

Therefore, among us the irascible and appetitive faculties are not always to be reckoned as proceeding from the irrational, since we are certain that in the Lord they operated rationally.

[Note: [Col. 0674C] “In the Lord” (*Domino*), Pamelius and François.]

God will be indignant rationally—that is, against those who deserve it—and God will rationally desire things worthy of himself. For he will both be indignant at evil and, for good, desire salvation.

[Note: [Col. 0674D] “God will be indignant rationally.” If this is understood hypothetically, in the manner in which Scripture speaks—so that God’s indignation is taken for God’s vengeance—nothing prevents its attribution to God. Elsewhere, however, we have noted that it must be numbered among Tertullian’s paradoxes that he appears to attribute anger properly so called to God. PAM.]

The Apostle too grants us desire (1 Timothy 3:1): “If anyone desires the episcopate, he desires a good work”; and by calling it a good work he shows the desire to be rational. He also permits indignation; why should he not, when he himself entertained it?

[Note: [Col. 0674D] “I wish they would even cut themselves off.” Paul directs this against the Jews in Galatians, chapter 5: “I wish,” he says, “that those who recall you to circumcision and the other Mosaic observances would not merely be circumcised but cut themselves off as well”: *ophelon kai apokopsontai* (ὄφελον καὶ ἀποκόψονται). Chrysostom: *mē peritemnesthōsan monon, alla kai perikoptesthōsan* (μὴ περιτεμνέσθωσαν μόνον, ἀλλὰ καὶ περικοπτέσθωσαν), “Let them not only be circumcised, but also be cut off.” And in Primasius of Hadrumetum: “Let them not only be circumcised but also be cut off, if the removal of the member is advantageous.” RIG.]

“I wish,” he says (Galatians 5:12), “that those who unsettle you would even cut themselves off!” Indignation which proceeds from zeal for discipline is rational.

[Col. 0673C]

But when he says (Ephesians 2:3), “We were once by nature children of wrath,” he stigmatizes the irrational irascible faculty, which does not derive from that nature which is from God, but from the nature introduced by the devil. The devil himself is called the lord of his own order (Matthew 6:24): “You cannot serve two lords”; and he is also given the name father (John 8:44): “You are of your father the devil.” Do not be afraid, then, to ascribe to him possession of another nature, a later and adulterous one, since you read that he is the sower of darnel over the crop (Matthew 13) and the nocturnal corrupter of the wheat-field.

[Col. 0674A]

## CHAPTER XVII

We must also encounter the question concerning those five senses which we learn in our first lessons, since from this subject too some support is furnished to the heretics. These senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch.

The Academics condemn their reliability rather harshly; according to some, Heraclitus, Diocles, and Empedocles do so as well; and Plato certainly does in the *Timaeus*, where he declares sense-perception irrational and entangled with opinion.

[Col. 0674B]

[Note: [Col. 0674C] “Declares” (*pronuntiat*), Pamelius and Rhenanus.]

Thus sight is charged with falsehood because it asserts, contrary to our knowledge of their straightness, that oars in water are bent or broken; because it persuades us that a square tower is round when seen from a distance; because it misrepresents a perfectly uniform portico as narrower at its far end; and because it joins the heaven, suspended at so great a height, to the sea. Hearing is likewise accused of deception: as when we suppose a rumbling to be in the sky although it is a wagon; or, when thunder is gathering, confidently believe the sound comes from a wagon.

Smell and taste are similarly impeached, since the same perfumes and the same wines decline in value when used again. Touch is similarly criticized, since the same pavements are judged rougher by the hands but smoother by the feet; and in the baths, the same pool of hot water is reported first to be extremely hot and afterward perfectly temperate.

[Col. 0674C]

“To such a degree,” they say, “are we also deceived by the senses when we change our judgments.”

[Note: [Col. 0674D] The Stoics are more moderate. The Stoics declare that the senses are truthful, but that some impressions are true and others false, as Plutarch observes in book IV, chapter 9, *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*. Epicurus asserted that every impression and all the senses are true, but that some opinions are true and others are not. In Clement of Alexandria the senses are *epibathra tēs epistēmēs* (ἐπίβαθρα τῆς ἐπιστήμης), “stepping-stones of knowledge.” LE PR.]

The Stoics are more moderate and do not burden every sense, or any sense at all times, with the charge of falsehood. The Epicureans more resolutely defend an equal and perpetual truth in all the senses, though by a different route: it is not the sense, they say, that lies, but opinion; for sense undergoes an impression but does not form an opinion, whereas the soul forms an opinion.

[Col. 0675A]

[Note: [Col. 0675C] Junius deletes “for”; Rigault and the Venetian edition do not add it.]

They have severed opinion from sensation and sensation from the soul. Yet whence comes opinion, if not from sensation? Indeed, unless sight has perceived the tower as round, no opinion of roundness will arise. And whence comes sensation, if not from the soul? Indeed, a body lacking a soul will also lack sensation. Thus sensation comes from the soul, opinion from sensation, and the soul is the whole.

But it will be most properly proposed that there is assuredly something which causes a thing to be reported by the senses otherwise than it exists in reality. Moreover, if something which does not exist in reality can be reported, why can it not likewise be reported through something which does not reside in the senses, but in those conditions which intervene on their own account? And so it will be possible to identify those conditions.

[Note: [Col. 0675D] For example, an oar bent in water. If an oar immersed in water sometimes appears broken, or a square tower appears round, this should not be attributed to a defect and error in the senses, but to the understanding. For sensation is a faculty devoid of reasoning, and therefore that appearance cannot deceive it; yet the same does not hold true of the understanding. The understanding participates in reason; and when it becomes aware that sensation has occurred, it does not proceed farther to examine it but renders a hasty judgment. Consequently, every error must be attributed to it, since it does not weigh the matters which belong to its own function. LE PR.]

For example, water is the cause of an oar’s appearing bent or broken in water. Indeed, outside the water the oar appears intact to sight. The delicacy of that substance, through which a mirror-like effect is produced from light, according as it is struck or moved, likewise distorts the straight line when it reflects the image.

[Col. 0675B]

Again, the conditions of distance in an open space compel the tower’s shape to deceive us: for the uniformity of the surrounding air, clothing the angles with equal light, effaces their lines. Similarly, the uniformity of a portico sharpens to a point at its far end, because the visual ray, crowded into a confined space, grows thinner precisely where it is prolonged. Likewise, the sky is joined to the sea where vision is exhausted; for as long as sight retains its force, it keeps them separate.

What else deceives hearing but the similarity of sounds? And if perfume afterward breathes less fragrance, wine tastes less strongly, and the pool feels less hot, in almost every case the first impression possesses the whole force.

[Note: [Col. 0675C] “The whole” (*totum*), Junius.]

As for the rough and the smooth, the hands and feet rightly disagree, since the former are delicate members and the latter calloused.

[Col. 0675C]

Thus, in this manner, no deception of the senses will be without a cause. But if causes deceive the senses, and opinions through the senses, falsehood must no longer be located either in the senses, which follow the causes, or in opinions, which are directed by the senses that follow those causes.

The insane see one person in another: Orestes sees his mother in his sister, Ajax sees Ulysses in a herd of cattle, while Athamas and Agave see wild beasts in their children. Will you reproach their eyes for this falsehood, or their madness?

Those who are jaundiced through an excess of bile find everything bitter. Will you therefore reproach their taste for betrayal, or their state of health?

[Note: [Col. 0675D] Those who are jaundiced through an excess of bile. George of Baetica also treated this passage against the Arians in his discussion of the Wisdom of God: “They behold nothing in it of the most unalloyed beauty,” he says, “just as no vision of truth exists for inflamed eyes, since their sight reports otherwise than accords with the reality of things. So too, if the excessive bitterness of his own bile has corrupted someone’s proper sense of taste, then, should he taste sweet honey, he slanders the honey’s sweetness as bitter, failing to recognize the defect of his own bitterness.” LE PR.]

[Col. 0676A]

All the senses, therefore, are overthrown or circumvented only temporarily, so that they lack the inherent property of falsehood. Indeed, the verdict of falsehood must no longer be assigned even to the causes themselves. For if these things occur according to reason, reason does not deserve to be called falsehood. What must occur in this way is not a lie.

Accordingly, if the causes themselves are freed from reproach, how much more are the senses, before which the causes now proceed free of blame! Indeed, truth, reliability, and integrity must be vindicated for the senses chiefly on this ground: that they report nothing other than what that rational condition commanded which causes a thing to be reported by the senses otherwise than it exists in reality.

What are you doing, most shameless Academy? You overthrow the whole condition of life; you disturb the entire order of nature; you blind the providence of God himself, who, for understanding, inhabiting, administering, and enjoying all his works, would in that case have placed over them deceitful and lying masters—the senses.

[Note: [Col. 0675D] “Most shameless Academic,” Pamelius and Rhenanus.]

[Col. 0676B]

Is not the entire creation supplied to us through them? Did not the world also receive through them its secondary equipment: so many arts, talents, studies, occupations, duties, commercial exchanges, remedies, plans, comforts, and every provision, refinement, and ornament of life? They have seasoned the entire savor of life, since through these senses man alone among all creatures is recognized as a rational animal, capable of understanding—and capable even of the Academy itself.

But Plato, to avoid affixing any attestation to the senses, therefore denies in the *Phaedrus*, speaking in the person of Socrates, that he can know himself, as the Delphic inscription admonishes; and in the *Theaetetus* he deprives himself both of knowing and of perceiving. In the *Phaedrus* he postpones the judgment of truth until after death, making it posthumous; yet, although not yet dead, he was philosophizing.

[Note: [Col. 0676D] “And in the *Phaedrus* he postpones it until after death.” I could not find the precise words in Plato’s *Phaedrus*, but the reference seems to me to concern his philosophical account there of souls which, after this life, are deemed worthy to contemplate in the supracelestial place that which truly exists, or truth itself, and which, after beholding the things that truly exist, return again to the inner region of heaven; all of them, indeed, are held by an intense desire to see where the plain of truth may be. PAM.—“And in the *Phaedrus*.” I am very nearly persuaded that *Phaedo* should be read, as in the following chapter, where he adduces the testimony. Observe also the words “he postpones it until after death.” For Socrates, being about to die, composed the *Phaedo* and philosophized after taking the poison. LE PR.]

[Col. 0676C]

We may not, we may not call these senses into doubt, lest their reliability in Christ too be called into question.

[Note: [Col. 0676C] Pamelius and Rhenanus write “We may not” only once.]

[Note: [Col. 0676D] “Be weighed,” Junius.]

Otherwise it might perhaps be said that he falsely saw Satan hurled down from heaven (Luke 10:18), or falsely heard the Father’s voice testifying about him (Matthew 3:17); or that he was deceived when he touched Peter’s mother-in-law (Matthew 8:15); or that he afterward perceived a different fragrance in the ointment which he accepted for his burial, and afterward a different taste in the wine which he consecrated in memory of his blood. For in this way Marcion too preferred to believe him a phantom, disdaining the reality of the whole body in him.

[Col. 0677A]

Yet not even in his Apostles was nature made the object of mockery. Both sight and hearing were trustworthy on the mountain (Matthew 17); the taste of that wine at the wedding in Galilee was trustworthy, although it had previously been water (John 2); and thereafter the touch of believing Thomas was trustworthy (John 20).

[Note: [Col. 0677D] The taste of that wine was trustworthy, although it had previously been water. Away, then, with that trifler Nicephorus, who speaks of Christ’s first miracle in such a way that someone might consider it the fraud of an illusionist: “Wine sprang from unseen clusters,” he says—*ex adēlōn botryōn oinos epēgazeto* (ἐξ ἀδήλων βοτρύων οἶνος ἐπηγάζετο). No: it sprang “from lowly water-jars”—*ex atimōn hydriōn* (ἐξ ἀτίμων ὑδριῶν). RIG.]

Recite John’s testimony: “What we have seen,” he says (1 John 1:1), “what we have heard, we have seen with our eyes, and our hands have handled, concerning the Word of life.” The testimony is certainly false if the natural sense of eyes, ears, and hands lies.

[Note: [Col. 0677D] “What we have seen,” he says, “what we have heard.” The Author perhaps cites this passage somewhat differently, so as to preserve the order of the senses of sight and hearing, and more clearly than it is read in Greek, Latin, or Syriac; namely: “What we have heard, what we have seen with our eyes, what we have looked upon.” PAM.]

## CHAPTER XVIII

I turn to the province of intellectual things: how Plato, after separating it from bodily things, recommended it to the heretics, having attained recognition before death.

[Col. 0677B]

For he says in the *Phaedo*:

> What, then, of the possession of wisdom itself? Will the body be an impediment, or not, if anyone takes it as a partner in the inquiry? I mean something like this: do sight and hearing possess any truth for human beings, or not? Do not even the poets continually mutter these things to us—that we neither hear anything certain nor see anything certain?
