But to speak also of the Churches of the West, St. Ambrose declares as follows in Book II of *On Penance*, ch. 10: “They are deservedly rebuked who think that penance should be performed repeatedly, who indulge themselves wantonly in Christ; for if they truly performed penance, they would not think it should afterwards be repeated, because, just as there is ONE BAPTISM, so there is ONE PENANCE—namely, that which is performed PUBLICLY.”

Accordingly, those guilty of certain graver crimes customarily received absolution only in the final struggle of death—indeed, as some plausibly believe, not even then—meaning solemn and public absolution, according to the discipline of most Churches.

Nor should this rigor, when account is taken of the times and other circumstances, be regarded as excessive or as displaying a kind of cruelty. For the greater strength of human nature at that time, its extreme corruption—especially among peoples recently converted to Christ’s Church—and its propensity toward certain kinds of offenses, particularly in the hotter [Col. 1224B] regions, needed to be restrained by a salutary terror from committing those offenses further and from performing penance too sluggishly. Otherwise, the Church’s laxity, which would then have been harmful, and the hope of obtaining pardon from her repeatedly might have encouraged new and more frequent relapses, with examples of this kind readily producing pernicious scandals among others also.

Furthermore, the penance of those who had relapsed after performing it once was judged not to have been sufficiently earnest and efficacious; and therefore the superiors of the Churches believed that no safe reliance could any longer be placed upon a second penance, whatever works might demonstrate it. Δόκησις τοίνυν μετανοίας, οὐ μετάνοια, τὸ πολλάκις αἰτεῖσθαι συγγνώμην, ἐφ’ οἷς πλημμελοῦμεν πολλάκις: “For it is the appearance of repentance, not repentance itself, repeatedly to ask pardon for those things in which we repeatedly offend,” as Clement of Alexandria again says in Book II of the *Stromata*. Consequently, it came about that persons who relapsed in this fashion were granted absolution only at the end of life—indeed, in at least some Churches, not even then, as many [Col. 1224C] plausibly believe. Yet those sinners, now fallen a second time, were not therefore to despair as though they must necessarily perish; for, as we read above from St. Augustine, they were left to the patience and mercy of God, so that through perfect contrition they might still be freed from their sins, if they truly and earnestly repented, and thereafter be saved. Indeed, it is highly probable that assistance was given to them, not only in the final struggle but even while they were still healthy and vigorous, through private sacramental absolution, according to the nature and requirements of their circumstances.

Yet these persons undoubtedly remained in a condition still sufficiently wretched and not to be envied by others, since they would henceforth lack both the public prayers of the Church, which were otherwise poured forth over public penitents, and, above all, communion in the Holy Eucharist. Finally, they would have to make satisfaction by suffering more severely and for a longer time in purgatory, unless they devoted themselves all the more earnestly to making satisfaction even in this life through internal acts of penance and external works of penance [Col. 1224D], undertaken voluntarily or at the judgment of priests.

For the rest, just as that severity of discipline was not the same in every Church, neither did it endure equally everywhere. In the Eastern Church, when, toward the close of the fourth century, Nectarius, Patriarch of Constantinople, abolished the public confession and penance of secret sins—and when the same practice, though later, prevailed in the Western Church—secret sins also were subjected only to secret confession. This could be repeated frequently and after any relapse, with the hope of again obtaining private absolution.

Eventually, the practice of public penance almost entirely fell into disuse, when even for public and more atrocious offenses nothing but private and secret penance was imposed in sacramental confession, as the circumstances of the times required; the offenses themselves, however, were left to the ecclesiastical judge to punish by canonical censures and the like. [Col. 1225A] For when that primitive fervor of the faithful had already cooled considerably, and their relaxed conscience had broken through the barriers of public penance, while the Church’s former rigor no longer seemed capable of being adapted to the political condition—and especially to the position of rulers—because of the hardness of their hearts, the Church, as a loving and provident mother, judged that she must at least come to the aid of her disobedient children’s salvation through leniency, and especially through opening more liberally the treasury of indulgences, by which that public penance could be supplied and compensated for. Let it suffice to have said these few things, despite the great breadth of this subject, in accordance with the purpose of our little work and as occasion prompted; the rest may be learned more fully from our dogmatic theologians.

From this it is now evident that the ancient severity of the Church in excluding a second penance lent no support whatever to the errors either of the Montanists or of the Novatians. For they attacked the Church’s very power to forgive the sins of those who relapsed after [Col. 1225B] Baptism, and attempted utterly to overthrow it. The Church, however, having received from Christ the POWER both of loosing and of binding, in Matthew 18:18 and John 20:23, regulated only the USE of each power, as something placed within her own discretion, so that at such a time, as often, and in such a manner as, when, and however frequently she judged it expedient, she might by her own authority and through her priests either forgive or retain the sins duly disclosed by penitents, while preserving inviolate all other things required for this sacrament by the institution of Christ himself.

**Quanto in arcto**: for *quanto arctius*, “the more constrainedly.”

**Tanto operosior probatio est**: that is, it must be demonstrated by correspondingly more numerous and arduous works of penance, so that proof may be given that it is earnest and sincere.

**Sola conscientia**: that is, by the mind alone, or by an internal act.

**Proferatur.** Junius reads *perferatur*, which I cannot disapprove; nevertheless, I prefer to retain the commonly received reading. [Col. 1225C]

**Aliquo etiam actu**: that is, by some external and perceptible act; for here he takes *actus* in the sense of a deed, as is gathered from the note **Agitur** on Chapter III.

**Exomologesis.** Ἐξομολόγησις (*exomologēsis*) is derived from ἐξομολογέω (*exomologeō*), which in the sacred writers properly means “to confess.” As Tertullian himself defines it in *On Prayer*, ch. 6, it is a petition for pardon, because one who asks pardon confesses an offense. It is useful to offer some examples. Thus in Psalm 27:7, where the Hebrew has [Hebrew text absent in the supplied source], Aquila translates: Καὶ ἀπὸ ἄσματός μου ἐξομολογήσομαι αὐτῷ, “And from my song I shall confess to him”; but the Septuagint has: Καὶ ἐκ θελήματός μου ἐξομολογήσομαι αὐτῷ, which our Vulgate renders: “And from my will I shall confess to him.” It does not say “IN my will,” but “FROM my will,” so that the confession is not retained merely within the will but is also outwardly manifested. So too in Psalm 29:10, where Symmachus renders the Hebrew thus: μὴ ἐξομολογήσεταί σοι κόνις, ἢ ἀπαγγελεῖ τὴν ἀλήθειάν σου; “Will dust confess to you, or proclaim your truth?” [Col. 1225D] Here, therefore, it signifies a confession that is a kind of proclamation, consisting in an external act and metaphorically applied to dust. Many other examples to this effect could be produced from the Old Law.

To come also to the New Testament, Matthew 3:6 says of John: “They were baptized by him, confessing [ἐξομολογούμενοι, *exomologoumenoi*] their sins.” This was certainly an external confession, demonstrated, that is, by external acts. For John did not administer his baptism indiscriminately, but only to those whose earnest repentance could be recognized from external works. So too Acts 19:18 states: “And many of those who believed came, confessing”—where again the word is ἐξομολογούμενοι—“and declaring,” externally, of course, “their deeds.” And what could be clearer than the Apostle’s words in Romans 10:10: “With the heart belief is made unto justice, but WITH THE MOUTH CONFESSION IS MADE [στόματι δὲ ὁμολογεῖται] unto salvation”? Although [Col. 1226A] I am not unaware that among secular writers the word is sometimes used in another sense, nevertheless in Holy Scripture ἐξομολόγησις is regularly expressed by confession performed with the mouth. Moreover, it is also used specifically for the confession of sins in 1 John 1:9: Ἐὰν ὁμολογῶμεν τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν, κ.τ.λ., “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all iniquity.” Likewise James 5:16: Ἐξομολογεῖσθε ἀλλήλοις τὰ παραπτώματα ὑμῶν, “Confess your sins to one another,” etc. Hence among Greek theologians *exomologesis*, used without qualification, is generally taken for the confession of sins, indeed for the entire penitential action insofar as it also includes works of satisfaction. In this very meaning it was transferred to the Latins also and has continued in the same use to the present.

But in the eighth century it began to be extended somewhat more broadly even to litanies, or public supplications, as St. Isidore bears witness in *Etymologies*, Book VI, ch. 19, near the end:

> The difference between LITANIES and EXOMOLOGESIS is that acts of exomologesis are performed solely for the confession of sins, whereas litanies are so called because they are used to entreat the Lord and obtain his mercy in some matter. But now both words are treated as having one meaning, and in common usage it makes no difference whether they are called litanies or exomologesis.

[Col. 1226B] Meanwhile, among the Easterners that ancient meaning remained, as is gathered from Chapter II of the first letter sent to the Lutherans in 1574 by Jeremiah, Patriarch of Constantinople; indeed, it is also still frequently employed by our Latin theologians today.

Nor have we dwelt somewhat longer upon this word without reason. For since our theologians regularly employ it particularly against the Calvinists, “one must proceed cautiously and prudently,” as Petavius observes in his *Annotations on St. Epiphanius*, at Heresy 89, “in interpreting this word, so that from the very context of the account we may judge in what sense it was employed by the Fathers.”

**Confessione**: that is, “through confession,” for this precedes satisfaction. An injury done to another is not repaired through satisfaction unless that very satisfaction has proceeded from a penitent heart and a confessing mouth. [Col. 1226C]

**Poenitentia nascitur.** Here he is speaking of the works of penance, which he enumerates more precisely immediately afterward.

**Prosternendi.** There were formerly four orders, or as it were degrees, of penitents: namely, the Weepers, the Hearers, the Prostrated, and the Co-standers.

The **WEEPERS** (προσκλαίοντες, *prosklaiontes*) were those who, outside the church door, beneath the portico or some lower part of its roof, clothed in sackcloth, sprinkled with ashes, etc., knelt or, in proportion to the gravity of their crimes, even lay prostrate with their whole body. Amid bitter tears, and sometimes amid sobbing and wailing, they implored those entering and leaving to assist them with prayers and to intercede with the bishop for their reception into the Church.

The **HEARERS** (ἀκροώμενοι, *akroōmenoi*, or “listeners”), having now been admitted within the church door, stood in the place called the νάρθηξ (*narthex*), or “rod”—as though designated specifically for those who were still under the rod of ecclesiastical discipline—which was separated from the church itself by wooden partitions or screens. They were admitted there to hear the divine Scriptures and other sacred readings, but were expelled again when the Mass of the catechumens began, as not yet worthy to share in the prayers of the rest of the faithful. [Col. 1226D]

The **PROSTRATED** (ὑποπίπτοντες, *hypopiptontes*) were those who were allowed a place within the church itself as far as the ambo, the place appointed for reading the Epistle and Gospel. They could thus both join their prayers to those of the rest of the faithful and, while kneeling or prostrate upon the ground, receive frequent impositions of hands—of which some believe at least the final one to have been sacramental, that is, an absolution from sins. But when the Oblation, which we call the Offertory, began, they were compelled to leave the church. [Col. 1227A] This order of the Prostrated was often called absolutely and simply “Penance” by the Latin Fathers, and the Prostrated themselves were called “penitents.” The first two classes were regarded merely as dispositions toward penance itself; but in this third class satisfaction itself was performed through laborious works of penance—fasts, vigils, and other practices tormenting the flesh—which were undertaken not now spontaneously and at one’s own discretion, but by ordinance of the Church herself, as she measured them in proportion to the offenders’ crimes. This may be seen in St. Pacian’s exceedingly beautiful *Exhortation to Penance*, in Juenin’s *Treatise on Penance*, Chapter IV, art. 3, and in others.

Finally, the **CO-STANDERS** (συνεστῶτες, *synestōtes*) were those who stood between the ambo and the sanctuary and were permitted, together with the rest of the faithful, to be present at the Sacrifice, but were excluded solely from participation in the sacred Table, or Eucharist, in order to prove their constancy.

Only when penitents had passed through these orders did they obtain public and solemn absolution from their offenses—an absolution that was finally sacramental—to be given by the bishop or, at his command, by a presbyter; thereafter they were restored to communion at the Holy Table and with the entire Church. [Col. 1227B]

Now, just as there is no doubt that from the times of the Apostles all Churches everywhere exercised great severity, admitting only those whom they had proved over a considerable period to be truly penitent from the heart, so it is also certain that neither the manner nor the order of admitting them was observed identically at all times or in every place. Certainly our Tertullian, otherwise so diligent in enumerating the traditions of his own times and the observances of discipline—especially those of the African Churches, already more rigorous than others—nevertheless nowhere makes an explicit distinction among those four classes.

Nor do they appear to have been in use in the Roman Church at that time. For Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book V, ch. 27, speaks only as follows concerning the penitent Natalius:

> In the morning he arose, clothed in haircloth and sackcloth and sprinkled with ashes, and with great grief and tears cast himself as a suppliant at the feet of Pope Zephyrinus—who held his see during Tertullian’s own period, from the year 197 to 217—and prostrated himself not only at the knees of the clergy but also at those of the laity, so that the Church of the merciful Christ, greatly moved with compassion for him, joined him in lamentation.

[Col. 1227C] Yet he—observe the rigor of the discipline of those times—although he employed many entreaties and displayed the marks of the blows he had endured, for he had been chastised very severely one night by an angel while living in the company of heretics, was only with difficulty at last received into communion in the Church. Yet no order of the aforesaid classes is recorded as having been observed here.

Observe also the custom of performing penance that prevailed in the Church of Carthage in the time of St. Cyprian, who came after Tertullian. Thus he says in his treatise *On the Lapsed*:

> One must pray more earnestly and make supplication, pass the day in mourning, spend the nights in vigils and weeping, occupy all one’s time with tearful lamentations, lie stretched upon the ground and cling to the ashes, roll in sackcloth and filth; after losing the garment of Christ, desire no garment any longer; after the devil’s food, prefer fasting; apply oneself to just works by which sins are purged; persist frequently in almsgiving, etc.

Here again no succession of those classes is observed—a matter St. Cyprian certainly would not have passed over in silence when he speaks so carefully elsewhere also, and indeed expressly, about the use of public penance. It is nevertheless more probable that those classes of penitents were introduced around Cyprian’s own time, not indeed in Africa but in the East. For St. Gregory, Bishop of Neocaesarea in Pontus—commonly called the Wonderworker because of the holiness of his life and the multitude of miracles performed by him—who went to heaven in A.D. 265, is found to have been the first to enumerate them in his *Canonical Epistle*: provided, however, that its final canon belongs to Gregory himself, which some wish to doubt, and was not rather appended by some later Greek author. [Col. 1228A]

At least from the final canon of St. Basil the Great, Bishop of Caesarea, in his *Canonical Epistle to Amphilochius*, it is certain that this division of penance into four degrees—πρόσκλαυσις (*prosklausis*), weeping; ἀκρόασις (*akroasis*), hearing; ὑπόπτωσις (*hypoptōsis*), prostration; and σύστασις (*systasis*), standing together—already prevailed in the fourth century, for St. Basil is known to have departed to heaven in the year 379. But occasion has so carried me along that, contrary to the design of this little work I have undertaken, I have again become somewhat more prolix. Yet our dogmatic theologians will provide far more, especially that most learned work of Fr. Jean Morin, priest of the Congregation of the Oratory, the *Historical Commentary on the Discipline of Penance*, etc., in which he appears almost single-handedly to have exhausted this vast subject.

**Humilificandi.** In more classical Latin one would say *humiliandi*, “to be humbled.”

**Conversationem.** Here it is taken for the manner and mode of associating with people and conducting oneself among them, which the Greeks customarily call ὁμιλία (*homilia*).

[Col. 1228B] **Misericordiae illicem**: that is, something capable of attracting and eliciting God’s mercy. *Illex, illicis*, as Festus says, is a word derived from *illicere*, “to entice.” In this sense our author, *Apology*, ch. 41, calls the pagans “enticers of evils”; and in *On Patience*, ch. 13, he says that patience draws forth God’s clemency. There is also *illex, illegis*, meaning one who lives without law; but that is irrelevant to our subject.

**Habitu**: that is, in clothing.

**Sacco.** A sackcloth garment was a coarse robe woven from the rougher hair of goats, asses, or camels, and was worn as a sign of mourning. The word itself is of Hebrew origin; in that language a mournful garment of this kind is called [Hebrew term absent in the supplied source]. Thus in 2 Samuel 3:31 David said to Joab and to all the people: “Tear your garments, and gird yourselves with () SACKCLOTH.” And in 1 Kings 21:27, when Ahab heard the words of the prophet Elijah, “he covered his flesh with HAIRCLOTH, fasted, and slept IN SACKCLOTH,” etc. See also 2 Kings 14:1 and Nehemiah 9:1, as well as many other places.

[Col. 1228C] Moreover, this word passed from the Hebrews to the Greeks, and from them to the Latins. Thus St. John the Evangelist—writing, of course, in Greek—says in Revelation 6:12: “The sun became black like σάκκος τρίχινος,” or, as our Vulgate renders it, “HAIR SACKCLOTH.” What then? Is it not remarkable that the same word penetrated to the Italians, French, Spaniards, English, Flemings, Hungarians, etc., and that the same Hebrew word, *Sack*, even spread into Germany itself?

**Cineri incubare**: to lie upon ashes. Ashes also—that is, the dust of the earth—were used as a public sign of mourning, as almost innumerable examples, including those of the Old Testament, demonstrate. Especially notable are those found in the Book of Esther, such as 4:1 concerning Mordecai: “He tore his garments, was clothed in SACKCLOTH, AND sprinkled ASHES upon his head.” And in the same chapter, verse 3: “There was great mourning among the Jews, fasting, wailing, and weeping, many using SACKCLOTH AND ASHES as their bed.” Likewise Nehemiah 9:1: “The children of Israel assembled with fasting, in sackcloth, AND EARTH”—that is, the dust of the ground—“upon [Col. 1228D] them.”

**Corpus sordibus obscurare**: to darken the body with filth, by obscuring its brightness.

**Quae peccavit.** Nor is it unusual among Latin authors to construe the verb *peccare*, “to offend,” with an accusative. Thus in Plautus’ *Bacchides*: “If you had offended by a single syllable,” etc. There is something similar in Cicero, *On Duties*, Book I, where he says: “Many offenses are committed in the republic”; and in the *Paradoxes*: “Whatever offense is committed is committed in a disturbance of reason,” etc.

**Tractationi mutare**: for *retractare*, “to handle again,” as frequently elsewhere.

**Pura**: that is, simple. Thus in *On Patience*, ch. 13, he says: “Content with simple food and a pure drink of water.”

**Jejuniis preces alere**: to nourish prayers with fasts. An elegant metaphor, joined antithetically to fasting. And indeed, the more that is withdrawn from the body, the more the soul is to be nourished and, as it were, fattened.

**Mugire**: to bellow. A bellow, elsewhere proper to cattle, is here taken for wailing expressive of the greatest force of grief.

[Col. 1229A] **Charis.** Some manuscripts have *aris*, “altars”; but I have retained *charis*, or, as the ancients customarily wrote it, *caris*: that is, those dear to God, namely the devout faithful, and especially confessors and martyrs.

**Legationes dep. suae injungere**: to entrust to them embassies of his supplication. That is, so that they might intercede with God on behalf of the penitents. And why should those who are already beyond this mortality, nearer to God and indeed intimately united with him, not be all the more capable of doing this? Or why should they not hear our prayers? Why should God hear their prayers for us less than those of the living?

**Timore Dominum honoret**: let him honor the Lord by fear. For above, in Chapter VII, he had already said: “A man’s fear”—that is, when he salutarily fears God whom he has offended—“is honor to God.”

**Pronuntians sententiam**: pronouncing a sentence, as one to be delivered against a guilty person.

**Pro Dei indignatione fungatur**: that is, let him perform the office of God’s wrath and vengeance, forestalling it.

**Afflictione.** *Afflictio* affects the mind; *afflictatio*, as Cicero says in *Tusculan Disputations*, Book IV, is distress accompanied by torment of the body.

[Col. 1229B] **Non modo frustretur, sed expungat**: let it not merely elude but erase. One *frustrates* who avoids and evades; one *expunges* who utterly deletes and abolishes, barring every possibility of return. But how elegant are the antitheses that immediately follow! For the rest, we have spoken more fully above about the verb *expungere*, in the note **Expungit** on Chapter III.

### Notes on Chapter X

**Praesumo** is used here for “I think,” “I suppose,” or “I judge in anticipation.” Thus our author says in the *Apology*, ch. 2: “Since you prejudge our crimes from the confession of the name,” etc.

**Conscientiam**: that is, knowledge obtained from disclosure.

**Prodactae**: that is, rashly cast away or poured forth. Thus in *On the Soul*, ch. 48, he says: “They affirm that more certain and fully unfolded dreams occur toward the close of night, as though the vigor of souls were already emerging and sleep had been prolonged.”

[Col. 1229C] **Reformari**: that is, to be re-formed for salvation, by restoring it in oneself.

**Nae tu verecundia bonus**: “Truly, you are good through your modesty.” A sufficiently bitter irony.

**Expandens frontem**: spreading out the brow. The brow, as the index of the mind, is broadened by audacity.

**Ad deprecandum vero subducens**: but drawing it back when it comes to supplication. That same brow is lowered through dejection and faintheartedness.

**De detrimento ejus.** This is a ὑστερολογία (*hysterologia*, an inversion of natural word order), and should be restored to its proper order thus: “I incur more loss from it,” or “more of the loss that is customarily inflicted through it.”

**In risiloquio**: in mocking speech; the Greeks customarily call this σαρκασμός (*sarkasmos*) or εἰρωνεία (*eirōneia*), and the Latins *irrisio*, ridicule.

**Prostrato superscenditur**: one climbs over a person who has been laid low. Surely nothing baser or more ignoble can be said than that someone should wish to rise by depressing others when unable to do so by his own virtue.

**Spes.** I read *spes*, “hope,” most emphatically, in place of *species*, a word that must surely be expelled from this passage as utterly absurd here.

**Plausores**: that is, mockers. For he likewise has in the *Apology*, ch. 35: “Mockers are exposed every day.”

**Ad remedium conlabor et necesse est**: I must of necessity labor together toward the remedy. He is certainly alluding to Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 12:26: “If one member suffers anything, all the members suffer together. Now you are the body of Christ, and members belonging to a member.” [Col. 1229D]

**Ecclesia vero Christus**: but the Church is Christ. For, as the Apostle again says in Ephesians 5:23: “Christ is the head of the Church”; and in Colossians 1:18: “He is the HEAD of the body, the CHURCH.”

**Christum exoras**: you entreat Christ. For Christ is sometimes taken not merely for the head of the Church, but for the head and members together—that is, for the entire body of the Church. Thus St. Augustine says in *Eighty-Three Questions*, question 69: “The whole may also rightly be called Christ—that is, the head together with his body, which is the CHURCH.” Hence the same author says in his *Letter to Honoratus on the Grace of the New Testament*, ch. 7: “The Church suffered in Christ when he suffered for the Church, just as he himself suffered in the Church when the Church suffered for him.” Again, in his exposition of Psalm 88, he argues thus:

> When he was already seated in heaven, he did not cry out from heaven, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute my servants?” nor “Why do you persecute [Col. 1230A] my saints?” nor “Why do you persecute my disciples?” but “Why do you persecute ME?” Therefore, just as, when no one was persecuting him as he sat in heaven, he cried, “Why do you persecute ME?” because the HEAD acknowledged his members, and love did not separate the HEAD from the union of his body, so, etc.

Thus, I say, to our subject: what is done to the brethren, the members of the Church, is done to CHRIST. For does he not himself say in Matthew 25:40: “As long as you did it to one of the least of these my brethren, you did it to ME”?

**Pollicetur**: he promises. An irony continued also through the following sentence.

**Conscientia**: for knowledge simply.

**Pervenire**: to arrive. This anticipates a certain minor objection.

**Malo**: by evil, namely through sin.

**Cauterio.** *Cauterium*, “cautery,” is not derived from *cavere*, “to take care,” but is of Greek origin: καυτήριον (*kautērion*) is named ἀπὸ τοῦ καίειν, “from burning,” because it possesses the power to burn, like the powder by which flesh is customarily eaten away when inserting issues (*fontanelles*) into the human body. [Col. 1230B] Hence something is called *cauteriatum*, “cauterized,” when it has, as it were, been burned and cut away by a cautery. Thus in 1 Timothy 4:2 the Apostle says: κεκαυτηριασμένοι τὴν συνείδησιν, “having their conscience cauterized”: once it had become rotten and corrupted by sins, it was burned and cut away by a cautery. See Calmet on this passage.

**Commendat**: he commends. Surely he could have said nothing more elegantly.

### Notes on Chapter XI

**Potiorem**: that is, the principal cause capable of deterring one from penance, as something pertaining to the soul, which is far more delicate and is often irritated much more sharply by certain comparatively slight things than the body is by far graver pains and even tortures.

**Sordulentos.** A rather rough-sounding word to Latin ears. But who would execrate it so vehemently when one finds even *sorditudo* used for *sordities*, “filth,” in Plautus’ *Poenulus*? [Col. 1230C]

**Deversari**: simply for *versari*, “to remain” or “to live,” as we have already observed repeatedly elsewhere regarding compounds of this kind.

**In asperitudine**: in roughness. The roughness of this noun would be tolerated only with difficulty by the more cultivated soil of Latium, as though it had been born in Africa’s parched deserts. Indeed, as the proverb has it, Africa always produces something new, and from of old carried its verbal monsters across the seas into foreign lands from time to time.

**Vanitate.** For when emaciation has been contracted through excessive fasting, all beauty seems, as it were, to vanish from the wasted face or become evanescent. Yet what loss is this, when by a kind of accretion the soul gains correspondingly greater beauty?

**In coccino**: in a scarlet-dyed garment. Although the Greek word otherwise signifies every kind of berry or grain in general, it particularly denotes the fruit of a certain tree not unlike the holm oak. In its berries, they say, a little worm of red color is born, from which wool or silk is customarily dyed scarlet or purple.

[Col. 1230D] Certainly the scarlet and purple colors seem to be treated interchangeably in the Gospel. For Matthew 27:28, speaking of the Jews mocking Christ as their king, says: “They placed around him χλαμίδα κοκκίνην, a SCARLET cloak”; but Mark 15:17 says: ἐνδύουσιν αὐτὸν πορφύραν, “They clothed him in PURPLE,” with whom John 19 also agrees: “They placed upon him ἱμάτιον πορφυροῦν, a purple garment.”

For the rest, there is no doubt that purple and scarlet are otherwise distinguished, if we speak more strictly. For we call that “purple” which is red but suffused with violet, whereas scarlet is brighter and more cheerful, such as is seen especially in the blooming color of young people’s blood.

**Tyrio**: Tyrian. This color is properly purple and was formerly the most precious of all. Concerning its inventor, Pollux, as cited by Samuel Pitiscus in his *Lexicon* under the word **PURPURA**, gives the following account:

> The Tyrians relate that Hercules was seized by love for a native nymph. The nymph’s name was Tyro. A dog accompanied Hercules; when it noticed a PURPLE SHELLFISH—a kind of precious mollusk—creeping among the rocks, it seized the protruding flesh in its teeth and used it for food, and the blood stained the dog’s lips scarlet. When its master returned to the girl, she saw the dog’s lips stained with an unfamiliar dye and declared that she would have nothing more to do with him unless he brought her a garment more splendid than that dog’s lips. Thus Hercules, having found the creature and collected its blood, brought the gift to the girl and was, as the Tyrians claim, the first inventor of scarlet dye.

[Col. 1231A] But St. Gregory Nazianzen, in Oration III against Julian, attributes the invention of this purple dye to a certain shepherd, saying:

> What? Will you not leave it to the Tyrians, from whom ἡ ποιμενικὴ κύων, ἡ τῇ κόχλῳ βρωθείσῃ, καὶ τὰ χείλη καθαιμαξάσῃ, τῷ ποιμένι τὸ ἄνθος γνωρίσασα—“that shepherd’s dog, which, after devouring the murex and staining its lips with blood, made the dye known to the shepherd”—presented through its mouth to you emperors that cloth, mournful and proud for the wicked?

For the rest, the purple robe was formerly peculiar to kings; it was later shared also with their household and with the nobles of the realm, as a sign that they had been admitted to participation in royal majesty. It is clear from Judges 8:26 that the kings of Midian used purple garments and that similar garments taken from them were sent as a gift to Gideon, judge of the Israelites. Daniel himself testifies in Chapter 5:4 that, when by his prophetic spirit he had revealed the hidden meaning of the writing shown to King Belshazzar, purple and a golden chain fell to him as a royal gift. Likewise, to omit other examples of the same kind, 1 Maccabees 10:20 records that Alexander Balas, king of Syria, rewarded Jonathan Maccabaeus with a golden crown and purple.

**Cedo**: used adverbially for “give” or “show me.”

**Acum crinibus distinguendis**: a pin for arranging the hair. This was an implement of women’s adornment, called a *hairpin* from its function and devised for fastening curls of hair. Martial writes of it in Book XIV, Epigram 24:

> Let the pin fasten and support the scattered hair.

And in Book II, Epigram 66:

> Of the whole circle of her hair, one curl alone  
> Had gone astray, not properly fixed by the inserted pin.

[Col. 1231C] There was also another kind called the *discriminalis*, so named from separating the hair, as it were, strand by strand—with perverse and surely reprehensible industry. The distinction in hair between virgins and married women was that the former wore it curled, while the latter wore it parted from the forehead. Hence our author says in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, ch. 12:

> And as soon as they have understood themselves to be women . . . they turn—“sweep,” I almost wrote—the hair, and lift up their coiffure upon a more wanton pin—why not rather “insert it”?—professing their womanhood openly by hair parted from the forehead.

**Dentibus elimandis**: for polishing the teeth. In former times teeth also were customarily cleaned with powder; hence the verses in Apuleius, *Apology*, Book I, which he quotes from Catullus:

> I have sent, as you requested, a cleanser for the teeth,  
> A fine, whitening, noble little powder,  
> A smoother of the slightly swollen gum,  
> A sweeper-away of yesterday’s residue,  
> Lest any hideous stain of filth be seen  
> If perchance you smile with parted lips.

[Col. 1231D] **Bisulcum**: forked. He means scissors, an iron or bronze implement with two blades, etc., borrowed from tailors.

**Repastinandis.** A rustic word, especially proper to vine-dressers, who use *repastinare* for digging a field over again with the *pastinum*, likewise a forked implement, though of a very different kind: *den Acker umhacken*, “to hoe the field over.” Because a field is cleansed by digging it over again, foreign shrubs or stones being uprooted, any removal of impurities, including even the cutting of nails, is customarily called *repastinatio* by a metaphorical extension of the word. He is found to have used it in almost this sense for the cutting away of superfluous or harmful things in *On the Apparel of Women*, ch. 9: “How shall we be able to attain humility without cutting away the use of your riches or finery?” etc. Likewise in *On the Soul*, ch. 50: “No one will grant such power even to magic that it can remove death or, in the manner of a vine—meaning after the likeness of one—prune life back, with youth renewed.” Again, in *Against Marcion*, Book II, ch. 18: “So that the commission of injury might be pruned away by fear of immediate retaliation.” But in the *Exhortation to Chastity*, ch. 6, he also uses the word *repastinatio* for uprooting or cutting away.

**Coacti ruboris**: forced redness. Here he censures the cosmetics of soft and effeminate men, with which they are accustomed to repaint their former—indeed, now aged and as it were exhausted—beauty with a fresh falsehood, and thus to compel and, as it were, extort with rouge a blush already unwilling to appear.

**Balneas**: baths. Cicero himself also uses this noun as a feminine plural, as when in the speech *For Marcus Caelius* he mentions the Baths of Xenia. [Col. 1232B]

**Hortulani**: for *hortensis*, “of the garden.” This adjectival use of the word *hortulanus* will scarcely be found among other ancient authors.

**Senectutem vini**: the old age of wine. An elegant metaphor for signifying aged wine, which the ancients called “toothless,” as though lacking teeth, because of its mildness and the biting quality it had now cast off. Thus Plautus again in the *Poenulus*, Act III, scene 3:

> Water your years with toothless wine made mellow by age.

To make the age of wine known, tablets used to be hung from wine vessels, displaying the year and the consuls under whom each wine had been laid down. Hence Tibullus’ verse:

> Now bring me smoky Falernian wines from the time of an ancient consul.

Hence “consular wine” came to be used for the oldest and finest wine. Among these, the Opimian was most celebrated, named from the [Col. 1232C] consulship of Lucius Opimius—under whom, as Pliny testifies in Book XIV, ch. 14, there was an exceptionally fine vintage—and was praised especially by Petronius Arbiter in chapter 34. Cicero also mentions it in *On Famous Orators*, ch. 83, when discussing Falernian wine: wine neither so new that he would want it produced under the most recent consuls, nor, conversely, so old that he would seek Opimius and Anicius as consul. But since the consulship of Opimius fell in the year 632 from the founding of Rome, 119 before Christ, while Petronius died around A.D. 66, or 818 from the founding of Rome, it is clear that in Petronius’ time that wine could easily have been 180 years old.
