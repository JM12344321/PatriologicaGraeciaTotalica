And here we might end our discussion, since it is not our purpose to examine Tertullian’s other writings, which have either been wrongly attributed to him or no longer survive. But it occurred to me to say something about those Acts of Saints Perpetua and Felicity, which some have also attributed to Tertullian. Among these I propose to challenge Whiston alone, who made every effort to vindicate these Acts for Tertullian [Col. 0172A] (*In the Sacred History of the Jewish and Christian Church*, part II, vol. 6, pp. 477–78). He does not indeed think that those holy martyrs belonged to the Montanist discipline, as some have maintained, Samuel Basnage among them (*Annales politico-ecclesiastici*, vol. II, p. 224 ff.); but he plainly thinks that the man who collected these materials was not only a Montanist but Tertullian himself.

On this matter I hold the contrary opinion. For, first, there are many things in the memoirs left by Perpetua herself which show beyond doubt that she belonged to the Montanist community. Basnage has already made this argument, but he found an opponent in Joseph Augustine Orsi, who, although he considers the compiler of these Acts a Montanist, nevertheless labors strenuously to defend the orthodoxy of these martyrs [Col. 0172B] (*Dissertatio apologetica pro SS. Perpetuae, Felicitatis et sociorum martyrum orthodoxia, adversus Sam. Basnagium*, Florence, 1728, quarto). I remember that, when I once read his arguments, they did not persuade me of this point, but contained a great many matters wholly foreign to the present discussion. The work, however, is not now at hand; let us therefore pursue Whiston alone.

He says that there is not even a single letter savoring of Montanism in these memoirs of Perpetua and Saturus, from which those Acts are almost entirely composed. How far this is from the truth we shall show later. He then thinks this single point sufficient to settle the whole controversy: that Tertullian was the first to introduce the errors of the Montanists into Africa, at approximately the time when these martyrs suffered punishment. Therefore, since those doctrines had scarcely been brought into [Col. 0172C] Africa at that time, it cannot be said that persons unlearned in letters and living far from Carthage, as these people were, had already embraced that discipline.

More than one error is found in this argument. First, he undoubtedly assumes that these martyrs died at Thuburbo. I know that this city is mentioned in spurious copies of these Acts, and Whiston likewise expressly names it; but this has been sufficiently rejected both by the better [non-Latin text omitted] and by the arguments of Valesius and Ruinart (*Notes on the Acts of the Martyrs*, pp. 90–91, Amsterdam ed.). Accordingly, if we believe, as those scholars demonstrated, that they were killed at Carthage, they could easily have learned of the errors of the Montanists, since everyone knows that Tertullian lived at Carthage [Col. 0172D]. But from what source will Whiston prove that Tertullian was the first to spread these errors in Africa, and that he did so at approximately the time when these martyrs suffered? And even if I grant this too, what, I ask, is incredible in supposing that these martyrs had learned of errors disseminated in Africa by Tertullian and already defended by him in writing before the year 205—for Whiston assigned the death of the holy martyrs to that year—especially since very many who joined the society of Montanus were without literary education, and therefore resembled these martyrs?

Still farther from the truth is the assertion that none of Montanus’s delusions is sprinkled through the memoirs of Perpetua and Saturus themselves. For, first, there is strong reason to suspect a merely human character in the very appearances presented to these persons. Perpetua not only declares that immediately [Col. 0173A] with baptism she received the gift of foretelling future events [Note: [Col. 0173D] *Acts of Perpetua and Felicity*, in Ruinart’s *Sincere Acts of the Martyrs*, Amsterdam ed., p. 93, § 3: “Within the space of a few days we were baptized. But the Spirit dictated to me that nothing else was to be sought in the water except endurance of the flesh.”]; she also speaks so confidently of that gift that at will she could predict the future and even release the dead from the very punishments of hell [Note: [Col. 0173D] Ibid., p. 94, § 4: “My brother said to me: Lady sister, you are now in great dignity, so great that you may request a vision and it may be shown to you whether there is to be suffering or a reprieve”—that is, whether it would come to pass that she escaped the danger of death. See Holstenius’s note on this passage, ibid., p. 106; he aptly observes a passage of Tertullian in which he records that the sisters obtain from the Lord a *dignatio*, that is, a singular favor, whenever they desire it. She continues: “And I, knowing that I conversed with the Lord, whose benefits I had experienced in so great measure, confidently promised him, saying: Tomorrow I shall report to you….” Then, p. 96, § 7, “the name Dinocrates came forth from her lips,” her brother, who was already dead, “and she immediately understood herself to be worthy and bound to petition for him…. That very night it was shown to her in a vision that the dead Dinocrates was in distress.” But in § 8, after frequent prayers, she understood that he had been transferred out of punishment. We have never read that even the prayers of the Apostles, or of any great envoy of God, possessed such power.].

I shall not now examine this last presumption, but I nevertheless strongly doubt whether, in the period in which Perpetua lived—that is, toward the close of the second century after the birth of Christ—that miraculous faculty and, as it were, indwelling power which had been given to the Apostles and the Apostolic Church ought rightly to be attributed to her. To be brief, I shall offer two reasons for this opinion.

The first is that it cannot in any way be shown that anyone apart from the Apostles either received such a faculty except through the laying on of their hands, or could in turn communicate it to others [Col. 0173B]; by contrast, there are several sufficiently striking passages that may be argued in support of the opposite view. To use this example, why were the Apostles Peter and John finally sent by the others to Samaria (Acts 8:14 ff.)? Why would you say that, through the laying on of hands, they distributed to the new Christians the power to perform miracles, when Philip the Evangelist himself had already won renown in Samaria through many miracles? Why could the Ephesian Christians (Acts 19:2 ff.), whom the Apostle Paul was now visiting for the first time, have said both that they had not received the Holy Spirit and that they did not even know what this was, unless matters stood as we have said? If this is so, I do not venture to affirm that toward the end of the second century there was any Christian who shone with a faculty of this kind; for at that time [Col. 0173C] none who could have received it from the Apostles was still living.

The second reason is contained in Ephesians 4:7 ff. After Paul had argued at length above, vindicating for those who had previously followed the worship of the gods the same share and divine grace as the Jews, in order to reconcile the disagreements between Jewish and Gentile Christians, he then observed that miraculous faculties greatly contributed to fostering discord between the two groups. He therefore plainly affirms both that these faculties were most wisely distributed at Christ’s discretion, what end they served, and that they would not continue to exist except for a fixed period. In this passage no one, I think, will doubt that the faculties to be understood are not those spiritual faculties common to all Christians, but those suited to certain [Col. 0174A] Christians for producing actual miracles, provided that he compares these statements with what the Apostle discusses in Romans 12:3–8 and 1 Corinthians 12 ff.

Paul further adds in Ephesians 4:13–14 that they were given [non-Latin text omitted]. [non-Latin text omitted]. [non-Latin text omitted]. [non-Latin text omitted]. From this it follows that these faculties became extinct when both those who had become Christians from among the Jews and those from among the Gentiles ([non-Latin text omitted]) had agreed in believing the same things and acknowledging that one Son of God, and when the entire society of Christians gathered from both groups—which here, by a comparison drawn from the Mosaic tabernacle filled with divine splendor, is called [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0174B] [non-Latin text omitted]; compare 1:22–23—had been brought, as it were, to mature manhood and no longer allowed itself to be tossed about by the deceits of teachers [non-Latin text omitted].

But I suspect that this point was reached not very long after the death of all the Apostles, or around the middle of the second century. For when Hadrian had forbidden the Jews to celebrate public worship in Jerusalem, Sulpicius Severus says (*Sacred History*, book II, ch. 31) that, with few exceptions, the Christians whose minds had still inclined toward the religion of the Jews both abandoned this error and appointed Mark, a Gentile, bishop of Jerusalem. Their example was doubtless followed by others of the same kind; for after this we never find the Christian community torn apart by the former disputes over observance of the Mosaic law [Col. 0174C]. For although someone might object by citing the groups of Cerinthians, Ebionites, Nazarenes, and Elcesaites, nevertheless, after the first half of the second century had passed, very few joined them; nor can there ever exist such complete concord among Christians that absolutely no one professes disagreement with the rest [Note: [Col. 0174D] This decidedly ill-omened opinion of Valesius and Basnage, so ungrateful to such great martyrs, which our present author improperly revives, has been vigorously routed and driven from the field in the decisive apologetic dissertation of Joseph Augustine Orsi, afterward Cardinal of the Holy Roman Church, *On the Orthodoxy of Saints Perpetua, Felicity, and the Holy Martyrs*, Florence, 1728. In it the following points in particular are established: (1) that the most weighty testimonies prove that honor has in every age been rendered to our holy martyrs in the Church; (2) that even if the Acts of the holy martyrs flowed from Montanist sources, no prejudice against the martyrs themselves arises from this; (3) that in the Acts themselves nothing in fact occurs that smacks of Montanus’s workshop, or that does not agree perfectly with the genuine Acts of the other martyrs; (4) finally, that the virtues of our martyrs, the fruits of their visions, the fulfillment of their prophecies, and the marks of their miracles, teaching, and entire conduct are such as to make it plain that they were inspired by the divine Spirit and wholly removed from Montanist practices. These matters may be seen below in volume III of this *Bibliotheca*, where the Acts of St. Perpetua and the saints are to be published in their proper place, together with the notes of various ancient and modern scholars. EDD.].

§ 4.—On the Montanist Artotyrites

There is, moreover, a vision of Perpetua which had aroused strong suspicion of Montanism, and from which she acknowledges that she understood that a violent death was imminent for her (§ 4). In the vision, a morsel of cheese is offered to her by a certain shepherd; she eats it, and all who stand by say “Amen.” What is this? Surely a signification of violent death. [Col. 0174D] How does this follow: a piece of cheese, [Col. 0175A] an “Amen,” and a violent manner of death? If I see the matter correctly, these things do not appear connected in any way unless I say that this virgin belonged to the Montanist community; for Epiphanius (*Haer.* 49, § 2) and others say that they—or at least some of them—used cheese in the sacred supper (perhaps those less-than-precise writers meant in the agapes).

Now it had become customary among Christians for those present from the congregation to add “Amen” at the sacred meal, as is evident from Justin Martyr (*Greater Apology*, chs. 85 and 87, Grabe ed., pp. 125, 131–32); and that meal was also ordinarily distributed in prison to those destined for death. Everything is therefore perfectly clear if that virgin is thought to have belonged to the discipline of Montanus; if this is denied, the details cannot, as it seems to me, be explained in any way [Note: [Col. 0175B] Certain points arise from this account of Perpetua which may with considerable reason be argued against what I have said; they have also been employed with much ingenuity by the highly esteemed historian of the Montanists, Theophilus Wernsdorf, § 18. I propose to answer them briefly and modestly.

The statements of some ancient writers that the Montanists employed cheese in their sacred meals, from which they derived the name Artotyrites, seem to me by no means negligible. For even if this is wrongly said of the form of their sacred Supper, and even if that distinguished learned man much more correctly refers it to the Montanists’ agapes and offerings, our case requires nothing more than that the use of cheese be established for these agapes of theirs. This can readily be done, not only because no other explanation can be given for the origin of so ridiculous a charge and appellation, but also because it is agreed that the Montanists were especially devoted to dry fasts. It may therefore readily be understood that their sacred banquets were not sumptuous; nor will anyone who has learned from those Acts how wretched the martyrs’ lot was in prison readily doubt that Perpetua and the other martyrs also used such meager food as cheese in prison when they held what were, as it were, agapes.

But our employment of this circumstance to establish the Montanism of these martyrs [Col. 0175C], and especially of Perpetua, seems to be overturned by the arguments which that most learned man has presented in the passage cited. First he appeals to the authority of Augustine and Tertullian: the former praised that vision of Perpetua, while the latter did not mention it in his book *On the Soul*. Neither point, I think, is surprising. In Tertullian’s case, it may be doubted whether he referred to the very vision now in question, since he says only that martyrs were seen by Perpetua in paradise; one might perhaps miss any mention of this in our vision. Nor, for the purpose of his argument—which is concerned exclusively with asserting that perfect happiness before the final day of judgment belongs only to martyrs—would he suitably and relevantly have mentioned the cheese received by Perpetua.

As for Augustine, who is wholly occupied in praising Perpetua and interprets the morsel of cheese given to her in another way, who would be surprised that he nevertheless praised this circumstance? But the second objection is weightier. Augustine thought that the morsel of cheese was a sign of the sweetness of Perpetua’s happiness, and this interpretation persuaded that distinguished man, who appears to find the entire signification of violent death in the ladder fortified with thorns, swords, and other objects which Perpetua had to ascend. He says [Col. 0175D] that the reason cheese was used to foreshadow everlasting happiness lay in consistently pursuing the metaphor drawn from the image of the Shepherd.

But if this alone had been the significance of receiving the cheese, I would ask why cheese had been offered to Perpetua by the Shepherd as he was milking, rather than some milk, which would have been far better suited to foreshadow the happiness of the heavenly Canaan, flowing as it were with milk and honey. Next, let someone tell me why Perpetua records that it was presented to her mind that she received that morsel of cheese with joined hands—in accordance with the custom adopted at the sacred supper among the ancient Christians—and that all those standing around said “Amen.” I doubt whether anyone can give a reason for this unless he favors our case.

Finally, if receiving that cheese signifies something that was to happen to Perpetua in the heavenly kingdom, and perhaps foreshadows the supper of the Lamb described in John’s Apocalypse—from which nearly all those visions in the Acts under discussion were drawn—then, if you call Perpetua a Montanist, it is much easier to understand why a morsel of cheese was given to her to signify this, since, as we have said, the Montanists used cheese in their agapes. But if you say that the cheese does not refer to anything heavenly, but to some other circumstance connected with Perpetua’s violent death, the vision can be explained from the free meal which those Acts [Col. 0176D] (§ 8) report that the martyrs took as an agape on the day before they underwent punishment.].

§ 5.—Whiston’s Arguments for Tertullian as the Author of These Acts Are Examined

[Col. 0176A]

But let us come to the second question, which consists in this: whether the person who collected these Acts was either a writer from the Montanist sect or Tertullian himself. Whiston accepts both propositions—correctly, indeed, if the former is in question, but very differently if Tertullian himself is meant.

I therefore agree completely with Whiston on the former point, because that writer says in the preface that he acknowledges and honors new prophecies and visions, and consequently admits that he has added these new records of martyrs and revelations. He also boasts (§ 26) that, since the Holy Spirit permitted—and by permitting willed—the course of this contest (that is, the martyrs’ combat with beasts in the theater) [Col. 0176B] to be written down, he composed this account. I hardly know whether this does not disclose a writer who could only have been Montanist, principally because the orthodox did not customarily esteem Montanist martyrs highly, and much less would they have committed to writing their sufferings and steadfastness together with visions which they assuredly despised.

But on the principal point of this discussion—that Tertullian was that very writer—I shall not yield to Whiston’s arguments, which he sets forth more fully in this manner. First, as the preface shows, these Acts were composed not very long after the event itself. Next, they were written in Latin, and by an African, of which the harsh African style employed here is no slight evidence. Likewise, many words occur which are familiar to Tertullian [Col. 0176C] (as Holstenius’s notes on these Acts prove by several examples). Finally, the writer undoubtedly belonged to the school of Montanus, which Tertullian was the first to propagate in Africa at approximately the time when these martyrs were killed. These Acts must therefore also be Tertullian’s.

If I see the matter correctly, all this proves nothing except that Tertullian could be the man to whom the collection of these memoirs was owed; Whiston has not established by these arguments that he actually composed them. Indeed, among these premises are assertions which, being arbitrarily fabricated, either prove more than he wishes or prove nothing at all. For, as we have said, the claim that Tertullian was the first author of Montanism’s dissemination in Africa is fictitious; and the words common to Tertullian and these Acts do not come from his own distinctive style but [Col. 0176D] from African usage. For if the presence of many words customary in Tertullian’s speech showed that Tertullian himself composed such a work, it would certainly follow that the memoirs of Perpetua and Saturus themselves were composed not by them but by Tertullian; for, as Holstenius’s observations show, many words occur in those very memoirs which may also be found in Tertullian’s writings.

Whiston’s suspicions can also be met by contrary arguments. In *On the Soul*, ch. 55, Tertullian mentions a vision of Perpetua narrated in these Acts and adduces it to confirm a certain doctrine; [Col. 0177A] but there is not even a word there by which he indicates that he was the writer of these Acts. I do not doubt that, as is his habit, he would have done so had he been the author of this history. Furthermore, it seems to me that no one who knows Tertullian’s writings and manner of expression ought to dare say that the style found at the end of these memoirs is the same as that found in Tertullian’s other writings. These Acts must therefore be removed from the corpus of Tertullian’s works, and consequently there is no need to discuss their date itself.

§ 6.—The Usefulness of This Entire Discussion Is Demonstrated by Examples Drawn from Christian History

And as we bring our writing to an end, we do not doubt that there will be some to whom all the labor spent in discussing [Col. 0177B] these matters will seem trivial and badly applied. For there are some who think that every discussion concerned with matters that are uncertain and, in a manner of speaking, bloodless should be despised; others pronounce wholly useless those subjects whose utility does not offer itself at first glance, as it were, but lies deeply concealed.

We could meet them with the examples of great men who did not consider it disgraceful to engage in discussions of this kind, and with the splendid words of the venerable Ernesti, who certainly knows, if anyone does, what value is to be assigned to every discipline. He maintains (*Prolusio on the Use of Engraving*, p. 5) that each art should be judged in such a way that we measure its dignity and excellence not so much by the magnitude and necessity of its usefulness as by the difficulty of discovering and practicing it, and that we should consider not [Col. 0177C] how useful each art is, but how much each is worth—unless, because it is more necessary and useful to bake loaves than to paint pictures, we are therefore to prefer the craft of bakers to the art of painting.

I myself think it far more proper to apply industry to elucidating somewhat obscure matters than to other subjects already widely traversed, even though the latter may appear better adapted to common use; for in these there is little or nothing that has escaped the diligence of many, while in the former there is always something that can be discussed further. In matters that are not certain but must be established by probability, there is far greater usefulness for acquiring the faculty of understanding and writing than in matters that are certain and, as it were, necessary, because the former possess many [Col. 0177D] different shapes, so to speak, and can be conceived and argued on either side. The ancients long ago taught, and most correctly, that this has great power to cultivate subtlety of understanding itself.

But why should I gather together every consideration, when I have also taken pains to interpose matters extraneous to the immediate case, as far as this could be done, yet not too remote from the purpose, in order to relieve somewhat the weariness produced by similar topics, and to employ examples showing that the arguments we have offered, although perhaps comparatively slight, are nevertheless necessary if anyone wishes to examine questions of greater importance in sacred history? In presenting these I am not one who wishes to criticize learned men; rather, I shall adduce a few examples that occur without much searching, so that the usefulness of this discussion may nonetheless be understood in some measure.

To begin with this example, learned men debate [Col. 0178A] what caused Tertullian to join the Montanists. Many among them think Jerome should be heard, who says that Tertullian, inflamed by hatred of the teachers of the Roman Church, took leave of the common Church. Passing over other considerations, this is scarcely credible because in the Montanist book *On Prescription*, as we have said, he continues to extol the Roman Church with high praises (ch. 36); nor in *Against Praxeas*, ch. 1, where he fiercely assails Praxeas for corrupting that Church with errors, does he cast any insult upon that Church itself or its bishop.

Again, in the history of the Montanists, how many mistakes could have been avoided and how many arguments corrected if it had been determined more precisely which books were written before Tertullian’s defection and which afterward! [Col. 0178B] To take one example, some have numbered among Montanus’s disgraceful practices a ridiculous and effeminate devotion to adorning the eyes, hair, and other things, led by the matters which Apollonius recorded in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* V.18. If I see correctly, his words in no way confirm this point, and everyone has erred in believing that the prophet mentioned there was Montanus; for Apollonius’s words must be understood generally of vices which ought to have been absent from prophets, of whom the Montanists boasted that they had many. But that slander can also be refuted by Tertullian’s very severe statements in the books *On the Veiling of Virgins* and *On the Dress of Women* against excessive concern for feminine adornment.

Moreover, Epiphanius, *Haer.* 49, § 14, says that the Montanists brought a hand to the mouth while praying, whence [Col. 0178C] they earned the name Tascodrugites. Wernsdorf, that distinguished man, wishes to refute this charge, loc. cit., § 21, by a passage of Tertullian from *On Prayer*, ch. 13, where Tertullian says that it is inconsistent with modesty to raise the hands too high during prayer. Since that passage comes from a book written while Tertullian was still orthodox in belief, it has no force for repelling the charge—not to mention that Epiphanius reports this only of certain Montanists.

Furthermore, how confused are the discussions of Tillemont and others concerning the calamities of Christians under Severus, and how disorderly and inverted the arrangement of all their passages, for this one reason: that they did not correctly distinguish the books Tertullian wrote during the earlier persecution under Severus, when he agreed with the orthodox, from those written during the later persecution, when he had separated himself from them [Col. 0178D].

Finally, nothing is more effective than our discussion for overthrowing Whiston’s fantasies, which he proposed in the book cited above (*The True Origin of the Sabellian and Athanasian Doctrines of the Trinity*, London, 1720, octavo). In it he imagines that the doctrines of the Sabellians and of those whom he calls Athanasians concerning the most holy Triad were invented by Simon Magus and revived by the Montanists. He therefore attempts to demonstrate that Tertullian’s opinion on this matter was threefold, from three classes of books written by him: those composed when he believed correctly, those written when he inclined toward Montanus’s errors, and those written when he had been wholly imbued with them. Yet within each class he assigns books which we have established plainly belong to another class, and by this fact alone Whiston’s entire argument is reduced to nothing.

[Col. 0179A] But at long last we must stop, lest we appear excessive in these matters, and entrust our little book to the judgment of learned men. We ask that their judgment be all the more equitable toward us, insofar as either the very nature of the matters discussed, or the other labors by which we were burdened while presenting them, prevented us from furnishing a more exact treatment. [Col. 0180A]

Tertullian’s Paradoxes, with the Antidote of Jacobus Pamelius

[Col. 0179]

TERTULLIAN’S PARADOXES, WITH THE ANTIDOTE OF JACOBUS PAMELIUS

[Col. 0179A] 1. On the Rebel Angels Who Took the Daughters of Men. Vol. II, book *On Idolatry*, ch. 9, no. 47; book [Col. 0179B] *On Women’s Dress*, ch. 1, no. 17; book *On the Apparel of Women*, ch. 10, no. 56; and book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, ch. 7, no. 32. Likewise vol. III, book V *Against Marcion*, ch. 18, no. 291. (According to Pamelius’s edition, Antwerp, 1579; Paris, 1583.)

Tertullian was neither the first nor the last author of this paradox. As we have observed in part on the book *On the Dress of Virgins*, where he treats it most deliberately, before him it was held by Josephus, *Antiquities*, book I; St. Justin Martyr in both *Apologies*; Athenagoras in the *Apology*; and Clement of Alexandria in books III and V of the *Stromata*; after him by Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, book II, ch. 15; St. Cyprian, *On the Discipline and Dress of Virgins*, no. 54; Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book V; and Methodius, discourse *On the Resurrection*.

The occasion for the error was that the old Greek Septuagint edition which they followed [Col. 0179C], following Philo, read [non-Latin text omitted], and the Latin Itala, as Saints Ambrose and Augustine and Procopius attest, read “angels of God,” in place of what is found to have been read later, [non-Latin text omitted], or in Latin, “sons of God.” St. Augustine and Procopius were acquainted with both readings in their time.

But St. Chrysostom admirably refutes that opinion by reducing it to absurdity in homily 22 on Genesis, asserting that nowhere else in Scripture—namely, according to the Septuagint edition, the only one he knew—are angels called sons of God. So too do St. Ambrose, *On Noah and the Ark*, ch. 4; St. Augustine, question 3 on Genesis, and more fully in *The City of God*, book XV, ch. 15; St. Epiphanius, *Epitome of Divine Dogmas*; and Procopius in his *Commentary*. Of these, Chrysostom and Augustine also contest the authority of the Book of Enoch, on which Tertullian appears to rely [Col. 0179D]. They all interpret both the “angels” and the “sons of God” as the descendants of Seth, inasmuch as they were worshipers of God; so likewise does Blessed Jerome in the book *Hebrew Traditions on Genesis*. Finally, the Venerable Bede, our Rabanus, and Haymo, writing commentaries on Genesis, state that faithful and righteous men are frequently called “angels” in Holy Scripture. What of the fact that Philastrius, in his book *On Heresies*, ch. 108, numbers among the heresies those who assert that angels had intercourse with women before the Flood and that giants were born from this union?

2. On the Apparitions of Angels in True Human Flesh. Vol. III, book III *Against Marcion*, ch. 9, no. 61, and ch. 11, no. 72.

Those words of Tertullian do indeed appear to express something of the kind: [Col. 0180A] “Then know that not even this is conceded to you—that the angels’ flesh was merely apparent—but that it was of true and solid human substance.” [Col. 0180B] Yet in other books written at the same time he explains himself, as we noted on *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 6, no. 60, in such a way that he did not intend to assert anything certain on this matter; otherwise he would appear to contradict himself. “It is agreed,” he says there, “that angels did not bear flesh of their own, since they are by nature of spiritual substance; and even if they possess some sort of body, it is nevertheless of their own kind, though capable of being transformed temporarily into human flesh, so that they may be seen and associate with human beings.”

He speaks still more clearly in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 62: “For the angels too,” he says, “were sometimes as though human beings, eating, drinking, and extending their feet for washing; for they had put on a human outward appearance while preserving inwardly their proper substance.” It everywhere seemed sufficient to the Author that he did not agree with the doctrine of Apelles [Col. 0180C], who said that angels had been created from an airy substance; thus he would not appear to agree with him also in holding that Christ’s body derived its origin from ethereal matter.

But what kind of bodies were assumed by angels, and whence they were taken, remains controversial even today in the *Master of the Sentences*, book II, distinction 8, § 4. In the passage already cited, we preferred, with St. Bernard, to suspend our judgment on this matter rather than define anything as certain. Whether, however, the Author believed—as certain persons charge against him—that angels and demons are corporeal may not unreasonably be doubted. For although the Author here seems to attribute a body of their own kind to angels, he nevertheless makes them of spiritual substance, just as he also repeatedly does in the *Apologeticum*, and specifically in ch. 22, where he attributes spiritual powers both to angels [Col. 0180D] and to demons and calls them spirits. More generally accepted, however, and pleasing to me, is the opinion of those who make them incorporeal. Among these should be mentioned St. Dionysius the Areopagite, *Celestial Hierarchy*, ch. 2; Athanasius, *On the Common Essence of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit*; Chrysostom, homily 22 on Genesis; and Cyril, book IV, on John, chapter.

3. On the Soul of the First Man Being from the Substance of God. Vol. III, book IV *Against Marcion*, ch. 38, no. 611.

The following words of the Author, explaining Luke 20, “Render to God the things that are God’s,” do indeed appear pertinent here: “He therefore commands that man be rendered to the Creator, in whose image, likeness, name, and substance he was fashioned.” This passage was perhaps the reason why Gennadius wrote thus of him [Col. 0181A] in the book *On Ecclesiastical Dogmas*: “Let us not believe that anything from the essence of the Trinity was derived into the nature of creatures, as Plato and Tertullian did.”

But since St. Augustine, who so often demonstrates that the soul cannot be called a part of God, attributes nothing of the kind to him, I too think that he should be cleared of so absurd a heresy. Indeed, he meant to say nothing other than that the soul takes its origin from the breath of God, in accordance with Genesis 2: “And God breathed the breath of life into the face of man, and man became a living soul,” as he himself very frequently insists, but especially in *On the Soul*, ch. 11. In that passage, as also in *On Baptism*, ch. 5, no. 43, we showed that this interpretation agrees with Saints Irenaeus, Prudentius, Chrysostom, Augustine, and Theodoret.

The last of these says: “By the term ‘breath’ we do not understand any part of the divine essence [Col. 0181B], according to the madness of Cerdo and Marcion, but signify by it the nature of the soul, because the soul is a rational and intellectual spirit.” These words also suggest to me another conjecture: that the Author speaks not according to his own opinion but according to Marcion’s, especially since it is clear from the fragments of the book *On the Origin of the Soul Against Hermogenes* that he believed the soul to be far inferior to God, as being passible, although immortal.

Indeed, one must also repeat what we noted in both places: St. Augustine rightly judged in his book *On Heresies* that Philastrius had listed many heresies which are not heresies. Of this kind is the interpretation of the breath, or breath of life, as the rational soul, which is not a heresy but the catholic opinion of the Fathers.
