They say, namely, that the Word proceeded from the seed of God but the Holy Spirit did not. What is this seed of God? They answer: an intelligible species, or God’s knowledge of himself. But let the Scholastics see to this. Tertullian did not explain it individually, but spoke more generally: “That is, from the Spirit of God.” LAC.]—that is, from the Spirit—so that he might also be [Note: [Col. 0783C] “Also” omitted by Pam. Rhen.] the Son of Man, only his flesh [Note: [Col. 0783C] “That alone which,” Rhen. Seml.] had to be taken from human flesh, without a man’s seed. A man’s seed [Note: [Col. 0783C] “The seed of a man,” Rhen. Seml.] was superfluous for one who possessed the seed of God. Accordingly, just as, before being born of the virgin, he could have God as Father without having a human mother [Note: [Col. 0783C] “Mother” omitted by Rhen.], so also, when he was born of the virgin, he could have a human mother [Note: [Col. 0783C] “Human” omitted by Rhen.] without a human father. Thus, finally, there is a human being together with God, since human flesh is together with the Spirit of God: flesh without seed, from a human being; Spirit with seed, from God.

[Note: [Col. 0783D] Therefore, if there was a rational dispensation, etc. He says this because the Valentinians maintained that Christ the Lord was brought forth through the Virgin and came forth into the world, yet received nothing from her. They are likewise refuted by Origen, Dialogue II, *On Christ the Man*; Irenaeus, Book III, *Against the Valentinians*; Euthymius, Part II of the *Panoply*; and others cited by Canisius, Book III, *On the Mother of God*, chapter 16. LAC.]

Therefore, if there was a rational dispensation for bringing forth the Son [Col. 0783B] of God from the virgin, why did he not receive from the virgin the body that he brought forth from the virgin? “Because what he took from God is something else. For,” they say, “the Word was made flesh” (John 1). This expression bears witness to what was made flesh, yet it is not thereby endangered—as though what was made flesh were immediately something other than the Word. If the Word was made flesh from flesh, or if it was made from the seed itself [Note: [Col. 0783C] “From itself,” Pam. Rig.], let Scripture say so. Since Scripture says only that it was made, and not also from what it was made, it consequently suggests that it was made from something else, not from the seed itself [Note: [Col. 0784C] “From itself,” Par. Rig. Ven.]. If it was not made from the seed itself [Note: [Col. 0784C] “From itself,” Par. Rig. Ven.] but from something else, then infer from this what it is more fitting to believe the Word was made flesh from, unless from the flesh in which it was also made.

Or consider that the Lord himself pronounced, sententiously and definitively (John 3): “What is born in flesh [Col. 0783C] is flesh,” because it was born from flesh. But if he spoke only about a human being and not also about himself, then plainly deny that Christ was a human being, and in that way maintain that this did not apply to him. Yet he adds (ibid.): “And what is born from the Spirit is spirit,” because God is Spirit and he was born from God. This certainly applies to him all the more, [Col. 0784A] even if it also applies to those who believe in him. If, therefore, the latter statement applies to him, why not the former as well? For you cannot divide the text so that the latter applies to him while the former applies to all other human beings, since you do not deny Christ’s two substances, flesh and Spirit.

Furthermore, if he possessed flesh as well as Spirit, then when he makes a declaration concerning the condition of the two substances that he himself also bears, he cannot appear to have determined something concerning his own Spirit but not his own flesh. Thus, since he himself is from the Spirit of God, and the Spirit is God, he himself is God [Note: [Col. 0784C] “And God” is absent from Rhen. Par. Fran. Pam.] born from God; and since he is from human flesh, he is a human being generated in flesh.

## CHAPTER XIX

What, then, does this mean: “Who were born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a man, but of God” [Note: [Col. 0784C] “Who was born,” Pam. Rhen.]? I shall rather use this very passage myself, [Note: [Col. 0784D] When I have overthrown its adulterators, etc. The “adulterators” are *κάπηλοι* or *καπηλεύοντες λόγον τοῦ θεοῦ*—“peddlers who adulterate the word of God.” Or he calls the heretics adulterators of Scripture because they corrupted it with the worst interpretations. LE PR.—*Obduxero*, etc.: “I have routed” or “destroyed,” as he uses the term in chapter 46 of the *Apology* and in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*. He often calls those who grossly misused this passage from John to support the Valentinian fantasy “adulterators,” not because they corrupted the wording itself. LAC.] once I have overthrown its adulterators. For they contend that it is written thus: “Who was born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of a man, but of God,” as though it referred to those previously described as believing in his name, so as to show that there exists [Note: [Col. 0784D] That secret seed. He alludes to the Valentinian fantasy concerning the psychical and spiritual seed, which, according to their doctrine, was introduced into the elect by the Demiurge and Achamoth, as he himself relates in chapter 25 of *Against the Valentinians*. They wished the phrase “of God” to signify this seed. Others, however, read “his seed.” LAC.] that [Note: [Col. 0784C] “His,” Rhen. Seml.] secret seed of the elect and the spiritual [Note: [Col. 0784C] “Spiritual,” Par. Fran. Pam.], which they claim for themselves.

But how can it be so, when all who believe in the Lord’s name are born, under the common law of the human race, from blood and from the will of the flesh and of a man—even Valentinus himself? It is so distinctly and individually stated because it was written concerning the Lord: “And he was born of God.” Rightly so, because Christ is the Word of God, and with the Word of God is the Spirit, and in the Spirit of God is power, and Christ is whatever belongs to God. But insofar as he is flesh, he was born neither of blood nor of the will of the flesh and of a man, because the Word was made flesh by God’s will.

For the formal account [Note: [Col. 0784C] “Negation,” Pam. Rhen. Seml.] of our manner of birth pertains to the flesh, not to the Word, because it was the flesh that had to be born in this manner, not the Word. But when he denies that he was born even from the will of the flesh, why did he not also deny that he was born from the substance of the flesh? For by denying that he was born of blood, he did not reject the substance of the flesh, but the material of the seed, which is known to be the heat of the blood, transformed by foaming into a coagulum of the woman’s blood. For in the coagulum used in cheese there is the active power of the substance that it congeals by treating it—that is, of milk.

We therefore understand that what is denied is the Lord’s birth from sexual union, which is what “the will of a man and of the flesh” signifies, not participation in the womb. And why did he press so emphatically and repeatedly that he was born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh or of a man, unless his was the sort of flesh that no one would doubt to have been born from sexual union? Moreover, by denying birth from sexual union, he did not deny birth from flesh; indeed, he confirmed it, because he did not deny birth from flesh in the same way that he denied birth from sexual union.

I ask you: if the Spirit [Note: [Col. 0785C] “Son,” Fran. Par. Pam. Rhen.] of God descended into the womb without intending to receive flesh from the womb, why did he descend into the womb? Spiritual flesh could have been made outside it far more simply than it could be made within the womb yet outside the womb [Note: [Col. 0785C] “Outside the womb” is absent from Fran. Pam.]. Without cause he introduced himself into a place from which he brought nothing out. But he did not descend into the womb without cause; therefore, he received something from it. For if he received nothing from it, he descended into it without cause—especially if he was to become flesh of a quality that did not belong to the womb, namely, spiritual flesh.
