“Give to everyone who asks you”—to the needy, of course; or all the more to the needy, if even to one who has abundance. Therefore, that no one may be in need, you have in Deuteronomy the Creator’s rule prepared for the giver [Note: [Col. 0396D] “Enjoined upon the giver,” Rig., Venet.; “prepared against the giver,” Pam.; “the giver enjoined,” Seml.]: “There shall be,” he says (Deut. 15:4), “no needy person among you, so that the Lord your God, in blessing, may bless you”—the giver, that is, who has brought it about that there is no needy person. [Col. 0397A] For a loan is not ordinarily given except to someone who asks. But more about lending later.

Now if anyone should wish to argue that the Creator indeed commanded giving to brothers, whereas Christ commanded giving to everyone who asks, so that this teaching is new and different: on the contrary, it will be one of those things through which the Creator’s law is present in Christ. For Christ commanded [Note: [Col. 0397D] “Commands,” Fran.] toward everyone nothing other than what the Creator commanded toward brothers. Although the goodness exercised toward strangers is greater, it is not prior to the goodness owed beforehand [Note: [Col. 0397D] “Would be owed,” Seml.] to those nearest us. For who, without loving those nearest him [Note: [Col. 0397D] Seml. and Oberth. omit “without loving those nearest him”], will be able to love strangers? But if goodness toward strangers is the second degree, while goodness toward those nearest us is the first, the second degree will belong to the same one to whom the first belongs—more readily than the second could belong to one in whom the first did not exist [Note: [Col. 0397D] “Does not exist,” Pamel.].

Thus, in accordance with the order of nature, the Creator first [Note: [Col. 0397D] “First” absent from Paris.] taught benevolence toward [Col. 0397B] those nearest us, intending afterward to extend it also [Note: [Col. 0397D] Pamel. omits “also”] to strangers; and, in accordance with the order of his dispensation, he taught it first toward the Jews, and afterward toward the whole human race as well. Accordingly, as long as the mystery was confined within Israel, he rightly commanded mercy toward brothers alone. But when he gave to Christ (Ps. 2:8) the nations [Note: [Col. 0397D] Paris and Rhen. add “as”] for his inheritance, and the ends of the earth for his possession, and what had been spoken through Hosea (Hos. 1–2) began to be fulfilled—“Not my people” became “my people,” and “she who had not obtained mercy” became “she who obtained mercy,” namely, the nation—from then onward Christ extended to everyone the law of his Father’s benevolence, excluding no one from compassion, just as he excluded no one from the calling. Thus, even if he taught something more, this too he received together with the inheritance of the nations.

“And just as you wish men to do to you, do likewise to them” (Luke 6). In this precept, of course, its other part is understood: “And just as you do not wish men to do to you, likewise do not do to them.” If a new God, formerly unknown and even now not yet fully revealed, gave this command—a God who had shaped me by no previous instruction through which I might first know what I ought to desire or not desire for myself, and so what I should do to others because I desired it for myself [Note: [Col. 0397D] Seml. and Oberth. insert “also.”], and what I should not do because I did not desire it for myself—then he has left my judgment unrestricted and has not bound me to consistency between will and deed, so that I should do to others what I desire for myself, and should not do to others what I do not desire for myself. For he did not define what I ought to desire or not desire for myself and others, so that I might conform the deed to the law of the will, and [Note: [Col. 0397D] “So that,” Jun.] might withhold from another what I desire another to provide for me—love, obedience, comfort, protection, and goods of that kind—and, likewise, might [Note: [Col. 0397D] “And,” Seml.] do to another what I do not wish another to do to me—violence, injury, insult, fraud, and evils of that kind.

Indeed, through this inconsistency between will and deed the gentiles act, for they have not yet been instructed by God [Note: [Col. 0398D] “By him,” Oberth., following Seml.]. For although good and evil are known by nature, God’s discipline is not; once it has become known, only then does consistency between will and deed proceed from faith, because conduct is governed by the fear of God. Accordingly, even though Marcion’s god [Note: [Col. 0398D] Latin inserts “even if”] has only now been revealed, once revealed he could not have issued so terse, obscure, and still-blind an abridgment of the precept under discussion—one more readily interpretable according to my own judgment—when he had laid down beforehand [Note: [Col. 0398D] “Restricted beforehand,” Seml., Oberth.] no distinctions for it.

But [Col. 0398B] my Creator, both [Note: [Col. 0398D] Oberth. omits the first “and.”] long ago and everywhere, commanded that the needy, the poor, orphans, and widows be protected, helped, and refreshed. Thus also through Isaiah (Isa. 58:7): “Break your bread for the hungry [Note: [Col. 0398D] Seml. omits “for the hungry”; Seml. adds “beggars”]; and bring into your house the beggars [Note: [Col. 0398D] Seml. omits “beggars”] who are without shelter; and if you see someone naked, clothe him.” Likewise through Ezekiel (Ezek. 18:7), concerning the righteous man: “He will give his bread to the hungry and clothe the naked.” Therefore, even then [Note: [Col. 0398D] “Now,” Paris.] he sufficiently taught me to do to others what I desire to be done to me. Likewise, by declaring, “You shall not kill; you shall not commit adultery; you shall not steal; you shall not bear false witness,” he taught me not to do to others what I would not wish to be done to me. Therefore, the precept in the Gospel will belong to him who formerly both laid its foundation and distinguished its parts, and arranged it according to the rule of his own discipline. And he rightly compressed it now into an abridgment, since the Lord—that is, Christ—had also been foretold as one who would make “a shortened word upon the earth” (Isa. 10:23).

## CHAPTER XVII

That is, the surplus from the loan, which is usury. The first step, therefore, was to root out the profit [Col. 0399A] of lending, so that man might more easily be accustomed to surrendering the principal of the loan as well, if necessary, once he had learned to forgo its profit. For we say that this was the work of the Law as it prepared the way for the Gospel. By its earliest commandments, in which benevolence was still stammering, it gradually formed the faith of certain people toward the perfect splendor of Christian discipline. For immediately above it also says: “And he shall return [Note: [Col. 0399D] “You shall return,” Seml., Oberth.] the pledge”—obviously if the debtor is unable to pay; for if he were able to pay, would it be necessary for a man to write that the pledge must, of course, be restored to the payer [Note: [Col. 0399D] “To the one paying,” Pamel.]?

This is stated far more plainly in Deuteronomy (Deut. 24:12): “You shall not sleep upon his pledge; by returning, you shall return his cloak to him around sunset, and he shall sleep in his cloak.” Still more clearly, earlier (Deut. 15:2): “You shall remit every debt that your neighbor owes you, and you shall not [Col. 0399B] demand payment from your brother, because the remission of the Lord your God has been proclaimed.” Now, when he commands that a debt be remitted, obviously to someone unable to repay, this is even more [Note: [Col. 0399D] “For,” Pamel.]; and if the debtor is able to pay, yet he forbids demanding repayment, what else does he teach than that we should lend to one unable to repay, since he has already decreed loss upon the loan [Note: [Col. 0399D] “So great,” Pam., Venet., Seml.]?

[Note: [Col. 0399D] “And you shall be sons of God.” In the Greek text of blessed Luke it is **ὑψίστου** (*hypsistou*), “of the Most High”; here he translates it “of God.” It is not unusual in the Scriptures, however, for “Most High” to be translated in place of “God.” LE PR.]

“And you shall be sons of God.” Nothing could be more shameless, if he should make sons for himself [Note: [Col. 0399D] “He who did not permit us to make sons, by abolishing marriage”: namely, Marcion’s god. Even if the Creator had not joined male and female, and even if he had not granted offspring to every kind of animal as well, I was his son before paradise, [Col. 0399C] before the transgression, before exile, before the two were one [Note: [Col. 0399D] “Before the two were one”: before God had said, “And the two shall be one flesh.” RIG.]. I was made his son anew immediately when he brought me forth with his hands and set me in motion with his own breath. He now calls me his son once again, begetting me no longer into soul but into spirit.

“Because he himself,” it says, “is kind toward the ungrateful and the wicked.” Well done, Marcion! Quite ingeniously you removed the rains and the suns from him, lest he appear to be the Creator. But who is this kind being who has not even been known until now? How is he kind, when no benefits from him have gone before [Note: [Col. 0399D] “Had gone before,” Pam.]? This is rather the kind of kindness displayed by him who had lent out suns and rains as the Creator, though he would receive no repayment from the human race: he who, despite such generosity with the elements, has until now endured men who render to idols rather than to him the debt of gratitude. He is truly kind in spiritual benefits as well; for “the utterances of the Lord are sweeter than honey and the honeycomb” (Ps. 18:11). He, therefore, rebuked the ungrateful, since he deserved to find them grateful—the one whose sun [Note: [Col. 0399D] “Sun,” Pam.] and rains even you, ungrateful Marcion, have enjoyed. Your god, by contrast, could not yet complain of the ungrateful, since he had not prepared anyone to be grateful.

In commanding mercy as well, he says: “Be merciful, just as your Father [Note: [Col. 0400D] Seml. omits “your”] has had mercy on you.” This will correspond to: “Break your bread for the hungry, and bring into your house the beggars [Note: [Col. 0400D] Oberth. omits “beggars”] who are without shelter, and if you see someone naked, clothe him” (Isa. 58:7); and: “Judge for the orphan, and vindicate the widow” (Isa. 1:17). I recognize his ancient teaching, who prefers “mercy rather than sacrifice” (Hos. 6:6). Or if another has now commanded mercy because he too is merciful, why was he not merciful to me for so long an age?

“Do not judge, lest you be judged. Do not condemn, lest you be condemned. Forgive, and you shall be forgiven. Give, and it shall be given to you. They shall give a good measure, pressed down and overflowing, into your lap. With the same measure with which you have measured, it shall be measured back to you” (Luke 6). In my judgment, these words express recompense elicited in accordance with merits [Note: [Col. 0400D] “For merits,” another reading; “expresses,” Seml., Oberth.]. From whom, then, comes the recompense? If merely from men, then he teaches a human discipline and reward, and we shall obey men entirely. If it comes from the Creator, as judge and assessor of merits, then he directs our obedience toward him, with whom he establishes that recompense is to be sought or feared according as each person has judged, condemned, forgiven, or measured. If it comes from himself, then he too now judges—which Marcion denies.

Let the Marcionites therefore choose, lest it cost as much to depart from the rule of their master as [Note: [Col. 0400D] “As,” Pamel.] to maintain that Christ teaches obedience either to men or to the Creator. But the blind leads the blind into the pit. Some believe Marcion. Yet the disciple is not above the master. Apelles too [Note: [Col. 0400D] Oberth. omits “too.”], the corrector of Marcion after having been his disciple, ought to have remembered this.

Let the heretic remove the beam from his own eye; then let him convict the speck, if he thinks there is one, in the Christian’s eye. Likewise, let a good tree not produce evil fruit, because neither does truth produce heresy; nor an evil tree good fruit, because neither does heresy produce truth. Thus Marcion produced nothing good from the evil treasury of Cerdo, nor Apelles from Marcion’s. For we shall interpret these sayings, which Christ allegorized concerning men, [Col. 0401A] far more fittingly against the heretics themselves than concerning two gods, according to Marcion’s stumbling block.

I think that I have not rashly maintained up to this point the line by which I determine that absolutely nowhere was another God revealed by Christ. I marvel that Marcion’s adulterating hand stood paralyzed [Note: [Col. 0401D] “At this act of adulteration,” Jun.] at this one passage alone [Note: [Col. 0401D] “I marvel that Marcion’s adulterating hand stood paralyzed.” He means the unheard-of crime by which Marcion corrupted blessed Luke’s Gospel, as we said above. LE PR.]—except that even robbers feel fear. No crime is without apprehension [Note: [Col. 0401D] “No crime is without apprehension.” One who fears is generally convicted of being conscious of a crime; for guilt weighs upon him as his companion, and:

Up to that time, therefore, the Jews knew no other God than the one besides whom they had thus far known no one; nor did they call any other “Lord” than the one whom alone they knew. If so, who will appear to have said, “Why do you call [Note: [Col. 0401D] “Do you call,” singular, Seml.] me ‘Lord, Lord’?” Was it the one who had never been called this, since he had nowhere yet been revealed? Or was it the one who had always been regarded as Lord because he had been known from the beginning—namely, [Col. 0401B] the God of the Jews?

Who likewise could have added, “And you do not do [Note: [Col. 0401D] “You do,” singular, Jun.] what I say”? Was it the one who was only then attempting to teach them, or the one who from the beginning had entrusted to them the utterances of both the Law and the Prophets, and who could therefore reproach them for disobedience even if he had never reproached them otherwise? Moreover, he who before Christ had proclaimed, “This people loves me with its lips, but their heart stands far from me” (Isa. 29:13), was surely charging them with an old stubbornness. Otherwise, how absurd that a new God, a new Christ, the illuminator of so new and so great a religion, should declare those people stubborn and disobedient whom he could never have put to the test!

## CHAPTER XVIII

Likewise, when the centurion’s faith is extolled (Luke 7), it is incredible that he should have professed that he had not found such faith even [Col. 0401C] in Israel if the faith of Israel did not pertain to him. Nor could it yet have pertained to him merely from that point onward, for it would have been too undeveloped to be tested or compared—not to say that it did not yet exist at all.

But why should he not have been permitted to use an example drawn from another’s faith? Because, if that were so, he would have said that such faith had never existed in Israel. By saying instead that such faith ought to have been found [Note: [Col. 0401D] “To find,” Seml.] in Israel, and by having come for the very purpose of finding it, he shows himself to be the God and Christ of Israel; nor would he have censured that faith unless he were its claimant and advocate. A rival, by contrast, would actually have preferred to find that faith in such a state, since he had come rather to weaken and destroy it, not to approve it.

He also raised the widow’s dead son—no new proof. The Creator’s prophets had performed this as well; how much more his Son? Indeed, up to that very moment the Lord Christ had introduced no other God, so that all who were there [Col. 0402A] gave glory to the Creator, saying: “A great prophet has arisen among us,” and “God has visited his people.” Which God? Obviously the God whose people they were and from whom the prophets came. But if they were glorifying the Creator, while Christ, though hearing and knowing it, did not correct them—and this precisely amid so great a proof, the raising of the dead, while they still honored the Creator—then beyond doubt either he was carrying about no other God than the one whom he permitted to be honored in his benefits and mighty works, or else what sense does it make that he should endure them in error for so long when he had come for the very purpose of healing their error?

But John takes offense [Note: [Col. 0402D] “But John takes offense,” etc. This is said according to Marcion’s absurd opinion, which one may learn from Epiphanius, *Against Marcion*. LE PR.] upon hearing of Christ’s mighty works, as though they were those of another. I, however, will first explain the reason for his offense, so that I may more easily dispose of the heretic’s stumbling block.

Now that the Lord himself—the Word and Spirit of the Father, the Lord of mighty works—was active and preaching on earth, it was necessary for the portion of the Holy Spirit which had operated in John, according to the form and measure of prophecy, to prepare the Lord’s ways [Note: [Col. 0402D] “To prepare the Lord’s ways.” Rhen. says that he used *praeparatum*, “that which has been prepared,” for *praeparatio*, “preparation,” just as he often uses *paratura*. Yet this too is a Tertullianic expression: *massalis summa*, “the integral total,” from a word derived from *massa*, “mass.” PAM.] now to depart from John, having, of course, been gathered back into the Lord as into its full and integral sum.

Accordingly, John, now an ordinary man and now one [Note: [Col. 0401D] Oberth. omits “now”] of the crowd, was indeed offended insofar as he was a man, but not because he hoped for or imagined another Christ. For neither could he have hoped for the same Christ [Note: [Col. 0402D] “Whence,” Pamel.] as one who taught or performed nothing new. No one will hesitate concerning someone whom he knows not to exist, and whom he therefore neither hopes for nor imagines. But John was certain that there was no God besides the Creator, both as a Jew and, still more certainly, as a prophet. Thus he hesitated, as it were, concerning one whom he knew to exist but did not know whether this was he.

With this concern, therefore, John too asks: “Are you,” he says, “the one who is coming, or do we await [Col. 0402C] [Note: [Col. 0402D] “Hope for,” Seml., Oberth.] another?” He is simply asking whether the one whom he awaited had himself come. “Are you the one who is coming?”—that is [Note: [Col. 0402D] “Or the one who is to come,” Oberth.], “Are you the one who is to come, or do we await another?”—that is, “Is the one we await someone else, if you are not the one whose coming we await?”

For, as everyone supposed from the similarity of the proofs, he hoped that a prophet might perhaps have been sent in the meantime, distinct from another—that is, from someone greater, namely the Lord himself, whose coming was awaited. Indeed, this was John’s stumbling: he doubted whether the very one whom they awaited had come, though they should have recognized him by the works that had been foretold. Thus the Lord informed John that he was to be recognized by those same works.

Since it is established that these works had been foretold of the Creator’s Christ, as we have shown in each individual case, it is sufficiently perverse to understand that a Christ [Note: [Col. 0402D] “Christ,” Pamel.] not belonging to the Creator proclaimed himself by the very means [Note: [Col. 0402D] “Proclaimed himself through them,” Seml.; “proclaimed himself through it,” Pamel.] through which he all the more compelled people to recognize the Creator’s Christ.

It is far more perverse if a Christ who is not John’s Christ also bears testimony to John, confirming him as a prophet—indeed, as more than a prophet, namely an angel—and even adduces what was written about him: “Behold, I send my angel before your face, who shall prepare your way.” He elegantly recalls the prophecy in relation to the preceding sense of John’s offense, so that, by confirming that John the forerunner had already come, he might remove the doubt expressed in John’s question: “Are you the one who is coming, or do we await another?” For, now that the forerunner had discharged his office and the Lord’s way had been prepared, he himself was to be understood as the one whom the forerunner had served.

John was indeed greater [Note: [Col. 0403D] “Than,” Pamel., Fran.; “greater than,” Rig., Venet.] than all born of women, yet he was not thereby made subject to him who was lesser in the kingdom of God—as if the kingdom in which some little one will be greater than John belonged to one God, while John, who is greater than all born of women, belonged to another. For whether he speaks of any “little one” because of humility, or of himself because he was held to be less than John—all, of course, running out into the wilderness to John rather than to Christ: “What did you go out into the wilderness to see?”—the whole matter equally suits the Creator: both that John, who belongs to him, is greater than those born of women, and that Christ, or any little one who will be greater than John in the kingdom, likewise belongs to the Creator; and that one who has not taken offense at Christ is greater than so great a prophet, since this offense then diminished John.

We have spoken about the remission of sins [Note: [Col. 0403D] “The remitted state of sins.” He uses *remissa* for *remissio*, “remission,” as does St. Cyprian in *On the Good of Patience* and Epistle XIV. From this, *Missa*, “Mass,” the term for the most holy sacrifice of the altar, is said to derive from a twofold dismissal. After the Gospel had been expounded, the deacon used to say two things: “If any catechumen remains, let him depart,” or “Let the catechumens depart”; and likewise at [Col. 0404D] the end, “Go, the dismissal has taken place.” From that twofold sending away or dismissal of the people and the catechumens, the Mass received its name. The initiates of the Calvinists execrate this word as if it were monstrous, although it is found in St. Ambrose. On this subject see Durantus’s extensive discussion *On Rites*. LE PR.].

The argument concerning that sinful woman will likewise serve to show that, when she fastened kisses upon the Lord’s feet, flooded them with tears, wiped them with her hair, and covered them with ointment, she touched the reality of a solid body, [Col. 0403C] not an empty phantom. It also shows that, in accordance with the Creator, the sinful woman’s repentance merited pardon, which he was accustomed to prefer to sacrifice. But even if the impulse to repentance had arisen from faith, then, having been justified through repentance arising from faith, she heard from him, “Your faith has saved you”—from the one who had proclaimed through Habakkuk (Hab. 2:4), “The righteous shall live [Note: [Col. 0403D] “Lives,” Seml., Oberth.] by his faith.”

## CHAPTER XIX

That wealthy women adhered to Christ and ministered to him from their possessions—including the wife of the king’s steward (Luke 8)—comes from prophecy. For he called them through Isaiah (Isa. 32:9): “Wealthy women, arise and hear my voice,” so as to show them first as disciples, then as workers and ministers: “Daughters, hear my words in hope. Remember the days of the year [Col. 0403D] with labor in hope.” For they ministered amid the labor with which they followed him, and on account of their hope.

Likewise, concerning parables, let it suffice to have proved once that this kind of discourse was promised by the Creator. But now that declaration of his to the people—“With the ear you shall hear, [Col. 0404A] and you shall not hear” (Isa. 6:10)—gave Christ occasion to repeat frequently: “He who has ears, let him hear.” It was not as though Christ, in opposition, permitted the hearing that the Creator had taken away; rather, the exhortation followed the threat: first, “With the ear you shall hear,” and then, “He who has ears, let him hear.” For those who had ears were not hearing of their own accord; he was showing that ears of the heart were necessary, those by which the Creator had said that they would not hear. Therefore, through Christ he adds: “Take heed how you hear—and do not hear,” that is, hearing with the ear but not with the heart. If you adapt a fitting meaning to the declaration in accordance with the intention of the one who was awakening them to hear, then even by saying, “Take heed how you hear,” he was threatening those who would not hear.

To be sure, he is called an exceedingly gentle God, because he neither judges nor grows angry. The sense that follows proves this as well: “To him who has, it shall be given; but from him who does not have, even what he thinks he has shall be taken away.” What shall be given? An increase of faith, or understanding, or salvation itself. What shall be taken away? Obviously what is to be given. By whom shall it be given and taken away? If it is taken away by the Creator, it will also [Note: [Col. 0404D] “Also” absent from Paris.] be given by him; if it is given by Marcion’s god, it will also be taken away by him. Yet under whatever description he threatens deprivation, he cannot belong to the God who does not know how to threaten because he does not know how to be angry.

I marvel, moreover, that he denies that a lamp is ordinarily hidden, when he himself had remained hidden for so long an age, though he was a greater and more necessary light; and that he promises that everything hidden will be brought into the open, when he continues even now to overshadow his own God—waiting, I suppose, for Marcion to be born.

We have come to the most steadfast argument of all who call the Lord’s birth into question. “He himself,” they say, “testifies that he was not born when he says, ‘Who is my mother, and who are my brothers?’” Thus heretics always either seize naked and simple expressions and drag them by conjecture wherever they wish, or, conversely, dissolve expressions that are conditional and reasoned into a condition of simplicity, as in this passage.

We, on the contrary, say first that it could not have been announced to him that his mother and brothers were standing outside seeking to see him if he had no mother and no brothers. The person making the announcement certainly knew them—either because they had been known previously, or because they were identified there and then when they desired to see him, or because they themselves sent the message.

To this first proposition of ours the opposite party customarily replies: “What if it was reported to him in order to test him?” But Scripture does not say this. Since it is accustomed to indicate when something was done as a test—“Behold, [Col. 0404D] an expert in the Law rose up to test him” (Luke 10); and concerning the consultation about tribute, “The Pharisees approached him, testing him” (Luke 20)—where it makes no mention of testing, it does not admit an interpretation based on testing.

And yet, for good measure, I demand the grounds for the test: for what purpose did they test him by naming [Col. 0405A] his mother and brothers? If it was to learn whether he had been born or not, when had there ever been a question about this that they needed to resolve through that test? Who, moreover, would doubt that a man had been born when he saw him as a man, heard him profess himself Son of Man, and—because of the appearance of every human quality—would hesitate to believe him God or Son of God? They more readily regarded him as a prophet, even if a great one, yet certainly as one who had been born.

Even if he had needed to be tested in an inquiry concerning his birth, any other argument would have suited the test better than mention of those persons whom even a man who had been born might not have. Tell me: Does every person born have a mother who survives him? Is every person born also provided with brothers? Is it not possible to have a father and sisters instead, or even no one at all?

## CHAPTER XX

Who, then, is this who commands even the winds and the sea (Luke 8)? A new master and possessor of the elements, no doubt, now that the Creator has been subdued and excluded? It is not so. Rather, the substances recognized their own author, since they had been accustomed to obey even his servants.

Examine Exodus (Exod. 14), Marcion. Look at the Red Sea, broader than all the lakes of Judea, and at Moses’ rod commanding it: split entirely to its depths and held fixed in an equal marvel of separation on both sides, it allowed the people to pass through its interior pathway on dry foot; and then, at the command of the same rod, as nature returned, it overwhelmed the Egyptian army through the reunion of the waves. For this work even the south winds rendered service.
