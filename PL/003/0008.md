## CLASS V. The Editions of Rigault

1648.

**FIRST RIGAULT EDITION, TWELFTH PARIS EDITION.** Lutetia Parisiorum [Paris], at the press of the widow of Mathurin Dupuis, rue Saint-Jacques, under the sign of the Crown, folio.—1. The complete works of St. Caecilius Cyprian, revised according to the authority of ancient copies and illustrated with observations by Nicolas Rigault. 2. Nicolas Rigault’s *Observations on the Epistles and Treatises of Caecilius Cyprian*. A discourse by an anonymous ancient author *On the Baptism of Heretics*, now published for the first time from the Library of Remi.

“In the *Epistles* and *Treatises*,” says Rigault, “I have displayed the principal features of the ancient text, faithfully preserved from the author’s own style in the excellent Verona and Benevento copies, collated long ago by Marcello—later Pope Marcellus II—and Antonio Agustín, then bishop of Alife. These readings, copied into a volume of Cyprian in the hand of Cardinal de Montchal, archbishop of Toulouse, with the greatest diligence and fidelity, were constantly available for our use. I do not deny that all of them ought indeed to have been reproduced here within the actual text of Cyprian’s works; nor would I wish it to be thought that I was ever uncertain about this plan. I was exceedingly distressed that, because the printing work was being hurried, I could not be present.”

The *Observations*, therefore, says Schoenemann, are concerned partly with adjudicating among the variant readings of the manuscripts cited, and partly serve in place of scholia. Most of them are brief, but they are exceedingly useful, display sound and elegant judgment, and are remarkable for their admirable παρρησία (*frankness*). In addition there is a supplement of observations on Tertullian, published in earlier years, and the famous treatise *On the Beauty of Christ’s Body*. The order is the same as in the Pamelian edition, although the books are not divided into three volumes. Rigault also omitted the first epistle—or rather the fragment of an epistle—which Pamelius had first published. Four copies of this edition, collated with manuscript codices, were held in the Baluze Library.

Dupin judges Rigault’s edition as follows:

> Following Pamelius’s model, M. Rigault, after bringing Tertullian to light, published Cyprian. Without changing anything in the order observed by Pamelius, he merely corrected this author’s works from two Italian manuscripts whose readings de Montchal, archbishop of Toulouse, had entered in the margins of his own Cyprian. He also composed notes explaining difficult passages and observations illustrating the discipline of those times. Some of these observations do indeed appear bold, but he attempts to excuse them in his preface.

Remi Ceillier adds that Rigault’s unconstrained boldness, by which in his commentaries he endeavored to weaken certain passages of St. Cyprian establishing the prerogatives of the bishops of Rome, raised up not a few opponents against him. Chief among them was Albaspinaeus, who, according to Hugo Grotius (*Letter* [Col. LXXX] *to Salmasius*, p. 323), would have denounced him as a heretic had death not interrupted his efforts. He is also ill spoken of among theologians because he denied that infant baptism was of apostolic tradition.

Altdorf, printed and published by Heinrich Meyer, quarto: eighty-three [printed “XXCIII,” uncertain] epistles by Cyprian and certain others, written to him or on a similar controversy. They are arranged in the Pamelian order, divided into sections according to the Goulart edition, and furnished in the appropriate places with St. Augustine’s responses to Cyprian’s arguments concerning the baptism of heretics. The various readings and conjectures of Pamelius, Rigault, and others are also supplied, as are marginal references to Gratian’s *Decretum*, Lombard’s *Books of Sentences*, and Thomas Aquinas’s *Summa Theologiae*; finally, several useful indexes were published separately. The whole was prepared through the efforts of Lucas Friedrich Reinhart, professor of theology at the University of Altdorf, etc.

The text, like the order of the epistles, is Pamelian, with marginal variants added from nearly all the manuscripts encountered in Pamelius, Rigault, and Goulart. The editor says that he burned with desire [Col. LXXXI] to obtain several manuscripts from Germany, but that all the patrons whom he approached for this purpose complained of a lack of them. He did, however, receive from the Nuremberg Library for his use the Roman and Venetian editions of 1471, as well as an unnamed edition. He ought at least to have examined these for the benefit of the curious and to have indicated their variant readings more explicitly. In Dupin’s judgment, this edition contains nothing that is not found in others, except that it was revised from a great many manuscripts.

## CLASS VI. The Oxford Editions

1682.

Oxford, at the Sheldonian Theatre, folio: *The Works of St. Cyprian*, revised and illustrated by John Fell, bishop of Oxford. Added are the *Cyprianic Annals; or, A Brief History, Chronologically Arranged, of the Thirteen Years during Which St. Cyprian Lived among the Christians*, by John, bishop of Chester.

The first merit of this edition is that treatises falsely attributed to Cyprian—which previous editors had also demoted, so to speak, and placed separately, yet in such a way that they either passed over the question of their status without examining it or themselves vouched for some of them, as Pamelius did—were entirely removed from the remaining body of writings. Yet, lest readers miss all of them, the editor arranged for them to be printed separately, together with certain other works still unpublished, and in such a format that they could conveniently be bound with the two companion volumes.

As for the genuine writings, he arranged the *Epistles* in a new order, widely differing from that of Pamelius. To the annotations usefully recorded by earlier editors, especially Rigault, which he reproduced in their own words but in a distinctive typeface, he added his own. Of these he speaks almost too modestly, also warning readers that, if he should perhaps seem to anyone excessively devoted to Cyprian’s opinions, they should remember that he had written notes *on* Cyprian, not *against* him. An editor assumes a certain kind of guardianship and cannot acquit himself honorably unless he defends his author.

Among the merits of the arrangement is the considerable convenience afforded readers by placing first the variant readings and then the annotations beneath the text. Moreover, the text itself is divided into short paragraphs, each supplied with marginal summaries. In addition to what the title promises, the Pamelian indexes to the *Life of Cyprian* were prefixed, together with comparative tables of contents for the editions of Erasmus, Manutius, Pamelius, and Oxford. I need say nothing about the typographical elegance and ornament of this edition.

## CLASS VII. The Editions of Baluze

1726.

**FIRST BALUZE EDITION.** Paris, from the Royal Press, largest folio: *The Complete Works of St. Caecilius Cyprian, Bishop of Carthage and Martyr, Revised according to Manuscript Codices and Illustrated through the Study and Labor of Étienne Baluze of Tulle*. After Baluze, the work was completed, and the preface and *Life of Cyprian* prepared, by one of the monks of the Congregation of St. Maur, D. Prudentius Maran.

Baluze had planned to append all the spurious writings to Cyprian’s genuine works in the form in which they appear in the Oxford edition, omitting only the *Paschal Computation*, while nevertheless including among the genuine works *On Spectacles*, *On the Praise of Martyrdom*, and the work entitled *An Anonymous Author on Rebaptism*. Accordingly, with the aid of the variant readings accumulated by earlier editions and of nearly thirty new manuscript codices, he had notably corrected the text in many places. He had also furnished it with notes, some explaining the reasons for the corrections, others serving to explain the language or elucidate the subject matter.

Most of the work had already been completed, with the exception of two or three sheets of the treatise *On the Principal Works of Christ*, when sudden death overtook him in the midst of his labor. Since Maran therefore had only to work on what remained and understood that he was merely acting as a substitute—for he admits that, if the preparation of a new edition of Cyprian had fallen to him, he would have relieved himself of no small part of so heavy a burden—he retained, because Baluze had so determined, everything contained in Pamelius’s edition. Of the works with which the English had burdened Cyprian, however, he expunged the compositions of Arnold of Bonneval: *On the Works of the Six Days*, *On the Praises of the Blessed Virgin*, and the *Meditations*.

The English had supplied Cyprian’s *Confession* in a Latin translation that was defective in many places and corrupt in innumerable others. Maran instead presented the Greek text supplied from the library of the comte de Seignelay, accompanied by a new translation. Furthermore, because Baluze had seen that the spurious works of Cyprian—which he had planned to reproduce as they stood in the Oxford edition—had acquired many blemishes through the numerous editions of Cyprian, Maran corrected them from old editions and [Col. LXXXIV] manuscript codices, placing variant readings in the margin. Baluze’s emendations, by contrast, since they are often intermingled with explanations and elucidations, are appended separately after the text.

In place of a preface, Maran also discussed at length Cyprian’s works, editions, doctrine, and deeds, though often departing from the Oxford editors and bringing Cyprian into conformity with the doctrines received by the Catholic Church.

The distinguished Oberthür himself explains in the following words the plan he followed:

> It reproduces Maran’s edition—not indeed the Paris edition of 1727 (correct this to 1726), nor the Venetian edition of 1728, but the other Venetian edition of 1758, produced by Girolamo Dorigoni—because the earlier editions, which were certainly better than this last, were not at hand. Before committing it to our press for reprinting, however, I corrected the errors with which a rather negligent editor had caused it to abound. This required great labor and frequent recourse to another Paris edition of Cyprian, prepared by Rigault, which was available to me.
>
> I preferred Maran’s recension of St. Cyprian’s works, which I reproduce here, to Rigault’s because it is more recent and more abundant—even to the point of prodigality—and indeed presents a confused chaos of various works: some that cannot be attributed to Cyprian with sufficient certainty; others that sounder criticism requires rather to be denied to Cyprian; others whose author is unknown; and still others that are indeed owed to known authors, but authors far removed from the age of our Cyprian. It caused me no small difficulty to make a selection: what from this medley of pamphlets I should undertake to reprint in this new edition, what I should pass over, and how, after separating them, [Col. LXXXVI] I should arrange the selected works in some prescribed order.

**New Baluze and Oxford Edition.**—Paris, edited by J.-P. Migne, publisher of this Library. From the synopsis of this edition prefixed to the opening of vol. IV, everyone will perceive what we have added to the Baluze edition, what we have removed, and what we have altered. The following were added:

1. Throughout the entire Cyprianic text, gleanings of selected variant readings, derived chiefly from the Oxford edition.

2. Here and there, several annotations borrowed from Pamelius, Fell, Routh, and Goldhorn.

3. In the book *On the Vanity of Idols*, a richer harvest of notes adapted for our use from Lindner’s edition.

4. In the doubtful works, the notes of Pamelius retained by the Oxford editor.

5. Three short works: the first omitted by Baluze, namely the *Computation of Easter*, published in the appendix of the Oxford edition; the second first brought to light by J. C. Trombellius, namely St. Cyprian’s *Exhortation to Repentance*; and finally the last, the poem to Felix *On the Resurrection of the Dead*, published by Edmond Martène in the *Very Extensive Collection of Ancient Writers and Monuments*, vol. IV.

6. D. Nicolas Le Nourry’s dissertation on Cyprian’s books *To Demetrianus* and *On the Vanity of Idols*, distinguished by its great abundance of material.

7. Finally, at the very end [Col. LXXXVIII] of the edition, we placed a considerable body of selected Oxford and Routhian notes. In this way, not only the Baluze edition but also, in its best part, the English edition was presented anew in ours.

We decided, however, that the following should either be placed elsewhere or omitted entirely:

1. The *Life of St. Cyprian*, written by Pontius the deacon, and the proconsular Acts are given separately below, under their proper year.

2. The letters of the pontiffs—whether those of Cornelius to Cyprian or those of Cyprian and others to the Roman pontiffs—we were obliged to insert in this third volume, where SS. Cornelius, Lucius, and Stephen are treated. Yet we did not fail repeatedly to notify the reader, and even briefly to indicate what had been omitted, whenever anything had to be referred elsewhere.

3. We set aside the synodical epistles in the same volume, especially among the records concerning the baptism of heretics and the Councils of Carthage.

4. We removed from the Cyprianic corpus two books written by anonymous authors in the same dispute and placed them elsewhere.

5. The preface by a certain Celsus, addressed to Bishop Vigilius, to the disputation of Jason and Papiscus *On Jewish Unbelief*—which was perhaps adopted among Cyprian’s works with excessively hasty judgment because in the Colbert manuscript numbered 1403 its opening reads, “Cyprian to his brother Vigilius, greetings”—we transferred, after correction, to the following volumes, where the complete records of this disputation are to be given.

6. The *Exposition of the Creed*, deservedly attributed to Rufinus of Aquileia, and the treatise *On the Principal Works of Christ*, plainly composed by Arnold, abbot of Bonneval, are restored to their proper authors and return to their respective places.

7. The poems *Genesis* and *Sodom* are given above in the appendices to Tertullian.

Indeed, to complete this edition, as it seems, there was added—whether in this third or in the fifth volume—a long series of materials, monuments, and dissertations: first, those that now follow concerning the Roman pontiffs contemporary with Cyprian, along with documents of every kind, both ancient and recent, concerning the celebrated controversy over rebaptism; and then those that immediately follow the Cyprianic works, namely three selected dissertations by Henry Dodwell. Through these, unless I am mistaken, our edition readily becomes more complete and more abundant than all those published hitherto.

Enough, then, has been given to Cyprian and to the other Fathers of the same age. As we now prepare, so to speak, to take leave of them and address our readers for the last time, we hold nothing more fitting or more sacred than, as before when we reached the end of Tertullian, to give thanks to God and to the Mother of God, and once again to venture to offer and dedicate this little work, such as it is, to the Church, our most loving mother on earth. We trust especially in that aid and favorable divine power under which we complete this work just as the welcome feast of the holy Apostles Peter and Paul is about to begin. We therefore earnestly beseech them to stand graciously beside us and our affairs, to preserve both us and our readers safe and unharmed under the same protection, and peacefully and happily to assist us forever.

Written at Paris, on the fourth day before the Kalends of July [June 28], in the year of the Lord 1844.

## THIRD CENTURY, ABOUT THE YEAR OF CHRIST 203

# ACTS OF THE HOLY MARTYRS PERPETUA AND FELICITY

[Col. 0009]

## PROLEGOMENA

FROM THE *AUTHENTIC ACTS OF THE MARTYRS* OF THE DISTINGUISHED THEODORIC RUINART, P. 77 FF.

## SYNOPSIS

[Col. 0009A] I. On the printed editions and manuscripts of these Acts. II. Were Perpetua and Felicity of Thuburbo? III. They are known to have suffered martyrdom not at Thuburbo but at Carthage. IV. The date of their passion is investigated. V. On the author—or rather compiler—of these Acts. Was it Tertullian? [Col. 0010A] VI. He was not a Montanist: against Valesius. VII. The fiction concerning the author’s Montanism is again refuted. VIII. On the cult and commemoration of these martyrs. IX. Acts of this kind were long ago defended against Samuel Basnage by a most learned man. How highly they should be valued. The proconsular Acts are henceforth to be omitted.

I. The authentic Acts of Saints Perpetua and Felicity, greatly desired and long sought in vain in various libraries, were finally discovered by Lucas Holstenius, that most diligent investigator of sacred antiquity, in a manuscript codex [Col. 0009B] of the holy monastery of Monte Cassino. After they had been brought forth from there and published at Rome, Henri Valois later published the same Acts at Paris with notes by Holstenius.

We, however, present them from two manuscript codices collated with that edition. One belongs to the Church of Salzburg; we received the variant readings of this codex through the kindness of the most wise Antoine Faure, a Parisian theologian and provost of the Church of Reims. The other, which approaches eight hundred years in age, belongs to the library of our monastery of Saint-Corneille at Compiègne. We also found among the papers of our Mabillon excerpts from these same Acts that are not to be disregarded, although there is no indication of the codex from which they were copied.

Moreover, in those passages where the manuscript codices differ from one another or from the printed editions, yet either reading [Col. 0009C] is admissible, we have placed the variation in the notes, lest we appear rashly to have altered anything in so precious a monument of the Church. We have exercised the same caution whenever anything worthy of notice occurs in manuscript codices in which these same Acts survive in a corrupt form.

II. In his preface to these Acts, the learned Valesius investigates three questions: first, where these holy women suffered; second, at what time they consummated their martyrdom; and finally, who was the author—or rather compiler—of these Acts. All these matters must also be treated briefly here.

As for the first, before [Col. 0010A] the authentic Acts of these martyrs had been discovered, scarcely anyone called into doubt that they were the same women whom various authors celebrated under the name of the Holy Women of Thuburbo. This was suggested not only by the authority of certain martyrologies, including ancient ones, but also by the Acts circulated under the names of Perpetua and Felicity, [Col. 0010B] in which it was expressly stated that they had suffered at Thuburbo. Although learned men regarded some things in these Acts as faulty and corrupt, no one had hitherto raised any question concerning the place of their martyrdom.

But when the matter was examined more precisely on the occasion of Holstenius’s edition, the more learned took a different view, especially Henri Valois. After weighing the considerations that could be advanced on both sides, he firmly pronounced that these martyrs had consummated their martyrdom not at Thuburbo but at Carthage. That distinguished scholar proves this opinion both from the Acts themselves and from the authority of ancient writers. [Col. 0010C] From the Acts it is established that these martyrs were condemned by proconsular authority, and consequently, as he says, not at Thuburbo in Mauretania, since proconsuls possessed no jurisdiction in that province. Yet, as was observed in the new edition of Augustine, because there were two places named Thuburbo situated in the proconsular province, because there may perhaps have been an amphitheater in one or the other of those cities, and because one of them was called Thuburbo Maius, this argument of Valesius has little force—unless it be said that nearly all the martyrologies expressly located Thuburbo in Mauretania.
