[Col. 1143A] We count very many of Tertullian’s books as printed, but few as surviving in manuscript in libraries. Hence it has come about that even now we do not possess his works reproduced in print with the greatest accuracy: some have appeared publicly in a mutilated state, disfigured by hideous wounds; certain works are also missing which we know Tertullian published, and which, we equally suspect and lament, have been wholly snatched from us by time’s devouring power. [Col. 1144A] The book *On Prayer* has not thus far enjoyed better fortune. John Gagney, a Parisian theologian, was the first of all to publish it at Paris from a very ancient manuscript, in the year of Christ 1545—but by no means complete, and deprived of its splendid conclusion. Nor, for want of manuscripts, did the editions that followed bring assistance to this one. Rigault alone observed that the old manuscript contained the book in a mutilated form; after adding merely a few lines at the end, he left the remedy for others to administer.

When, therefore, I came upon [Col. 1145A] an ancient manuscript of the Ambrosian Library in which those passages absent from the others could be read, I immediately added them to my *Anecdota*, as gems especially welcome to the republic of letters. For whatever was committed to writing by Tertullian—certainly the most learned man of his age—and especially whatever he wrote while in the Catholic camp, is highly valued by all students of ecclesiastical matters, as is evident even to beginners.

The Ambrosian manuscript, however, did not begin the little work with the exposition of the Lord’s Prayer, as we have it in the printed editions, but with the material that follows such an exposition, under this title: “Here begins Tertullian’s work on various necessary matters.” Then the first chapter, whose opening is “By means of the brevity of a few words,” etc., was headed as follows: “Recapitulation.” “That we may add something further” was the title [Col. 1145B] of the following chapter. The remaining chapters likewise bore their own titles, which we have judged should also be retained in this edition of ours.

[Note: [Col. 1146D] Hence the new editors likewise thought that readers would be greatly pleased if, after the annotations of the most illustrious Panciroli and Muratori, they appended notes selected from Rigault, Pamelius, La Cerda, Albaspinaeus, and Priorius.]

Moreover, we have chosen to adorn and elucidate this little work with such annotations, so that what highly learned men have provided for Tertullian’s other books may not be lacking here, and so that certain overly delicate readers may not complain of the darkness and the harsh, iron style of this author and thereby be deterred from reading him. Indeed, among the ancient Christian authors you could point to none upon whom the illumination of learning and scholia seems more necessary to cast than upon Tertullian; for I do not know whether obscurity in expression could in any way have been avoided by him, or whether he cherished it as a delight.

When I had arranged all these matters to the best of my ability, it occurred to me that scholars would not find it unwelcome if I appended to [Col. 1145C] my notes those also which Guido Panciroli, a jurist of Reggio, had written upon the same book *On Prayer*. I hoped all the more readily that my plan would meet with approval because I observed that the illustrious man’s chief concern had been placed in explaining Tertullian’s obscure meanings, whereas I, by contrast, had set myself rather to elucidate the historical and learned remains contained in this little book.

It is, however, to be regretted that no one has yet arisen in Italy who, for the glory of our nation, has taken care to publish Panciroli’s remaining commentaries on Tertullian. We have scarcely a brief mention of them in Thomasinus’s *Life of Panciroli*, in Dupin, William [Col. 1146A] Cave, and a few other writers; indeed, they scarcely knew that any part of them survived. That labor of the very celebrated Panciroli certainly deserved a better fate, for he won for himself an eminent place among learned men through distinction not only in jurisprudence but also in sacred and secular learning.

And indeed I think—as Possevino also thought in his *Apparatus*—that those very abundant commentaries upon Tertullian which Beatus Rhenanus, Pamelius, La Cerda, Salmasius, and other most learned men published prevented Panciroli’s from appearing. Those whose responsibility it was to enhance Panciroli’s posthumous reputation evidently thought that they would expend labor to little advantage if they served the same dishes to the palates of scholars. But Tertullian is not an author who is satisfied with a few interpreters; and since his more recent [Col. 1146B] interpreters were by no means deterred by the efforts of the earlier ones from bringing forth their own, why should this ill-considered fear have dislodged Panciroli’s admirers from so noble a plan?

It can readily happen that, when several different intellects unite in explaining a single author, his mind and writings become clearer day by day and are freed from the darkness arising either from the antiquity of the times, the carelessness of copyists, or the author’s austerity. Although these things may truly be said of each of the ancients, they must be affirmed especially of Tertullian, whom, after the labors of so many interpreters, we still find obscure.

[Note: [Col. 1146D] Hence the new editors likewise thought that readers would be greatly pleased if, after the annotations of the most illustrious Panciroli and Muratori, they appended notes selected from Rigault, Pamelius, La Cerda, Albaspinaeus, and Priorius.]

At the same time that Pamelius was engaged in explaining Tertullian, Panciroli also left us complete commentaries upon all of Tertullian’s writings published down to his own death. [Col. 1146C] Their autograph manuscript lies concealed at Reggio Emilia, his native city. I happened to see and copy only a transcript of it, written with the greatest accuracy and preserved in his library by Father Giovanni Battista Cattaneo, an ornament of the Franciscan Order of the Observance, as they call it. And since by his favor it came about that I could draw whatever I pleased from that manuscript work, I have decided to present here also the letter prefixed to those same commentaries; from it what Panciroli accomplished upon Tertullian will become evident. He therefore writes as follows:

## PANCIROLI’S PREFACE TO THE READER

[Col. 1145]

[Col. 1145D] I consider my arrival at Turin from Padua to have been fortunate chiefly for this reason: among the other highly learned men whom I found there, foremost of all I encountered Vincenzo Lauro, bishop of Mondovì and apostolic nuncio of Our Holy Lord Pope Gregory XIII to the Most Serene Emmanuel Philibert, Duke of Savoy, and a cardinal versed in every branch of learning. By judging me worthy of his private conversations, he continually made me more learned; and just as Marcus Tullius [Col. 1146D] Cicero used to leave the side of Quintus Mucius Scaevola more learned than before (*Laelius*, or *On Friendship*), so I departed richer after drawing no small amount from his deeply learned conversations. At one time he would recount events performed since the foundation of the world; at another he would bring forth the mysteries of philosophy, explain [Col. 1147A] certain obscure authors, correct a corrupt reading from approved copies, or, in accordance with his pious purpose, interpret some passage from Holy Scripture.

Because he took marvelous delight in Tertullian’s writings (St. Jerome, *On Ecclesiastical Writers*, under “Tertullian”) and, like another Cyprian, had him in his hands every day, he was deeply grieved to find him so obscure that at times no meaning could be extracted from him. But since he was overwhelmed by the many affairs pressing upon him as bishop of Mondovì from his own jurisdiction and, as the supreme pontiff’s legate, arising everywhere—either entrusted to him from Rome or emerging from the Subalpine regions—he had no leisure to explain this author and sought someone else who might be equal to undertaking so great a burden.

He saw that, to interpret a man imbued with every branch of learning and thoroughly skilled in the [Col. 1147B] Greek language and in history, there was need of a scholar acquainted with many subjects, especially civil law, since Tertullian himself, as Eusebius writes in *Ecclesiastical History*, book I, chapter 2, was a jurist who everywhere inserted in his writings many things drawn from civil law. But although there was no lack of other learned men who could have performed this task more exactly, he expected more from me than I could bear and considered me suited for this office. For when he observed that I had long taught civil law at Padua and for several years at Turin, and since he had not yet recognized the slenderness of my abilities, he was misled, I suppose, by the public office of teaching entrusted to me and judged me fit to undertake so great a burden, as one who could interpret not only this jurist in my capacity as an interpreter of law, [Col. 1147C] but also many of his more obscure passages.

He therefore first offered me this province; and when I refused once and again, he persisted and finally, by his authority, prevailed upon me to accept the commission. I certainly perceived that, as a man of rather slight learning, I lacked many things necessary for explaining so great an author; that a moderate knowledge of the laws did not suffice to elucidate the many matters he derived from recondite histories, philosophy, medicine, and Holy Scripture; and that my time, entangled in numerous affairs of the courts, did not permit it. But when I said that I could not do it, he believed that I was unwilling:

> What could I do? I was not free to leave his service,  
> Nor was it right not to acknowledge gods so manifestly present.

[Col. 1147D] (Virgil, *Eclogue* 1.)

I was therefore not permitted to resist him, and by obeying I preferred that he should investigate my ability rather than my willingness, and rebuke my weakness rather than my obstinacy.

I consequently placed my neck beneath the yoke and began to undertake in my spare hours what neither my strength allowed nor my time conveniently permitted. But while accommodating him, I fear that what happened to Servius Galba may happen to me: while a private citizen he was judged worthy of imperial power, but once made emperor he appeared unequal to it (Tacitus, book I of the *Histories*, or book XVII of the *Annals*). I too fear that, whereas in silence I seemed to him to know something, in speaking I shall be revealed to everyone as most ignorant. Whatever the outcome may be, however, I wished, by accepting the commands imposed upon me, to test under his authority what I could accomplish. [Col. 1148A]

I found Tertullian obscure in his style, as both Lactantius Firmianus and St. Jerome indicate (Lactantius Firmianus, *Divine Institutes*, V.1; St. Jerome, Epistle 13 to Paulinus), because he either wishes omitted words to be understood, employs unusual expressions, or even fashions a novel phraseology for himself. To assist readers, I compiled a special index of these, so that everyone might see in what sense he takes particular expressions or words.

Since corrupt manuscripts, however, present an even greater difficulty, I emended many passages—but only from things which the same author often repeats or which St. Jerome and others after him quote. On my own initiative I did not dare to change anything beyond a letter or syllable. I preferred that meanings should remain ambiguous rather than that false or alien meanings should be introduced, and that something should be missing rather than corrupted. [Col. 1148B] Nevertheless, I left nothing entirely untouched; and amid great obscurities I thought it better that my ability should be put at risk than that readers should be sent away confused.

Protogenes, although an excellent painter, could not depict the foam of a panting horse, though it was a trivial detail; in anger at his art he struck a sponge laden with every color against the painting and applied it to the animal’s lips, thereby producing by chance what he desired (Pliny, XXXV.10). In the same way, while enveloping the author’s mind in numerous interpretations, I thought it possible that by some effort I might perhaps attain some part of his meaning. The philosophers judge that Averroes accomplished this in commenting on Aristotle, and our own writers judge that Angelo degli Ubaldi did so in explaining Justinian’s *Novellae*, which had been badly translated into another language.

I have also narrated from approved authors all the histories which [Col. 1148C] Tertullian touches upon rather than relates, wherever necessary, so as to leave no one any ground for uncertainty. Among these, the books *On the Soldier’s Crown*, *To the Martyrs*, *On the Dress of Women*, *On the Shows*, and the *Apologeticum*, but especially the little work *On the Pallium*, caused me no small trouble. Some have explained certain matters from these works; but what they and I myself have accomplished will be for others to judge. In any case, passing over all the slighter matters and those which seemed easy, I explained only the difficult ones; and, altering Tertullian’s own order, I divided his works into three parts according to their diversity of subject matter, for the benefit of students.

If I have assisted readers in any respect, I shall ascribe it to God, the true light of all; if otherwise, I shall charge it to my own weakness and to the indulgence of the man who chose a poor interpreter. [Col. 1148D] We certainly possess in Tertullian an author who is great, a teacher of the ancient rites of the Church, and worthy to be read by all. He also refutes the errors of more recent heretics when he frequently teaches that the Eucharist is the true body of Christ—concerning which, however, no one ever doubted or could have doubted before Berengar—admonishes that confession (*exomologesis*) of sins must be made, indicates that offerings were customarily made for the dead, proclaims the authority of the Roman see, and confirms that the souls of the dead are purified by fire, as well as fasts and other similar matters which it would take too long to enumerate. But madmen are not readily persuaded by arguments or authorities. You will therefore receive these modest lucubrations of mine kindly and interpret them equitably and favorably. Farewell.

## THE BOOK ON PRAYER

[Note: [Col. 1149D] Among the outstanding qualities of the Lord’s Prayer, this is plainly marvelous and divine: although it can be grasped by humble and limited minds, it nevertheless satisfies minds that are lofty and most capacious. For the former it is a familiar, daily formula of prayer; for the latter, moreover, a compendium of the entire Christian discipline. The ancient Fathers vied with one another in proclaiming this pledge of heavenly grace. No one explained it more learnedly than Tertullian; no one demonstrated with holier reverence that it should be honored by posterity. He gave his little book the title *On Prayer*, a title as excellent as it is simple, since it signifies the Christian prayer dictated by the Son of God himself. Petitions presented to the emperor were customarily drafted with the assistance of a jurist. Our heavenly jurist, our Lord Jesus Christ, composed for us our petitions to God. RIG.]

## SUMMARY

[Col. 1149]

[Col. 1149A] That golden little book was certainly written by Tertullian while he was still a Catholic. Indeed—if you wish to except his exhortation *To the Martyrs*—it was, in the judgment of Dom Lumper, the first fruit of that great intellect, now coming to life again in its newly acquired faith. It concerns partly dogma and partly morals and discipline.

In its first part, our Septimius—the first of all writers known to us to do so—lucidly explains the Lord’s Prayer and presents in it the doctrine of the entire Gospel gathered together as though in the mirror of a compendium. Many after him traveled this same well-worn road, and Cyprian in particular, manifestly his disciple in this matter, followed him wholly. In the latter part, by an exceptionally rare exposition given the antiquity of the period, he indicates especially in what manner one should participate in sacred rites, and which practices of prayer should either be omitted [Col. 1149B] or diligently performed.

The principal headings of the first part are as follows:

1. How great is that prayer which was delivered by the Word of God and the Reason of God, the word of reason and the reason of the word, Our Lord Jesus Christ. Chapter 1.

2. Next its opening is examined, in which the Father is acknowledged, the Son is invoked in the Father, and not even Mother Church is passed over. Chapter 2.

3. Then each petition is separately explained. In the first, we too, as candidates for the company of angels, ask for God’s heavenly glory and for his name to be sanctified in us. Chapter 3. In the second, we pray that his will may be done in us, who are heaven and earth. Chapter 4. [Col. 1149C] In the third, moreover, we must hasten to the embrace of our hope, the desire of Christians, the confusion of the nations, and the exultation of the angels. Chapter 5. The fourth admits a threefold explanation: first concerning ordinary bread; then concerning Christ, as the bread of believers; and finally concerning the Eucharistic body of Christ. Chapter 6. The fifth then implores God’s clemency. Chapter 7. The sixth and seventh, finally, pray for the removal of temptation and evil. Chapters 7–9.

The latter part, however, is manifold, though it may without very great difficulty be reduced to these three divisions:

1. What things fruitfully prepare the soul before prayer: namely, remembrance of the commandments and, above all, the duties of charity; and a free concentration of the mind, by which the Spirit’s inexpressible groanings are drawn forth to the Father. Chapters 10–15.

2. What may be omitted either before or after prayer: namely, washing the hands, removing one’s cloak, or sitting down. Chapters 16–17.

3. What practices are fitting or must necessarily be performed: namely, the raising of the hands; the lowering of the face and voice; the kiss of peace; the station at the altar after the Lord’s body has been received and reserved; the modest attire of women and the veiling of virgins; the practice of bending the knees; the place of prayer and its more solemn hours; the antiphonal singing of psalms; and the devout *agape*, crowned with a procession of good works and conducted to God’s altar amid psalms and hymns. Chapters 16–28. To this densely packed and marvelously rich store of ancient rites is added, as a crowning conclusion, an ornate and very ample description of the power and effect of prayer. Chapter 29. [Col. 1150C] THE EDITORS.

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 1149C]

The Spirit of God,

[Note: [Col. 1149C] **The Spirit of God.** Not only here but elsewhere too Christ is called the Spirit of God by Tertullian, as may be seen in *Apologeticum*, chapter 21; *Against Marcion*, book I, chapter 10, book III, chapters 6 and 16, and book IV, chapter 21; and *Against Praxeas*, chapter 26. The designation certainly sounds improper to our ears now, but it was incautiously employed by certain ancients who did not have before their eyes the heresies that arose afterward. Lactantius Firmianus especially held this view, according to St. Jerome’s testimony in his letter to Pammachius and Oceanus *On the Errors of Origen*: “Lactantius,” he says, “in his books, and especially in the letters [Col. 1149D] to Demetrianus, wholly denies the substance of the Holy Spirit; and, by a Jewish error, says that he is referred either to the Father or to the Son, and that the sanctification of each person is indicated under his name.” Jerome repeats the same statement in his commentary on chapter IV of the Epistle to the Galatians. It would therefore seem beyond doubt that Tertullian errs here too. Yet because in other books he distinguishes the Holy Spirit from the Father and the Son with the greatest clarity, calling him the third divine being of the Godhead and the third name of majesty, [Col. 1150C] I therefore think that he called Christ the Spirit of God because he believed that, in a general sense, God was said to be spirit, as the apostle John writes: “God is spirit.” L. A. MURATORI.

—**The Spirit of God.** Before interpreting the Lord’s Prayer, he states who taught it. It was Christ, whom he calls by several names: for he is Spirit, by which he performed miracles; the Word of God; and Reason, or Wisdom, which came into the world, as he adds below. Tertullian likewise says in chapter 17 of the *Apology*: “What we worship is one God, who from nothing brought forth this entire mass, together with its whole equipment of elements, bodies, and spirits, by the word with which he commanded, [Col. 1150D] the reason with which he arranged, and the power with which he was able, as an adornment of his majesty.” G. PANCIROLI.]

and the Word of God, and the Reason of God,

[Note: [Col. 1150D] **The Word of God and the Reason of God.** The Greek term *Logos* (Λόγος), a proper name of the Son of God among the Greeks, has had several Latin renderings: *Verbum* (“Word”), *Sermo* (“Word” or “Discourse”), and *Ratio* (“Reason”). The ancient writers of the Church used these Latin names interchangeably to signify the same thing. We now prefer to translate it as “the Word.” MURATORI.]

and [Col. 1150C] the word of reason and the reason of the word,

[Note: [Col. 1150D] **The word of reason, and the reason of the word.** Whoever wishes to measure the language of the ancients by modern usage will be surprised that Tertullian here bestows upon God [Col. 1151B] the Father too the designation of Word and Reason. But because the term *logos* (τοῦ λόγου) is ambiguous and signifies reason, thought, mind, and understanding, in that sense the designation both of *Logos* (τοῦ Λόγου) and of reason and word was made common to the Father as well. Therefore the Son was called by some of the Fathers *Logos from Logos* (Λόγος ἐκ Λόγου), “Word from Word”; and St. Irenaeus, book II, chapter 48, calls the Father *logon* (λογόν), or word, and mind, writing as follows: “For God, being wholly Mind and wholly Logos, speaks what he thinks and thinks what he speaks. [Col. 1151C] For his thought is Logos, and Logos is Mind; and the Mind that encompasses all things is the Father himself.”

But by whatever terms the Father and the Son may be called, inadequate and ambiguous terms for the Godhead are excused by the magnitude and majesty of the ineffable reality, provided that it is agreed that we distinguish the Son from the Father and proclaim one essence and nature in them and in the Holy Spirit. St. Ambrose held this position most admirably in *On the Divinity of the Son*, chapter 5. We wish to reproduce his words so that it may be evident that not only Tertullian but other ecclesiastical writers also designated both the Father and the Son by the names Word, Reason, Discourse, and Spirit. “This, then,” he says, “is the rationale of the name: that in the Father and the Son you should believe unity of substance, although you cannot define the reality itself, which is ineffable; so that, whether you say Light from Light, Word from Word, Spirit from Spirit, or Lord from Lord—whatever you say concerning him—[Col. 1151D] you may nevertheless believe the Father and the Son to be of one essence.” See also St. Irenaeus, book II, chapter 48. John too had taught his disciples to pray; but everything belonging to John was being prepared in advance for Christ, until, when Christ himself had increased—as the same John foretold, saying that he must increase while John himself must decrease (John III.30)—the whole work of the forerunner, together with his spirit, passed over to the Lord. For this reason the words in which John taught them to pray are not extant, because earthly things yielded to heavenly things. “He who is from the earth,” he says, “speaks earthly things; and he who is present from heaven speaks the things he has seen” (John III.31). Let us therefore consider, blessed brethren, his heavenly Sophia (σοφία, “Wisdom”),

[Note: [Col. 1152B] **Let us therefore consider, etc.** What are these three precepts—or rather counsels—concerning prayer which Tertullian enumerates here and in what follows? You will understand at a glance from the Gospel of John, chapter VI, verse 6 [sic; the quotations are from Matthew VI.5–7], where Christ, [Col. 1152C] the divine Sophia, or Wisdom, speaks in these words: “And when you pray, you shall not be like the hypocrites, who love to pray standing in synagogues and at street corners, so that they may be seen by men.” Here is the first precept: namely, that we flee ambition in praying, as though God could hear and see us only in public, and that we trust that both the sight and hearing of almighty God are present even under roofs and in hidden places; this pertains to a person’s faith.

Christ then continues: “But when you pray, enter your chamber, and after closing the door pray to your Father in secret.” Here is the second precept, as Tertullian explains it: that we offer our devotion to him alone whom we trust hears and sees everywhere; this concerns the modesty of faith.

The divine Teacher then continues: “But when you pray, do not speak at length like the Gentiles; for they think they will be heard because of their many words.” This is the third precept: namely, to speak with Tertullian, that we should not suppose the Lord must be approached with a column [Col. 1152D] of words, since we are certain that he looks after his own of his own accord. [Note: [Col. 1152D] **To pray in secret.** *Adorandi* is redundant and stands for *orandi*. this brevity, insofar as it belongs to the third stage of Sophia, is sustained by the substance of a great and blessed interpretation: to the same extent that it is compressed in words, it expands in meanings. For it has embraced not only the proper duties of prayer—reverence toward God and human petition—but almost every discourse of the Lord and every recollection of his discipline, so that in truth [Col. 1153B] the whole Gospel is comprehended in this prayer as in a compendium.

For we both pray to God and commend our faith, whose reward is this form of address. It is written: “To those who have believed in him he gave the power to be called sons of God” (John III; Matthew VII.9; X and XV).
