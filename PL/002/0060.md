## CHAPTER XXXIII

Finally, as a kind of concluding flourish after so great a fable, I shall bring forward also those doctrines which—lest they disrupt the order and scatter the reader’s attention by their insertion—I preferred to postpone until this point, though they have been recommended in various different forms by Ptolemy’s revisers.

For pupils from his own school arose above their master, who assigned a double conjugal pair to their Bythos: Thought and Will. For a single Thought, by which he could have produced nothing, was not enough; with two, production was very easy. [Note: [Col. 0589B] “Produced very easily,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] The first conjugal pair was Only-Begotten and Truth: Truth, the female, in the image of Thought; Only-Begotten, the male, in the image [Col. 0589B] of Will. For the power of Will, as that which gives effect to Thought, possesses the status of the male. [Note: [Col. 0589B] “And” is absent from Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] [Note: [Col. 0589B] “As indeed,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] [Note: [Col. 0589D] *Status.* Tertullian has passed over a great many things and omitted everything that made up Irenaeus, chapters 8, 9, 10, and 11. How successfully, as he intended, he took care that nothing should disrupt the order, I should like you to see and judge at your pleasure. Irenaeus, book II, ch. 12, §1: “Ptolemy and those with him, being even more expert than their own teacher in these matters … devised and bestowed upon God, whom they call Bythos, two spouses, which he also called dispositions: Thought and Will. Therefore, from these two dispositions … the production of Only-Begotten and Truth took place as a conjugal pair; they came forth as certain types and images of the Father’s two dispositions … the male became the image of ungenerated Thought, and the female of Will; Will, therefore, became the power of Thought.”

## CHAPTER XXXV

## CHAPTER XXXVI

How much better are those who, having removed this whole wearisome business from the way, maintained that no Aeon was constructed from another by steps that were truly Gemonian, [Note: [Col. 0590D] *Steps that were truly Gemonian.* The names “Gemonian Steps” and “Gemonian Stairs” are very well known among philologists. They bear their names, finally, from his own activity. When, they say, he thought of producing, in this respect he was called Father. ## CHAPTER XXXVII

Receive now the other conceptions of Ennius’ Curruca, [Note: [Col. 0591C] *Receive the other conceptions.* Latinius thought it should be emended to “Receive other huge, merchant-vessel-like things.” A *cercurus*, he says, is the name of a very large Asian ship. Festus indeed handed down this information. But this is far too feeble in comparison with the stupidity and madness of the Valentinian whom Tertullian is ridiculing. The wholly consistent reading of the old copies, however, is: “Receive other Circurianian conceptions.” From this we conjecture that our author wrote: “Receive the other conceptions of Ennius’ Curruca, a more distinguished teacher among them,” etc. *Curruca* was the name of a certain foolish mimic actor, as we learn from the ancient commentator on this verse from Juvenal’s sixth Satire:

> Now, Curruca, you are pleased with yourself, and from her lips  
> You drink the tears—

where some fool is clearly being portrayed. Ennius, it seems, was the first to introduce this Curruca as a character well suited to ridiculous performances. Hence Tertullian here called one of the leading Valentinians—who uttered his ravings as though they were pontifical pronouncements, with words contrived for the occasion—“Ennius’ Curruca.” Jerome, imitating Tertullian, said in the second book *Against Rufinus*: “You translate a scholion of Didymus in which, with fruitless labor, he attempts to defend another man’s work, claiming that Origen indeed spoke correctly, but that we, simple men and Ennius’ Currucae, cannot understand either his wisdom or yours, who translated him.” For this is also how the corrupt reading there, “Ennius’ tame creatures,” should be emended. Moreover, our conjecture may also appear to be confirmed by the fact that Irenaeus, attacking this Valentinian whom Septimius calls Curruca, played upon the words for melon, gourd, and cucumber. This Henotes and Monotes, being one, emitted, although they emitted nothing, the Beginning of all things, Noeton and Agenneton and Aoraton, which the discourse calls Monad. With this Monad is a power of the same substance as it, which I also call Hen. These powers, therefore—that is, Monotes and Henotes, Monad and Hen—emitted the remaining emissions of the Aeons.”]

## CHAPTER XXXVIII

Secundus is now more humane, because more concise. He divides the Ogdoad into two Tetrads, right and left, light and darkness. His only qualification is that he does not derive that power which leapt away and fell short from any one of the [Col. 0592B] thirty [Note: [Col. 0592B] “Thirty” is absent from Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] Aeons, but from the fruits proceeding from their substance. [Note: [Col. 0592D] “Proceed.” Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, ch. 11, §2: “Secundus says that the first Ogdoad consists of a right-hand Tetrad and a left-hand Tetrad, handing down that they are called respectively light and darkness; but he says that the power which separated and fell short did not come from the thirty Aeons, but…”

The old translation.—“Secundus, however, handed down the first Ogdoad as follows, saying that there is a right-hand quaternion and a left-hand quaternion, light and darkness; and he says that the power which departed and was deprived did not come from the thirty Aeons, but from their fruits.”] [Note: [Col. 0592B] “Proceed from the substance,” Rhen., Seml., Oberth.]
