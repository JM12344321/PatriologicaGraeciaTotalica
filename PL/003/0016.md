This is indeed a very serious presumption against the compiler of the Acts, as they call him; yet I do not know whether the argument drawn from the following words is not still weightier. For he continues thus: “Accordingly, we too, who acknowledge and honor new visions as likewise promised, just as we do prophecies.” These words are plainly decisive: the author could not have declared in clearer language what sect he himself professed. They are the watchword of Montanism and a clear and open profession of Montanist doctrine. For at that time the dispute between Catholics and Montanists concerned precisely this question: whether new—that is, extraordinary and unprecedented—visions and prophecies were to be acknowledged and honored, [Col. 0078B] or, on the contrary, repudiated and rejected. Catholics abhorred the new visions and prophecies as a deception of an evil spirit, not because they recognized no visions or prophecies in the Church at that time, or maintained that the fountain of charisms had dried up; rather, by the name “new visions” and “new prophecy” they understood things that were unusual, as I have said, and, in the words of Asterius as quoted by Eusebius, [Note: [Col. 0078D] Παρὰ τὸ κατὰ παράδοσιν καὶ κατὰ διαδοχὴν ἄνωθεν τῆς Ἐκκλησίας ἔθος δῆθεν, “contrary, so it was alleged, to the custom of the Church handed down from above by tradition and continuous succession.” *Hist.* V, ch. 16.] “Contrary to the custom and ordinance of the Church handed down by the forefathers and thereafter propagated through continuous succession.”

The Montanists, by contrast, highly esteemed, embraced, and honored these new revelations and prophecies; and this was the first pretext with which they cloaked their secession from the Church. From it, as is evident from Serapion, [Note: [Col. 0078D] Τῆς ἐπικαλουμένης νέας προφητείας, “of the so-called new prophecy,” etc. *Epistle to Caricus and Ponticus*, in Eusebius, book V, ch. 19.] the wicked faction also received its name and [Col. 0078C] “was called the New Prophecy.” Asterius Urbanus clearly and openly testifies in these words that they themselves gave their sect this name: [Note: [Col. 0078D] Καὶ καταλαβὼν τὴν κατὰ πόντον (read τόπον) Ἐκκλησίαν ὑπὸ τῆς νέας ταύτης, οὐκ ὡς αὐτοί φασι, προφητείας, etc., “And finding the Church of that place troubled by this new—not, as they themselves say—prophecy,” etc. Book I, *Against the Cataphrygians*, in Eusebius, ibid., ch. 16.]: “And I found the Church of that place afflicted by that new—not, as they themselves call it—prophecy, etc.”

After defecting to the Montanists, Tertullian everywhere boasts of and proclaims new prophecies. In the book *On Fasting* [Note: [Col. 0078D] Ch. 1.]: “For this reason the new prophecies are rejected.” And in book III *Against Marcion* [Note: [Col. 0078D] Ch. 24.]: “The word of the New Prophecy, which abides with our faith, bears witness.” And in book IV [Note: [Col. 0078D] Ch. 22.]: “Which we defend in the cause of the New Prophecy.” And in the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* [Note: [Col. 0078D] Final chapter.]: “Through the New Prophecy, which pours forth from the Paraclete.” According to Firmilian, [Note: [Col. 0078D] Epistle 75 among those of Cyprian.] the Cataphrygians are those who “endeavor to appropriate new prophecies.” From this we also understand that the Catholics likewise called them “new prophets.” Since this is so, anyone who, amid the disputes then raging between Catholics and Montanists concerning new visions and [Col. 0079A] prophecies, openly and expressly testifies that he acknowledges and honors new visions and prophecies, plainly reveals himself and most openly professes himself to be a Montanist. Certainly no Catholic can be shown to have spoken in this manner. It is the profession of the Montanists, to which Tertullian, after embracing the doctrines of this sect, gave a parallel expression in these words: [Note: [Col. 0079D] *On the Soul*, ch. 19.] “For since we acknowledge spiritual charisms, etc.” So too the editor of the Acts: “We acknowledge and honor new visions and prophecies.” But according to Firmilian, those who endeavor to appropriate new prophecies are called Κατάφρυγες (*Cataphrygians*). The compiler of the Acts can therefore by no reasoning be removed from the catalogue of the Cataphrygians or Montanists.

I derive a third—and likewise extremely weighty—argument from a particular observation made by the author [Col. 0079B] when he relates how Perpetua was not conscious of having fought against the most savage cow: [Note: [Col. 0079D] In the Acts, no. 20.] “There Perpetua . . . awoke as if from sleep (so deeply,” says the author, “had she been in the spirit and in ecstasy), began to look around, and, while everyone stood astonished, said: ‘When are we to be brought out against that cow? I do not know.’” To what purpose can that observation—“so deeply had she been in the spirit and in ecstasy”—have been directed, unless to establish the Montanist doctrine concerning ecstasy?

It is indeed true that the Fathers sometimes used the word *ecstasy* in a favorable sense. Yet in those times when the Montanist heresy was especially rampant, it was generally understood in the worse sense, as signifying alienation of mind, frenzy, and madness. Hence, as Tertullian testifies, when Catholics and Montanists at that time disputed [Col. 0079C] whether ecstasy was compatible with true prophecy, the Montanists defended the affirmative opinion and the Catholics the negative. “In the cause of the New Prophecy,” says Tertullian, [Note: [Col. 0079D] Book IV, *Against Marcion*, ch. 22.] “we maintain that ecstasy—that is, madness—is compatible with grace . . . a matter concerning which there is a dispute between us and the Psychics.” Hence the books Περὶ ἐκστάσεως (*On Ecstasy*) written by that same Tertullian on behalf of the Montanists against the Church, as Jerome records. [Note: [Col. 0079D] *Catalogue*, under Apollonius and Tertullian.] Conversely, Miltiades, as Eusebius records, [Note: [Col. 0079D] Book V, ch. 17, from Asterius Urbanus, book III, *Against the Cataphrygians*.] wrote against the same Montanists under the title Περὶ τοῦ μὴ δεῖν προφήτην ἐν ἐκστάσει λαλεῖν, “That a prophet ought not to speak in ecstasy.”

On this passage of Eusebius, Valesius aptly observes that the Catholics made this their chief objection to the Montanists, who boasted that they were filled with the prophetic spirit: that they prophesied in ecstasy, [Col. 0079D] although neither in the New nor in the Old Testament do we ever read that the prophets prophesied in ecstasy. Who, then, could have so dull a nose that he did not scent Montanism in the foregoing words of our author?

I certainly do not intend to cast the odium of Montanism upon every writer who numbers ecstasies among the charisms of God. I am not unaware that this word was also sometimes understood in a favorable sense by the most ancient Fathers. Besides nocturnal visions, we learn from Cyprian of ecstasies sent by the Holy Spirit to innocent children during the day; and no suspicion of a corrupt opinion on this subject can be transferred to him: [Note: [Col. 0080D] Epistle 16, otherwise 10.] “Besides nocturnal visions, the innocent age of children among us is also filled by day with the Holy Spirit, and in ecstasy sees with its eyes and hears, etc.”

The case of the compiler of the Acts is nevertheless not the same. He committed these celebrated records to writing, not, like Cyprian and the other ancient Fathers afterward, once the disturbances caused by the Montanists had subsided, but while factions and dissensions were growing from day to day. At that time he certainly ought either to have abstained from the word *ecstasy*, or to have used it in such a way as to remove every reproach or suspicion of a corrupt meaning from it.

[Col. 0080B] III. I must now take up Dodwell, whose opinion, as I have already said, is that the compiler of the Acts not only belonged to the Catholic communion and was hostile to the Montanists, but also labored to publish these renowned records in order to convict their new prophecies of falsehood. He gives the following reason for this truly remarkable opinion of his:

“Maximilla had predicted of herself that after her there would be no other prophetess, but the consummation. Her words in Epiphanius are these: [Note: [Col. 0080D] Heresy XLVIII, §2.] Μετ᾽ ἐμὲ προφῆτις οὐκέτι ἔσται, ἀλλὰ συντέλεια, ‘After me there will no longer be a prophetess, but the consummation.’ It is most probable that Maximilla died toward the end of Marcus’s reign. For since Asterius Urbanus [Note: [Col. 0080D] In Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, chs. 16–17.] counts thirteen or fourteen years begun from her death to the year in which he was writing, learned men judge that it cannot more suitably be referred to any period other than the reign of Commodus. Catholics, by contrast, thought that the falsity of their prophetic pretensions could be proved from this very fact: that, even by their own admission, an end would at some point have to be assigned to their prophecy, whereas true prophecy would be perpetual and would in fact endure until the consummation of the world. By this reasoning they were so far from denying the prophecies of their own times that any church in which there were no prophets was not even to be regarded as a Church.”

Asterius Urbanus argued in this fashion: [Note: [Col. 0080D] Εἰ γὰρ μετὰ Καδράτον καὶ τὴν ἐν Φιλαδελφείᾳ Ἀμμίαν, ὥς φασιν, αἱ περὶ Μοντανὸν διεδέξαντο γυναῖκες τὸ προφητικὸν χάρισμα, τοὺς ἀπὸ Μοντανοῦ, καὶ τῶν γυναικῶν τίνες παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς διεδέξαντο, δειξάτωσαν· δεῖν γὰρ εἶναι τὸ προφητικὸν χάρισμα ἐν πάσῃ τῇ Ἐκκλησίᾳ μέχρι τῆς τελείας παρουσίας, ὁ Ἀποστόλος ἀξιοῖ, “For if, after Quadratus and Ammia of Philadelphia, as they say, the women associated with Montanus succeeded to the prophetic gift, let them show who among them succeeded the followers of Montanus and the women; for the Apostle declares that the prophetic gift must exist throughout the whole Church until the final coming.”] “For if, as they proclaim, after Quadratus and Ammia of Philadelphia those women associated with Montanus succeeded to the grace of prophecy, let them show us who among them succeeded Montanus and the women. For the Apostle declares that the gift of prophecy must remain throughout the whole Church until the final [Col. 0080D] coming of the Lord.”

“The ancient author of the Acts of Perpetua and Felicity, whoever he was, a contemporary of the martyrs, argues according to the same pattern. Having proved, by adducing the passage from Joel, that the prophecies of the New Testament were so far from falling short of those of the Old Testament that, on the contrary, even greater prophecies were to be expected under the New Testament, [Col. 0081A] he then adds the following in the sense I have stated: ‘Accordingly, we too, who acknowledge new visions as well as prophecies, etc.’ That whole discussion has precisely this purpose: to prevent the visions that would follow from being discredited because God had deigned to reveal them in the last times of the Church. To prevent this, he undertook to prove that they were chiefly to be expected in the last times, and always until the consummation. These things could perhaps be thought to have been said against the pagans; they could also have been said against the Montanists. I would rather believe that they were said against the Montanists, because he confirmed by the testimony of Scripture what he had said. It is certainly manifest that the points he advanced here were admitted among his own people, something that a Montanist could by no means have written.

[Col. 0081B] “For it should first be noted that these things were written by the author in A.D. 202, that is, about twenty-three years after Maximilla’s death. For we have said that she died in the last year of Marcus, namely A.D. 179. But if she had been a true prophetess, no other prophet was now to be expected. Therefore, one who still expected others, and believed that they were always to be expected, cannot possibly have regarded her as a true prophetess, and consequently could not have been a Montanist. For although that heresy was afterward divided into various sects, all nevertheless agreed that those first women were to be acknowledged as prophetesses.

“Secondly, even if we interpret Maximilla’s words in the most favorable sense and understand them as referring to female prophetesses, not to male prophets, [Col. 0081C] this preface still could not have been written by a Montanist. For he was about to include the visions of the prophetess Perpetua. Yet it is certain that this interpretation cannot be accommodated to Maximilla’s meaning. For when she said that after her there would be the συντέλεια (*consummation*), it necessarily followed that this would utterly exclude every further exercise of prophecy.

“Thirdly, since Asterius Urbanus had used this very argument at least ten years earlier in refuting that sect, who could believe that a patron of the sect would employ the same argument?

“Fourthly, it is also evident from Urbanus himself that they had in fact brought their entire succession of prophets to an end with Maximilla and had admitted no successor whatsoever after her. Epiphanius, employing the same argument, confirms that after her death no one else was acknowledged by the Montanists down to his own time. It is therefore impossible that a Montanist should have enrolled Saturus and Perpetua in the list of the prophets. At the very least, a reader who argues in this way must concede from the reasoning of those others that he ought not on that account to be regarded as a Montanist. Yet the admittedly learned men who think otherwise offer no other evidence.”

Thus Dodwell. Yet although his arguments are learnedly and subtly made, they could by no means wrest from me my assent. For in such matters the records of events possessing unquestionable credibility must prevail over all our subtle and learned observations. From those records, as I shall presently [Col. 0082A] show, it is manifest that Dodwell’s entire discussion contains nothing strong or solid enough to remove the suspicion of Montanism from our author.

IV. The learned man’s entire argument, then, rests upon these two foundations. First, Maximilla died toward the end of Marcus’s reign, about twenty-three years before Perpetua and Felicity and their companions underwent martyrdom. Secondly, the Montanists were persuaded that there would be no prophet after Maximilla’s death, and still less that another prophetess was to be expected after her. Consequently, the writer of the Acts—who regarded Saturus and Perpetua as distinguished by the gifts of prophecy and heavenly revelations, and believed that these same charisms would remain in the Church until the consummation—could not possibly have been imbued with the errors of the Montanists. To me, however, neither of the learned man’s assumptions seems admissible.

As regards the first point, Maximilla’s death, I know indeed that many hold the opinion that it should be assigned to the last year of Marcus. But, as I have shown elsewhere [Note: [Col. 0082D] Dissertation *De capit. crimin. absol.*, §2.] and Valesius observed long ago, [Note: [Col. 0082D] Annotation on Eusebius, book V, ch. 18.] their error arose from their belief that Asterius—who testifies that he wrote his books fourteen years after the death of Montanus and Maximilla—was earlier than Apollonius, by whom it was recorded that Montanus had introduced his prophecy forty years before Apollonius wrote. [Note: [Col. 0082D] In Eusebius, loc. cit.] But that was not how matters stood.

“For,” as Valesius observes, “Apollonius composed his book while Montanus was still alive, together with his frenzied prophetesses [Col. 0082C] Priscilla and Maximilla, as may be gathered from the fragments that Eusebius adduces, and this in more than one place. Thus he said: Ἡ προφῆτις ἡμῖν εἰπάτω τὰ κατὰ Ἀλέξανδρον τὸν λέγοντα ἑαυτὸν μάρτυρα, ᾧ συνεστιᾶται, ᾧ προσκυνοῦσι, καὶ αὐτῷ πολλοί, ‘Let the prophetess tell us about Alexander, who calls himself a martyr, with whom she feasts, and whom many even worship.’ And a little later, speaking about the same Alexander, he says: Ὃν ὁ προφήτης συνόντα πολλοῖς ἔτεσιν ἀνοεῖ, ‘Whom the prophet, though he has associated with him for many years, fails to recognize.’ From these words it is apparent that Alexander, Montanus himself, and Maximilla were still alive when Apollonius wrote these things. Nor does Apollonius say that forty years had elapsed from the death of Montanus to the time at which he was writing; he merely says that Montanus had introduced his false prophecy forty years before Apollonius himself undertook to write.”

Accordingly, since Apollonius composed his books during Severus’s reign, while Maximilla was still alive, [Col. 0082D] I demonstrated in the passage cited above that the fourteen years of undisturbed government and peace for the Church which Asterius mentions as having elapsed since Maximilla’s death must be reckoned from Caracalla’s accession after his father’s death to the earlier years of Alexander, and that Maximilla died around A.D. 212. Once these facts are recognized, Dodwell’s constructions upon his false date for Maximilla’s death readily collapse. For the most probable opinion of learned men, including Dodwell, is that Perpetua [Col. 0083A] and Felicity died as martyrs around A.D. 203, almost at the very beginning of the Severan persecution. Perpetua’s martyrdom therefore preceded Maximilla’s death by at least ten years. Thus a Montanist writer could acknowledge and venerate her prophecies and honor her as a prophetess, even though he knew—indeed, unshakably and firmly believed—that there would be no prophetess after Maximilla.

V. Furthermore, if we suppose that Dodwell’s conclusions concerning Maximilla’s death are true—namely, that she died in the last year of Marcus—it necessarily follows that what he states in the second place regarding Montanist agreement upon the proposition that there would be no prophetess in the Church after Maximilla is altogether false. For it can be demonstrated so clearly and openly that the Montanists acknowledged other prophetesses after Maximilla—if her death is assigned to the last year of Marcus—that it is astonishing that the learned man made this a foundation of his opinion.

Had Tertullian not defected to the Montanists when he wrote the book *On the Soul*? Did he not compose that book nearly thirty years after Maximilla’s death, if she died in the last year of Aurelius? Yet there he professes that he acknowledged other false prophetesses after that frenzied woman: [Note: [Col. 0083D] Ch. 9.] “Today among us there is a sister who has obtained charisms of revelations, which she experiences in the Church amid the Lord’s solemn rites through ecstasy in the spirit. She converses with angels, sometimes even with the Lord, and sees and hears mysteries, discerns the hearts of some, and provides remedies to those who desire them. Moreover, according as the Scriptures are read, or psalms sung, or addresses delivered, or petitions offered, material is thereby supplied for her visions. [Col. 0083C] We happened to have been discussing something about the soul while that sister was in the spirit. After the solemn rites had concluded and the congregation had been dismissed, according to the custom by which she regularly reports to us what she has seen—for the accounts are most carefully arranged so that they may also be tested—she said, among other things, ‘The soul was shown to me corporeally,’ etc.”

Behold an outstanding Montanist prophetess after Maximilla! Moreover, in the same book [Note: [Col. 0083D] Ch. 59.] Tertullian acknowledges and commends Perpetua’s revelations no less than does the author of the Passion, and therefore judged, no less than the compiler of the Acts, that Perpetua should be enrolled in the list of prophets: “Perpetua, the most courageous martyr, in a revelation of the glory of paradise saw there only her fellow martyrs.” Yet Perpetua had scarcely been born when Maximilla died, if she ceased to live toward the end of Marcus’s reign, namely in [Col. 0083D] 179; for in 203, the year in which she died as a martyr, she was, as the Acts state, about twenty-two years old.

Moreover, in the same book Tertullian is so far from believing that the spirit of prophecy failed in the Church after Montanus and Maximilla that he instead indicates that they had opened for him the way to receive new charisms, since those charisms had by no means been spurned in those false prophets. He establishes and confirms this perpetual and unfailing abundance of divine charisms from the testimony both of the Apostle in that passage and of Joel elsewhere, [Note: [Col. 0083D] Book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, near the end.] just as does the writer of the Acts: [Col. 0084A] “For since we acknowledge spiritual charisms, we too have deserved, even after John, to obtain prophecy. And today there is a sister, etc.” After relating the frenzied prophetess’s vision, he adds: “This is the vision; God is witness, and the Apostle a competent guarantor of the charisms that would exist in the Church.”

Finally, in his book *On Fasting against the Psychics*, does he not reproach them precisely because they set boundaries—or, as he calls them, boundary stakes—to the charisms of divine grace? This charge could readily have been retorted against the Montanists themselves if they had set limits to those same gifts of grace in Maximilla: “But again,” he says, [Note: [Col. 0084D] Ch. 11.] “you fix boundary stakes for God, in discipline as in grace, and in solemn observances as in charisms, as though duties had ceased just as [Col. 0084B] his benefits had.”

While I was reflecting upon these matters, it occurred to me that perhaps the Montanists in Africa were not fully acquainted with all the oracles Maximilla had uttered in Asia. But I was recalled from this thought by Firmilian’s letter to Cyprian, [Note: [Col. 0084D] Epistle LXXV among those of Cyprian.] from which it is evident that even in Asia other prophetesses arose among the Montanists after Maximilla:

“About twenty-two years ago,” he says, “in the period after the emperor Alexander, many conflicts and afflictions occurred there, either commonly to all people or privately to Christians. . . . . . . . While the faithful were situated amid this disturbance . . . . . . a certain woman suddenly appeared there who, being placed in ecstasy, presented herself as a prophet and acted as though filled with the Holy Spirit. But she was borne along by such an impulse of the principal demons that for a long time she agitated and deceived the brotherhood, performing certain marvelous and portentous acts and promising that she would cause the earth to move. . . . . . . . . . . By these falsehoods and boasts she had subdued the mind of each person, so that they obeyed her and followed wherever she commanded and led. That spirit even caused the woman to walk barefoot through harsh snow in the depth of winter, without being distressed or harmed in any respect by that wandering; and she also said that she was hastening to Judaea and Jerusalem, pretending that she had come from there, etc.”

I have copied these words at such length so that everyone may understand what outstanding prophetesses the Montanists had even in Asia after Maximilla. Indeed, if the chronology agreed, one would believe this woman to have been Maximilla herself, so closely [Col. 0084D] does everything correspond to what Eusebius reports of Maximilla from Asterius Urbanus and other writers. For, in addition to what has already been said, the Catholics undertook to expose and refute the spirit of each woman; and just as Maximilla, in Tertullian’s words, [Note: [Col. 0084D] *On Fasting*, ch. 1.] “preached no other God nor,” in the judgment of the same Tertullian, “overthrew any rule of faith or hope,” so this woman, as Firmilian records, baptized “many, employing the customary and legitimate words of interrogation, so that she appeared to differ in no respect from the ecclesiastical rule.” A little earlier he had said of the same woman that “she also frequently dared to sanctify bread by an invocation that was not contemptible, to pretend to celebrate the Eucharist, and to offer sacrifice to the Lord, not without the mystery of the customary formula.” All these things are marks of the Montanist spirit, and ancient writers have recorded that Montanus permitted such things to his prophetesses.

It is therefore evident that no one should be erased from the catalogue of Montanists merely because he acknowledged another prophetess after Maximilla. For although this frenzied prophetess proclaimed concerning herself, Μετ᾽ ἐμὲ προφῆτις οὐκέτι ἔσται, “After me there will no longer be a prophetess,” etc., it could nevertheless happen—and this very frequently befalls sectarians—that they were unwilling to attend to such manifest falsehoods, or that they understood her to mean another kind of prophetess, one who would introduce a new and more perfect doctrine and discipline. In this sense the patriarchs of the Montanists themselves called themselves “new prophets,” because in their own judgment they had inaugurated after the Apostles a new prophecy. However that may be, it is now clear, I think, that Dodwell rests upon a far too feeble prop when he removes the compiler of the Acts from the catalogue of Montanists on the ground that the latter regarded Perpetua as a prophet after Maximilla, since Tertullian too, a professed Montanist, placed her in the same rank and category.

VI. From this point, however, as I said above, Basnage derives his first argument against the holy martyrs:

“From the compiler of the Acts there arises for us a highly probable conjecture that Perpetua and those martyrs who joined themselves to her as companions in death belonged to the flock [Col. 0085C] of the Montanists. Certainly no one of this household would have published the Acts of Catholic martyrs, whom they held in contempt as Psychics. It is also evident that Perpetua, Felicity, and Saturus professed the same faith as the author of the Acts, as is clear from his own words: ‘And so we too announce to you, brethren and little children, what we have heard and touched, so that those of you who were present may remember the glory of the Lord, and that those who now learn of it by hearing may have communion with the holy martyrs.’ But the compiler of the Acts reveals himself by his own testimony to be a Montanist.

“Furthermore, Tertullian, most bitterly hostile to the Catholics and a most ardent defender of Montanus, bestows great praises upon Perpetua and uses her visions to prove that only martyrs are received into heaven before the day of solemn judgment: [Col. 0085D] ‘How,’ he says, ‘did Perpetua, the most courageous martyr, etc.’ This argument is all the weightier because Tertullian, both a contemporary of Perpetua and a resident of Carthage, where Perpetua exchanged life for death, could not have been ignorant of the sect to which either that woman or the compiler of her Passion belonged. It is more certain than certainty itself that Tertullian would not have wished such numerous and great praises to be bestowed upon any of the Psychics, much less to have that person’s words and authority employed to prove the proposition he was maintaining.”

In order that this argument—which, particularly to me, might at first sight appear capable of creating some difficulty—may be thoroughly dispelled and overthrown, I must make certain observations about the Montanists. The entire plausibility of that argument derives from a failure to recognize them.

[Col. 0086A] Let the first observation be that Catholics were not regarded as heretics by those Montanists who had not altogether lost their reason, such as Tertullian. This is established first from his book *On Monogamy*, where at the very beginning he distinguishes the Psychics from heretics: “Heretics abolish marriage; Psychics multiply it.” If one asks how this could be, the explanation is readily available, and the same author adds it a little later: in his judgment, the whole difference between the Montanists and Catholics was a matter not of faith but of discipline. For by this reasoning he endeavors to free his Paraclete from the suspicion of heresy:

“For the adversarial spirit would reveal itself by the diversity of its preaching, first corrupting the rule of faith and then corrupting the order of discipline. For the corruption of that which occupies the prior rank comes first—that is, of faith, which precedes discipline. A person must first be a heretic concerning God and only then concerning an ordinance. But the Paraclete, having many things to teach which the Lord deferred to him, will, in accordance with the prior definition, first attest Christ himself as we believe him, together with the whole order of God the Creator; and he will glorify him and bring him to remembrance. Once recognized in this way by the principal rule, he will reveal those many things that belong to disciplines, the integrity of his preaching concerning them pledging the faith, although they are new because they are only now revealed.”

By the same reasoning, in the book *On Fasting*, [Note: [Col. 0086D] Ch. 11.] he also attempted to repel the charge of heresy raised by Catholics against Montanus and set forth the same rule for judging heresies. For when the Catholics objected against the discipline and institution introduced through Montanus that it was “either false prophecy, if a spiritual voice instituted those solemn observances, or heresy, if human presumption invented them,” he answered as follows: “Without doubt, both heresy and false prophecy will be judged among us by their difference concerning the deity—we who are ministers of the one God, the Creator, and of his Christ.”

He has similar statements near the beginning of his book *On the Veiling of Virgins*. There he explains more clearly the rule of faith mentioned in the preceding passages: one who corrupts that rule is a heretic; one who preserves it, although he corrupts or alters the order of discipline, should not, in Tertullian’s judgment, be regarded as a heretic. He maintains that the things Montanus added to the preaching and institutions of the Apostles were of this kind: they were ordained, while the same rule of faith remained intact, to perfect discipline. Therefore, one should not raise a prescription of antiquity against Montanus; nor should the Montanists be numbered among heretics because of these new [Col. 0086D] institutions; nor, as we shall see below, should they be regarded as excluded from the communion of the apostolic churches:

“The rule of faith is indeed absolutely one, alone immovable and irreformable: namely, belief in one almighty God, Creator of the world, and in his Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin Mary, crucified under Pontius Pilate, raised from the dead on the third day, received into heaven, now sitting at the right hand of the Father, and coming to judge the living and the dead, together with the resurrection of the flesh. While this law of faith remains, the other matters of discipline and conduct admit the novelty of correction. . . . [Col. 0087A] What, then, is the administration of the Paraclete, if not this: that discipline is set in order, etc.?”

There follows later a discussion of the communion which the Montanists boasted that they had with the apostolic churches because of their agreement in the same faith and the same sacraments of faith; this we shall examine below.

Meanwhile, I draw the same argument from what Tertullian teaches at the beginning of the book *Against Praxeas*. Although he openly admits that, because of his acknowledgment and defense of the Paraclete, he was separated from the Psychics, he nevertheless sets forth the same rule for judging heresies. From that rule it plainly follows that, in his judgment, this disagreement between Montanists and Psychics did not concern matters that injured the faith or from which heresies could spring:

“But we, both always and now still more, as persons better instructed through the Paraclete, who is indeed the guide into all truth, believe in one God, yet under this dispensation, which we call οἰκονομία (*economy*): that the one God also has a Son, his Word, who proceeded from him, through whom all things were made and without whom nothing was made. He was sent by the Father into the Virgin and was born of her, man and God, Son of Man and Son of God, and surnamed Jesus Christ. He suffered, died, and was buried according to the Scriptures; he was raised by the Father and taken back into heaven; he sits at the right hand of the Father and will come to judge the living and the dead. From there, according to his promise, he sent from the Father the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete, the sanctifier of the faith of those who believe in the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit.

“That this rule has run its course from the beginning of the Gospel, [Col. 0087C] even before all the earlier heretics, much more before yesterday’s Praxeas, will be proved as much by the lateness of all the heretics as by the very novelty of yesterday’s Praxeas. Thus from this point onward a prior judgment may be established equally against all heresies: whatever is first is true; whatever is later is corrupted.”

Tertullian certainly was not ignorant—indeed, he openly admitted—that the prophecy of Montanus was new, that his preaching was new, and that his institutions had by no means run their course from the beginning of the Gospel, but had, as the Montanists babbled, been deferred by Christ to these later times. Hence he could remove from his Montanus the blame and infamy of heresy or heretical corruption in no other way than by saying that the traditions introduced by him only yesterday did not corrupt the rule of faith but perfected discipline. Accordingly, the controversies raised between Montanists and Catholics concerned discipline, not faith; and neither the one group nor the other was to be entered upon the register of heretics merely because, while preserving the rule of faith, they disagreed with one another in matters of conduct and discipline.

The same point is also evident—to omit very many other examples—from the entire book *On Prescriptions against Heretics*. Under that title he certainly does not include the Psychics or Catholics, since nothing does he press more strongly against heretics than ancient and apostolic traditions and the authority of the apostolic churches; nothing does he reproach in heretics more strongly than novelty. Indeed, his declared purpose, as we learn from him, was to refute all the sects of heretics on this ground alone: [Col. 0088A] “But another little book,” he says, [Note: [Col. 0088D] Book I, *Against Marcion*, ch. 1.] “will maintain this position against the heretics, who may be refuted even without an examination of their doctrines, because by the prescription of novelty they are shown to be what they are.”

Yet the book just cited, as Basnage himself also teaches, [Note: [Col. 0088D] Under A.D. 200, no. IX. See also Pagi, under A.D. 195, no. IV.] was composed by Tertullian after he had defected to the Montanists. It was written against all heresies, as Tertullian himself expressly testifies elsewhere: [Note: [Col. 0088D] *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 2.] “But elsewhere we have already employed such prescriptions more fully against all heresies.” Since, therefore, nothing occurs in that book that could offend Catholics—indeed, nothing that was not stated in accordance with their position—so much so that many have held the opinion [Note: [Col. 0088D] See Pamelius, *Life of Tertullian*, under A.D. 203, vol. I, col. 65; compare Aug. Noesselt, *Dissertations*, vol. I, cols. 171–177.] that Tertullian composed it before embracing the doctrines of Montanus, it follows that the Psychics or Catholics were not [Col. 0088B] numbered by Tertullian among the heretics.
