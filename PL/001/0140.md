## CHAPTER IX

## CHAPTER XI

What cause have you for going forth in more elaborate array, since you are removed from those occasions that require such things? For you do not make the rounds of temples, demand spectacles, or recognize the pagans’ feast days. It is for such gatherings, and for mutual seeing and being seen, that every display is brought out in public, so that either lust may ply its trade or glory grow insolent.

But you have no reason for going out that is not grave [Note: [Col. 1329D] “No reason for going out that is not grave.” [Col. 1330D] That is: no reason for going out that is not weighty, serious, and holy. RIG.]: either some sick brother is to be visited, or the sacrifice is offered [Note: [Col. 1330D] “The sacrifice is offered.” Thus he spoke of “the prayers of the sacrifices,” in which, namely, the eucharistic mysteries are offered to be shared by the faithful. RIG.—“Or the sacrifice is offered.” That this means the sacred rite of the Mass is clearer than to require demonstration, despite the impudence of Junius, who understands it generically as some prayers. See our Gretser in his notes against Junius, chapter 3. LAC.], or the word of God is administered. Any of these is a matter of seriousness [Col. 1329C] and holiness, one that has no need of extraordinary, carefully arranged, and flowing attire. [Note: [Col. 1329C] A. Neander emends: “extraordinary and flowing attire, but carefully arranged.”]

And if the necessity of friendships and obligations toward pagans calls you, why do you not go out clothed in your own armor—all the more when going among those alien to the faith? Let there be a distinction between the handmaids of the devil and those of God; let you be an example to them; let them be built up through you; let, as the Apostle says, God be magnified in your body (Phil. 1:20; 1 Cor. 6:20). God is magnified in the body through chastity, and certainly through attire appropriate to chastity.

But some say that his name may be blasphemed in us (Rom. 2:24) if we take anything away from our former clothing and finery. Then let us not take away our former vices either [Note: [Col. 1330C] Rigaltius deleted “from us also.”]; let us remain the same in morals if we remain the same in outward appearance, and then the nations truly will not blaspheme! What a dreadful blasphemy it is when someone says [Note: [Col. 1330C] Gelenius and others: “It is a blasphemy if someone says... she walks.” Rigaltius: “It is a blasphemy from which someone says... she walks.”]: “Since she became a Christian, she goes about more poorly dressed.” Will you fear appearing poorer now that you have become richer, and more squalid now that you have become purer? Ought Christians to walk according to the approval of the pagans, or according to God’s?

## CHAPTER XII

Because that Tamar (Gen. 38:14ff.) had painted and adorned herself, Judah therefore saw her and suspected that she was sitting there for hire. ## CHAPTER XIII

Someone may perhaps say: “I have no need to be approved by human beings, for I do not seek human testimony. God is the inspector of the heart” (1 Sam. 16:7). We all know this. Nevertheless [Note: [Col. 1331B] Franciscus Junius conjectures: “We all know this; nevertheless, let us remember what [Col. 1331C] the same one,” etc.], let us remember what the same God said through the Apostle: “Let your probity [Note: [Col. 1331D] “Your probity before,” etc. He rendered literally the Greek expression used by the Apostle—a title by which he everywhere means Saint Paul the Apostle—at Philippians 4: τὸ ἐπιεικὲς ὑμῶν, “your forbearance,” for which our Vulgate translator paraphrastically has *modestia vestra*, “your modesty.” PAM.] appear before human beings” (Phil. 4:5). For what purpose, except that wickedness may have no access to you at all, and that you may be both an example and a testimony to the wicked?

Or what does “Let your works shine” mean? Or why did the Lord call us “the light of the earth” [Note: [Col. 1331D] “Or why did the Lord call us...” Since Matthew’s Greek text reads τὸ φῶς τοῦ κόσμου, “the light of the world,” while κόσμος is nowhere used for “earth,” the author appears here too to have suffered a lapse of memory because “the salt of the earth” had shortly preceded it; otherwise he ought to have said “the light of the world,” just as one says “lights of the world.” PAM.]? Why did he compare us to a city established upon a mountain (Matt. 5:14), if we do not shine among those in darkness [Note: [Col. 1331C] Rigaltius: “shine in the darkness”; others: “shine among the darkness.”] and stand out among those submerged?

If you hide your lamp beneath a bushel, you must necessarily, after being abandoned in darkness, be run into by many. [Note: [Col. 1332C] “After being abandoned in darkness, you must necessarily be run into by many.” The common reading is “you will be assailed by many.” The Agobardine codex has “you will have enclosed.” One should write “you will be run into,” in this sense: it cannot happen otherwise than that many will stumble against you when you are left in darkness. The word *incursari* is ambiguous here, for a woman is also called *incursa* when she is overwhelmed by the blind assault of lustful persons. Thus Tertullian himself, *To the Nations* I: “How often had he committed incest in the darkness?” And: “Take careful notice, so that when darkness falls you do not go astray and assault a stranger.” And in the book *On Modesty*: “For it makes no difference whether someone assaults another man’s wife or a widow.” PAM.]

These are the things that make us lights of the world—our good deeds, of course. But the good, provided that it is true and complete, [Col. 1332A] does not love darkness; it rejoices to be seen and exults in being pointed out for what it is. [Note: [Col. 1332D] “At the very indications,” etc. Fabius, *Declamation* 19: “condemned by everyone’s denunciation.” PAM.] [Note: [Col. 1331C] Rigaltius: “exults at the very indications.”]

For Christian chastity, it is not enough to exist; it must also be visible. Its fullness ought to be so great that it flows out from the soul into the clothing and erupts from the conscience onto the outward appearance, so that it also examines externally, as it were, its own equipment—the equipment fitting for the perpetual maintenance of faith. Luxuries must be cast off, for their softness and fluidity can render the strength of faith effeminate.

Otherwise, I do not know [Note: [Col. 1332D] “Whether a hand accustomed to being surrounded by a *spathalium*.” A *spathalium* is a woman’s ornament, from σπαθᾶν, which signifies “to live luxuriously.” RHEN.] whether a hand accustomed to being encircled by a *spathalium* [bracelet] can endure becoming numb in the hardness of a chain. I do not know whether a leg that has delighted in a *periscelium* [anklet] [Note: [Col. 1332B] Rigaltius: “a leg delighted by an anklet,” etc.] will allow itself to be tightly confined in the stocks. [Note: [Col. 1332D] “I do not know whether a leg with an anklet.” Legs or shins were adorned with the *periscelis* or *periscelium*; the Greeks call the leg σκέλος. RHEN.] I fear that a neck occupied by necklaces of pearls and emeralds will not leave room for the sword.

Therefore, blessed women, let us rehearse harder things, and we shall not feel them; [Col. 1332B] let us relinquish more pleasant things, and we shall not desire them. Let us stand unencumbered against every violence, possessing nothing that we fear to leave behind. These things are the bonds that hold back our hope. Let us cast away earthly ornaments if we desire heavenly ones.

Do not love gold, in which the first [Note: [Col. 1332B] Rigaltius: “all.”] sins of the people of Israel are marked out (cf. Exod. 32:2ff.). You ought to hate that which destroyed the Jews [Note: [Col. 1332B] Rigaltius: “the fathers.”], that which they worshiped when they abandoned God. Even then gold was food for fire. [Note: [Col. 1332D] “Even then gold was fire.” In the preceding book he said of gold: “It left behind the name of earth in the fire.” Accordingly, gold already derives its being gold from fire. By mentioning fire, moreover, he continually alludes to the fire prepared for the punishment of the profane. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 1332B] Rigaltius: “that which was worshiped by those abandoning God. Even [Col. 1332C] (others: already) then gold was fire. But,” etc.]

In any case, the times of Christians are always—and now especially—passed not in gold but in iron. The robes of martyrdom are being prepared; supported by angels as bearers [Note: [Col. 1332C] Rigaltius: “the angels as bearers support them.”] [Note: [Col. 1332D] “Supported by angels as bearers.” That is, angels are expected to lift them into heaven to meet Christ. Thus Tertullian himself in the preceding book: “I shall see whether you rise with white lead, purple pigment, saffron, and that arrangement of the head, [Col. 1334A] or whether angels lift you, painted in such a fashion, into the clouds to meet Christ in the air.” RIG.].

Go forth now built up [Col. 1333A] with the cosmetics and ornaments of the prophets [Note: [Col. 1333A] Semler omits “of the prophets.”] and apostles: drawing whiteness from simplicity and blush from chastity; painting your eyes with modesty and your mouth with silence [Note: [Col. 1333A] Others: “and your spirit with silence.”]; inserting the word of God into your ears; fastening the yoke of Christ around your necks. Subject your head [Col. 1334A] to your husbands, and you will be sufficiently adorned. Occupy your hands with wool [Note: [Col. 1334A] “Occupied,” the Paris edition.]; plant your feet at home, and they will please more than if they were in gold. [Note: [Col. 1334A] Others: “you will please.”] Clothe yourselves in the silk of probity, the fine linen of holiness, and the purple of chastity. Painted in this fashion, you will have God as your lover.
