[non-Latin text omitted], etc.; that is: “Why is one who has stolen from the baths, or from the wrestling school, or from the marketplace, or from some such place, punished with death, while one who steals from a private house pays twice the value of the theft?” And he answers: [non-Latin text omitted], etc.; that is: “Is it because in houses we can guard property more effectively? For there is both a strong wall and a key, and all the members of the household take care that domestic possessions are kept safe. Thus an opportunity for theft is afforded even if he turns his gaze aside for a moment. ## CHAPTER XLV

The innocence of Christians proceeds not from fear of man [Col. 0498A] but from reverence for God. By his instruction they have received the most perfect laws and learned to forestall every evil at its very origin, reserving themselves holy and inviolate for God as judge.

45. We, therefore, alone are innocent. What wonder, if we must be? Indeed, we must be. Having been taught innocence by God, we both know it perfectly, as revealed by a perfect teacher, and guard it faithfully, as [Note: [Col. 0497A] Thus the Agobard manuscript, Pamelius, Junius, Rigaltius, and Havercamp. But the Fulda manuscript reads: “enjoined by God, a teacher who cannot be scorned.” Barraeus: “entrusted by [Col. 0498A] a steward who cannot be adorned.” Aldus: “as something entrusted by an inconceivable steward.” Rhenanus and Heraldus: “entrusted by a steward who cannot be scorned.”] entrusted by an inspector who cannot be scorned. But human judgment [Note: [Col. 0499B] The Fulda manuscript reads “human teaching”; in the following passage Rhenanus and Aldus read “human condemnation.”] has handed innocence down to you, and human dominion has likewise commanded it; consequently, your system of discipline is neither complete nor sufficiently formidable to attain the truth of innocence. How great is human wisdom for demonstrating what is truly good [Note: [Col. 0499B] “Such is the wisdom of man for demonstrating what is truly good.” Fran., Paris.]? How great is human authority for exacting it? The former is as liable to error as the latter is to contempt. And so, which command is more comprehensive: “You shall not kill,” or “You shall not even be angry”? Which is more perfect: to forbid adultery, or to restrain even the solitary lust of the eyes? Which is more refined [Note: [Col. 0499C] “Which is more refined?” A person is not sufficiently trained in virtue if only wrongdoing is forbidden him, unless evil speaking is forbidden as well. More clearly: one who merely abstains from wrongdoing will still be untrained in virtue, for he must also abstain from evil speaking. Understand the following statements similarly: for a person to be instructed in virtue, he must abstain not only from injury, but even from the shadow of injury and from everything that entails a return of injury. LAC.]: to forbid wrongdoing, or evil speaking as well? Which is more fully instructive: not to permit injury, or not even to allow injury to be repaid in kind [Note: [Col. 0499C] “Or not even injury in return.” That is, not to inflict an injury, but also not to repay one. Thus in book II *Against Marcion*: “God prohibits retaliation”; and in book IV: “Christ restrains even retaliation for an injury.” RIG.—“To allow injury in return.” He uses this expression most elegantly for “to repay an injury.” Virgil, *Aeneid* II, verse 432:

> I call you to witness that, when you fell, I did not shun the weapons  
> Or any reprisals of the Greeks.

[Col. 0499D] Here Servius talks nonsense. For the “reprisals” that he swears he did not avoid are not alternations in the fighting, as Servius explains, but wounds and slaughter: just as he himself had inflicted these on the Greeks, so, if it had been his fate to fall, he had not refused to suffer them himself in battle. HAV.]? Provided, however, that you know that your own laws, which appear to advance toward innocence, borrowed their pattern from the divine law as the more ancient [Col. 0499B] law [Note: [Col. 0499B] “As almost the more ancient.” Aldus reads “as the more ancient pattern”; Rhenanus, “from the pattern of the more ancient”; Fran., “as more ancient patterns”; the Leiden manuscript, “as more ancient forms.”]. We have already spoken of the age of Moses. But how great is the authority of human laws, when it may befall a man both [Note: [Col. 0500B] Fran. reads “even if he may escape them”; Junius, “even escape them.”] to escape them and, in most offenses, to remain concealed [Note: [Col. 0500B] Fran. reads “he might remain concealed”; Semler, “for one remaining concealed.”], and sometimes to despise them because of the intention [Note: [Col. 0500B] “Through involuntariness.” Semler.] or necessity of offending [Note: [Col. 0499C] “To despise them because of the involuntary nature or necessity of the offense.” I follow Rigaltius and explain that human laws, in comparison with divine laws, possess no authority. This is shown in three ways: first, because it is often possible to escape punishment; second, because the law frequently does not see the offender, and therefore his crime remains hidden; third, because their force is sometimes despised when an involuntary sin is alleged as a pretext for an act to which people were driven by force and necessity. With divine laws the matter is far otherwise. To these three points he will now add a fourth: the brevity of punishment and thus the fact that death need not be so greatly feared, whereas under divine laws punishment extends through immeasurable eternity. Add Rigaltius, who illustrates this with examples. HAV.—“For one offending through involuntariness or necessity.” [Col. 0500B] “Involuntariness,” that is, accident. Marcianus provides an example of such an offense in law XI, *On Punishments*: when a weapon cast at a wild beast during a hunt killed a man; “necessity,” when one could not otherwise protect one’s own life. Let us suppose, then, that Sempronius cast a weapon at a wild beast while hunting, and with that weapon killed not the beast but an enemy who happened to be passing by. Sempronius will rejoice at his fortunate error, because by that accident he took his enemy’s life with impunity. Yet it was with impunity before Caesar, not before Christ. For by his approval or ratification he made voluntary the blow that was involuntary in its origin and purpose. The same must be said if Titius killed a madman who had named him heir in his will before going mad and who attacked him when Titius could not otherwise protect his own life. He will rejoice inwardly that he has deprived the testator of the power to change his will during lucid intervals; and by that joy, before Christ, he willingly killed the man whom, before Caesar, he appears to have killed unwillingly and under compulsion of necessity, since Christ’s discernment is far more powerful and perfect than Caesar’s. RIG.], when account is also taken [Note: [Col. 0500B] Semler adds “of.”] of the brevity of any punishment whatsoever, which will not continue beyond death? Thus [Col. 0500A] Epicurus too [Note: [Col. 0500D] “Thus Epicurus too.” Lucretius, describing his doctrine in most brilliant verses, enlarged and multiplied his error by the greatness of his own genius, especially in book III, which must be read cautiously and with the mind forearmed. > We may know that there is nothing for us to fear in death,  
> That one who does not exist cannot become wretched, and that it makes not the slightest  
> Difference whether he had never at any time been born,  
> Once immortal death has taken away his mortal life.

HAV.] depreciates every torment [Note: [Col. 0500D] “Depreciates torment and pain.” This word does not savor of Golden Latin; it appears to have been adopted from Tertullian’s usage and manner. He uses it again in book IV *Against Marcion*. Sidonius Apollinaris also used it in *Bargus*: “Their pigments, depreciated, disfigure the figures”; likewise in letter 10 of book II, and Symmachus, letter 41 of book VIII. But we, who are examined under God, the observer of all things, and who foresee eternal punishment from him, rightly take precautions for innocence—we alone—both because of the fullness of his knowledge, the difficulty of finding concealment, and the magnitude of the torment, which is not of long duration but everlasting. We fear him whom even the man who judges those who fear will himself have to fear: we fear God, not the proconsul.

## CHAPTER XLVI

He next meets those who suppose that Christians do not derive their wisdom from God but regard their faith and hope as a kind of philosophy, weighing in each the same classes of virtues. He shows them how far the name “philosopher” sinks below “Christian,” since philosophers are found to be ignorant of the truth and guilty of every crime, whereas Christians understand God and, to the amazement even of their enemies, are as far removed as possible from every kind of wickedness.

46. We have taken our stand [Note: [Col. 0500D] “We have taken our stand, I think.” The expression is drawn from military practice, where men, standing firm, defended the position they had occupied. [Col. 0501B] Thus above, at chapter IV, we noted that our author frequently uses formulas derived from that source, and there we explained the use of this verb. Barraeus and the Leiden manuscript read “we have established.” I do not approve; rather, with the Fulda manuscript and the rest, I write “we have taken our stand.” Akin to this is the statement in *On the Shows*, chapter XIII: “We have sufficiently completed, I think, our orderly account of which people, and in what ways,” etc. HAV.], I think, against the allegation [Note: [Col. 0500B] Semler reads “threatened allegation.”] of every crime, an allegation that demands the blood of Christians. We have demonstrated our whole position and [Col. 0501A] the ways in which we can prove that it is as we have shown: namely, from the trustworthiness and antiquity of the divine writings, and likewise from the confession of spiritual [Note: [Col. 0501A] Rigaltius reads “spiritual.”] powers. Who will dare to refute us [Note: [Col. 0501A] The Fulda manuscript and Havercamp read “Let someone come forward who will dare to refute us.”], not through verbal artifice, but by the same method by which we have established our proof concerning the truth?
