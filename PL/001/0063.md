Henry Noris indicates that he supported the same opinion (*Dissertation on the Decennial Vows of the Emperors*, p. 93, Padua, 1686), but he does not disclose his reasons. I see that he cites Spartian, who, so far as I know, said nothing of the kind. I therefore turn at once to Antoine Pagi, whose express purpose was to establish this date (*Critique of Baronius*, at the year 198, no. V, p. 89). For Tillemont is mistaken when he says that Pagi agrees with him [Note: [Col. 0554D] *History of the Emperors*, vol. III, p. 408: “Father Pagi, who, like us, places Albinus’ defeat in 198.”]. The sum of his arguments—which I collect into one place from their remarkably scattered presentation—is this: Severus’ Quinquennalia were celebrated at Rome, without any dispute, in the year 198; but Herodian relates that immediately after the battle at Lyons he hastened to Rome with immense speed [Col. 0554B] and, as soon as he arrived, presented those Quinquennalia. Therefore, if the emperor entered Rome shortly after Albinus was killed—whose defeat no one denies occurred on the nineteenth day of February—and if no delay, or only a slight one, intervened between this return of his to the city and the Quinquennalia, then it is beyond doubt that the killing of Albinus and the Quinquennalia must belong to the same year.

If I did not know how easy it was for him to slip in such matters, I should certainly be surprised that such feeble little arguments could have proceeded from so learned a man. What displeases me about them generally is that he thus assigns three years to the Albinian disturbances, contrary to all credibility. He admits that arms were taken up in 196. Elsewhere he censures Petavius, [Col. 0554C] who, because of the opinion that he shared with Pagi, had preferred the following year (*Dissertatio Hypatica*, Part II, ch. XII, p. 221). Yet he does not perceive that by this means he falls into snares far more difficult than Petavius’, since, despite every opposing consideration, he prolongs that war into a third year.

There is also another difficulty that weighs upon this opinion. Severus’ journey to the East for the Parthian expedition—which the learned man concedes took place in the same year, 198—is joined with those Quinquennalia and his return to the city. But this conflicts with the testimony of every author, who presents these events in such a way that Severus’ return to Rome from Gaul must without any doubt be assigned to one year, and his departure to subdue the Parthians to another.

What am I to say of his particular arguments, [Col. 0554D] not one of which can support itself even slightly? I shall examine each one distinctly. It is entirely uncertain that Severus presented the Quinquennalia in 198. The celebrated Pagi himself repeatedly emphasized this rule: Quinquennalia, Decennalia, and festivals of that kind were celebrated indiscriminately, sometimes as the fifth year began, sometimes as it ended (*Dissertatio Hypatica*, Part II, ch. II, § V, p. 87). I am not unaware that he placed it upon an unstable foundation—namely, the opinion, of which he was the principal author, that emperors always assumed consular rank at solemnities of this kind. Yet otherwise the rule is not so devoid of its own strength as some [Col. 0555A] persuade themselves. Examples survive both of those who presented these games at the beginning of the fifth or tenth year and of those who did so at its end. Among the former is Constantine the Great, whose first day of his thirtieth year, 25 July, is known to have been distinguished by the ceremonies of the Tricennalia.

Let us apply this to Severus’ years, and we shall see that he could have presented those spectacles to the people either in 197 or in 198. He was placed at the head of the empire perhaps around the middle of 193. It matters little to know the day and month, about which others dispute (see Tillemont, *History of the Emperors*, vol. III, Note VII on Severus, p. 393). Meanwhile, I shall follow Pagi himself, who thinks that this occurred on the Ides of May (*Dissertatio Hypatica*, Part II, ch. XII, p. 220). Thus the fifth year of his reign will have begun in May 197. What argument, then, can be advanced that demonstrates invincibly that he gave the Quinquennalia not in 197, when [Col. 0555B] Severus’ fifth year began, but in 198, when it ended? For it is certain that either was possible.

The condition of the remaining arguments is worse still. It is false that Herodian says that the emperor presented to the people immediately after his arrival in the city the gifts that Pagi calls the Quinquennalia. Let us hear the man’s own words (*Histories*, Book III, ch. VIII, p. 145): Ὁ δ` οὖν Σεβῆρὸς εἰς τὸ τοῦ Διὸς τέμενος ἀνελθὼν, καὶ τὰς λοιπὰς τελέσας ἱερουργίας, ἐπανῆλθεν εἰς τὰ βασίλεια, καὶ τῷ δήμῳ προὒθηκεν ἐπὶ ταῖς νίκαις μεγίστας νομὰς, τοῖς δὲ στρατιώταίς ἐπέδωκε χρήματα πλεῖστα, ἄλλα τε πολλὰ συνεχώρησεν ἃ μὴ πρότερον εἶχον (“Severus, then, went up to the precinct of Jupiter and, after performing the remaining sacred rites, returned to the palace; in celebration of his victories he presented the people with very great distributions, gave the soldiers a vast sum of money, and granted many other privileges that they had not possessed before”). Politian translates it thus:

> But after he entered the temple of Jupiter and performed all the customary sacrifices, he returned to the palace and bestowed a great largess upon the Roman people in honor of the victory, distributed money to the soldiers, and at the same time granted them many other things that they had never received before.

I look here for a mention of the Quinquennalia, but in vain. I do indeed hear Pagi say: “These novel favors were tokens of Severus’ Quinquennalia.” But what an unfortunate conjecture! As though the people could not have been presented with things of that kind at times other than the Quinquennalia. And what prevented you, most learned Pagi, from seeing those words of Herodian, ἐπὶ ταῖς νίμαις (“in celebration of the victories”; νίμαις is apparently corrupt, probably for νίκαις)? Severus exercised this generosity because of the victory won over Albinus, not on account of the fifth anniversary of his reign—games of which Herodian afterward speaks separately.

He is no less mistaken in saying that it is apparent from Herodian [Col. 0555D] that the emperor hastened to Rome immediately after Albinus’ defeat. Herodian does indeed say that, when he was making for Rome, πολλῷ τάχει (“with great speed”) he traveled. But he does not record that this journey immediately followed the slaughter of Albinus (*loc. cit.*, p. 145). Rather, he relates that Severus did not return to Rome before he had put Britain’s affairs in order, settled Gaul, and exacted punishment from the great host of Albinus’ friends. These were matters of such importance that much time was undoubtedly consumed in accomplishing them.

We therefore now affirm that nothing is more certain than that the Albinian disturbances were quelled in 197 [Note: [Col. 0555D] Yet I do not agree here with Baronius, who, for reasons unknown to me, although he concedes that Albinus was defeated in 197, nevertheless denies that Severus returned to Rome before 198. *Annals*, vol. II, at the year 200, no. I, p. 296. Everything conflicts with this opinion and confirms that the emperor returned to the city perhaps after the middle of 197. I pass over the fact that, in Adolf Occo’s *On Coins*, p. 849, certain indications occur on coins that this emperor’s arrival took place in the fourth year of his tribunician power.], once their instigator had been killed.

[Col. 0556A] § XIV.—And thus I proceed to the remaining matters without stumbling, and the year of our *Apologeticum* can more easily be established. I therefore declare without hesitation that in 198 Tertullian wished to come to the aid of his brethren in their distress. The passage that supports this opinion is as follows:

> But those who are now daily being exposed as accomplices or applauders of criminal factions—the gleaning left after the harvest of parricides—with what very fresh and luxuriant laurels did they cover their doorposts? With what very lofty and brilliant lamps did they clear the gloom from their vestibules? With what very elegant and sumptuous couches did they divide up the forum for themselves? They did so not to celebrate public rejoicings, but to proclaim their own wishes at another’s solemnity and to inaugurate the exemplar and image of their hope, changing the name of the prince in their hearts. Those who consult astrologers, haruspices, augurs, and [Col. 0556B] magicians concerning the life of the Caesars likewise neglect the same duties.

(*Apologeticum*, ch. XXXV, p. 458.)

This illustrious passage did not escape Baronius, nor did it pass Tillemont by. But neither man—if I may say so without diminishing such great scholars—was able to use it correctly, because they erred in other matters. Baronius saw something of the truth and correctly advised that it should be interpreted of the slaughter of Albinus’ surviving adherents (*Annals*, at the year 200, no. III, p. 198). Yet he could not correctly determine the book’s date, because he held that Severus returned to Rome in 198 and that war was not finally carried against the Parthians until 199.

Tillemont, however, whose astonishing industry I otherwise acknowledge, incautiously understood the passage of Niger’s followers, whom Severus persecuted at Plautianus’ prompting when he was already threatening the Parthians, as Spartian [Col. 0556C] reports (*Life of Severus*, ch. XV, p. 181). He therefore held that the *Apologeticum* was composed in 200 or 201 [Note: [Col. 0556D] *History of the Emperors*, vol. III, p. 94, at the year 200: “One of Severus’ occupations in the East was to make new searches for Niger’s supporters and, as Tertullian says concerning this, to glean after the harvest.”]. And yet I do not even understand why this learned man thinks that the slaughter of Niger’s adherents—which Spartian places at the beginnings of the Parthian war—occurred after the exceedingly difficult siege of the city of Hatra itself.

If we carefully examine Tertullian’s passage, we shall easily resolve the matter. It is plain who the “parricides” are in it: namely, those who had aided Albinus, when he was aspiring to the imperial power, either by deed or counsel. Cicero had long before expressed the opinion (*Philippic* II, ch. XIII, p. 446, vol. II of the works, Gruter’s edition) that men of this kind were even worse than parricides, since, as he adds, it is more atrocious to kill the father of one’s country than one’s own father. But by far the greater part of these men, whether truly guilty [Col. 0556D] of that crime or falsely regarded as such, had subsequently been executed both in Gaul and at Rome, apart from those who had openly taken up arms. Herodian and Spartian may be consulted here. And this is what Tertullian calls the “harvest.”

A certain gleaning, as the emperor at least believed, still remained. [Col. 0557A] The suspicious prince, who was at the same time greedy for money, although everything had already been restored to tranquility, nevertheless dragged certain men, as it were, from their hiding places, saying that they had plotted against him, and therefore ordered them to be hurried away to death. Tertullian was writing while that “gleaning,” as he calls it, was under way. To know when Severus searched for the remnants of the rebels is therefore to know the very year of the *Apologeticum*.

Tertullian himself indicates that this occurred after certain solemnities held at Rome, during which these enemies of the emperor had made a great but insincere show of rejoicing. He does not add how much time intervened. It is nevertheless apparent that it should not be understood as very long. There is also a passage in another of his books that, for the present point, explains this one, as it were (*To Scapula*, Book I, ch. I, p. 552 of the works):

> Christians could never be found among the Albiniani, the Nigriani, or the Cassiani; rather, they were those very men who until the day before had sworn by their guardian spirits and who had both vowed and offered sacrifices for their welfare.

[Col. 0557B] This ingenious man is speaking of the same matter as in the passages we are expounding. What, then, prevents the word “the day before,” which denotes a brief interval, from being applied to both passages?

But what were those public rejoicings to which he refers? Unless I am mistaken, he is indicating the Quinquennalia. It is true that when Severus entered Rome after Albinus had been killed, everything resounded with rejoicings and applause (see Herodian, *Histories*, Book III, ch. VIII, p. 145). Nor would it damage our argument if anyone said that this public rejoicing was indicated here. Yet I consider it much more probable that the reference is to the Quinquennalia, both for other reasons and because Tertullian implies that vows for the emperor’s safety were then made: “But to proclaim their own wishes at another’s solemnity,” and the rest.

What, indeed, is better known than that this custom was observed especially at the Quinquennial games? What is more celebrated than these inscriptions on coins: **VOTA DECENN. SUSCEP.**? Herodian undoubtedly touches upon these ceremonies when, besides the gift that the emperor had presented to the people upon his return to Rome, he commemorates another generosity of this kind shortly before the Parthian expedition: νομάς τε μεγαλοφρόνως ἐπεδίδου, ἐπετέλεσε δὲ καὶ ἐπινικίον ἀγῶνα, τοὺς παντάχοθεν μούσης τε ὑποκρίτας, καὶ ἀνδρείας μαθήτας μεταπεμψάμενος (“He bestowed distributions with great munificence and also celebrated a victory contest, summoning from every quarter performers in the arts of the Muses and practitioners of athletic prowess”) (Book III, ch. VIII, p. 147).

> He also gave most lavish distributions and instituted a contest, summoning performers and athletes from every quarter.

Spartian reports the same thing (*Life of Severus*, ch. XIV, p. 150), in these words: [Col. 0557D]

> He then set out for the Parthian war, after presenting a gladiatorial show and giving a largess to the people.

See how amicably these two agree, and how aptly each reports that a solemnity of great munificence preceded the Parthian expedition. Had the most celebrated Tillemont attended to these facts, he would undoubtedly have arranged the matter differently. He thinks the passage refers to the followers of Pescennius Niger whom Severus hunted down in Syria. But Tertullian introduces men who had been present at Roman spectacles. Such were not Niger’s followers who lay concealed in Syria.

I care little whether the emperor proclaimed these festive days in 197 or 198. My only task is to show that the blood of those believed to be Albinus’ followers was shed anew when he was on the point of invading the Parthians. Even if no witness could be produced, my computation would nevertheless remain certain, since I could commend Tertullian as equivalent to them all. Spartian, however, is conveniently at hand and reports that the auspices of the new war were polluted by human slaughter (*Life of Severus*, ch. XIV, p. 180):

> Amid these events—while he was preparing himself for a new war and presenting the people with games to watch, concerning which and other matters the author had spoken above—he killed many on either true or fabricated charges. Most were condemned because they had made jokes; others, because they had remained silent; others, because they had said many things figuratively—so that the emperor might truly live up to his name, being truly both Pertinax and Severus.

I also refer to this point the words that follow a little later, which, in my opinion, do not pertain solely to the persecution of Niger’s adherents in the East [Col. 0558B] and should be compared with Tertullian’s final words:

> He also killed many on the pretext that they had consulted Chaldeans or prophets concerning his safety, especially anyone whom he suspected of being fit for imperial rule.

The matter is settled. By universal agreement, Severus set out against the Parthians in 198. Then, after the Quinquennalia had been presented, when he was breathing nothing but battles and the slaughter of Parthians, there occurred that gleaning of rebels of which Tertullian speaks. It can therefore no longer be doubted that the *Apologeticum* was composed in the same year.

We may now also identify the first year of the calamities inflicted upon Christians under the reign of Severus. They had already endured the fury of their enemies for some time when our author spoke on their behalf. Certainly, he nowhere speaks of wounds inflicted only recently, but of evils that had already become inveterate. [Col. 0558C] This shows that, if their miseries are not to be traced back still earlier, they must at the very least be reckoned from the preceding year, 197.

§ XV.—You now have what I resolved to communicate to you concerning the date of Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*. For my part, I have striven according to my abilities to give these arguments both strength and clarity; whether I have attained either must be left to the judgment of others. If some obscurity still adheres to them, you will consider that this is due less to my fault than to that of the subject itself. Investigations of this kind have the peculiarity that they always carry some of their native grime along with them. Nor are they written for those serving their first campaigns, but for those who have already attained some standing among learned citizens and are not ignorant of either kind of history or of critical studies. What appears exceedingly obscure to the former usually presents [Col. 0558D] no difficulty to the latter.

If fate permits, and if my labor is not entirely without supporters of its own, I intend to proceed along this path. I shall discuss the rest of Tertullian’s works with not merely the same diligence, but shall also treat his life—considerably more carefully than I see has been done thus far—in a separate book, to which I shall append certain dissertations on his works.

For a long time, the more learned have desired a new edition of them. And no one would more willingly than I have taken this burden upon himself, had such a lot in life fallen to me that I could believe myself capable of duly discharging it. Since this has been denied me, I have nevertheless resolved, by the method I have described, to render service not so much to this particular field as to sacred and civil history itself.

Henry Dodwell, a man of immortal memory, undertook [Col. 0559A] a work of this kind (on his life, written by Francis Brokesby, see La Roche, *Bibliothèque Anglaise*, vol. I, part I, pp. 86–87). Death prevented him from bringing it to completion. For my part, I greatly doubt whether his learning would have been matched by equal candor and maturity of judgment.

We possess a life of Tertullian by Pierre Allix; but if I say that it is less than perfect and carefully wrought, I shall not depart from the truth. Let the points that we have considered from it in this dissertation serve as a sample. Neither I, nor perhaps all who live outside France, have seen the life of Tertullian by Pierre-Thomas du Fossé, which Dupin testifies was published without the author’s name (*Bibliothèque des auteurs ecclésiastiques*, vol. XVIII, p. 258). The palm therefore still lies in the midst, and, if I live, I shall strive to claim it for myself. I am not a man unaware that I am far inferior in learning to those whom I have named. Yet I promise—perhaps too boldly—that I shall surpass Dodwell in love of truth, and the others in assiduity and diligence.

## Two Books *To the Nations*

[Col. 0559]

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, *TO THE NATIONS* (C)

## BOOK I

[Col. 0559B] **1.** The evidence of your ignorance, which refutes injustice even while defending it, is readily at hand: all who previously shared your ignorance and shared your hatred cease to hate as soon as it happens that they come to know; when they cease to be ignorant, they even become themselves what they had hated, and begin to hate what they had been. Thus you daily groan over the growing number of Christians. You cry out that the city is besieged; that there are Christians in the countryside, in the fortresses, and on the islands; and you lament as though it were a loss that every sex, every age, and, finally, every rank is passing over from you.

Yet even this does not move your minds to consider whether some hidden good may be present. You are not permitted to suspect more correctly; you have no desire to investigate more closely. At this point alone human curiosity [Col. 0559C] grows torpid. You love to remain ignorant of what others rejoice to have discovered. You prefer not to know because you already hate, as though you knew for certain that, if you came to know, you would no longer hate. And yet, if no cause for hatred is found, it will certainly be best to abandon your former injustice; but if the cause is established, nothing will be taken away from your hatred, which will instead be increased all the more by the knowledge of its justice—unless, perhaps, you are ashamed to be corrected or reluctant to be excused.

I know perfectly well the answer with which you usually confront the evidence of our proliferation. You say that a thing is not thereby prejudged to be good merely because it converts a great many people and draws them to itself. I know the mind’s tendency to change, even toward the worse. How many deserters are there from a good life? How many fugitives to depravity? Many, certainly—and now even more, in keeping with the extremity of the times.

But the comparison fails to correspond. For evil is so universally recognized [Col. 0559D] that not even the guilty themselves—those who pass over to evil and turn aside from you into depravity—dare to defend evil as good. They regard shameful deeds as objects of fear, and impious deeds as objects of shame. In short, they are eager to remain hidden, avoid appearing in public, tremble when caught, and deny when accused. Even under torture they do not readily or invariably confess; certainly, when condemned, they grieve. For they reproach in themselves what they had become: [Col. 0560B] they attribute their passage from innocence to an evil disposition, or else to fate. So unwilling are they to regard the evil as their own, because they cannot deny that it is evil.

But what comparable conduct do Christians display? [Note: [Col. 0559D] “But Christians.” Christians, compelled by no force, declared themselves Christians and gloried exceedingly in that fact. Thus in the *Apologeticum*, ch. I: “If he is denounced, he glories; if he is accused, he does not defend himself; when questioned, he confesses, even voluntarily; when condemned, he gives thanks.” But those who confessed it voluntarily were not for that reason condemned or treated as defendants. LE PR.] No one is ashamed; no one repents, except of what he formerly was. If he is denounced, he glories; if he is dragged away, he offers no resistance. If he is accused, he does not defend himself; when questioned, he confesses; when condemned, he glories. What kind of evil is this, in which the nature of evil ceases to exist?

**2.** In this matter you yourselves even judge contrary to the established method of investigating crimes. For when guilty men who have been brought before you deny the offense, you press them with tortures toward confession; but Christians, who confess voluntarily, you crush with tortures toward denial. What great perversity is this: [Col. 0560C] that you oppose confession, alter the function of torture, and compel a defendant who could escape freely to deny against his will? You who preside over the extraction of truth seek a lie from us alone, so that we may say that we are not what we are. I suppose you do not want us to be evil, and therefore eagerly strive to exclude us from that name.

To be sure, you batter [Note: [Col. 0559D] Le Prieur reads: “batter.”] and torture all other men so that they may deny that they are what they are said to be. Yet when they deny, you do not believe them; when we deny, you believe us at once. If you are certain that we are utterly criminal, why are we treated by you differently from criminals even in this?

I do not mean merely that you grant us no opportunity either to answer the accusation or to refuse it; for you are ordinarily not hasty in condemning men who have neither been accused nor defended. But, for example, if an inquiry is held concerning a murderer [Note: [Col. 0560D] Rigault conjectured: “is consulted.”], the case is not immediately struck from the record, nor is the investigation satisfied, once he has confessed to the name of murderer. Although you are slow to believe those who confess, you additionally demand the attendant circumstances: how often he committed murder, in what places, with what spoils, accomplices, and receivers—so that nothing whatever belonging to the criminal may remain concealed and nothing may be lacking to the truth required for judgment.

But in our case, although you charge us with more numerous and more atrocious crimes, you draw up briefer and slighter records. [Col. 0561A] I suppose you do not wish those whom you wish utterly destroyed to be burdened with charges, or you do not think it necessary to investigate what you already know. This, then, is all the more perverse if you compel those of whom you possess the greatest certainty to deny. Indeed, what would have accorded better with your hatred—once the proper judicial procedure had been set aside—would have been to strive by your own zeal not for denial, lest you release those whom you hate, but for confession of each individual crime, so that your hostility might be more fully satisfied by the accumulation of punishments, as it was determined how many times each man had already celebrated those banquets and how often, in the darkness, he had committed the incestuous acts of the onslaught.

What of the fact that, to eradicate the race, the investigation ought to have been extended and the questioning prolonged to include accomplices and accessories? The child-slaughterers [Note: [Col. 0561C] “The child-slaughterers and cooks, and the very dogs that acted as groomsmen, should have been brought forward.” They were, of course, accused of three crimes: impiety, Thyestean feasts, and incestuous intercourse. Thus Athenagoras: τρία ἐπιφημίζουσιν ἐμῖν ἡγκλήματα· ἀθεότητα, Θυέστεια δεῖπνα, Οἰδιποδείοι μεξεισ (“They impute three crimes to us: atheism, Thyestean banquets, and Oedipal intercourse”; several recovered spellings are corrupt, but the sense is clear). For the pagans used to say that Christians, when they assembled, customarily killed an infant; that, after eating its flesh and drinking its blood, they entered into a covenant with one another; and that after the banquet they coupled promiscuously with any women whatever. It was furthermore customary, they said, to employ dogs tied to the lampstands, which, when a morsel was thrown to them, overturned the lamps; once the lights had been extinguished, they claimed that the crimes of those most shameful couplings then took place. LE PR.] and the cooks and the very dogs [Note: [Col. 0561D] “And the very dogs that acted as groomsmen.” In the *Apologeticum*, those who are here called “groomsmen” are called “pimps”—the overturners of the lights. RIG.] that acted as groomsmen should have been brought forward; then the matter would have been properly corrected [Note: [Col. 0561D] “Then the matter would have been properly corrected.” Only then would the procedure of investigation have been just and lawful, if the child-slaughterers, cooks, and so forth were brought forward. The same commentator.].

The spectacles, too, would have acquired an added attraction. With what eagerness people would gather in the amphitheater [Col. 0561B] if some man who had devoured a hundred infants were going to fight! But such horrifying and monstrous charges are laid against us. Surely they ought to have been brought to light, lest they appear incredible and public hatred against us grow cold. For many people moderate their belief in such allegations, recoiling from the notion that nature should seek a wild beast’s food, though intercourse with humankind has barred it from belonging to the human race.

[Note: [Col. 0561D] “Recoiling from the notion that nature should seek a wild beast’s food, though intercourse with humankind has barred it from belonging to the human race.” We have thus restored what appears in the Codex Agobardinus without any meaning. For it has been corrupted as follows: “Honoring nature to seek a wild beast’s food, which intercourse with humankind has barred.” Tertullian says that such horrifying and monstrous charges are laid against us that many moderate their belief in them—that is, do not give credence to such things—recoiling from the idea that nature should seek a wild beast’s food: namely, that human beings are so born and constituted as to seek and desire a wild beast’s food; that their nature is so bestial that they willingly feed upon human flesh, which is the nature of wild beasts. But intercourse with humankind has barred that nature of beasts—that is, beasts themselves—from the human race. From where, then, were these humans born, this race of beasts? He said “a nature that seeks,” in the Greek manner, meaning something so made and born that it seeks. The same commentator.]

**3.** You, therefore, who are otherwise the most diligent and persistent investigators of far lesser crimes, abandon that diligence in the face of such horrors, surpassing every form of impiety. You neither accept confession—though judges must always labor to obtain it—nor arrange an investigation—though those who condemn must always take counsel from it. It is now apparent that every accusation against us is directed not against any particular crime, but against the name.

Indeed, if the truth of the crimes were established, the names of the crimes themselves would be applied to those condemned, and the sentence against us would be pronounced in this form: “It is decreed that this murderer, or incestuous person, or whatever else we are alleged to be, be led away, crucified, or given to the beasts.” But your sentences [Col. 0562A] record nothing except that someone has confessed to being a Christian. No name of a crime appears—unless the name itself is the crime. For this is in reality the whole basis of the hatred against us.

The name [Note: [Col. 0562C] “The name is the issue.” Since the pagans suffered from a shortage of crimes by which to oppress the Christians, they accused the name itself. Tertullian says much about this here and previously in the *Apologeticum*. For at that time Christians were ὑπὸ συκοφαντῶν σφαττόμενοι (“being slaughtered by false accusers”), as Athenagoras says in his *Apology for the Christians*. They were summoned to court and condemned on account of the name alone, for no inquiry was made whether Christians had committed any evil, as the same author says: ἀλλ` εἰς τὸ ὄνομα, ὡς εἰς ἀδὶκημα (“but against the name, as though against a crime”). The charge was drawn up; for no Christian had been detected in any criminal act: οὐδεὶς ἀδικῶν Χριστιανὸς ἐλήλεγκται (“no Christian has been proved guilty of wrongdoing”). Everywhere only the name was considered and treated as a crime: οὐδὲν δὲ ὄνομα, ἐφ` ἑαυτοῦ καὶ δι` αὑτοῦ, οὐ πονηρὸν οὒτε χρηστὸν νομίζεσθαι (“no name, considered in and of itself, is to be deemed either evil or good”), and earlier: οὐ γὰρ τὰ ὀνόματα μίσοις ἅξια, λλὰ τὸ ἀδίκημα δίκης καὶ τιμωρίας (“for names are not deserving of hatred, but wrongdoing is deserving of judgment and punishment”; μίσοις ἅξια is textually irregular, and λλὰ apparently lacks the initial ἀ of ἀλλὰ). Thus Athenagoras throughout the opening of his *Apology*. LE PR.] is the issue: some hidden force assails it through your ignorance, so that you are unwilling to know for certain what you are certain that you do not know for certain. Thus you neither believe allegations that are not proved nor wish to investigate them, lest they readily be proved false [Note: [Col. 0561C] Havercamp corrects: “lest they be proved false,” etc. In what follows, read “is answered or denied”—“is recited.”], so that a hostile name may be punished under a presumption of crimes. Indeed, to make us abandon the hostile name, we are compelled to deny it; then, when we have denied, we are released with complete immunity for our past acts. We are no longer bloodstained or incestuous, because we have relinquished that name.

But while this reasoning is demonstrated in its proper place, disclose the basis upon which you proceed in your assault upon the name. What is the name’s crime, what its offense, [Col. 0562B] what its fault? For you are barred from alleging crimes that no ordinance [Note: [Col. 0562D] “No ordinance directs.” That is, no command. Below, p. 51: “Nero’s ordinance.” RIG.] directs you to allege, no proof [Note: [Col. 0562C] Rigault reads “production of evidence.” In what follows, write “is answered or denied”—“is recited.”] establishes, and no sentence enumerates. What is presented to the governor, what is investigated concerning the defendant, what is answered or denied, what is recited from the council’s deliberation—that I recognize as the charge.

Therefore, as regards the merit of a name—if names can incur any guilt, if words can be accused—I think that no complaint can be brought against a word or name unless it either sounds barbarous, conveys something ill-omened or indecent, or otherwise displeases the hearer when it is pronounced. These are the crimes of words or names, just as barbarism, solecism, and an excessively tasteless figure are faults of words and speech.

But the name “Christian,” so far as its meaning is concerned, is interpreted from “anointing.” Even when it is incorrectly pronounced by you as “Chrestian” (for you are not even clearly certain of the name itself), it is still formed from “sweetness” or “goodness” [Note: [Col. 0562D] “From sweetness or goodness”: from sweetness, *Christianus*; from goodness, *Chrestianus*. RIG.]. Thus in innocent human beings you detain even our innocent name, which is neither awkward [Col. 0563A] [Note: [Col. 0563] This note appears at the foot of column 563, but the callout number (1) has not been printed in the text.] [Note: [Col. 0563C] Le Prieur conjectures: “Others, whom,” etc.] upon the tongue, nor harsh to the ears, nor harmful to a person, nor hostile to the country, but Greek, like other names, melodious, and pleasant in its meaning. Surely names are not to be punished by sword, cross, or beasts.

**4.** But you say that the sect is punished under the name of its founder. First, it is certainly a proper and customary rule for a sect to be designated by the title of its founder, since philosophers too receive their names from their founders—the Pythagoreans and Platonists [Note: [Col. 0563C] “Pythagoreans and Platonists.” He had said earlier in the *Apologeticum*: “Are not philosophers named from their founders—Platonists, Epicureans, Pythagoreans?” Thus Christians regarded it as glorious to be called by the name of their leader. Hence Chrysostom, concerning the blessed Lucian: ποίας εἶ πατρίδος; Χριστιανός εἰμι, φησί (“‘What country are you from?’ ‘I am a Christian,’ he says”). τί ἔχεις ἐπιτήδευμα; Χριστιανός εἰμι (“‘What occupation do you have?’ ‘I am a Christian’”). τίνας (“‘What…’”) [Col. 0563D] προγόνους; ὁ δὲ πρὸς ἅπαντα ἔλεγεν, ὅτι Χριστιανός εἰμι (“‘…ancestors do you have?’ But to every question he replied, ‘I am a Christian’”). “What is your religion?” “I am a Christian.” “What art do you profess?” “I am a Christian.” “Who are your ancestors?” To every question he replied, “I am a Christian.” LE PR.]—just as physicians are called Erasistrateans and grammarians Aristarchians. Accordingly, if a sect is evil because its founder is evil, it is punished as an offshoot of an evil name.

And yet this would be rashly presumed. It was first necessary to examine the founder so that the sect might be known, rather than to preserve an opinion of the founder from an inspection of the sect. But now, since you necessarily remain ignorant of the sect because you are ignorant of its founder—or fail to examine [Col. 0563B] the founder because you do not examine the sect—you strike at the name alone, as though in it you were apprehending both the sect and the founder, whom you do not know at all.

And yet philosophers have complete liberty to pass over from you to a sect, a founder, and a name of their own; and no one stirs up hatred against them when they publicly and openly bark all the bitterness of their eloquence against your morals, rites, worship, and ways of life, with contempt for the laws and without regard for persons—so that some even hurl their freedom of speech with impunity against the emperors themselves.

But philosophers only affect the truth, which is most burdensome to the world, whereas Christians possess it. Those who possess it therefore give greater offense, because one who affects it makes a mockery of it [Note: [Col. 0563D] “Because one who affects it confines it.” In the *Apologeticum*: “corrupts it.” RIG.], while one who possesses it defends it. In short, Socrates was condemned precisely because he had approached the truth more closely by destroying your gods. [Col. 0563C] Although the Christian name did not yet exist on earth at that time, the truth was nevertheless always condemned.

Accordingly, you will not deny that he was wise, since even your Pythian god had borne witness to him: “Of all men,” it said, “Socrates is the wisest” [Note: [Col. 0563D] “Of all men,” it said, “Socrates.” It is commonly known that Socrates was judged the wisest of all mortals by Apollo’s oracle: Ἀνδρῶν ἁπάντων Σωκράτης σοφώτατος (“Of all men Socrates is the wisest”). Hence Valerius Maximus, Book III, ch. 4: “Socrates was judged the wisest not only by human consensus but also by Apollo’s oracle.” And Salvian, Book VIII of *On the Governance of God*: “Nevertheless, Apollo the Pythian spoke against himself, and truth proved superior; for when he pronounced Socrates the wisest of men, he denied that he himself was a god.” Socrates, indeed, was summoned to trial by the Athenians and condemned to death because he held wrong opinions concerning the gods. Worthy of attention are the words of [Col. 0564C] Minucius Felix: “Yet if there is a desire to philosophize, let whoever among you is great enough imitate Socrates, the foremost figure in wisdom, if he can. Truth defeated Apollo, causing him to pronounce judgment against himself. [Col. 0564A] For he confessed that he was not a god by affirming that the man who denied the gods was the wisest. Yet among you Socrates is regarded as all the less wise because he denied the gods, although he was wise precisely because he denied them.

In the same fashion you also customarily say of us: “Lucius Titius is a good man—except that he is a Christian”; and similarly of another: “I am surprised that Gaius Seius, a respectable man, has become a Christian.” In the blindness of their folly, they praise what they know, censure what they do not know, and corrupt what they know by means of what they do not know. It occurs to no one that a man may be good and prudent precisely because he is a Christian, or a Christian because he is prudent and good, although it is more humane to judge hidden things from manifest ones than to prejudge manifest things from what is hidden.

They had formerly known certain men, before they bore this name, as vagrants, worthless, and depraved. They marvel at their sudden reform, and yet they know how to marvel rather than how to understand. Others contend with such obstinacy that they fight against their own advantages, which they might derive from association with people bearing this name.
