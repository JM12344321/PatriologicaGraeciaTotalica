That of which he says: “It is good not to eat meat or drink wine. For he who serves in these matters is acceptable and pleasing to our God. One man believes that all things may be eaten; but another, being weak, eats vegetables. Let him who eats not nullify [Note: [Col. 0975C] *Disparage*, Semler.] him who does not eat. But since he forbids controversy when the matter rests on human discretion, how much more when it rests on divine authority! Thus he knew how to censure certain disciplinarians and prohibitors of food—those who abstained out of disgust, not those who abstained as a duty—whereas he approved those who abstained in honor of, not in insult to, the Creator.

And if he has handed you the keys of the meat-market, permitting all things to be eaten in order to establish the exception concerning meat sacrificed to idols, he has nevertheless not enclosed God’s kingdom within the [Col. 0975B] meat-market. “For,” he says, “the kingdom of God is not eating or drinking,” and, “Food does not commend us to God”—lest you suppose this to have been said about dry fare, rather than about rich and carefully prepared food. How unworthily, too, you interpret the Lord according to your own appetite because he ate and drank openly! Yet I think he also fasted, [Note: [Col. 0975D] *Had fasted*, Semler.] he who pronounced blessed not the satiated but those who hunger and thirst; who declared his food to be not what the disciples had supposed, but the completion of his Father’s work; who taught men to labor for the food that endures to eternal life; and who, even in the customary prayer, commanded that bread be requested, [Col. 0975C] not Attalic riches. [Note: [Col. 0975D] *He rejects riches*, Semler.]

So too Isaiah did not deny that God had chosen a fast, but enumerated the kind he had not chosen. “For on the days,” he says (Isa. LVIII, 3), “of your fasts, your pleasures [Note: [Col. 0975D] *Your voluntary acts*, Franeker.] are found, and you shake down all those subject to you; or you fast for insults and quarrels and strike with fists. For even though he prefers works of justice, he nevertheless does not prefer them without sacrifice, which is a soul afflicted by fasts.

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, *ON MODESTY* (C)

## [SUMMARY]

[Col. 0979A] I. Having fallen into heresy through his love of chastity, Tertullian pursues in this book Montanus’s error concerning the refusal to readmit penitents, at least adulterers and fornicators. IV. Then, after teaching that under the names of adultery and fornication he includes every sin of lust,

Knowledge also has its stages of life in Christ, through which the Apostle himself passed. “When I was a child,” he says, “I spoke as a child, I understood as a child; but when I became a man, I put away the things that belonged to childhood” (1 Cor. And would that these men too were cut off—men who mutilate the pure and true integrity [Col. 0982B] of the flesh, cutting away not the outermost surface but the innermost image of modesty itself when they promise pardon to adulterers and fornicators against the principal discipline of the Christian name. But now this glory is being extinguished, and indeed by those who ought all the more steadfastly to have subscribed no pardon to such stains, inasmuch as, whenever they wish, they marry [Note: [Col. 0983D] *May marry*, Oberthür, Semler.] precisely so that they may not be compelled to fall into adultery and fornication: “for it is better to marry than to burn” (1 Cor. VII, 9). Evidently, incontinence is necessary for the sake of continence; fire will be extinguished by fires!

Why, then, do they afterward pardon in the name of repentance the crimes for which they have prescribed remedies through the right of multiple marriages? For the remedies will be meaningless when the crimes are pardoned, and the crimes will remain if the remedies are meaningless. Thus in both respects they make sport of vigilance and negligence: most vainly guarding against offenses that they pardon, and most foolishly pardoning offenses against which they have guarded; [Col. 0983B] whereas there should either be no guarding against an offense where it is pardoned, or no pardon where it is guarded against. For they guard against it as though unwilling that any such thing should be committed, yet pardon it as though willing that it should be committed; whereas, if they are unwilling that it be committed, they ought not to pardon it, and if they are willing to pardon it, they ought not to guard against it.

Among us, however, the greatest or supreme sins are guarded against in this further way: we are not permitted even to know a second marriage after faith, although marriage and dowry contracts perhaps distinguish such unions from the deed of adultery and fornication.
