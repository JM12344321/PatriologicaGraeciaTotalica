## CHAPTER III

Nor do I think it necessary to debate whether God is right to forbid his name and honor from being assigned to a falsehood; whether he is right to refuse to allow those whom he has torn away from the error of superstition to return again to Egypt; or whether he is right not to permit [Col. 0128C] those whom he has chosen for himself to withdraw from him. And so [Note: [Col. 0128C] “Thus,” Semler, Oberthür] neither will he expect [Note: [Col. 0128D] “Will it be expected,” Wouwer manuscript] us to reconsider whether he wished the discipline which he chose to institute to be observed, or whether he rightly avenges its abandonment when he wished it to be observed. For he would have instituted it in vain if he had not wanted it observed; and he would have wished it observed in vain if he had not wanted to vindicate it.

It follows, then, that I must prove these determinations of God against superstitions to have been not only established [Note: [Col. 0128D] “Both established and also vindicated.” So the Agobard manuscript reads; in Ursinus’s book, “promulgated.” RIG.] but also vindicated [Note: [Col. 0128D] “Promulgated,” Wouwer manuscript], since the entire rationale of martyrdoms will rest upon them.

Moses was away with God upon the mountain when the people, unable to endure even his necessary absence, demanded that gods be produced for them—gods which the people themselves would more properly have destroyed [Note: [Col. 0128D] “By which they would more properly have destroyed themselves,” Junius]. [Col. 0129A] Aaron was pressed, and ordered the earrings of their women to be cast into the fire. For in judgment upon themselves they were about to lose the true ornaments of the ears: the words of God. The discerning fire cast the figure of a calf for them [Note: [Col. 0129C] “Poured them abroad,” Wouwer manuscript], thereby reproaching them for having their heart where their treasure also was—that is, in Egypt, which, among other animals, consecrated a certain kind of ox as well. And so three thousand men, slain by their nearest relatives [Note: [Col. 0129C] The Venetian edition lacks “and”], because they had offended their nearest Parent, dedicated both the beginning and the recompense of transgression.

[Note: [Col. 0129D] “In *Arithmoi*.” He retained the Greek word for *Numbers*, just as a little later he has *Kritai* for *Judges*. LE PR.]

In *Arithmoi* (Numbers XXV), when Israel had turned aside at Shittim, they went off to commit fornication with the daughters of Moab; they were invited to the idols so that they might also fornicate in spirit; finally, they ate some of the things polluted by them. Then they also worshiped the gods of that nation and were initiated into Baal-peor. After Joshua son of Nun had died (Judges II), they abandoned the God of their fathers and served [Note: [Col. 0129D] “The idols of the Baals and Astartes.” Here *Baalim* means the gods of the nations, or idols; sometimes *Baal* is used for a place. And thus throughout almost all the annals of the Judges, and thereafter of the Kings, he administered his wrath against Israel by preserving the strength of the surrounding nations—through war, captivity, and the yoke of foreigners—whenever they most especially strayed from him into idolatry [Note: [Col. 0130C] “Into idolatry,” Semler].

## CHAPTER IV

If, therefore, this [Note: [Col. 0130D] “If from the beginning it is established that it was forbidden.” It is absolutely certain that idolatry was forbidden as the supreme crime. Hence Tertullian proves in his book *On Idolatry* that all crimes are comprised in that one, beginning the work with these words: “The principal crime of the human race,” etc. Gregory of Nazianzus does not depart from this view in Oration 38, *On Idolatry*: ἔσχατοι τῶν κακῶν καὶ πρῶτοι, “the last of evils and the first.” LE PR.] is established from the beginning—both that idolatry was forbidden by so many and such great commandments, and that it was never committed with impunity, as shown by so many and such great examples—and if no crime is reckoned so presumptuous before God as a transgression of this kind, we must understand without further prompting the purpose of God’s warnings and executions of judgment. Even then it was giving support to martyrdoms, which not only must not be doubted but must also be endured, since by forbidding idolatry it had made room for them. Otherwise martyrdoms would not occur; and God had plainly constructed his authority in advance, willing those things to occur for which he had made room.

For now we are stung concerning the will of God, and [Note: [Col. 0130C] Semler, following Rhenanus, omits “and”] the scorpion redoubles the wound, denying and accusing this will, so that it may either insinuate another God whose will this is not; or destroy our God all the same, because his will is such; or deny that there is any will of God at all, if it cannot deny [Col. 0130B] God himself.

We, however, having elsewhere contended concerning God and the rest of the whole body of every heretical doctrine, now draw beforehand the fixed lines for a single kind of encounter. We defend the will of no God other than Israel’s—the will which made room for martyrdoms through both the commandments by which idolatry was always forbidden and the judgments by which it was punished.

For if the keeping of a commandment [Note: [Col. 0130C] “By keeping,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] suffers violence, this will in some fashion be the commandment’s way of being kept: that I should suffer whatever will enable me to keep the commandment—namely, whatever violence threatens me as I guard against idolatry. And surely he who imposes a commandment exacts obedience. He therefore cannot have been unwilling for those things to occur through which obedience will be established.

I am forbidden to name any other as God, lest even by naming him I should fashion a god with my tongue no less than with my hand; I am forbidden to worship or in any way [Note: [Col. 0130C] “How,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür, Pamelius; “in any way,” Junius] venerate any other besides the one God who so commands. I am also commanded to fear him, lest I be abandoned by him, and to love him with my whole being, so that I may die for him. Serving under this oath of allegiance, I am challenged by enemies; I become their equal if [Note: [Col. 0130C] “If to them,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, Oberthür; “unless to them,” Latinus] I surrender. In defending this oath I fight to the finish in the battle line; I am wounded, cut down, killed. Who willed such an end for his soldier, if not the one who marked him with such an oath of allegiance?

## CHAPTER V

You have, therefore, the will of my God: this wound has been met. [Col. 0131A] Let us consider another stroke [Note: [Col. 0131C] “Nor,” Latinus], concerning the character of that will.

It would take too long to demonstrate that my God is good—a truth the Marcionites have already learned from us. For the moment it is enough that he is called God, so that he must necessarily be believed good. For whoever [Note: [Col. 0131C] “Who,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] has presumed there to be an evil God cannot remain consistent in both terms: either he will have to deny that one to be God whom he has judged evil, or call him good whom he has pronounced God. Therefore the will of him who, unless he is good, will not be God, will also be good.

The goodness of the very thing that God has willed will prove this as well—I mean martyrdom—for only one who is good has willed what is good [Note: [Col. 0131C] Others read: “Only one who is good has willed what is good”]. I maintain that martyrdom is good in the sight of the same God by whom idolatry is both [Note: [Col. 0131C] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür omit “both”] forbidden and punished. For martyrdom opposes and resists idolatry. Nothing can oppose and resist evil unless it is good [Note: [Col. 0131C] “Be observed,” Franeker edition].

We do not thereby deny that rivalry exists among evils themselves as well as among goods. But the present proposition has a different character. [Note: [Col. 0131C] “For martyrdom does not contend with idolatry over some malice common to both, but through its own grace.” [Col. 0131D] The manuscript reads *malitia* (“malice”), whereas the common editions then read “but through its own grace.” We shall therefore owe one part of the correction to those ancient parchments of Agobard and the other to the common books. For the true reading will emerge if we read: “For martyrdom does not contend with idolatry over some malice common to both, but through its own grace; for it delivers from idolatry.” Tertullian says that martyrdom is good because it opposes the evil which is idolatry, and that it cannot oppose evil unless it is good. He does not thereby deny that opposition, conflict, and rivalry exist among vices or evils themselves; for we sometimes experience greed contending with cruelty, gluttony with lust, since both sides in such cases are evils and vices. But, he says, the discourse which he has undertaken concerning martyrdom’s contest with idolatry has a different character. For martyrdom does not contend with idolatry over some malice common to both, as though it were a contest between two evils as to which is stronger; rather, it contends through its own grace, [Col. 0132C] through its own goodness, because it delivers from idolatry. RIG.]

For martyrdom does not contend with idolatry over some malice [Note: [Col. 0131C] “Military service,” Pamelius, Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür] common to both, but through [Note: [Col. 0131C] “With,” Venetian edition, Semler] its own grace; for it delivers from idolatry. Who will not pronounce good that which delivers from evil? What else is the opposition [Note: [Col. 0131C] “To the adversary,” Rhenanus, Semler, Venetian edition] between idolatry and martyrdom than that between death and life? Life will be assigned to martyrdom in the same measure as [Note: [Col. 0131C] “Insofar as,” Latinus] death [Note: [Col. 0132C] “In the same measure as idolatry is assigned to death”: so it stands in the Agobard book; [Col. 0132D] but in Ursinus’s book, “in the same measure as death is assigned to idolatry.” RIG.] to idolatry [Note: [Col. 0131C] “In the same measure as idolatry is assigned to death,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Venetian edition, Semler, Oberthür]. Whoever calls life an evil has death available to call a good.

This too is human perversity: to cast away what brings health, embrace what brings destruction, seek what is dangerous [Note: [Col. 0131C] “And everything,” Semler], and wrongly avoid what heals; in short, to desire to die sooner than to be cured. For many also flee the protection of medicine [Note: [Col. 0132C] Semler inserts “who”]: many, indeed [Note: [Col. 0132C] Semler omits “indeed”], are foolish, many are timid and wrongly ashamed.

[Col. 0131C] And plainly there is a kind of savagery in medicine [Note: [Col. 0132C] “Medicine,” Semler], arising from the scalpel, the cautery, [Note: [Col. 0132D] “From the burning of mustard.” Pliny describes the fiery effect of mustard as exceedingly beneficial to the body. Caustic force and burning heat are also attributed to it. RIG.] and the burning of mustard. Yet being cut, cauterized, and stretched [Note: [Col. 0132C] Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, and Oberthür add “and bitten”] is not for that reason [Col. 0132A] evil, because it inflicts useful pains. Nor will it be rejected merely because it causes distress; rather, because it necessarily causes distress, it will be applied. The fruit excuses the horror of the procedure.

That man who howls, groans, and bellows beneath the physician’s hands will afterward heap payment upon those same hands, proclaim them supremely skillful, and deny that they were ever savage. Thus martyrdoms too rage fiercely, but for salvation; and God too must be permitted to heal unto eternal life through fires, swords, and every bitter thing.

Yet you will admire the physician even for the fact that he generally applies remedies whose qualities match those of the complaints. He gives aid as though by contraries, bringing relief through the very things by which the patient suffers. For he restrains fevers by burdening them still further with heat; he quenches burning conditions by wasting them through thirst instead [Note: [Col. 0132C] “Restrains,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, Oberthür]; he gathers up [Note: [Col. 0132C] “Corrects,” Latinus] an excess of bile with every sort of bitter little potion [Note: [Col. 0132C] “Potions,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, Oberthür]; and he checks a flow of blood by opening yet another small vein.

But will you think God—and indeed a jealous God—deserving of blame if he chose to contend with the cause of the injury and to benefit us by matching it: to dissolve death by death, scatter killing by killing, drive torments away by torments, dissipate punishments by punishments, confer life by taking it away, aid the flesh by wounding it, and save the soul by snatching it away? What you consider perversity is reason; what you regard as savagery is grace: thus [Note: [Col. 0132D] “God heals eternal things by means of momentary things.” So reads the old manuscript. Tertullian says that God, who is cruel and grim for our good because he wills human beings to arrive at the glory of eternal felicity even through the tortures of martyrdom, must be worshiped and loved by us much more than a physician who restores our bodily health only by burning and cutting. He then explains and softens what otherwise seemed harsh and grim. Lucifer of Cagliari apparently wished to imitate this passage in his book *On Despising Death for Christ*: “For although the physician may cut, burn with a cautery, and by the burning of mustard lead the sick man through every pain, nonetheless being cut, cauterized, eaten away, and bitten is not hostile,” etc. But there is a great difference between the two artists. RIG.] God heals eternal things by means of momentary things [Note: [Col. 0132C] “Contemplates,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, Oberthür].

Magnify God, though grim, for your good. You have fallen into his hands, but you have fallen happily; he too has fallen upon your illnesses. Man always gives the physician his business first: indeed, he himself brought the peril of death upon himself. [Col. 0132C] He had received from his Lord, as from a physician, a sufficiently beneficial discipline according to the law of life: he was to eat from everything, but abstain from one little tree alone, which the physician himself knew to be harmful for the time being. He listened to the one he preferred, broke his abstinence, ate what was forbidden, and, sated with transgression, [Note: [Col. 0133D] “He suffered lethal indigestion unto death.” After the unlawful food, he was afflicted by deadly indigestion. Tertullian uses the same verb in his book *On Fasting*: “suffering indigestion from quails even to the point of cholera.” RIG.] suffered indigestion unto death [Note: [Col. 0133C] “Belched unto death,” Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler]. He thoroughly deserved, in good faith, to perish [Note: [Col. 0133C] “To have perished,” Pamelius, Semler] altogether, since he had so willed.

But the Lord, restraining the fever of the offense [Note: [Col. 0133C] “When the fever had been restrained,” Semler] until it might be moderated in due time by medicine [Note: [Col. 0133C] “Is moderated,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür], gradually compounded remedies: all the disciplines of faith, themselves set in rivalry against the vice, annulling the word of death by the word of life and wearing away the hearing of transgression by the hearing of devotion. Thus, even when that physician commands man to die, he expels the lethargy of death.

Why is man burdened by suffering now from the remedy what he was not burdened by suffering then from the vice? Does he dislike being killed for salvation, when he did not dislike being killed for destruction? Will he feel nausea at the antidote, who gaped eagerly for the poison?
