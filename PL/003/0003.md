IX. Renunciation of the World and Its Pleasures and Pomps.—Neophytes were likewise required to renounce the devil, the world, and its pleasures and pomps. This is most clearly proved by the authority of St. Cyprian, who, in his treatise *On the Dress of Virgins*, says that the riches of the world are to be despised just as the world itself is despised, “whose pomps and pleasures we renounced at that very time [Note: [Col. XXI] Id., treatise *On the Dress of Virgins*, vol. IV, col. 446 B.].” “We had renounced the world,” he says elsewhere [Note: [Col. XXI] Id., Ep. VII, vol. IV, col. 241 A.], “when we were baptized.” And in his treatise *On the Lapsed*, he expresses amazement that some Christians were found to be so perverse that they dared to renounce Jesus Christ, though they had already renounced the devil and the world. “The servant of God,” says the holy Doctor [Note: [Col. XXI] Id., book *On the Lapsed*, vol. IV, col. 334 A and col. 467 A. “But he who remembers that he has renounced the world recognizes no day belonging to the world.”], “was able to stand there . . . and to renounce Christ, though he had already renounced the devil and the world.”

X. Baptism Conferred by Immersion.—In the first centuries of the Church it was customary for those being baptized to be immersed in water, as may be gathered from St. Cyprian’s Epistle LXIX, addressed to Magnus [Note: [Col. XXI] Id., Ep. LXIX.]. Nowhere in this most ancient Father, however, is mention made of three immersions, although it is absolutely certain that these were in use both before and after his time. On this matter we possess testimony of unquestionable reliability from Clement of Alexandria [Note: [Col. XXI] Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata*, book V, p. 690. See *Critical History of Theology*, part V, p. 155.] and Tertullian [Note: [Col. XXI] Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*, ch. 26. See *Critical History of Theology*, part VI, p. 635.]. The fiftieth Apostolic Canon furthermore commands the deposition of a bishop or presbyter who used a single immersion [Note: [Col. XXI] Apostolic Canons, ch. 26.]. At the present time, however, Baptism is conferred by pouring.

XI. Baptism Conferred by Pouring or Sprinkling.—Formerly, the sick were baptized in this manner and were accordingly called *clinics* (*clinici*), without even the slightest doubt arising about the validity of Baptism conferred in this way. When St. Cyprian was consulted on this point by Magnus, he answered that those baptized in this manner received the grace of God no less than those immersed in water [Note: [Col. XXI] Cyprian, Epistle to Magnus.]. Then, after citing several scriptural pronouncements in which the pouring and sprinkling of water are mentioned, he concludes that the sprinkling of water has the efficacy of the saving bath: “Hence it is apparent that the sprinkling of water likewise has the efficacy of the saving bath.” Shortly thereafter he gives this reason for the fact: namely, that those who had been baptized in this manner were not baptized again; for Baptism would have had to be repeated if any doubt had arisen concerning its validity. He also disapproves of their being called *clinics* [Col. XXII] and commands that they be regarded as true Christians, like all others, and that in no respect should they be judged different from the rest of the faithful. We nevertheless acknowledge that at Rome those who had been baptized by pouring or sprinkling were excluded from the clergy; for this was one of the defects in Novatian’s ordination that Pope Cornelius adduces in his Epistle to Fabius, Bishop of Antioch [Note: [Col. XXII] Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VI, ch. 43. See *Critical History of Theology*, part XI, p. 17. See also the notes 3, 4, 5, and 6 of our most learned Coustant on this Epistle of Cornelius to Fabius, in Mansi, vol. I, *Collection of Councils*, cols. 823–824.]. The Council of Neocaesarea likewise excludes clinics from the presbyterate [Note: [Col. XXII] In Mansi, *Collection of Councils*, vol. II, col. 541, canon 12; and the note of the most illustrious Albaspinaeus on this canon in the cited *Collection of Councils* of Mansi, p. 552.], because, according to the mind of that Council, their faith was not voluntary but had been produced by fear of death. It must moreover be observed that Baptism by pouring was conferred only in a case of necessity.

XII. The Anointings of Baptism.—After immersion, the baptized person was anointed. “It is also necessary,” says the bishop [Note: [Col. XXII] Cyprian, Ep. LXX, vol. III, col. 1040 A.], “that one who has been baptized should be anointed, so that, having received chrism, that is, anointing, he may be one anointed by God and may possess within himself the grace of Christ.” The consecration of this chrism was performed over the altar by acts of thanksgiving: “Moreover, it is the Eucharist from which the baptized are anointed, the oil sanctified upon the altar [Note: [Col. XXII] Id., ibid.].” The opening words of this last text appear somewhat obscure. Some indeed hold that the word *Eucharistia* should be understood in its proper sense, namely, as the Sacrament of the altar; from this they conjecture that in the ancient Church the Eucharist was conferred there together with Baptism [Note: [Col. XXII] See Baluze’s note on this passage; he thinks it should read “with oil sanctified upon the altar.”]; others, however, maintain that the word signifies thanksgiving alone.

XIII. The Kiss Given to the Newly Baptized.—We learn from St. Cyprian’s Epistle to Fidus that the minister of Baptism was accustomed to give a kiss to the newly baptized person. Fidus maintained that, since an infant’s foot in the first days after birth was not clean, everyone ought to recoil from kissing it, and therefore that baptismal grace could not be conferred upon the infant. But the holy bishop replies that none of us ought to recoil from doing what God has deigned to do: “For in kissing an infant, each of us, in keeping with his religious devotion, ought to think of the still-recent hands of God themselves, etc. [Note: [Col. XXII] Cyprian, Epistle to Fidus, vol. III, col. 1017 A.].”

XIV. Confirmation Conferred Immediately after Baptism.—Baptism was generally followed immediately by Confirmation. This is manifestly established from St. Cyprian; for he says that those baptized in the Church were presented to its prelates, received the Holy Spirit, and were perfected through their prayer and the imposition of hands. [Col. XXIII] “This is also done among us now: those who are presented in the Church obtain the Holy Spirit through our prayer and imposition of hands and are brought to completion with the seal of the Lord [Note: [Col. XXIII] Cyprian, Ep. XXIII to Jubaianus, vol. III, col. 1115 A.].”

XV. Infants Baptized at the Beginning of Their Life.—St. Cyprian mentions in two places the custom of baptizing infants at the beginning of their life: namely, in his treatise *On the Lapsed*, where he writes: “And lest anything should be lacking to complete the accumulation of guilt, little children too, either placed in their parents’ arms or drawn along by them, lost what they had obtained at the very beginning of their life.” And in his Epistle to Fidus [Note: [Col. XXIII] Id., Ep. LIX, vol. III, col. 1016.], where he writes as follows: “We all judged that the mercy and grace of God ought to be denied to no human being who has been born. . . . There is for all, whether infants or those older in years, one equal measure of the divine gift. . . . For just as God shows no partiality toward persons, neither does he toward age; but, with impartial equality, he offers himself as Father to all for the attainment of heavenly grace.”

§ III.—Discipline Concerning the Eucharist

XVI. The Faithful Assembled Together with the Bishop to Celebrate the Eucharist.—That the faithful were accustomed to assemble together with the bishop to celebrate the divine mysteries may be gathered from St. Cyprian’s treatise *On the Lord’s Prayer* [Note: [Col. XXIII] Id., treatise *On the Lord’s Prayer*, vol. IV, col. 524 A.]: “And when,” he says, “we assemble together with the brethren and celebrate the divine sacrifices with the priest of God.” The place of this assembly was called the *Dominicum*, as appears to be inferable from St. Cyprian himself, who addresses wealthy women as follows in his treatise *On Works and Almsgiving* [Note: [Col. XXIII] Id., treatise *On Works and Almsgiving*, vol. IV, col. 612 B.]: “You are affluent and wealthy; and do you think that you celebrate the Lord’s rite, when you pay no attention whatever to the treasury; when you come to the Lord’s assembly without a sacrifice; when you take a portion of the sacrifice that the poor person has offered?”

XVII. The Time for Celebrating the Holy Sacrifice.—In St. Cyprian’s time the Eucharist was celebrated both in the morning and in the evening. This is proved by the sixty-third Epistle of this holy Doctor. For in it, arguing against those who were unwilling to pour wine into the chalice at the morning sacrifices, lest the faithful, smelling of wine after Communion, should be recognized by the pagans, he says [Note: [Col. XXIII] Cyprian, Ep. LXIII, vol. IV, col. 386 B.]: “Does anyone flatter himself with the consideration that, although in the morning water alone appears to be offered, nevertheless, when we come to supper, we offer the mixed chalice? . . .” Cyprian’s position is nevertheless that the sacrifice should be celebrated in the morning rather than in the evening. To the objection raised by some—namely, that the Lord offered the mixed chalice not in the morning but after supper—the holy bishop replies that Christ had to offer at about the evening of the day, so that the very hour of the sacrifice might signify [Col. XXIV] the decline and evening of the world: “But we celebrate the Lord’s Resurrection in the morning.”

XVII. It Was Offered Daily, and the Faithful Received the Eucharist.—The holy sacrifice was offered daily. “We who celebrate the sacrifices daily,” says St. Cyprian in his Epistle to Pope Cornelius [Note: [Col. XXIV] Cyprian, Ep. LIV, vol. III, col. 857 A.]. The faithful received the Eucharist daily unless some grave fault intervened [Note: [Col. XXIV] Id., *On the Lord’s Prayer*, col. 533 A. Compare Tertullian, *To His Wife*, book II, vol. I.]: “But we ask that this bread be given to us daily, lest we who . . . receive the Eucharist daily as the food of salvation should, through the intervention of some graver offense . . . be separated from the body of Christ.” Every day they drank the chalice of Christ’s blood, “considering,” in the words of the same holy Doctor [Note: [Col. XXIV] Cyprian, Epistle to the Thibaritans, LVI, vol. IV, col. 350 A.], “that they drink the chalice of Christ’s blood daily for this reason: that they themselves might be able to shed their blood for Christ.”

XVIII. The Eucharist Was Often Given under the Species of Bread Alone.—Although from what has been said and from innumerable other most ancient monuments it may rightly be inferred that the faithful were accustomed to receive Communion under both species, it is nevertheless certain from St. Cyprian that the faithful were sometimes nourished by the Eucharist under the species of bread alone.

On three occasions St. Cyprian mentions the Eucharist being administered to the faithful under both species. First, in Epistle LIV to Pope Cornelius, where he writes [Note: [Col. XXIV] Cyprian, Ep. LIV, vol. III, col. 856 H.]: “But now peace is necessary not for the weak but for the strong; and Communion must be given by us not to the dying but to the living, so that those whom we stir up and exhort to battle may not be left unarmed and naked, but may be fortified with the protection of Christ’s blood and body; and since the Eucharist is instituted for this purpose, that it may be a safeguard to those who receive it.” Then, in two places in the treatise *On the Lapsed*; indeed, at its very beginning, he says [Note: [Col. XXIV] Id., treatise *On the Lapsed*, col. 466 A.]: “Mouths sanctified by heavenly food, after receiving the body and blood of the Lord, rejected profane contaminations and the remnants of idols.” Later, around the middle of the cited treatise, he writes [Note: [Col. XXIV] Id., book cited, vol. IV, cols. 478, 479 A, B, 484 A.]: “The swollen and proud spirit has not been broken even though it has been conquered. Prostrate, it threatens those who stand unharmed; and the sacrilegious man is angry with the priests because he does not immediately receive the Lord’s body with defiled hands or drink the Lord’s blood with a polluted mouth.” In the same treatise *On the Lapsed*, he adduces several examples from which it is more clearly apparent than the light of midday that Communion, or the Eucharist, was administered under one species—sometimes under the species of wine, sometimes under that of bread. Its administration under the species of wine alone is indeed plain from the account related by St. Cyprian in the book *On the Lapsed*, vol. IV, col. 484 C ff. St. Cyprian relates an example of the Eucharist being administered under the species of bread alone in the same book, vol. IV, col. 486 B: “When a certain woman attempted with her hands to open her chest, in which there was something holy belonging to the Lord, she was deterred from daring to touch it when fire rose from it.” This plainly concerns a woman who wished to receive the Eucharist, in accordance with the practice of the faithful of that age, who carried the Lord’s body to their own homes so that they might eat that sacred bread with their own hands. Yet they could carry only the species of bread with them; therefore, in private Communions they received the species of bread alone. Hence, only with the greatest injustice did some persons in later times reproach the Church, as though it were a crime to be expiated by no penance, for having withdrawn the chalice in the Communion of the faithful. For the very same reasons for which it withdrew the chalice in private Communions during the first centuries, it did the same in succeeding centuries in all Communions generally. If the species of wine had been essential to the reception of the Eucharist, as the Hussites and others among the sectaries believed, the Church would surely never have permitted abstention from the chalice, not even in private Communions themselves.

XIX. The Faithful Received the Body of Christ in Their Hands and Carried It Home.—In former times the faithful received the Eucharist in their hands, as may be gathered from St. Cyprian’s Epistle to the Thibaritans, in which he says that the right hand that has received the Lord’s body should reject deadly sacrifices. “Let us arm,” he says [Note: [Col. XXV] Cyprian, Ep. LVI, vol. IV, col. 357 A.], “the right hand also with the spiritual sword, so that it may resolutely reject the sacrifices . . . that hand which has received the Lord’s body.” “The sacrilegious man is angry,” says the holy Doctor elsewhere [Note: [Col. XXV] Id., book *On the Lapsed*, vol. IV, col. 484 A. And a little above, vol. IV, cols. 478 B, 479 B: “Returning from the altars of the Devil, they approach the Holy One of the Lord with hands filthy and tainted by the smell of sacrifice.”], “because he does not immediately receive the Lord’s body with defiled hands.” In the same place he relates that when a certain defiled person, after the sacrifice had been celebrated by the priest, dared to receive a portion with the others, “he could neither eat nor handle the Holy One of the Lord [Note: [Col. XXV] Id., ibid., col. 486 B.].” Finally, in his treatise *On the Good of Patience*, he says [Note: [Col. XXV] Id., treatise *On the Good of Patience*, vol. IV, col. 631 C.]: “Nor, after tasting the Eucharist, should the hand be stained by sword and blood.” St. Cyprian teaches in addition that the Eucharist must be received in the hands “with fear and honor” [Note: [Col. XXV] Id., *Testimonies*, book III, ch. 94, vol. IV, col. 774 C. Moreover, on the ancient custom of receiving the Eucharist in the hand and carrying it home, see Cardinal Bona, *Liturgical Matters*, book II, ch. 17, no. 3 ff., p. 360 ff., Antwerp ed., 1739; and Henry Valesius’s annotations on Eusebius, book VI, ch. 43, and book VII, ch. 9, where on p. 274 of my edition he writes: “It was the presbyter’s particular office to deliver the Eucharist and the Chalice into the hands of the faithful; and this was properly called ἐπιδοῦναι (*epidounai*, “to hand over”).” And on p. 289 of my edition he says: “The faithful who were to receive Communion approached the altar, and there, standing—not kneeling as today—received the body of Christ from the presbyter’s hand.” The faithful who were to receive the Lord’s body in the hand first approached the altar with their hands carefully washed, as St. Chrysostom teaches in Homily III on Paul’s Epistle to the Ephesians. He writes the same in Homily LII on Matthew. When about to receive the Eucharist, moreover, they extended the right hand slightly cupped, supporting it underneath with the left, lest any part of the sacred food should fall, as St. Cyril of Jerusalem teaches in his final Catechesis.]. From the same holy Doctor we learn that in his time it was customary for participants in the Eucharist, after receiving a portion of the consecrated bread, [Col. XXVI] not only to eat it during the celebration of the sacred rites, but also to reserve some of it, carry it home, and consume it there either before food, in times of danger, or at the commencement of some undertaking—as was afterward done with the *eulogiae*. We gather this from two passages. In the book *On the Public Shows* he writes [Note: [Col. XXVI] Cyprian, book *On the Public Shows*, col. 784 B, vol. IV.]: “One who hastens to a public show while still carrying with him, as is customary, the Eucharist.” And in the book *On the Lapsed*: “When a certain woman attempted with unworthy hands to open her chest, in which there was something holy belonging to the Lord, she was deterred from daring to touch it when fire rose from it.” Tertullian had already shown this in book II of *To His Wife*, ch. 5: “Will your husband not know what you taste secretly before all food? And if he learns that it is bread, he does not believe it to be the bread it is said to be.” And in *On Prayer*, ch. 14: “When the Lord’s body has been received and reserved, both are safeguarded: participation in the sacrifice and the discharge of one’s duty.” This custom appears to have endured until the Council of Caesaraugusta under Damasus.

XX. The Eucharist Was Denied to Those Who Did Penance Only as Death Approached.—We have already seen in the preceding volume [Note: [Col. XXVI] Id., book *On the Lapsed*, vol. IV, col. 486 B.] that St. Cyprian wished those who had lapsed during the persecution and had not yet completed the penance imposed upon them to be reconciled when their lives were in danger. But although the holy bishop exercised such indulgence toward the faithful who, while still in good health, had begun penance for their offenses, he nevertheless treated with great rigor those who gave some signs of conversion only at the time of death, after through their own pride and hardness of heart they had stored up punishments and torments for themselves on the day of judgment. “And therefore,” says St. Cyprian [Note: [Col. XXVI] Id., Ep. LII, vol. III, col. 789 C.], “we have judged that those who do not perform penance, nor attest their sorrow for their offenses with their whole heart and by an open profession of their lamentation, must be entirely excluded from the hope of Communion and peace if they begin to entreat during illness and danger; because it is not repentance for their offense that compels them to ask, but the warning of impending death; nor is he worthy to receive consolation in death who gave no thought to the fact that he would die.”

XXI. The Eucharist Administered to Infants and Martyrs, etc.—The passages of St. Cyprian already cited above establish that in former times the Eucharist was administered to children no less than to adults. St. Cyprian judged that martyrs should be fortified with the protection of the Lord’s body and blood [Note: [Col. XXVI] Id., Ep. LIV, vol. III, col. 856 A.]. From this it is permissible to infer that at that time the reception of the Holy Eucharist, which was also called *Viaticum*, was regarded as necessary for the faithful at the point of death.

XXII. The Preface and Prayers of the Mass.—In St. Cyprian’s time, during the celebration of the holy sacrifice, bishops were accustomed, by first pronouncing a preface, to prepare the minds of the faithful to lift up their hearts to the Lord. “Therefore, before the prayer,” says St. Cyprian in his treatise *On the Lord’s Prayer* [Note: [Col. XXVI] Id., *On the Lord’s Prayer*, vol. IV, col. 541 B in another edition.], “the priest, having first pronounced the preface, prepares [Col. XXVII] the minds of the brethren by saying, ‘Lift up your hearts’; and when the people respond, ‘We have them with the Lord,’ etc.” It is therefore utterly false that in the primitive Church the liturgy comprised only the words of Scripture, since it is established from the quoted words of St. Cyprian that prayers also were poured forth “before the Prayer,” and that the minds of the faithful were customarily prepared for these prayers by a preceding preface. Indeed, that preface was placed beforehand with the same intention with which it is placed beforehand among us today: namely, so that “the faithful may be admonished that they ought to think of nothing other than the Lord.”

XXIII. The Names of the Faithful Were Offered in the Sacrifice of the Mass.—In the sacrifice of the Mass certain faithful were mentioned by name to God through the priests, just as the custom still exists today in the Canon, where, in the words “Remember, Lord, your male and female servants,” certain persons are mentioned by name. This is manifestly established by two Epistles of St. Cyprian. In the first of these, addressed to his clergy [Note: [Col. XXVII] St. Cyprian, Ep. IX, vol. IV, col. 252 A.], he laments that the names of those who had lapsed during the persecution were being offered—offered, I say, before penance had been performed, before *exomologesis* had been made, and before hands had been imposed upon them by the bishop and clergy. “Their name,” says the holy Doctor, “is offered.” In another, addressed to the bishops of Numidia, he writes [Note: [Col. XXVII] Id., Ep. LX, vol. IV, col. 362 [uncertain numeral in source: “3”].]: “I have added the names of each person so that you may remember in your prayers, sacrifices, and petitions our brothers and sisters who contributed to this very necessary work of redemption; I have added the names of each person.” On these words the most learned Rigault makes the following annotation: “Not without purpose, not needlessly; for in the sacrifices of prayers the names were offered of those who had contributed promptly and willingly to this very necessary work.” Exclusion from this privilege was regarded as the gravest punishment, as is established by the Epistle to the people of Furni [Note: [Col. XXVII] Id., Ep. LXVI, vol. IV, col. 398. See vol. XI, *Critical History of Theology*, p. 259.].

XXIV. The Most Holy Sacrifice Was Offered in Prisons; and a Little Water Was Poured into the Chalice.—We learn from an Epistle of St. Cyprian that at that time the most holy sacrifice was performed in prisons. Writing to his clergy while persecution was raging, he says [Note: [Col. XXVII] Id., Ep. IV, col. 231 A, vol. IV.]: “Take counsel, therefore, and make provision that the presbyters who offer there among the confessors should alternate in turn, one presbyter with one deacon”; namely, so that they might not easily be recognized. The very learned Bernard Marechall [Note: [Col. XXVII] Marechall, *Concordance of the Holy Fathers*, vol. II, p. 117, Venice ed.] thinks that in this passage of St. Cyprian there is an example of a private Mass against the more recent sectaries who condemn private Masses; but it seems to me that this most learned man had not formed a sufficiently precise idea of a private Mass. For only that Mass is called private in which the priest alone receives the Eucharist and none of those present [Col. XXVIII] communicates. We have already noted elsewhere that a little water was mixed with the wine in the sacrifice [Note: [Col. XXVIII] See vol. XI, *Critical History of the Theology of the Holy Fathers*, p. 481 ff.; and p. 589, note Z.].

§ IV.—Discipline Concerning Penance

XXV. In the following volume, with the most learned Maran as our guide, we shall demonstrate from the testimonies of St. Cyprian that Penance was numbered among the sacraments; how necessary this holy Doctor judged this sacrament to be for the faithful; and how holily he wished it to be administered. There we have also discussed, first, whether in Africa before this holy bishop Communion after penance had been denied for the gravest crimes; next, whether a new discipline was introduced under this same holy Doctor and the former severity retained only for apostates and deserters; third, whether St. Cyprian denied peace to lapsed persons who died in the course of their penance, with the sole exception of those who had received certificates from the martyrs. Finally, we investigated whether the Council of Carthage imposed penance for the entirety of their lives upon those who had sacrificed.

XXVI. Which Sins Were Subject to Canonical Penance; Whether Even More Secret Sins Were.—Yet other questions concerning the discipline of penance remain to be resolved. First of all, it must be asked which sins were subject to canonical penance in the time of St. Cyprian. St. Cyprian testifies in his Epistles [Note: [Col. XXVIII] Cyprian, Ep. IX, vol. IV, col. 251 B; Ep. XI, col. 257 B.] that, besides idolatry and apostasy from the faith, there were other, less grievous crimes that were subjected to canonical penance. Thus canonical penance was imposed upon heretics and schismatics when they returned to the Church. In St. Cyprian’s time sinners were required to undergo the same penance even for more secret crimes, and their confession was public, provided that no grave misfortune threatening the penitents’ lives would result from it. St. Cyprian discusses this matter at great length in his treatise *On the Lapsed*, where he relates that certain hidden crimes for which the sinners had neither made confession nor performed canonical penance were miraculously punished [Note: [Col. XXVIII] Id., treatise *On the Lapsed*, cols. 484 ff.]. The faithful likewise confessed sins of thought alone and performed penance for them.

XXVII. The Order of Canonical Penance.—The order generally observed in canonical penance was as follows. A certain period of time was established for this penance; it was accompanied by *exomologeses*, or confessions, and examinations; and it was brought to an end through the imposition of the hands of the bishop and clergy [Note: [Col. XXVIII] Id., Ep. IX, vol. IV, col. 251 B, and XI, col. 257 B.]. First, the penitent confessed his own crimes so that he might receive penance from the one who heard that confession. Undoubted traces of this confession are found in the treatise *On the Lapsed*, specifically in the passage [Note: [Col. XXVIII] Id., treatise *On the Lapsed*, vol. IV, col. 488.] in which the holy Doctor reports that the faithful were accustomed [Col. XXIX] to confess even the mere thought of sacrificing to idols and to perform penance for it. These words make it clear that the passage concerns a confession preceding Penance. Once this confession had been completed, the penitent endeavored to make satisfaction to God through laborious works; and this was done throughout the entire period prescribed by the bishop, unless some very grave cause supervened on account of which some portion of the penance was remitted. When the penance, or satisfaction, had been completed, the penitent presented himself again to the bishop, to whom he also confessed his particular crime before the clergy. His dispositions were then subjected to examination. If they appeared to be of such a nature as to merit the grace of reconciliation, the bishop then imposed hands upon him together with the entire clergy and granted him absolution; once this had been bestowed, he became a participant in the sacred mysteries. It must further be noted, concerning the imposition of the bishop’s hands, that this took place both at the beginning of penance and after the penance had been completed: at the beginning, so that sinners might be enrolled among the penitents; and after the completion of penance, so that they might be granted reconciliation. While the faithful were passing through the course of canonical penance, hands were likewise imposed upon them; but this imposition was a kind of exorcism. St. Cyprian mentions these three impositions of hands—or at least the first and second—because he sometimes uses the words “to impose hands” to designate the beginning of penance, but at other times to indicate absolution.

XXVIII. Penance Imposed in Proportion to the Offenses.—Penance, or satisfaction, was imposed in due proportion to the crimes committed and confessed. It was greater for those sins committed directly against God, but less for those that, being committed against one’s neighbor, concerned God only indirectly. Hence idolaters were required to perform a more severe penance than adulterers. Indeed, some distinction was made even between sins of the same nature. A person, for example, who had voluntarily presented himself to sacrifice to idols was punished with a greater and more rigorous penance than one who had sacrificed only after a prolonged struggle. A greater penalty was imposed on one who had defiled himself with the crime of idolatry together with his entire household, but a lesser one on him who had exposed himself to danger in order to free the others [Note: [Col. XXIX] Cyprian, Epistle to Antonianus, LII, vol. III, cols. 779 B–C.]. Some obtained the grace of reconciliation after a very short period of penance, for example after four or five years; others, however, were not granted absolution except at the point of death, apart from a case of most serious illness or the recent outbreak of persecution, as we have said elsewhere [Note: [Col. XXIX] *Critical History of Theology*, vol. XI, p. 450.]. As for those who had not actually sacrificed and were called *libellatici* because they had given pagan magistrates certificates in which they acknowledged that they had sacrificed to idols—even though they had not in fact done so—in order to avoid the infamy of a public sacrifice, [Col. XXX] their penance was less severe [Note: [Col. XXX] Cyprian, Ep. LII, vol. III, col. 781 A.]. And after one year had elapsed, St. Cyprian granted reconciliation to those who had embraced penance immediately after their lapse [Note: [Col. XXX] Id., Ep. XIX, vol. IV, cols. 273 B, C. In Epistle LIII to Fortunatus, vol. IV, col. 347 B, Cyprian writes: “For the lapsed amid tortures, penance begun and continued for three years can suffice.” And in the treatise *On the Lapsed*, book I, col. 489 A, he says: “Pardon can quickly be granted to such a person.”].

XXIX. Bishops and Other Ministers Subject to Canonical Penance.—Canonical penance was imposed upon bishops and the other ministers of the Church just as upon others, whether they had sacrificed to idols or were *libellatici*. They were, however, excluded from the priesthood and from all ecclesiastical offices, and could not be reconciled until after full and completed penance. This is clear from St. Cyprian’s Epistle to Fidus, in which he complains of undue haste in granting reconciliation to Victor, formerly a presbyter, especially before he had performed full penance and when no necessity was pressing. “We have read your letter, dearest brother,” says the holy Doctor [Note: [Col. XXX] Id., Epistle to Fidus, LXIII, vol. III, col. 1014 A.], “in which you reported concerning a certain Victor, a presbyter, that before he had performed full penance . . . our colleague Therapius rashly . . . and with premature haste granted him peace . . . though no illness was pressing and no necessity compelled it.” We shall see in the following centuries that public penance was no longer imposed upon ministers of the Church, but that they were merely deposed and reduced to the Communion of the laity. In St. Cyprian’s time, however, no one could escape this penance, not even bishops, as may be seen in another Epistle of the same holy Doctor to the clergy and people residing in Spain. There he relates that a certain bishop, Basilides, after confessing that he had blasphemed against God, voluntarily resigned the episcopate and turned to performing penance, deeming it sufficient cause for gratitude if it should fall to his lot to communicate even as a layman [Note: [Col. XXX] Id., Ep. LVIII, vol. III, cols. 1029 ff.].

XXX. Certificates of the Martyrs Concerning the Reconciliation of Penitents.—Those who had sacrificed to idols devised a thoroughly deceitful expedient by which the Church’s discipline concerning penance could be notably weakened. They extorted certificates from martyrs and confessors so that they might themselves be granted reconciliation and the period of penance might be shortened [Note: [Col. XXX] Id., Ep. IX, vol. IV, cols. 252 A, B.]. But in order to prevent the abuse that such certificates could create, it was customary for those who had obtained them to present themselves to the bishop, so that he might examine both the requests and the wishes of those who had given the certificates [Note: [Col. XXX] Id., Ep. XXII, vol. IV, col. 284 A.]. This examination was conducted before the people, as may be gathered from Cyprian’s Epistle to the people of Carthage [Note: [Col. XXX] Id., Epistle to the people of Carthage, XI, vol. IV, col. 257 A.]; and the aforesaid certificates benefited only those who had already [Col. XXXI] completed the greater part of their penance: “Those whose penance you perceive to be near satisfaction,” says the holy Doctor in an Epistle to the Martyrs and Confessors [Note: [Col. XXXI] Cyprian, Ep. X, vol. IV, col. 257 A.], in which he warns them not to grant certificates indiscriminately to all who ask, but carefully to consider the penance already performed and to weigh every circumstance.

St. Cyprian was so moderate and prudent concerning these indulgences that he was unwilling to determine anything about them by himself, but only with the counsel of the other bishops. “It befits our sense of restraint,” he says in his Epistle to his clergy concerning those who were hastening toward peace [Note: [Col. XXXI] Id., Epistle to the Clergy of Carthage, XIII, vol. IV, col. 261 A.], “that the prelates, assembling together with the clergy and with the people who have stood firm also present . . . should be able to arrange everything with the conscientiousness of common counsel.” And in another Epistle to the same clergy he says [Note: [Col. XXXI] Id., Ep. XVII to the same clergy, vol. IV, col. 270 A.]: “I have read the letter of all the confessors, which they wished to be made known through me to all my colleagues”; and shortly afterward he continues: “Since this matter awaits the counsel and judgment of all of us, I do not dare to prejudge it or claim for myself alone a matter common to all.” From this it is evident what conception the faithful then had of the discipline concerning penance and how solicitous they were that not even its smallest part should be weakened. “For what,” said the Roman clergy in writing to St. Cyprian [Note: [Col. XXXI] Epistle of the Roman clergy, among the Cyprianic Epistles, XXXI, vol. IV, col. 308 B.], “is more fitting in peace, or more necessary amid the warfare of persecution, than to maintain the due severity of divine rigor? Whoever relaxes it must always wander amid the unstable course of events, be scattered this way and that by the varied and uncertain storms of circumstances, and, as though the rudder of counsel had been wrenched from his hands, drive the ship of ecclesiastical salvation upon the rocks. Thus it is apparent that provision can be made for the Church’s salvation in no other way than by preserving at all times the proper order of discipline itself, like a saving rudder in a storm.”
