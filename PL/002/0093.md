Hence comes that familiar subtlety of unbelief: “If,” they say, “the very same substance is restored with its own shape, outline, and qualities, then it is restored also with all its remaining distinctive marks; and thus the blind, the lame, the paralytic, and whatever deformity marked a person at death will return in the same condition.”

[Note: [Col. 0878C] Others read: *illae vulgaris in argutiae*.]

[Note: [Col. 0878C] “Even if all the rest.”]

[Note: [Col. 0878D] “Whatever deformity marked a person at death.” The word *insignis* is sometimes used for “deformed,” just as the Greek ἐπίσημος (*epísēmos*, “marked, conspicuous”) is. LE PR.]

What then? Even if it were so, would you disdain to obtain so great a grace from God, whatever your condition? For even now, when you admit the salvation of the soul alone, do you not ascribe that same salvation to half-men? [Col. 0878B]

[Note: [Col. 0878C] Rhenanus and Semler read *eadem*.]

What is it to believe in the resurrection, if not to believe it complete? For if the flesh is restored from dissolution, much more will it be recalled from mutilation.

[Note: [Col. 0878C] All editions except Junius read *vexatione* (“affliction”) instead of *vitiatione* (“mutilation”).]

The greater cases settle the lesser. Is not the severing or blunting of any limb the death of that limb? If universal death is annulled by resurrection, why not partial death?

[Note: [Col. 0878D] “If we are transformed into glory, how much more into soundness.” This will be the transformation of bodies in the resurrection: they will rise not in the condition in which they died, but in that in which the first parent existed before he touched the forbidden fruit. LE PR.]

If we are transformed into glory, how much more into soundness! Bodily mutilation is accidental; integrity is proper to the body: in integrity we are born. Even if we are injured in the womb, the injury is already something suffered by a human being.

[Note: [Col. 0878D] Rhenanus, Rigault, and the Venetian edition read *vexemur* (“are afflicted”).]

The kind exists before the accident. As life is conferred by God, so it is conferred again. As we receive it, so we receive it back. We are restored to nature, not to injury. We revive as what we are born, not as what we are harmed into. If God does not raise us whole, he does not raise the dead. [Col. 0878C] For what dead person is whole, even if he dies whole? Who is sound when lifeless? What body is uninjured when slain, cold, pallid, rigid, a corpse?

[Note: [Col. 0878D] “When is a man weaker than when he is so altogether?” That is, when he is wholly weak. RIG.]

When is a man weaker than when he is so altogether? When is he more completely paralysed than when he is motionless? Thus to raise the dead is nothing other than to make them whole, lest they should remain dead in that part in which they have not risen.

God is capable of restoring what he made. [Col. 0879A] He has already sufficiently pledged—indeed, displayed—both this power and this generosity of his in Christ, showing himself not merely the raiser of the flesh but also its restorer to wholeness. Accordingly, the Apostle too says: “And the dead shall rise incorrupt” (1 Cor. 15). How could they do so unless whole, when they had previously been corrupted both by bodily infirmity and by the decay of burial?

[Note: [Col. 0879D] “Both by bodily infirmity,” etc. The theologians agree that human beings will rise entire and whole; for although they died mutilated, that mutilation will be repaired. Thus Augustine, *The City of God*, book XXII, chapters 19 and 20: “Nor, if certain members were amputated and taken from the martyrs, will they be without those members in the resurrection of the dead, since it was said to them: ‘Not a hair of your head shall perish.’” Moreover, Basil of Seleucia infers the resurrection of the whole human being from the restoration of one human limb, in Oration 14, on the lame man sitting at the Beautiful Gate, where he says against the Jews: ἀναστάσεως ἀνδριάντα τὸν χωλὸν ἐχάλκευσε, γλώτταις ψευδομέναις ἀληθεύοντας ἀντεστράτευε πόδας: “In the lame man he fashioned a bronze statue of the resurrection, and against lying tongues he marshalled truthful feet.” LE PR.]

For when, above, he set forth both propositions—that “this corruptible must put on incorruption” and “this mortal must put on immortality”—he was not repeating the same thought, but assigning a distinction. By apportioning immortality to the annulment of death and incorruption to the obliteration of decay, he adapted the one to resurrection and the other to restoration. I think, moreover, that to the Thessalonians he also promised the integrity of the whole substance. [Col. 0879B]

Therefore bodily defects will not be feared in the world to come either. Integrity, whether preserved or restored, will be incapable of losing anything, since even whatever it had lost is restored to it.

[Note: [Col. 0879C] Rhenanus and Semler read *admittere* (“admit”) instead of *amittere* (“lose”).]

For when you object that the flesh will still undergo the same sufferings if it is said that the same flesh will rise, you rashly defend nature against its Lord and impiously uphold law against grace—as though the Lord God had no right both to alter nature and to save apart from its law.

[Note: [Col. 0879C] Rhenanus and Semler read *ad hoc* (“for this”) instead of *adhuc* (“still”).]

[Note: [Col. 0879C] “God” is omitted by Rhenanus and Semler.]

How, then, do we read: “Things impossible with men are possible with God” (Matt. 19), and: “God chose the foolish things of the world, that he might confound the wisdom of the world” (1 Cor. 1)?

[Note: [Col. 0879C] Rhenanus and Semler read *quo* instead of *quomodo*.]

[Note: [Col. 0879C] “Are” is omitted by Rhenanus and Semler.]

I ask you: if you transform your slave by granting him freedom, will it follow—because the same flesh and soul remain which had formerly been exposed to lashes, fetters, and brands—that they must therefore suffer the same things? [Col. 0879C]

[Note: [Col. 0879C] François reads *illos*; Pamelius, *illas*.]

I think not. On the contrary, he is honoured by the splendour of a white garment, the distinction of a gold ring, and the name, tribe, and table of his patron.

[Note: [Col. 0879D] “The distinction of a gold ring.” This was a prerogative of equestrian rank; yet it became so debased that not only did freedmen elevated to that equestrian grade assume such rings, but the practice gradually descended even to the lowest common people. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0880D] “The name, tribe, and table of his patron,” etc. Slaves called their masters “heroes” and “lords,” while freedmen called them “patrons”; and when they were manumitted, they prefixed the patron’s third name to their own two names. Those who were granted freedom also used to be enrolled in the urban tribes and to sit at their patrons’ tables, something they did not venture to do until they had been manumitted. LE PR.]

Allow God this power too: through the force of that transformation to reform the condition, not the nature, both removing its liabilities to suffering and conferring safeguards upon it.

[Note: [Col. 0879C] Rigault and the Venetian edition read “of that transformation” in the reverse word order.]

Thus the flesh will indeed remain even after the resurrection—capable of suffering insofar as it is itself and the same flesh; yet incapable of suffering insofar as it has been manumitted by the Lord precisely for this purpose, so that it may no longer be able to suffer. [Col. 0880A]

## CHAPTER LVIII

“Everlasting joy,” says Isaiah, “shall be upon their head” (Isa. 35).

[Note: [Col. 0880C] The Venetian edition reads *Jucunditas*.] Nothing is everlasting except after the resurrection. “Pain and sorrow and sighing,” he says, “have fled from them.” Similarly, the angel says to John: “And God shall wipe away every tear from their eyes” (Rev. 17). Certainly from the same eyes that had formerly wept and that could still have wept, had not divine mercy dried up every shower of tears. And again: “For God shall wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more” (Rev. 11). Therefore corruption too shall be no more, being likewise driven away [Col. 0880B] through incorruption, just as death is driven away through immortality.

[Note: [Col. 0880C] “Death” is absent from Rhenanus.]

If pain, sorrow, sighing, and death itself arise from injuries to both soul and flesh, how will they be removed unless their causes cease—that is, the injuries of flesh and soul? Where are adverse accidents in the presence of God, or hostile assaults in the presence of Christ? Where are demonic attacks in the presence of the Holy Spirit, when the devil himself and his angels have already been plunged into the flames?

[Note: [Col. 0880C] Rhenanus and Semler read “the Lord” instead of “God.”]

[Note: [Col. 0880C] Rigault and the Venetian edition read *daemoniaci* (“demonic”).]

Where is necessity, or what is called fortune or fate? What wound is there for the risen after pardon? What wrath for the reconciled after grace? What infirmity after power? What weakness after salvation?

The garments and shoes of the children of Israel remained unworn and unsoiled during those forty years; even in their bodies, justice checked the readily occurring growth of nails and hair at the measure of fitness and dignity, lest even excessive growth should be reckoned as corruption. [Col. 0880C]

[Note: [Col. 0880D] “That the garments and shoes of the children of Israel,” etc. Why, he asks, were the garments of the Israelite people not worn out during forty years in the desert, unless so that they might appear incorrupt and be believed immune from decay, God so arranging it? I discussed this miracle in my observations on the *Prae-Adamitae*, under the name of Eusebius Romanus, against the perverse opinion of Peyrère, who mocks the miracles of Scripture with both wickedness and madness. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0880C] Rhenanus and Semler read the singular *corruptela*.]

The Babylonian fires did not damage the tiaras or *sarabara* of the three brothers, although these were foreign to the Jews.

[Note: [Col. 0880D] “Neither tiaras nor *sarabara*.” *Tiaras* is a Persian word, just as *saraba* is perhaps Chaldaic. On this see *On the Pallium*, chapter 4. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0880C] Rigault and the Venetian edition read *sarabara*; others *saraboeas*; Rhenanus *sarabas*; others *saraballa*.]

[Note: [Col. 0880C] Rigault and the Venetian edition read the singular *aliena*.]

Jonah was swallowed by the sea-monster, in whose belly shipwrecks were digested, yet three days later he was vomited out unharmed. Enoch and Elijah, even today, have not yet discharged the debt of resurrection, because neither have they discharged that of death; nevertheless, having been removed from the world and thereby already made candidates for eternity, they are learning the flesh’s immunity from every defect, every loss, and every injury and outrage. [Col. 0881A]

[Note: [Col. 0881C] The Codex Pithoeanus, Rigault, and the Venetian edition read *emunitatem*.]

To what faith do these examples affix their testimony, unless to the faith by which we must believe that they are proofs of the integrity to come? For, on the Apostle’s authority, the things that were written were figures of us, so that we might believe the Lord superior to every law governing bodies, and certainly the preserver above all of the flesh, whose very garments and shoes he also protected.

[Note: [Col. 0881C] Rhenanus adds “and.”]

[Note: [Col. 0881C] Semler reads *calceamenta*.]

## CHAPTER LIX

“But,” you say, “the age to come belongs to a different and eternal order; therefore that different order cannot possess the non-eternal substance of this age.” Certainly—if man existed for the sake of the future order, and not the order for the sake of man. But the Apostle, writing, “Whether the world, or life, or death, or things to come, or things present—all are yours” (1 Cor. 3:22), appoints the same persons heirs even of the things to come. [Col. 0881B]

Isaiah grants you nothing when he says, “All flesh is grass,” and elsewhere, “And all flesh shall see the salvation of the Lord” (Isa. 40:5–6).

[Note: [Col. 0881C] Pamelius, François, Rigault, and the Venetian edition read “of God.”]

He distinguished destinies, not substances. For who does not place God’s judgment in a twofold sentence, one of salvation and one of punishment? Therefore all flesh destined for fire is grass; and all flesh appointed for salvation shall see the salvation of the Lord.

[Note: [Col. 0881C] Pamelius, François, Rigault, and the Venetian edition read “of God.”]

I know that I did not commit adulteries with one flesh and am not now striving after continence with another.

[Note: [Col. 0881D] “I know that I did not commit adulteries with one flesh,” etc. If the Author is speaking of himself, he appears not to have preserved continence in his youth before marriage. But since we indicated above, in book I *To His Wife*, that he was continent within marriage itself, and since he was so great a lover of continence that on that account he subsequently adhered to Montanus, he seems rather to be speaking generally of any human being under the person of himself. PAM.]

If anyone goes about carrying two sets of sexual organs, he can now remove the grass of the unclean flesh and reserve for himself only that flesh which is to see the Lord’s salvation. [Col. 0881C]

[Note: [Col. 0881C] Pamelius, François, Rigault, and the Venetian edition read “of God.”]

But since the same Prophet also presents the nations as at one time reckoned like dust and spittle, and at another as destined to hope and believe in the name and arm of the Lord, are we mistaken about the nations too? Are some nations destined to believe, and others reckoned as dust, because their substances differ?

Yet Christ, within the ocean and from this very heaven that hangs over us, shone forth as the true light for the nations; and the Valentinians themselves learned here how to go astray. [Col. 0882A]

[Note: [Col. 0881D] “Yet Christ within the ocean, and from this heaven that hangs over us.” The Valentinians also raved that beyond this circuit of the earth there were other human beings, of a different substance, whom they called “true,” *teletoi*, *abaskantoi*, and *akinētoi*—that is, “perfect,” “unenvied,” and “immovable.” For they said that our people were not true human beings, whom the Demiurge reckoned as a drop from a bucket, dust from the threshing floor, and locusts. Our Septimius discusses this more openly in *Scorpiace*. I do not think that these ravings are connected with what is read in the First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians about worlds situated beyond the ocean, which he calls ἀπεράντους (*aperántous*, “boundless”). That statement probably escaped this apostolic man through ignorance of geography rather than through a desire to affirm any doctrine. RIG.—Christ was of the same nature as we are; for he did not, as the heretics imagined, draw his flesh from heaven or from a world beyond the bounds of the Ocean. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0882C] Rhenanus and Semler read *incumbat*.]

[Note: [Col. 0882C] Pamelius, François, and the Paris edition read *effulsit*.]

Nor will the constitution of the believing nations in flesh and soul be other than that of the unbelieving nations. Therefore, just as he distinguished the same nations not by kind but by lot, so too he divided the fleshes—which in those nations are of one substance—not by matter but by recompense.

## CHAPTER LX

But now, to aggravate the controversy still further, they argue from the functions of the members, with particular reference to this same flesh. They say either that the members themselves must continue in their operations and fruits, since they are assigned to the same bodily mass; or, because it is agreed that the functions of the members will cease, they also eradicate the bodily mass itself. Its continuance, they say, surely cannot be believed without members, since members cannot be believed to exist without functions.

[Note: [Col. 0882C] Rhenanus and Semler read *exaggerant* (“they aggravate”).]

“What use will there now be,” they say, “for this cavern of the mouth, this rank of teeth, this descent of the throat, this crossroads of the stomach, this abyss of the belly, and this long, intricate system of intestines, when there will be no place for food and drink? [Col. 0882B]

[Note: [Col. 0882D] “The crossroads of the stomach.” He calls the stomach’s gathering-place and receptacle a “crossroads,” because everything edible introduced through the mouth comes together there. The metaphor is taken from roads that meet and converge; grammarians accordingly derive *compitum* from this. RHEN.]

“What will those members receive, work upon, pass downward, separate, digest, and expel? What use will there be for the hands themselves, the feet, and every working limb, when even concern for sustenance will cease? What use for the kidneys, accomplices in the production of seed; for the remaining organs of generation in either sex; for the receptacles of conception; and for the fountains of the breasts, when intercourse, childbirth, and child-rearing have ceased?

[Note: [Col. 0882C] Rigault and the Venetian edition read *desessuro*; Rhenanus and Semler, *discessuro*.]

“Finally, what use will there be for the whole body, when the whole body will plainly be idle?”

Against these arguments we have already laid the foundation that the orders of the future and the present must not be brought into collision, since a transformation will intervene. We now build further upon it: these functions of the members remain only as long as the necessities of this life, until life itself is transferred from temporality into eternity, just as the animal body is transferred into the spiritual when “this mortal puts on immortality and this corruptible puts on incorruption” (1 Cor. 15). [Col. 0882C]

When life itself is then freed from necessities, the members too will be freed from their functions; but they will not therefore be unnecessary. For although they are released from functions, they are retained for judgments, so that each may receive through the body according to what he has done (2 Cor. 5).

[Note: [Col. 0882D] “But they are retained for judgments.” The Author very often relies upon this argument to support the resurrection, particularly in chapter 14. Although the Fathers place great and deserved confidence in it, Athenagoras, who pursues it more fully, assigned it somewhat less weight. He offers three arguments to prove the resurrection, but somewhat diminished the force of this one concerning the necessity of divine judgment. Yet his second argument, drawn from human nature itself, has far less strength; nor does his first, drawn from the end for which God created man, possess greater weight, although I consider it by no means ineffective. PAM.]

For God’s tribunal requires a whole human being; yet without his members a human being cannot be whole, because he consists of their substances, not merely their functions. [Col. 0883A]

[Note: [Col. 0883C] The Codex Wouwerianus, Rigault, and the Venetian edition read *liquet*.]

Unless, perhaps, you would assert that a ship is whole without keel, prow, stern, and the integrity of its entire structure. And yet we have often seen a ship shattered by a storm or dissolved by decay become the same ship again once all its parts were restored and repaired, even glorying in the designation “restored.”

[Note: [Col. 0883D] “And yet a ship shattered by a storm.” Alfenus likewise held that, if a ship had been repaired so often that no plank remained the same because every one had been replaced, it was nevertheless to be considered the same ship. Law *Proponebatur*, *De judiciis*. Thus the Athenians, at the time when they mixed the hemlock for Socrates, said that the ship customarily sent to Delos every year was the very ship in which Theseus had carried their ancestors to Crete. “This is the ship, as the Athenians say” (τοῦτο ἐστι τὸ πλοῖον, ὡς φᾶσιν Ἀθηναῖοι), etc., says the Platonist Phaedo. RIG.]

Are we troubled by God’s craftsmanship, will, and right? Suppose, then, that a rich and generous master, satisfying his affection or his glory by the restoration of a ship alone, has wished to carry the restoration only thus far. Will you therefore deny that it needs its former structure because that structure will henceforth be idle, when the mere preservation of the ship without operation is appropriate to it?

[Note: [Col. 0883C] Rhenanus reads *tunc* (“then”) instead of *tu* (“you”).]

It is therefore enough to learn only this: whether the Lord, in destining man for salvation, has destined the flesh also; whether he wills the same flesh to exist again. You must not argue from the future idleness of its members that it cannot exist again. [Col. 0883B]

[Note: [Col. 0883C] Rhenanus and Semler read *et* (“and”) instead of *ex* (“from”).]

For a thing may exist again and nevertheless be idle; but it cannot be said to be idle unless it exists. Yet if it exists, it may also not be idle; for nothing will be idle in the presence of God.

## CHAPTER LXI

“But, man, you received a mouth for eating and drinking.” Why not rather for speaking, so that you might differ from the other animals? Why not rather for proclaiming God, so that you might also preside among human beings? In short, Adam pronounced names for the animals before he plucked from the tree; he also prophesied before he ate.

“But you received teeth to gnaw meat from the market.” [Col. 0883C]

[Note: [Col. 0883D] “But you received teeth to gnaw meat from the market.” Like that Maenius of Horace: “The ruin, storm, and abyss of the meat-market.” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0883C] The English manuscript and Pamelius read *coornandum*.]

Why not rather to furnish a crown for every opening and gape of your mouth? Why not rather to regulate the beats of the tongue and mark the articulations of the voice by their impact?

[Note: [Col. 0883C] François and Pamelius read *temporandos*.]

Hear and observe even the toothless, so that you may regard the array of teeth as serving also for the adornment of the mouth. [Col. 0884A]

[Note: [Col. 0883C] “Even the toothless” is omitted by Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0883C] Rigault, the Venetian edition, and Semler read *honorem*.]

The lower parts of man and woman have openings—surely, you say, so that lusts may flow through them. Why not rather so that the boiled residue of drink may be strained through them?

[Note: [Col. 0884C] Rhenanus and Semler read *defluxura* (“what is to flow down”) instead of *defruta* (“boiled-down liquids”).]

[Note: [Col. 0883D] “So that the boiled residue of drink may be strained through them.” Junius greatly approves the reading of the first edition, *qua defluxura colentur*, and wonders that the alternative reading was admitted. Yet *defruta* is more expressive, for digested drink is strained through the lower passages. *Defrutum* is boiled wine, so called from boiling down, as Palladius says—to one half, according to Pliny; or, according to Nonius, they called it *defrutum* if by boiling they reduced two portions to one third. LAC.]

Within women there is also a place where seed may be collected. Or is it rather a place where burdens of blood may be discharged, since the more sluggish sex is unable to disperse it?

[Note: [Col. 0884C] Pamelius and François add *in*.]

These functions of the members must indeed be mentioned, since our opponents, choosing whatever functions they please, of whatever organs they please, and in whatever manner they please, mockingly bark them out with the deliberate aim of shaming the resurrection. They fail to consider that the very causes of necessity will then become idle: hunger for food, thirst for drink, procreation through intercourse, and sustenance through labour.

[Note: [Col. 0884C] Pamelius, Rhenanus, and Semler transpose the phrases to read: “and in whatever manner they please, and of whatever organs they please.”]

[Note: [Col. 0884D] “These functions of the members must indeed be mentioned.” Jerome played with astonishing freedom upon functions of this kind in the first book *Against Jovinian*. RIG.]

For once death is removed, neither the supports of sustenance for preserving life nor the heavy apparatus of procreation will burden the members. Besides, even today it is possible for the intestines and sexual organs to be idle. Moses and Elijah completed forty days of fasting and were nourished by God alone. [Col. 0884B] Even then this saying was being inaugurated: “Man shall not live by bread, but by the word of God.” Behold outlines of the power to come.

We too, so far as we can, excuse the mouth from food; we even withdraw our sex from intercourse. How many voluntary eunuchs are there? How many virgins married to Christ? How many sterile persons of either sex, constructed with fruitless generative organs?

[Note: [Col. 0884C] “For” is absent from Pamelius, the Paris edition, and François; Rhenanus and Semler read, “for even if already here.”]

For if, already here, the functions and advantages of the members may be idle through a temporary idleness appropriate to a temporal order, and yet the human being is no less whole, then likewise, with the human being preserved—and indeed preserved more fully in the eternal order—we shall all the more have no need of those things which we have already grown accustomed not to need here.

## CHAPTER LXII

[Col. 0884C] But the Lord’s declaration puts an end to this dispute. “They shall be,” he says, “like angels” (Matt. 22).

[Note: [Col. 0884D] “They shall be, he says, like angels.” He touched upon this passage in chapter 36. Here, however, this argument may be attached to those preceding, as though increasing their force. He had said that in this life people are excused from carnal intercourse, and therefore live a heavenly life like the angels, as Bernard says in Sermon 2 *To the Brothers*, and Cyprian in *On the Singularity of Clerics*. Therefore in the other life too human beings will be able to conduct themselves in the manner of angels, as Christ confirms and defines: “They shall be like angels.” LAC.]

If they are like angels by not marrying, because they also do not die, then surely they are likewise so by not succumbing to any similar necessity of bodily existence. [Col. 0885A]

[Note: [Col. 0884D] “Succumbing to a similar necessity.” Above he used the same verb, *succidere*, in the sense of *succumbere*. As regards the meaning of the sentence, Aeneas of Gaza expresses it elegantly in the *Theophrastus*. LAC.]

For angels also were once like human beings, eating, drinking, and extending their feet for washing: they had put on a human appearance while their own inward substance remained intact. Therefore, if angels, when made like human beings, undertook fleshly activities while remaining in the same spiritual substance, why may not human beings, when made like angels, enter upon a spiritual order while remaining in the same substance of flesh? Under their angelic clothing they will no more be subject to the customary functions of flesh than the angels under their human clothing were then subject to the customary functions of spirit.

[Note: [Col. 0885D] “They undertook fleshly activities.” Indeed, some affirm that even after the resurrection angels will sometimes undertake fleshly activities by assuming human form and functions, and that they will accompany human beings in the customary actions of human limbs: using the mouth to praise God, as I noted in the preceding chapter, and the feet to form choruses and perform the most chaste and solemn dances as a sign of exultation. So affirm Salas, volume I, on *Prima Secundae*, question 5, article 5, disputation 4, and Aegidius, book IV, question 22, articles 4 and 6. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0885C] Pamelius, the Paris edition, and François read *spiritibus*.]

Nor will human beings therefore fail to remain in the flesh merely because they do not remain in the customary functions of the flesh, since the angels did not fail to remain in the spirit merely because they did not remain in the customary functions of the spirit.

[Note: [Col. 0885C] “In” is absent from Rhenanus and Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0885C] The Paris edition and François read “flesh” instead of “spirit.”]

Finally, he did not say, “They shall be angels,” lest he deny that they are human beings; but “like angels,” so that he might preserve them as human beings. [Col. 0885B] He did not take away the substance to which he attributed the likeness.

## CHAPTER LXIII

The flesh, therefore, shall rise: all of it, the very same flesh, and the whole of it.

[Note: [Col. 0885D] “The flesh, therefore, shall rise.” By recapitulation he concludes the argument and leaves it ἀνατίρρητον (*anatírrhēton*, “indisputable”): the flesh, both of the blessed and of the reprobate, will one day rise, and the same flesh—not another—will rise. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0885C] Semler reads “rises.”]

Wherever it is, it is held on deposit with God through Jesus Christ, the most faithful trustee between God and human beings. He will restore God to man and man to God, spirit to flesh and flesh to spirit.

[Note: [Col. 0886D] “Trustee between God and human beings.” That is, mediator—μεσίτης (*mesítēs*)—for a trustee is placed midway between plaintiff and defendant in order to compose and settle the dispute. Law *Ab executore*, *Quorum appellationes*. LE PR.]

He has already joined the two in covenant within himself; he has matched bride to bridegroom and bridegroom to bride. For even if anyone should contend that the soul is the bride, the flesh will accompany the soul at least in the name of her dowry. The soul will not be a prostitute, to be received naked by the bridegroom: she has her document, her adornment, her own servant—the flesh—which will accompany her as her foster-sister.

[Note: [Col. 0885C–D] Rhenanus and Semler read *collatanea*; Pamelius, François, and the Paris edition read *collectaneam*.]

But the flesh is the bride, for in Christ Jesus it contracted the Spirit as its bridegroom by blood. [Col. 0885C]

[Note: [Col. 0886C] “Spirit” is absent from Pamelius, the Paris edition, and François.]

[Note: [Col. 0886C] Rhenanus and Semler read *passa* (“suffered”) instead of *pacta* (“contracted”).]

What you suppose to be its destruction, understand to be its withdrawal. The soul is not the only thing laid aside; the flesh too has meanwhile its own places of withdrawal—in waters, in fires, in birds, in beasts.

[Note: [Col. 0886C] Pamelius, the Paris edition, and François read “its own recesses.”]

When it appears to be dissolved into these, it is as though poured into vessels. Even if the vessels themselves perish, when it has flowed out of them too, it is absorbed through circuitous routes into its matrix, the earth, so that from the earth Adam may once more be presented, [Col. 0886A] destined to hear from the Lord: “Behold, Adam has become as one of us”—then truly master of the evil which he has escaped and of the good which he has attained.

[Note: [Col. 0886C] Rhenanus and Semler read “may be presented” rather than “presented again.”]

Why, soul, do you envy the flesh? No one is so near to you, no one whom after the Lord you ought to love so much; no one is more your brother than the flesh, which is born together with you even in God. You should rather have entreated resurrection for her, since it was through you, perhaps, that she sinned.

[Note: [Col. 0886D] “Why, soul, do you envy the flesh?” Here he addresses chiefly the Marcionites and Valentinians, who thought and spoke so badly of the flesh that it was as though their soul were affected by jealousy and envy of another. Hence he also adds: “But it is no wonder if you have hated her whose author you also rejected.” For Marcion rejected and hated God the Creator. LAC.]

But it is no wonder if you have hated her whose author you also rejected, and whose existence in Christ you have habitually either denied or altered.

[Note: [Col. 0886D] “Whose author you also rejected.” The Marcionites and others rejected God the Creator with such bitter hatred that they sometimes abstained from foods in detestation of the Creator’s works. LAC.]

In the same manner you corrupt, either by your pen or by your interpretation, the Word of God himself who became flesh, while also introducing the mysteries of the apocrypha—fables of blasphemy.

But God Almighty, pouring out his most provident grace against these devices of unbelief and perversity, has in these last days poured out from his Spirit upon all flesh, upon his servants and handmaids (Joel 2:28–29). He has both revived the struggling faith in the resurrection of the flesh and purified the ancient Scriptures from every obscurity of ambiguity by manifest lights of words and meanings. [Col. 0886B]

For because there had to be heresies, so that all the approved might be made manifest (1 Cor. 11:19), while those heresies could not have dared to arise without certain occasions in the Scriptures, the ancient Scriptures accordingly appear to have supplied them with certain materials—though the heresies can indeed be refuted by those very same writings.

[Note: [Col. 0886C] “Them” is absent from Semler and Oberthür.]

But because the Holy Spirit ought not to be restrained from overflowing also with utterances of such a kind as would scatter no seeds for the cunning devices of heretics, but would even tear up their old sods, he has therefore now dispelled all former ambiguities—and whatever things they choose to call parables—through the open and transparent proclamation of the whole mystery, by means of the new prophecy pouring forth from the Paraclete.

[Note: [Col. 0886D] The Paris edition and François read *necdum* (“not yet”) instead of *nec* (“not”).]

If you drink from his springs, you will be able to thirst for no doctrine; no fever of questions will burn you. By drinking in the resurrection of the flesh everywhere, you will be refreshed.

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIANUS ON THE VEILING OF VIRGINS (C)

## Preface

[Col. 0887] Among all Tertullian’s Montanist works there is scarcely any in which he employs greater force to defend the Montanists’ discipline against the charge of novelty than the treatise *On the Veiling of Virgins*. Hence it is rightly believed to have been composed near the beginning of the period he spent among the Montanists. [Col. 0887A]

The very learned Rémi Ceillier indeed considers *On the Veiling of Virgins* to be the latest of all his books, because Tertullian cites it in no other work, not even in *On the Crown*, where the subject itself, treated in chapter 14, appears to have required such a citation. But this conjecture proves nothing, because *On the Crown* was composed before *On the Veiling of Virgins*, while we do not perceive that Tertullian had any occasion to cite the latter work in his other writings.

However these matters may stand, it is at least evident that this little work was written during the schism, since it all too openly bears the character of Montanus. For presently, in chapter 1, he says: “Righteousness was at first in its rudimentary stages; thereafter, through the Law and the Prophets, it advanced into infancy; thereafter, through the Gospel, it grew fervent into youth; now, through the Paraclete, it is being ordered into maturity.” [Col. 0887B]

Tertullian was given the occasion to write this little book by the custom of certain churches, both in the West and in many other parts of Greece or the East, of veiling virgins in church, whereas in the African church most Christian virgins went about with uncovered heads. [Col. 0888A] Therefore, in this book, against the custom of his own country, where married women alone were veiled, he shows that custom cannot prescribe against truth. According to the observation of the distinguished Dupin, this is indeed true when dogma is in question, but not when the matter is one of discipline of little importance. LUMP.

## ARGUMENT

Tertullian, having, as it appears, been assailed with abuse and harassed as a heretic because of a certain dispute with the Greeks about the veiling of virgins, begins by inveighing against his assailants, who boasted of custom, and declares that custom cannot prescribe against truth.

He then asserts that the custom of veiling virgins is better; that it is not condemned in Holy Scripture but, on the contrary, commended by the Apostle, confirmed by the discipline of the Church, supported [Col. 0888B] by many examples of saints, and ratified by the dangers incurred by unveiled virgins themselves. EDD.
