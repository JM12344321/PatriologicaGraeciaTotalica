You now encounter heaven described by the word “shield” in those most ancient verses of Ennius, from Euripides’ *Iphigenia*, which the most Latin of authors, Varro, preserved for us around the middle of book VI of *On the Latin Language*:

> What time of night does it seem to be? In the loud-resounding  
> Shield of heaven the Wagon’s Pole rises above  
> The stars, driving aloft again  
> And again the journey of night.

“He wishes to indicate the advanced hour of night from the movement of the Wagon’s Pole,” says Varro. Accordingly, these two coins of the emperors Trajan and Alexander deserve inspection; upon them the Sun and Moon, together with the twelve signs of the zodiac, appear engraved upon the shield, as though upon heaven.

Finally, it is clear from this that the reading should not be *utique*, as Wouwer wishes and Rigault follows in his edition, but *ubique*, “everywhere,” as not only other manuscripts but also the Agobard manuscript exhibit it. Septimius says that we do not look up to that Sun painted by a human hand upon some tablet, but to God’s own finger in its own place and throughout every land, since it shines everywhere. Indeed, I say that *ibique*, “there,” should be read, as the very words of Lucian can show. I have found that in many respects Lucian appealed remarkably to our Septimius—excluding his impiety and blasphemies. Thus, on p. 904 of *On the Syrian Goddess*, Lucian gives the following reason why images of the Sun and Moon are not found there alongside the other gods represented in visible form: Λέγουσι, he says, τοῖσι μὲν ἄλλοισι θεοῖσι, ὅσιον ἔμμεναι ξόανα ποιεῖσθαι· οὐ γὰρ σφέων ἐμφανέα πάνσι τὰ εἲδεα. Ἡέλιος δὲ, καὶ σεληναίη πάμπαν ἐναργέες, καὶ σφέας πάντες ὁρεουσὶ, ποίη οὖν αἰτίη ξοανουργίης, τοῖσι ἐν τῷ ἠέρι φαινομένοισι·, that is: “They say that it is lawful to make images of the other gods, whose forms are not visible to everyone. But the Sun and Moon are entirely manifest, and there is no one who does not see them. Why, then, should I fashion an image of them when they appear in the air?”

Likewise, if we devote Sunday to rejoicing, for a reason far removed from worship of the Sun [Note: [Col. 0371B] **From worship of the Sun.** Restore the text thus, not “the worship of S.”, on the authority of the excellent Fulda manuscript. “From worship” is used just as above “from justice,” “from wisdom,” and very often in our author, in the sense of “because of wisdom” or “on account of justice.” Christians were not reluctant to retain this name, though borrowed from the pagans, in the designation of that day, since, as Ambrose writes in Sermon LXI, “on that day the Savior, like the rising Sun, scattered the darkness of the underworld and shone forth with the light of the resurrection.” HAV.], we rank second to those [Note: [Col. 0371C] **We rank second.** Septimius says that it cannot be demonstrated that we worship the Sun, as the Persians do, from the fact that a Christian turns toward the east in the posture of prayer—a practice maintained in the early Church because Christ is compared to the rising Sun, Malachi IV.2, and because it was also believed that on the final day he would appear from there in judgment. Nor can it be demonstrated from the fact that the day which is festive for us was consecrated to the Sun among the nations. They may foolishly have suspected this, just as Tacitus fell into a humorous error when thinking about Saturn, *Histories* V, chapter 4: “Others say that this honor is paid to Saturn, either because the Idaeans who transmitted the beginnings of their religion—whom we have learned were expelled together with Saturn and became the founders of the nation—or because, among the seven stars by which mortals are governed, the star of Saturn moves in the highest orbit and possesses the greatest power, and because most heavenly bodies complete their influence and course through the number seven.”

What now follows—“we rank second to those who devote Saturn’s day,” etc.—is likewise splendidly sprinkled with satirical salt. For he touches here not upon the Jews, as commentators commonly persuaded themselves, but upon the pagans themselves. They had so degenerated from their ancestral institutions that there was no nation from whose rites they had not plucked some portion and made it their own, as Gothofredus beautifully demonstrates in his highly learned notes on Tertullian’s book *To the Nations*. I am indignant that this truly learned man is not generally valued more highly.

It will be worthwhile to append here the passage from *To the Nations*, since our author must be understood from it. In book I, chapter XIII, he says: “It is certainly you who have also received the Sun into the register of the seven days. From among their days you selected the day on which you either abstain from bathing, postpone it until evening, or devote yourselves to leisure and feasting. You do this by deviating from your own religions toward alien ones. For the Jews have feast days, Sabbaths, the pure supper, and the Jewish rites of lamps, fasts accompanied by unleavened bread, and prayers upon the shore—all of which are certainly alien to your gods. Therefore, to return from my digression: you who reproach us with the Sun and its day, acknowledge the proximity. We are not far removed from your Saturn and your Sabbaths.” Thus what he says there—“acknowledge the proximity,” etc.—is expressed here as “we rank second to them.”
