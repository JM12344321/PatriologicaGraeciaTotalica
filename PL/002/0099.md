After the ancient examples of the original persons, let us likewise pass to the ancient documents of the legal Scriptures, so that we may review in order [Note: [Col. 0937D] “Of all our equipment.” He means the Scriptures of both Instruments, or Testaments. RIG.] all our equipment. And since certain people sometimes say that they have nothing to do with the Law—which Christ did not abolish but fulfilled—while at other times they seize upon whatever parts of the Law they please, we too plainly say that the Law has passed away in such a manner that its burdens, which, according to the judgment of the apostles, not even the Fathers were willing to bear, have been remitted; but those provisions that concern righteousness not only remain preserved but have even been enlarged, so that our righteousness may, of course, exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees [Col. 0937C]. If righteousness, then chastity as well.

If, therefore, because the Law commands a man to take in marriage the wife of his brother who has died childless, in order to raise up offspring for his brother, and because this can happen repeatedly to a single person, as in that cunning question of the Sadducees, they consequently think that repeated marriages of other kinds are also permitted, they must first understand the rationale of that commandment. They will then know that, since its rationale has now ceased, it belongs among those provisions of the Law that have been annulled.

It was necessary to succeed to the marriage of a brother who had died childless. First, because that ancient blessing still had to run its course: “Increase and abound” (Gen. I, 28). Next, because the offenses of fathers were still exacted from their children. Third, because eunuchs and the sterile were regarded as dishonorable [Col. 0937D]. Accordingly, lest those who had died childless—not through any fault of nature, but because death had overtaken them—should on that account be judged accursed [Col. 0938A], a substitute and, as it were, posthumous offspring was procured for them from their own stock.

But when the end of the ages annulled “Increase and abound,” with the apostle declaring (I Cor. VII, 29), “It remains that even those who have wives should be as though they had none, because the time has been shortened”; and when “The sour grape eaten by the fathers has ceased to set the children’s teeth on edge, for each person shall die in his own offense” (Jer. XXXI, 29); and when eunuchs not only ceased to bear disgrace but even merited grace, being invited into the kingdoms of heaven—then, once the law of succeeding to a brother’s marriage had been buried, its contrary, the rule of not succeeding to a brother’s marriage, took its place. And thus, as we said before [Note: [Col. 0937D] “As we say beforehand,” Venetian edition.], a provision that has ceased to be valid because its rationale has ceased cannot furnish an argument for another matter [Col. 0938B].

Therefore, after her husband has died, a wife will not marry—for, if she does marry, she will of course marry a brother. For we are all brothers, and she who intends to marry must marry “in the Lord,” that is, not a gentile but a brother, because the ancient Law also forbids marriage with foreigners. But since Leviticus also provides, “Whoever takes his brother’s wife—it is uncleanness, a shameful thing—shall die childless” [Note: [Col. 0938D] “They shall die,” Frankfort and Venetian editions.] (Lev. XX, 31), there can be no doubt that, while he is forbidden to marry again, she too is forbidden, since she may marry none but a brother.

How this will agree with the apostle and with the Law, which he does not oppose altogether, will be shown when we come to his epistle. Meanwhile, so far as the Law is concerned, its arguments favor us more. Indeed, the same Law forbids priests to marry again (Lev. XXXI, 14). [Note: [Col. 0937D] “A priest’s daughter also,” etc. Blessed Jerome puts it more clearly: “A priest’s widow is commanded to remain in her father’s house and not to know a second marriage.” But [Col. 0938D] the Author follows the words of Scripture in Lev. XXII. PAM.] It also commands a priest’s daughter [Col. 0938C], if widowed or divorced and without offspring, to return to her father’s house and be sustained from his bread. It says “if she has no offspring” not so that, if she does have offspring, she may marry again—for how much less will she marry if she has children!—but so that, if she has a son, she may be supported by him rather than by her father, and the son may thereby fulfill God’s commandment, “Honor father and mother” (Lev. XXXI, 13).

But Jesus, the high and great priest of the Father, clothing us with what is his own—for “those who are baptized into Christ have put on Christ” (Gal. III, 27)—[Note: [Col. 0938D] “He made us priests to God his Father.” On the meaning of this passage and the words that follow, see the preceding book, chap. 7. PAM.] made us priests to God his Father, according to John (Apoc. I, 6). For he also calls back that young man who was hastening to his father’s funeral precisely to show that we are called by him to be priests, whom the Law forbade to attend the burial of their parents: [Note: [Col. 0938D] “Over any dead soul,” he says. Here too he compares the evangelical law with the Law of Moses, because the high priest is forbidden to bury his father, Lev. XXI. Therefore, in the Author as well, one must understand the description given of Jesus shortly before: “high priest,” or “great priest”; otherwise other priests were permitted to approach the body of a father, mother, brother, and virgin sister, as is stated at the beginning [Col. 0939D] of the same chapter. Hence blessed Jerome likewise cites this of the high priest in Epistle 25, on the death of Blaesilla, to Paula. PAM.] “The priest shall not approach any dead soul, and he shall not defile himself even for his father or his mother” [Col. 0938D] (Lev. XXII, 13).

Must we, then, observe this prohibition too [Col. 0939A]? Certainly not. For our one Father, God, lives, as does our mother, the Church; nor are we ourselves dead, since we live to God; nor do we bury the dead, because they too live in Christ. Yet we are certainly priests called by Christ, and therefore debtors to monogamy under the ancient Law of God, which at that time prefigured us in its priests.

## CHAPTER VIII

Now, turning to the law that is properly our own, that is, the Gospel, by what examples are we received! When we come to its pronouncements, behold, immediately, as though upon the threshold, two [Note: [Col. 0939D] “Two,” another reading.] presiding figures of Christian holiness meet us: monogamy and continence—the one chaste in Zechariah the priest, the other inviolate in John the Forerunner; the one propitiating God, the other proclaiming Christ; the one wholly representing the priest, the other presenting more [Col. 0939B] than a prophet [Note: [Col. 0939D] “Prophesying,” Rhenanus.], namely, the man who not only proclaimed Christ or pointed him out face to face, but even baptized him. For what flesh could more worthily initiate (that is, baptize) the Lord’s body [Note: [Col. 0939D] *initiarit*, Leopold, against all editions.] than flesh of the same kind as that which conceived and bore it?

[Note: [Col. 0939D] “And Christ indeed was brought forth by a virgin who was to marry once after giving birth.” Jerome contradicted Helvidius when the latter asserted this opinion of Tertullian: “Of Tertullian,” he says, “I say nothing more than that he was not a man of the Church.” By these words he indicates that Septimius wrote certain things by no means consonant with Catholic teaching. RIG.] And Christ indeed was brought forth by a virgin who was to marry once after giving birth, so that both titles of holiness might be entered to Christ’s account through a mother who was both virgin and once-married.

But when the infant is presented in the temple, who takes him into his hands? Who is the first to recognize him in the Spirit? A righteous and circumspect man, and certainly [Note: [Col. 0939D] “Each,” others.] not twice-married, lest Christ immediately be proclaimed more worthily [Note: [Col. 0940D] “More unworthily,” Frankfort and Oberthür.] by a woman—an aged widow, once-married and dedicated to the temple, who sufficiently prefigured in her own person what sort of women ought to cling to the spiritual temple, that is, the Church.

The infant Lord, having experienced such witnesses [Col. 0939C], had no others when grown. Peter alone I find to have been married, through the mention of his mother-in-law; I presume him once-married, on account of the Church built upon him, which was to establish every rank of its order from among the once-married. Since I do not find the others to have been husbands, I must understand them either as eunuchs or as continent.

For even if among the Greeks women and wives are designated by a common word, as a convenient matter of usage—although there is, in fact, a word proper to wives—we will not therefore interpret Paul as though he were showing that the apostles had wives. If he had been discussing marriages—which he does later, where he could more appropriately have named some apostle as an example—he might rightly seem to say [Col. 0939D], “Do we not have the right to take wives about with us [Col. 0940A], as do the other apostles and Cephas?” (I Cor. IX). But when he adds statements showing his abstinence from material support, saying, “Do we not have the right to eat and drink?” he does not indicate wives who were taken about by the apostles—for those who have no wives nevertheless have the right to eat and drink—but simply women who, under the same arrangement as those who accompanied the Lord, ministered to them.

Again, if Christ reproves the scribes and Pharisees who sit in Moses’ chair but do not do what they teach, how could he place upon his own chair [Note: [Col. 0940D] “Had placed,” Frankfort.] men who remembered to prescribe holiness of the flesh rather than also to practice it, when he had impressed [Note: [Col. 0940D] “Might impress,” Frankfort.] and taught by every means that it must both be taught and practiced: first by his own example, then [Note: [Col. 0940D] “Thereafter,” Latin edition.] by his other arguments [Col. 0940B]? He says that the kingdom of heaven belongs to children (Matt. XVIII, 3; XXIX, 14); he makes others into children after marriage, as their fellows; he summons us to the simplicity of the dove (Matt. XXIX, 16), a bird not only innocent but also chaste, [Note: [Col. 0939D] “One male knows one female.” He perhaps takes this from Pliny, book X, chap. 34, but careful historians [Col. 0940D] of nature attest that it is false. LE PR.] since one male knows only one female. He denies that the Samaritan woman’s man is her husband, [Note: [Col. 0940D] “So as to show that a husband multiplied in number is an adulterer.” This belongs to the heresy of Montanus; hence he also twists Christ’s words to the Samaritan woman in John IV so that they refer to second marriages, although Christ is speaking of the man who was attached to her not in marriage but in fornication, as blessed Augustine and the Greek commentaries interpret it. Although blessed Jerome said something similar in book I *Against Jovinian*, he later interprets it in his *Apology to Pammachius* as referring not to second—or indeed sixth—marriages, but to prostitution. PAM.] so as to show that a husband multiplied in number is an adulterer (John IV, 17). In the revelation of his glory, out of all the saints and prophets, he prefers to have Moses and Elijah with him, the one once-married, the other a eunuch! For Elijah was none other than John, who came in the power and spirit of Elijah.

And though he was “a gluttonous man and a drinker,” one who frequented luncheons and dinners with publicans and sinners (Matt. X; Luke XV, 13, 19), he dines on only one occasion at a single wedding, although there were surely many people marrying. For he wished to celebrate weddings as many times as he wished them to occur [Col. 0940C].

## CHAPTER IX

But let these arguments be judged forced conjectures, unless they are also supported by the pronouncements the Lord uttered in his reconsideration of divorce, which, though once permitted, he now forbids: first, because “from the beginning it was not so,” just as there was no plurality of marriage; then, because “what God has joined, man shall not separate” (Matt. XIX, 6, 8)—that is, lest man act against the Lord. For he alone who joined will also separate; but he will separate not through the hardness of divorce, which he reproaches and restrains, but through the obligation of death. Indeed, not one of two sparrows falls to the ground apart from the Father’s will (Matt. X, 29).

Therefore, if man is not to separate by divorce those whom God [Col. 0940D] has joined, it is equally consistent [Col. 0941A] that man should not join by marriage those whom God has separated by death. For in joining what has been separated he would likewise act against God’s will, just as if he had separated what was joined. So much for not destroying God’s will and for restoring the form of the beginning.

Furthermore, another rationale concurs—or rather, not another, but the very one that established the form of the beginning and moved God’s will to forbid divorce: “Because whoever dismisses his wife, except on account of adultery, causes her to commit adultery”; and, “He who marries a woman dismissed by her husband certainly commits adultery” (Matt. X, 22). For if a divorced woman cannot lawfully marry [Note: [Col. 0941D] “Cannot even lawfully marry,” Frankfort. Rigault: *non abest*, Paris.], and if she commits some such act without the name of marriage, how does she incur the charge of adultery, since adultery is an offense committed within marriage? God has judged differently from men: he pronounces the admission of another man, whether through marriage [Col. 0941B] or promiscuously, to be adultery altogether [Note: [Col. 0941D] “By God” is added by Rhenanus, Pamelius, Semler, and Leopold; omitted by Rigault and the Venetian edition.].

Let us therefore see what marriage is before God, and thus we shall likewise recognize what adultery is. Marriage exists when God joins two into one flesh, or, finding them joined in that same flesh, seals the union. [Note: [Col. 0941D] “Adultery is,” etc. Here too he includes fornication under the name of adultery, like the Greeks, who understand it under the name *μοιχείας* (*moicheias*, “adultery”). LE PR.] Adultery exists when, after two have in any manner been separated, another—indeed, an alien—flesh is mingled with one of them, of which it cannot be said, “This is flesh of my flesh and bone of my bones” (Gen. II, 23). For this was done and pronounced once; as at the beginning, so also now, it cannot apply to another flesh.

You will therefore say in vain that God does not wish a divorced woman to be joined to another man while her husband is alive, as though he wished it when the husband was dead. For if she is not bound to him when he is dead, then neither is she bound to him while he lives. Whether divorce or death dissolves the marriage [Col. 0941C], she will not be bound to the man from whom that by which she was bound has been severed. Thus it makes no difference [Note: [Col. 0941D] “To whom, then, will she be bound? It makes no difference to God whether the husband is living,” etc., Semler and Oberthür. Rhenanus: “It makes no difference at all whether”; Leopold: *an*.] whether she marries while her husband is alive or dead. For she sins not against him but against herself. “Every offense a person commits is outside the body; but one who commits adultery sins against his own body” (I Cor. VI, 18).

Now, as we established above, the one who commits adultery is he who mingles another flesh with himself over and above that former flesh which God either joined in the two persons or found already joined. Therefore he abolished divorce, which did not exist from the beginning, in order to safeguard what did exist from the beginning: the perseverance of two in one flesh, so that neither necessity nor occasion might introduce a third embodiment in flesh. He permits divorce for the sole cause in which the very thing being guarded against may already have occurred.

So completely was divorce absent from the beginning [Note: [Col. 0941D] “As among the Romans.” Servius Sulpicius, says Rhenanus in the book he composed *On Dowries*, wrote that legal guarantees concerning a wife’s property were first deemed necessary when Spurius Carvilius, surnamed Ruga, a nobleman, divorced his wife because, owing to a bodily defect, children could not be begotten from her. In the 523rd year from the founding of the City, when Marcus Atilius and Publius Valerius were consuls. PAM.] that among the Romans this kind of hardness is recorded as having been committed only after the six-hundredth year [Col. 0942A] from the founding of the City. But they commit adultery even without divorcing; [Note: [Col. 0942D] “As for us, even if we divorce, we shall not be permitted even to marry.” “Us,” that is, the Paracletans. For at the beginning of book I *To His Wife*, he indicates that among the Psychics another marriage after divorce was permitted. RIG.] as for us, even if we divorce, we shall not be permitted even to marry.

## CHAPTER X

I already see that from here we are being summoned to the apostle. To perceive his meaning more easily, it must first be impressed all the more insistently that women are all the more bound to a deceased husband, so that they may not admit another man.

Let us reflect that divorce either arises from discord or produces discord, whereas death occurs by the law of God, not through a human offense, and that it is a debt owed by all, even by the unmarried. Therefore, if a divorced woman, who through discord, anger, hatred, and the causes of these—injury, insult, or any grievance whatever—has been separated in both soul and body, remains bound to an enemy, not to call him a husband, how much more will the woman who, by neither her own fault nor her husband’s, but by the operation of the Lord’s law, has not been separated from marriage but merely left behind, still belong to him even when dead, since she owes concord to him even in death [Col. 0942B]!

She did not hear divorce pronounced by him; she did not depart from him; she did not write him a bill of divorce; she remains with him; she retains the man whom she did not wish to lose. [Note: [Col. 0942D] “She has with her the license of the mind, which represents to a person through imaginary enjoyment all the things he does not possess.” How licentiously Septimius speaks here!—even if the duties he describes in the second book *To His Wife* are meant. RIG.] She has with her the license of the mind, which represents to a person through imaginary enjoyment all the things he does not possess.

Finally, I ask the woman herself: “Tell me, sister, did you send your husband ahead in peace?” What will she answer? “In discord,” perhaps? Then she is bound all the more to the man with whom she has a case before God. She who remains bound has not departed. [Note: [Col. 0942D] “But in peace.” This answers what preceded: “In discord?” RIG.] “But in peace.” Then she must necessarily persevere in that peace with the man whom she can no longer divorce; nor could she have married another even if she had been able to divorce him [Col. 0942C].

Indeed, she also prays for his soul, asks refreshment for him in the meantime [Note: [Col. 0942D] “Let her ask, let her offer,” Paris; “for she asks,” Rhenanus and Semler.], asks fellowship with him in the first resurrection, and makes an offering on the anniversaries of his falling asleep. For if she does not do these things, she has truly divorced him, so far as lies within her; and that all the more unjustly, insofar as she did it in the only way she could because she could not do it otherwise; and all the more unworthily, insofar as it is now more unworthy, if because he did not deserve it. [Note: [Col. 0942D] Franciscus Junius emends this corrupt passage as follows: “all the more unjustly, because she could not; and all the more unworthily, because he did not deserve it.”]

Or shall we perhaps be nothing after death? According to some Epicurus, and not according to Christ? But if we believe in the resurrection of the dead, we shall surely remain bound to those with whom we are to rise, each required to render an account concerning the other. If, however, in that age “they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but will be equal to angels” (Matt. XXII, 30), one will not therefore cease to be bound to spouses [Col. 0943A] who have died merely because marriage will not be restored. On the contrary, we shall be bound all the more, because we are destined for a better condition: rising again into spiritual fellowship and recognizing both ourselves and those who are ours.

Otherwise, how shall we sing thanks to God forever if the sense and memory of this debt do not remain in us—if our substance is reconstituted, but not our consciousness? Therefore, when we are with God, we shall be together, since all of us are with the one God—even though the reward varies, even though there are many dwellings with the same Father (John XIV, 2)—as workers receiving the one denarius of the same reward, that is, eternal life. In that life God will be even less likely to separate those whom he has joined than he is in this lesser life to permit their separation.

Since these things are so, how will a woman be available to another man when she remains occupied by her own even in the future? We are speaking to both sexes, although the discourse is addressed to one [Col. 0943B], because one rule governs both. Will she have one man in the spirit, another in the flesh? This will be adultery: one woman’s consciousness directed toward two men. If one has been separated from the flesh but remains in the heart [Note: [Col. 0943C] “They remain,” Frankfort.], there—where even thought, without intercourse of the flesh, first completes adultery [Note: [Col. 0943C] “Is completed,” Rhenanus.] through desire, [Note: [Col. 0943D] “And marriage through the will,” etc. This confirms the common opinion of the Fathers and jurists that even without carnal intercourse it is the will, or consent, that constitutes marriage. Thus the marriage of the Blessed Virgin and Joseph is also confirmed in Augustine, book I *On Marriage and Concupiscence*, chap. 11; and blessed Chrysostom, homily 32 of the *Imperfect Work*, and blessed Ambrose, *On the Institution of Virgins*, chap. 16, expressly affirm it as well. Yet what the Author asserted before does not follow from it. PAM.] and marriage through the will—he is still her husband, [Note: [Col. 0943D] “Possessing that very thing through which he became a husband, namely the mind.” For, as the jurists say, consent, not intercourse, makes a marriage. RIG.] possessing that very thing through which he became a husband, namely the mind. If another man also dwells therein, that will be a crime. The former, moreover, has not been excluded merely because he has departed [Note: [Col. 0943D] “Withdrawn,” Rhenanus and Semler.] from the baser commerce of the flesh. He is a more honorable husband now that he has been made purer.

## CHAPTER XI

So, in order that you may marry “in the Lord” [Note: [Col. 0944C] “In God,” Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür.] according to the Law and the apostle—if indeed you care even about this—what sort of woman are you, requesting such a marriage [Col. 0943C] from those who are not permitted to have it: from a once-married bishop, [Note: [Col. 0943D] “From presbyters and deacons of the same sacrament.” That is, once-married. LE PR.] from presbyters and deacons bound by the same sacrament, and from widows whose way of life you have rejected in your own case?

[Note: [Col. 0944D] “And they, plainly, will give husbands and wives just as they give morsels”—that is, to everyone who asks. As though the saying “Give to everyone who asks you” had also been spoken about marriage, although in the book *On Baptism* he says that it has its own heading pertaining to almsgiving. Thus, with decorous words, he enfolds an indecent meaning, just as we read in the Proverbs of Solomon: “Such is the way of an adulterous woman: she eats and wipes her mouth.” LE PR.] And they, plainly, will give husbands and wives just as they give morsels. For this, among them, is the meaning of “You shall give to everyone who asks you.” And they will join you in the virgin Church, the one bride of the one Christ. And you will pray for your husbands, the new and the old. Choose to which of them you will present yourself as an adulteress. To both, I think.

But if you were wise, you would be silent concerning the dead husband. Let your silence be a bill of divorce written to him with another man’s [Note: [Col. 0944D] “Another man’s bridal property.” The Greeks call bridal goods brought by the woman in addition to the stipulated dowry *ἐξωπρόικα* (*exōproika*) and *παράφερνα* (*parapherna*). LE PR.] bridal property. In this way you will earn a new husband, if you forget the old one. You ought rather to please the man for whose sake you preferred not to please God [Col. 0944A].

The Psychics maintain that the apostle either approved these things or gave them no consideration at all when he wrote: “A woman is bound for as long as her husband lives; but if he has died, she is free: let her marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord” (I Cor. VII, 39). From this passage they defend the license of a second marriage—indeed, of any number, if a second is allowed; for that which has once ceased to be singular is subject to every number.

But the sense in which the apostle wrote will become clear if it is first established that he did not write in the sense employed by the Psychics. This will be established if one recalls the matters inconsistent with this interpretation in his teaching, his will, and Paul’s own personal discipline. For if he permits second marriages [Note: [Col. 0944C] “Admits,” another reading.], which did not exist from the beginning, how does he affirm that all things are gathered up again from the beginning [Col. 0944B] in Christ? If he wishes us to repeat marriages, how does he maintain that our offspring in Isaac derives from a once-married father? How does he arrange the entire order of the Church from among the once-married, unless this discipline first governs the laity, from whom the order of the Church is recruited? How, while people are still in marriage, does he call them away from the fruit of marriage, declaring that time has been shortened, if he summons those released from marriage by death back into marriage?

If these things are inconsistent with the passage under discussion, it will be established, as we said, that he did not write it in the sense employed by the Psychics. For it is easier that this one passage should have some rationale consonant with the others than that the apostle should appear to have taught mutually contradictory things. We shall be able to recognize that rationale in the subject matter itself.

What subject matter led the apostle [Col. 0944C] to write such things? The novice condition of a new Church only just coming into being—a Church that he was, of course, nourishing with milk, not yet with the solid food of stronger doctrine (I Cor. III). So great was that infancy of faith that they still did not know what they ought to do concerning the necessities of the flesh and sex. We understand the particular forms of their uncertainty from his replies, when he says: “Now concerning the matters about which you wrote: it is good for a man not to touch a woman; [Note: [Col. 0944D] “But because of fornications.” He translated literally the Greek *διὰ δὲ τὰς πορνείας* (*dia de tas porneias*, “but because of fornications”); in this the Author was also imitated by blessed Jerome three or four times in book I *Against Jovinian*. PAM.] but because of fornications, let each man have his own wife” (I Cor. VII). He shows that there were people whom faith had found already married, but who feared that from then on they might not be permitted to make use of their marriage because they had come to believe in the holy flesh of Christ.

And yet he grants this by way of permission [Col. 0945A], not command—that is, indulgently, not by prescribing that it be done. Rather, he preferred all people to be what he himself was. Likewise, in replying concerning divorce, he shows that some had been considering that matter too, especially because they thought they ought not to remain in marriages with gentiles after coming to faith.

They also asked for counsel concerning virgins—for there was no commandment of the Lord—that it was good for a person to remain as he was, that is, as faith had found him. “Are you bound [Note: [Col. 0945C] “For,” Rhenanus and Semler.] to a wife? Do not seek release. Are you released from a wife? Do not seek a wife. But if you take a wife, you have not sinned,” because for a man released from a wife before coming to faith, a wife taken after coming to faith will not be counted as a second wife: she is his first after faith. For even our life itself is reckoned from faith.

But here he says that he is sparing those who, because of the constraints of the times, refuse the impediments to marriage; otherwise distress of the flesh would follow them [Col. 0945B]. Rather, they should be concerned about meriting the Lord’s favor rather than about a husband [Note: [Col. 0945C] “Bringing forth,” Frankfort.]. And [Note: [Col. 0945C] “And” is absent from the Frankfort edition.] thus he retracts what he permitted.

Accordingly, in the very same chapter in which he determines that each person ought to remain in the calling in which he was called, he adds: “A woman is bound for as long as her husband lives; but if he has fallen asleep, she is free; let her marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord” (I Cor. VII, 39). From this too [Note: [Col. 0945C] “This,” Rhenanus.] he shows that the woman must be understood as one who was herself found already released from a husband, just as the man was released from a wife—the release having occurred through death, of course, not through divorce, because he would not permit divorced persons to marry, contrary to the preceding commandment [Col. 0945C].

Thus, if the woman marries, she will not sin [Col. 0946A], because this man will not be counted as a second husband either, since [Note: [Col. 0945C] “Who,” Rigault and Venetian edition.] he is her first from the time of faith. That this is so is shown by the fact that he added, “Only in the Lord,” because he was dealing with a woman who had had a gentile husband and, after losing him, had come to faith, lest she presume that even after faith she could marry a gentile—although the Psychics do not care about this either [Note: [Col. 0946C] “Lest” is absent from Rhenanus.].

[Note: [Col. 0945C] “Let us know plainly that it does not so read in the authentic Greek.” Since every document of both the Old and New Scripture was subsequently translated into the Latin language, the water of the fountain should be believed much purer than that of the streams, as Jerome says *Against Helvidius*. Accordingly, in the book *On the Prescription of Heretics*, he appeals to the apostolic churches, among which, he says, the authentic letters of the apostles are read aloud, sounding their voice and presenting their countenance. But Gregory of Nyssa long ago observed, in Oration II *On the Lord’s Resurrection*, that not all editions of that authentic Greek were entirely accurate. For he says that *ἐν τοῖς ἀκριβεστέροις ἀντιγράφοις* (*en tois akribesterois antigraphois*, “in the more accurate copies”) the Gospel [Col. 0945D] of Mark does not continue beyond the words *ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ* (*ephobounto gar*, “for they were afraid”). No such copy is yet known to survive today, except for a manuscript preserved in the royal library and written in rather large letters, where, after *ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ*, a sign marking the end is appended. Immediately afterward, in the same scribe’s hand, it is said that in certain books the following is found: *Πάντα δὲ παρηγγελμένα τοῖς περὶ τὸν Πέτρον συντόμως ἐξήγγειλον. Μετὰ δὲ ταῦτα καὶ αὐτὸς οἷς ἀπὸ ἀνατολῆς καὶ ἄχρι δύσεως ἐξαπέστειλεν δι’ αὐτῶν τὸ ἱερὸν καὶ ἄφθαρτον κήρυγμα τῆς ἀϊδίου σωτηρίας* (“And all that had been commanded they briefly reported to those around Peter. After these things, Jesus himself also sent out through them, from east even to west, the sacred and incorruptible proclamation of eternal salvation”). Then another note by the scribe says that in some manuscripts, after *ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ*, there also follows *Ἀναστὰς δὲ πρωῒ* (*Anastas de prōi*, “Now, having risen early”) and the rest found in the commonly printed editions. RIG.]

Let us know plainly that it does not so read in the authentic Greek. [Note: [Col. 0945D] “In the authentic Greek”—in the Greek truth, in the Greek original. Jerome, Preface to the Gospels. Augustine, book II *On Christian Doctrine*, chap. 13: “There is no doubt that, if anything wavers amid the variations of the Latin copies, the books of the New Testament ought to yield to the Greek, especially those found among the more learned and diligent churches.” And in book II, chap. 6, *Against Faustus the Manichaean*, where [Col. 0946C] he cites an emended Latin codex and an emended Greek codex of Paul’s epistles, he calls the latter “authentic,” meaning more worthy of authority, whether among Greek or Latin copies. Nor did Augustine shrink from examining several manuscripts, as he himself testifies on Psalm LXVII: “Several Latin manuscripts,” he says, “and especially the Greek, have the verses divided in this manner”; and shortly afterward: “This division, confirmed by the agreement of many manuscripts, and those of greater authority, must undoubtedly be preferred,” etc. The same author on that Psalm: “Not all manuscripts contain this repetition, and the more careful ones mark it beforehand with an appended asterisk.” RIG.] It passed into use [Note: [Col. 0946C] “By the transposition of two syllables.” In that “authentic” text of his he seems to have read *ἐὰν κοιμᾷ δέ* (*ean koima de*), “but if he sleeps,” “if he is dead”—the husband, that is, before the wife was called to faith. By a transposition of two syllables, *ἐὰν κοιμᾷ δέ* supposedly became *ἐὰν κοιμηθῇ* (*ean koimēthēi*), “if he shall have fallen asleep,” “if he shall have died.” “This also has the force of a future,” says Septimius, as though the apostle’s meaning extended even to the case in which the husband happened to die after the wife had received the faith, and as though in that case too the wife were permitted to marry another man. Yet in those extremely ancient parchments that learned men long ago praised from the most ample library of the illustrious Puteanus, it is written most explicitly: *ἐὰν δὲ κοιμήθη ὁ ἀνὴρ αὐτῆς* (*ean de koimēthē ho anēr autēs*), which is also rendered there in Latin, “But if her husband has received his falling asleep.” This form of expression certainly seems to signify a Christian husband more than a gentile one. RIG.] through either the cunning or the innocent transposition of two syllables [Note: [Col. 0946C] “Emergence,” Semler.]: “If her husband shall have fallen asleep” [Note: [Col. 0946C] Rhenanus and Semler insert “however.”], as though it referred to the future and therefore seemed to concern a woman who had lost her husband after already coming to faith.

If this were indeed so, a license extended without limit would give her a husband as many times as one was lost, without any modesty in marrying—even the sort of modesty customary among gentiles. Yet even if it did refer to the future—“If anyone’s husband shall have died” [Col. 0946B]—the future would apply just as much to a woman whose husband was going to die before she came to faith. Take whichever reading you please [Note: [Col. 0946C] “Although,” Semler and Pamelius.], provided you do not overturn the rest.

For since the following pronouncements also correspond: “Were you called as a slave? Do not concern yourself”; [Note: [Col. 0945D] “Were you called uncircumcised?”—“in uncircumcision,” *ἐν ἀκροβυστίᾳ* (*en akrobystia*). RIG.] “Were you called uncircumcised? Do not be circumcised”; “Were you called circumcised? [Note: [Col. 0946D] “Do not draw it over”—the foreskin, that is. Juvenal, Satire 6, used the expression “to draw over foreskins.” RIG.] Do not draw it over”; and since these are matched by, “Are you bound to a wife? Do not seek release. Are you released from a wife? Do not seek a wife,” it is sufficiently clear that these statements concern people who, in their new and recent calling, were consulting him about the conditions in which faith had found them.

This, then, will be the interpretation of that passage, to be tested as to whether it agrees with the time and the occasion; with both the preceding examples and arguments [Col. 0946C] and the subsequent pronouncements and meanings [Col. 0947A]; and above all, with the apostle’s own counsel and personal rule. For nothing must be guarded more carefully than that a person not be found inconsistent with himself [Note: [Col. 0947C] “A person” is absent from Semler.].
