RIG.]. For God already knew the man whom he had reckoned righteous. Abraham both heard patiently so grave a command—one whose completion did not please the Lord—and, if God had wished it, would have fulfilled it. Deservedly, therefore, was he blessed, because he was also faithful; deservedly faithful, because also patient.

Thus faith, illuminated by patience, when it was sown among the nations [Col. 1259C] through the seed of Abraham, which is Christ (Gal. 3), and [Col. 1260A] brought grace in addition to the law, placed patience, its assistant, at the head of the law that was to be enlarged and fulfilled, because patience alone had formerly been lacking from the teaching of righteousness.

For in former times they demanded an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth, and repaid evil with evil at interest (Exod. 21; Deut. 19). Patience was not yet upon the earth, because faith was not yet here either. Meanwhile, of course, impatience took advantage of the law’s opportunities. This was easy while the Lord and teacher of patience was absent [Note: [Col. 1259D] “And teacher.” Ignatius the Martyr, in his letter to the Smyrnaeans, says that the faithful were instructed by the Father through the Son: Πατέρα διὰ αὐτοῦ ὑμᾶς σοφίσαντα, “the Father who made you wise through him.” Gregory of Nyssa, in his work *On the Perfect Form of the Christian*, speaking of Christ: ὁ τῷ σῷ βίῳ δι’ ἑαυτοῦ νομοθετῶν τὸ μακρόθυμον, “he who through himself prescribed patience as the law for your life.” LAC.].

After he arrived [Note: [Col. 1260C] “After he arrived.” I believe Jerome had the same matter in view in his letter to Marcella, where he says: “He who becomes angry judges himself not to be a Christian.” Why? Because Christ and the new law have now removed anger, which formerly was not so strictly forbidden. Cyprian likewise said: “Patience itself directs our conduct, so that we may keep to the way of Christ while walking by means of his endurance.” LAC.] and shaped the grace of faith with patience, it was no longer permitted even to provoke another by a word, nor even to say “Fool!” without danger of judgment. Anger was prohibited, tempers restrained [Note: [Col. 1259C] Latinus and Junius read “extinguished.”], the insolent hand checked, and the tongue’s poison removed. The law gained more than it lost, when Christ said (Matt. 5:44): “Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, and pray for your persecutors, [Col. 1260B] that you may be children of your heavenly Father.”

You see what Father patience acquires for us. In this principal command the whole discipline of patience is gathered up, since we are not permitted to do evil even where it would be deserved [Note: [Col. 1260C] The Franeker edition reads “of the tongue”; Pamelius, on manuscript authority, reads “of faith,” and Junius approves.] [Note: [Col. 1260D] “We are not permitted to do evil even where it would be deserved.” This is the reading of the oldest copies, and there can be no doubt that Tertullian wrote it thus, as Rhenanus also published and very correctly explained it. Under the law of the Gospel it is not permitted to return evil for evil or to do evil to those who have deserved evil, though they might otherwise deserve retaliation and evil might therefore be done to them “deservedly,” that is, justly and meritoriously. RIG.].
