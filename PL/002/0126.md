**Necessary.** These words, “If it is not so necessary,” Junius thinks may safely be omitted, and he testifies that they are in fact absent from many copies. But why may they not equally well be retained? They certainly yield a sufficiently appropriate sense: “If necessity does not press from some other quarter.”

**Sponsors.** They are otherwise called godparents (*patrini*), because they are, as it were, spiritual fathers, or sureties. Concerning them Saint Chrysostom, on Psalm XIV, says: “Immediately the priest exacts covenants and agreements from a tender age, [Col. 1196D] and questions the sponsor as surety for the minor,” etc. For they must undertake and answer on behalf of the infant, etc.; hence the aforesaid designation “sponsors” was also applied to them.

From this passage of Tertullian it is certainly evident that godparents had already been customarily employed in baptism from the most ancient usage and, as it were, from the very cradle of the Church, as Saint Basil, Epistle 128, Saint Augustine in numerous passages, and others everywhere likewise testify.

**They can.** That is, if the sponsors themselves should die before the baptized infant has attained the use of reason, so that he could be instructed by them concerning the obligation he contracted in baptism, or what those godparents promised on his behalf.

**Be deceived.** If meanwhile the infant, having grown to adulthood, drives out the grace of baptism by a wicked life, and thereafter refuses to fulfill in his own person, although he is now able to do so, the promises made for him, etc.

[Col. 1197A] **Christians.** They are to become such through baptism, by which, of course, initiation into the Christian people takes place.

**An innocent age.** Yet that age is in truth sufficiently guilty, since it is liable for original sin, which the soul contracts immediately upon its first infusion into the body. Nor is it likely that by this expression Tertullian intended to make infants immune from that sin, since in his treatise *On the Testimony of the Soul*, chapter 3, he says so explicitly: “Man, circumvented from the beginning by the devil so that he transgressed God’s commandment, and for that reason given over to death, thereafter made the ENTIRE RACE descended from his seed infected by him and the transmitter also of his condemnation.”

**Of sins.** Tertullian therefore called the age of infants innocent because they are free from actual sins, that is, sins committed by their own will; and he had believed that the chief end and power of baptism consisted in the remission of these sins, with respect both to guilt and to punishment. Since, therefore, there is only one baptism, and it cannot thereafter be repeated (chapter XV, note **They do not have**), and consequently in adults every guilt can indeed be erased through Penance, but not equally every punishment [Col. 1197B] (chapter V, note **Punishment**), Tertullian judged it better that baptism should be reserved until adulthood, so that then, together with original sin, all actual sins and the entire liability to the punishment due for them might be removed at once—whereas otherwise lengthy labors of penance and works of satisfaction would be required to remove it. Meanwhile, however, our author failed to consider that infants who die without baptism are thereby deprived of every further hope of salvation, a loss that can no longer be repaired by any means.

**In secular matters,** that is, in the affairs of this world, or temporal matters.

**Earthly substance,** that is, the administration of an inheritance and temporal possessions, is not entrusted to them, nor indeed can it be.

**Should divine things be entrusted?** Junius approves Latinius’s reading of these words interrogatively (*εἰρωτικῶς*), and I readily agree with him. Yet this argument of Tertullian is weak. The administration of their affairs cannot be entrusted to wards because [Col. 1197C] they are wholly incapable of it; nevertheless, goods acquired by inheritance pass to them. “As long as the heir is a child,” says the Apostle in Galatians 4:1, “he differs in no respect from a slave, although he is lord of all; but he is under tutors and administrators until the appointed time,” etc. Meanwhile, their goods stand in the greatest need of administration, and it is altogether necessary that there be someone to protect them and defend them against the injuries of others, etc. But once grace has been entrusted to an infant, and thereafter rests secure against every force, what administration does it require? For who will take away grace once received by an infant? Who will offer violence to him? Nothing is contrary or hostile to grace except sin subsequently committed by one’s own will; but how can this occur in an infant while he lacks the use of reason? Thus no snares threaten grace in the infant, whereas they must meanwhile be feared for his temporal goods. Those goods, therefore, need to be entrusted to another’s guardianship, as does the infant’s own person, which is plainly very exposed to injuries from others.

[Col. 1197D] **You may appear to have given.** Why should it not also be given to one who does not ask for it, so long as he cannot ask for it by himself, yet needs it for his salvation just as much as an adult who can ask? Will food, then, also be denied to an infant because, however necessary it may be to him, he cannot ask for it?

**Of delaying.** This opinion of Tertullian is extraordinary and displays his rigid disposition. It was never asserted by anyone else, and rests upon the same feeble argument that we examined above.

**Full maturity.** That is, when they are marriageable and mature enough for a husband.

**Wandering about.** I do not grasp this unless Tertullian means that, being without a companion and, as it were, a safeguard of her chastity, she is alone and therefore wanders among men.

**Let them be strengthened,** that is, if she has remained in widowhood for a longer period and surrounded herself with another protection by [Col. 1198A] withdrawing herself entirely from social company; if, by preserving the state of her widowhood for a longer time, she has acquired a somewhat firmer habit of continence and thereafter seriously and completely renounced marriage.

**Weight,** that is, the importance and gravity of the matter.

**The consequence.** The things that may follow baptism: namely, the danger of losing grace through sins—which, particularly because of the ingratitude joined to them, will be more serious after baptism than if committed before it—and the difficulty of recovering that grace. Perhaps our author here had in view the Apostle’s words in Hebrews 6:4:

> Ἀδύνατον τοὺς ἅπαξ φωτισθέντας, γευσαμένους τε τῆς δωρεᾶς τῆς ἐπουρανίου, καὶ μετόχους γενηθέντας πνεύματος ἁγίου . . . . καὶ παραπεσόντας, πάλιν ἀνακαινίζειν εἰς μετάνοιαν, ἀνασταυροῦντας ἑαυτοῖς τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ Θεοῦ, καὶ παραδειγματίζοντας.

That is:

> It is impossible for those who have once been ILLUMINATED—that is, baptized, as in chapter I, note **Blindness**—and have also tasted the heavenly gift and become partakers of the HOLY SPIRIT . . . and have fallen away, to be renewed again to repentance, since they crucify again for themselves [Col. 1198B] the Son of God and expose him to public disgrace.

Here *τὸ ἀδύνατον* (“the impossible”) signifies not what is absolutely impossible but what is difficult—that is, what cannot indeed be done by human means, but can be done by divine means, though with somewhat greater difficulty and with the danger of relapse. So in Mark 10:27, when the disciples ask who can be saved, Christ replies:

> Παρὰ ἀνθρώποις ἀδύνατον, ἀλλ’ οὐ παρὰ τῷ Θεῷ· πάντα γὰρ δυνατά ἐστι παρὰ τῷ Θεῷ.

That is:

> Among men—namely, by human powers—this is impossible, but not with GOD; for all things are possible with God.

Here he did not say “possible *for* GOD,” but “possible *with* GOD,” that is, through the powers of divine grace, by whose assistance nothing is now so arduous that it cannot be overcome and accomplished by us. “All things are possible to one who believes,” says Christ in Mark 9:23. For those baptized while still infants, therefore, the attainment of grace through baptism is certainly safer than its postponement would have been. In postponement there lies the danger of losing salvation so that it can no longer be recovered; in the baptized, grace is lost with greater difficulty [Col. 1198C] and, once lost, can at least be restored through Penance.

**Intact,** that is, obtained in its fullness through baptism and thereafter preserved without contamination.

**Concerning salvation.** “But he who perseveres to the end shall be saved” (Matthew 10:22).

### Notes on Chapter XIX

**It is preferable.** At the beginning of the Church no particular time was prescribed for baptism. “At first everyone taught,” says the author of the *Commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians*, chapter IV, “and everyone baptized, whenever an opportunity arose on any day or at any time. For neither did PHILIP” (Acts 8) “seek a time or a day on which to baptize the eunuch. Nor did PAUL and SILAS” (Acts 16:33) “defer the time at which they baptized the jailer with all his household. Nor did PETER seek a particular day when he baptized Cornelius” (Acts 10:47ff.) “with his entire household. Thus, in order that the people might grow and multiply, permission was granted to all at the beginning both to evangelize and to baptize,” etc. “But when [Col. 1198D] the Church had embraced every place . . . it began to be governed by a different order and administration. Hence it is that now neither do deacons preach among the people, nor clerics or laypeople baptize, nor are believers immersed on just any day, unless they are sick.”

From this we see that it is not surprising if, in many other matters as well, the discipline that had existed in apostolic times, had been ordained by the Apostles themselves, and was then practiced, was nevertheless in many respects—namely, where no divine precept was attached—changed by the Church in the course of time, or augmented by the addition of further regulations. Such discipline was adapted variously and wisely to the requirements of different times and other circumstances, while those things belonging to the substance of the sacraments, since they are known to derive from a far higher and wholly divine institution, were always preserved unshaken.

But to return to our argument: from the most ancient times [Col. 1199A] the custom already prevailed that solemn baptism should be celebrated at EASTER and PENTECOST. Saint Siricius and Saint Gregory the Great, both supreme pontiffs, testify that this observance had proceeded from apostolic times themselves. The former, in his epistle to Himerius, Bishop of Tarragona, appeals to the custom of all the churches; the latter, in Epistle 136 to the bishops of Sicily, presents it as a rule instituted by the Apostles and handed down in the Church through an unbroken succession. According to Theophylact, *Commentary on Luke* 10, this very widespread practice endured almost until the end of the eleventh century. Finally it was entirely abrogated, and baptism was left to the discretion of its ministers, so that they might administer it whenever it seemed proper to them for a reasonable cause. This custom eventually prevailed among the Greeks as well, and indeed many centuries ago.

**The Passion of the Lord.** In his work *Against the Jews*, chapter 10, our author believed that *Pascha* was of Greek origin, derived from the Greek *πάσχω*, “I suffer.” In this matter he had Saint [Col. 1199B] Irenaeus before him, book IV *Against the Jews*, chapter 23, and several followers, especially Lactantius in book IV of the *Institutes*, chapter 26, where he says:

> The sacrifice of the animal—the paschal lamb—is called *PASCHA* by the very people who perform it, *ἀπὸ τοῦ πάσχειν*, “from suffering,” because it is a figure of the PASSION,

etc.

Yet no one doubts that they were gravely mistaken concerning this etymology of the name, and that the word must be assigned to the Hebrews. For in Exodus 12:12 it is *PESACH*, from the root [Hebrew text missing in the supplied source]; and from this the Chaldeans, by adding their own suffix as they frequently do, made [form missing in the supplied source], *PISCHA* or *PASCHA*. The Septuagint also adopted this word, writing *πάσχα*, especially because its ending is Greek and is declined in the genitive with *-ατος*. Our Vulgate, however, used *PHASE*, because the harshness of the guttural letter is not very agreeable to Latin speakers. Hence the Vulgate also commonly cut it off in other proper names, especially disyllables: for *NOACH, THARACH,* and *CORACH* it wrote *Noe, Thare,* and *Chore*, etc.

Furthermore, *pesach, phase,* or *pascha* [Col. 1199C] means a PASSAGE or PASSING OVER. Hence Aquila translated *pascha* as *ὑπέρβασις*, “a crossing over” or “passing beyond,” while Symmachus rendered it *ὑπερμάχησις*, “defense”; but none of the Greek translators rendered it “Passion.” Finally, our Latin translator interprets Exodus 12:12 thus: “For it is the PHASE—that is, the PASSAGE—of the Lord.” The Hebrews were to celebrate the annual Passover in memory of the angel’s striking the firstborn of Egypt and passing untouched over, or passing by, only the houses of the Hebrews.

**We are immersed.** We have already shown in chapter XVI, note **Baptism**, 1188A, that Christ’s baptism derives all its efficacy from his Passion. See also chapter XI, note **The Resurrection**, 1178B.—**Without the resurrection.** Ibid.

**Will be interpreted.** It is used here passively, as in the author of the *Rhetoric to Herennius*, book II.

**Pentecost.** *Πεντηκοστή*—with *ἡμέρα*, “day,” understood—means “the fiftieth day.” From the day after the Sabbath (Leviticus 23:15), that is, from the second day of Unleavened Bread, seven weeks had to be counted, making forty-nine days. On [Col. 1199D] the fiftieth day the FEAST OF WEEKS was to be celebrated, which in our Church is called the FEAST OF PENTECOST. Just as it was formerly celebrated by the Jews in memory of the Law given to Moses on Mount Sinai—which is believed to have occurred on the fiftieth day after the feast of Passover, or the departure from Egypt (Exodus 19:1, 11)—so now in our Church the feast of Pentecost, that is, the fiftieth day after the feast of Easter, is celebrated in memory of the Holy Spirit’s descent upon the Apostles in tongues of fire (Acts 2:1ff.).

Since baptism is therefore the work of the Holy Spirit, who descended in the form of a dove upon Christ when he was baptized in the Jordan, and who in a special manner sanctified the waters—see above, chapter X, note [reference unclear in the source]—it is not surprising that the primitive Church appointed Pentecost, no less than Easter, as a time for solemnly conferring baptism. “Let all priests,” says Pope Siricius, cited above in the note **It is preferable**, “observe this rule if they do not wish to be severed from the solidity of the apostolic ROCK upon which Christ built the universal Church.”

[Col. 1200A] Since, however, this matter belonged purely to discipline, it is not surprising that even after the time of Siricius a different custom was observed elsewhere: on the NATIVITY OF THE LORD, when Avitus, Bishop of Vienne, testifies in his epistle to Clovis that Clovis, the first king of the Franks, was baptized; or on the EPIPHANY of the Lord, because on that day Christ himself was baptized by Saint John; and elsewhere, again, on the feast of SAINT JOHN THE BAPTIST for a similar reason. Indeed, baptism was even administered on the feast days of the Apostles and Martyrs, not without opposition from the Roman Church, until at last, as the conditions of the times required, the individual churches were granted freedom in this matter. Nevertheless, according to the usage of almost the entire Church, the blessing of the baptismal font was restricted to HOLY SATURDAY of Easter and the VIGIL OF PENTECOST. On this subject Martène, *On the Rites of the Church*, book I, chapter 1, article 1; Juenin, *On Baptism*, question X, chapter 2; and many others may be consulted at greater length.

[Col. 1200B] **With waters.** That is, so that they may first have been solemnly blessed (chapter IV, note **God having been invoked**) and may always be prepared for the orderly administration of baptism to all.

**There is an interval.** From this it seems rightly to be inferred that throughout that intermediate period, namely from Easter to Pentecost, baptism was formerly customarily administered. I think this must be understood to mean that it was conferred solemnly at Easter and Pentecost, but during the intervening period only those were washed who had previously been hindered—for example by illness—or who had been absent for some other cause, as well as infants born during that intervening period, etc.

**Has been frequently celebrated,** that is, its annual commemoration was observed with frequent and more vivid remembrance.

**Dedicated,** that is, consecrated, or celebrated in religious worship amid hymns, prayers, thanksgivings, etc. From what has thus far been said, we clearly perceive that the feasts of EASTER and PENTECOST were instituted by the Apostles themselves, but [Col. 1200C] with rites different from those observed among the Hebrews and with different symbols, namely symbols of past events: Easter was to be celebrated in memory of Christ’s RESURRECTION, by which he raises us from the death of sin to the life of grace; Pentecost, in commemoration of the HOLY SPIRIT first poured out upon the Apostles and into the Church.

**Coming.** This must be understood of the Lord’s second coming, when he will come to judge the living and the dead.

**Partly disclosed.** That is, shown somewhat obscurely and, as it were, from a greater distance.

**Received back.** Some prefer *reciperato*, because it derives from *recipere*, “to receive.” But it is preferable to leave this dispute of grammarians undecided. It is sufficient here that the ASCENSION OF CHRIST, or his reception into heaven, is signified.

**At Pentecost.** For our author this name also denotes the entire period of fifty days between Easter and Pentecost. Hence in *On Idolatry*, chapter 14, he says: “Select the individual solemnities of the nations and arrange them in order: they will not be able to fill Pentecost.” [Col. 1200D] He also appears to be understood in that sense here. For Christ did not ascend into heaven on Pentecost if by that word one means the particular day, but during that intervening period, namely on the fortieth day after the Resurrection.

Moreover, the belief that the day of judgment will occur during Pentecost is Tertullian’s conjecture, weakly based on the words of Acts 1:11 cited above. For there it says only “in the same manner,” signifying the manner of his coming, not its time. For just as he ascended in glory and a cloud received him (Acts 1:9), so one day “they shall see the Son of Man coming in the clouds of heaven with great power and majesty” (Matthew 24:30). But why should we labor to determine something of which no one knows—not even the angels of heaven, but the Father alone (ibid., verse 36)?

**At the feast.** Jeremiah 31:8, where the Septuagint adds *ἐν ἑορτῇ φασέκ*, “at the solemnity of Phase,” or Passover. These words are absent from the Hebrew text and from all the other Greek versions—as Saint Jerome himself notes in his *Commentary on Jeremiah* at this passage [Col. 1201A]—and consequently also from our Latin Vulgate. Nor is it established by any testimony of Ezra, Nehemiah, or any other sacred writer that the Jews returned from the Babylonian captivity at the feast of Passover as they had once departed from Egyptian bondage; yet Jeremiah is speaking here of that return from Babylon. Hence this addition by the Seventy appears to have been introduced only for the sake of some mystical signification, fulfilled, of course, in Christ’s Passover and Passion, etc.

**Festive.** For it was called the SOLEMNITY OF WEEKS, as stated above in the note **Pentecost**.

**It makes no difference.** Time neither adds anything to nor takes anything from the effect of baptism, that is, grace; nor does one who receives baptism on a feast day acquire more grace than one who receives it on any other day. “One time is suited to one thing: one is proper for sleep, another for wakefulness, and likewise one for war and another for peace. The time for baptism is the whole of a person’s life,” says Saint Basil, Homily 13, *Exhortation to Baptism*.

### Notes on Chapter XX

**Frequent.** [Col. 1201B] Adults were admitted to baptism only when—apart, of course, from a case of urgent necessity—they had previously shown themselves worthy of this heavenly gift through various works of piety, especially works of penance, during their catechumenate. This was a time of probation and, as it were, a kind of novitiate before baptism. They also had to give great hope of their future perseverance in the grace once received. We have already discussed the catechumens and their classes in *On Prescription*, chapter XLI, and there is no leisure to repeat that material here. Catechumens were permitted to enter the church and pray with the others, and there before GOD to express their burning desire for baptism, etc. They were then dismissed from the church, while the deacon prayed to God for them, as is reported in book VIII, chapter 5 of the *Apostolic Constitutions*—whose author is not Saint Clement, but nevertheless some other very ancient writer:

> May the Lord mercifully hear their supplications and prayers; and, receiving their petitions, may he grant them assistance and fulfill the request of their hearts unto their benefit,

etc.

[Col. 1201C] **With fasts.** Thus the author of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, book VII, chapter 23, likewise states: “Before baptism, let the person who is to be baptized FAST.” And below: “The person initiated into his”—Christ’s—“death, who is to be buried with him in baptism, must first FAST and afterward be baptized.” Saint Augustine likewise says in *On Faith and Works*, chapter 6: “Without doubt they would not be admitted to baptism if, during the very days in which they were to receive that grace, they were not purified by abstinence, FASTS, and exorcisms,” etc. The Fourth Council of Carthage, canon 85, also decreed: “Those to be baptized, after being tested for a long time by abstinence from wine and meat and by frequent imposition of hands, are to receive baptism.” For the more the body, weakened by fasting, is restrained, the more the soul is enlarged and becomes capable of receiving the grace of baptism with correspondingly greater abundance.

**With kneelings.** Kneelings are what the Greeks call *γονυπετεῖαι* or *γονυκλισίαι*, genuflections, customarily employed in prayer from the most ancient times. Hence in *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 3, our author speaks of worshiping “from the knees.” In *To Scapula*, chapter 4, he says: [Col. 1201D] “When have even droughts not been driven away by our kneelings and fastings?” etc.

Kneeling, therefore, is a sign of reverence, submission, or adoration. Thus Saint PETER, when about to raise Tabitha from the dead, “knelt down and prayed” (Acts 9:40). Saint PAUL, when about to depart from Miletus, “knelt down and prayed with them all,” etc. (Acts 20:36). The same Apostle says in Ephesians 3:14: “For this reason I bend my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.” Indeed, knees were also bent during prayer in the Old Testament. For when SOLOMON delivered that lengthy prayer at the dedication of the Temple in 3 Kings 8, he had fixed both knees upon the ground (ibid., verse 54). DANIEL, while praying, “bent his knees and worshiped and made confession before his God, as he had been accustomed to do before” (Daniel 6:10). Likewise EZRA said of himself: “I bent my knees and spread out my hands to the Lord” (Ezra 9:5). So too in the New Testament, those who approached Christ [Col. 1202A] to ask for the grace of healing made their request upon bended knee. Thus Matthew 17:14: “A man approached him, *γονυπετῶν αὐτῷ*, falling on his knees before him,” etc. And Mark 1:40: “A leper came to him, beseeching him, and *γονυπετῶν αὐτόν*, bending his knee, said to him,” etc. So too others elsewhere. What, moreover, of the fact that Christ himself on the Mount of Olives (Luke 22:41), *θεὶς τὰ γόνατα προσηύχετο*, “knelt down and prayed”? And this is he whom GOD exalted . . . “so that at the Name of JESUS, *πᾶν γόνυ κάμψῃ*, every knee should bend,” etc. (Philippians 2:10).

It is therefore especially fitting that those to be baptized should also fall upon their knees and pray to GOD for so illustrious a gift, through which, although they had previously fallen into sin, they are raised anew to salvation. For, as the author of the *Questions to the Orthodox*, reply to Question 115, says: *γονυκλισία σύμβολόν ἐστι τῆς ἐν ταῖς ἁμαρτίαις πτώσεως*—“Bending the knees is a sign of the fall into sins.”

**With all-night vigils.** An all-night vigil, called *παννυχισμός* by the Greeks, properly speaking is a vigil of longer duration, continued through the entire night or at least a great part of it; [Col. 1202B] whereas a vigil simply so called is the suppression of sleep even for a shorter period. Martial accurately noted the distinction between the two in book V, Epigram 70:

> For keeping watch is easy; keeping watch all night is hard.

Hence Arnobius, book IV of the *Institutes*, says: “For whom those sacred all-night vigils and burdensome *pannychisms* are consecrated,” etc.

Here, however, we are by no means speaking of the all-night vigils of the gentiles, concerning which Cicero says in book II of *On the Laws*: “Thus Aristophanes assails the new gods and the all-night celebrations held in worshiping them,” etc. Tacitus also speaks of them (*Annals*, book V, chapter 44): “Women who had husbands celebrated sacred banquets and all-night vigils.” But away with such things, generally associated with the grossest obscenities! We must treat of far holier vigils, which were frequently observed in the earliest Church, as we learn sufficiently even from our Tertullian himself. These vigils were either public, celebrated by the entire assembly of a particular church, [Col. 1202C] or private, celebrated by individuals in their own homes. The former preceded every more solemn feast and also the commemorations of the martyrs. The latter were among the private works of penitents and were to be undertaken especially and more frequently by those preparing for baptism. Hence Saint Gregory Nazianzen, in his *Oration on Holy Baptism*, instructs the catechumen as follows:

> Good aids for obtaining the things you desire will be vigils, fasts, *chameuniae*—sleeping on the ground—prayers, tears, hardships, and assistance to the needy,

etc.

In the ancient Church fasts were always joined to these vigils. Later, however, when vigils were abolished because abuses had crept in, the fasts were retained together with the name alone of the vigils.

**Of sins.** Just as sins are remitted to no human beings in this order of providence without their first being retracted through some act of penance, so adults required certain acts of penance for the remission of actual sins provided by baptism, and therefore for the fruitful reception of baptism itself. [Col. 1202D] These acts had to be manifested by some outward sign, namely by public confession of sins. For the most ancient monuments likewise testify to this. Saint Justin Martyr, for example, says in his *Dialogue with Trypho*, page 177 of my edition, that even under the Old Testament washings did not profit unless those who received them had first repented:

> τοῦτο ἐκεῖνο πάλαι τὸ σωτήριον λουτρὸν ἦν ὃ εἵπετο τοῖς μεταγινώσκουσιν,

that is:

> This was that saving bath of old which followed upon those who repented.

After confirming this with an extended testimony from Isaiah, he adds:

> τοῦτ’ ἐκεῖνο τὸ βάπτισμα, τὸ μόνον καθαρίσαι τοὺς μετανοήσαντας δυνάμενον,

that is:

> This is that baptism which alone can purify those who repent.

How much more properly does he require this for our baptism in *Apology* II, page 73 of my edition, where he says:

> Εὔχεσθαί τε καὶ αἰτεῖν νηστεύοντες παρὰ τοῦ Θεοῦ τῶν προημαρτημένων ἄφεσιν διδάσκονται, ἡμῶν συνευχομένων καὶ συννηστευόντων αὐτοῖς· ἔπειτα ἄγονται ὑφ’ ἡμῶν ἔνθα ὕδωρ ἐστί, κ.τ.λ.

That is:

> They are taught, while fasting, to pray and ask GOD for the remission of their former sins [Col. 1203A]—yet how will they ask unless they confess those sins?—while we pray and fast together with them. Then they are led by us to the place where there is water,

etc.

This accords with what the very ancient author—formerly believed to have been Saint Jerome—of the *Sermon on Psalm 41 to the Newly Baptized* says:

> Throughout the whole of Lent they devoted themselves to prayer and fasting; they slept in sackcloth and ashes, seeking through confession of their sins the life they were to receive through baptism,

etc.

Eusebius, in the *Life of Constantine*, chapter 61, likewise reports that CONSTANTINE THE GREAT, EMPEROR, approached the reception of baptism after preparing himself in this manner:

> Having considered these things within himself, he fell upon the ground on bended knee, humbly begged pardon from GOD, and confessed his sins in the *martyrium*,

that is, in the place where the monuments or tombs of the martyrs were visited.

Lest we think so exalted an example had no followers, let us hear Socrates, a fifth-century writer. In book V of his *Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 17, while treating of the destruction of the temple of Serapis, he says: “Very many more people embraced the Christian religion and, confessing their sins, [Col. 1203B] received the baptism of Christ.” Whether this confession, which adults had to make before baptism, concerned specific sins or was merely general is still disputed among critics. It is certain, however, that our sacramental Penance had no place among those preparing for baptism, since baptism itself is a kind of initiation into the remaining sacraments of our Church, granting a person the capacity validly to receive and administer them.

**Let them set forth,** that is, let them display an example in themselves. As Junius says at this passage, the metaphor is taken from merchants setting out their wares. Accordingly, in our baptism we too must display the baptism of John as the forerunner of Christ’s baptism. See above, chapter X, note **He administered beforehand**.

**Their sins.** Junius here reads *nunc*, “now,” in place of *non*, “not,” just as in book I of *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 1, where most manuscripts have: “Those who have heard him”—Christ—“prophesying until NOT long ago veil the virgins,” but Junius judges that NOW should be substituted. Reason itself certainly appears to require this, [Col. 1203C] especially because, as the critics report, that copyists’ error is also accustomed to occur very frequently in other writings.

**For.** This particle causes me to approve Junius’s emendation. For what conflict of flesh and spirit would there otherwise be if that public confession were NOT required? Moreover, from this very point I am almost led to believe that at that time this confession was not merely general but also particular, at least regarding sins already publicly committed. Otherwise, what difficulty would it have involved? See the note **Of sins** above.

**By conflict,** that is, mortification, which produces serious discomfort for our nature from that very conflict. “For the flesh desires against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; for these are opposed to one another” (Galatians 5:17). This humiliation weighs heavily upon each: upon the spirit because of its native nobility and excellence, and indeed also because of the pride implanted in it through sin; upon the flesh [Col. 1203D] because of its native sluggishness, which shrinks from such noble endeavors. For even toward these “the spirit is sometimes willing, but the flesh is weak” (Matthew 26:41).

**What he will obtain.** For when Christ preached on the mountain, he called those blessed who suffer persecution for righteousness, “because theirs is the kingdom of heaven.” “Blessed are you,” he continues, “when they revile you and say every evil thing against you,” etc. “Rejoice and exult, because your reward is abundant in heaven; for so they persecuted the Prophets,” etc. (Matthew 5:10ff.). This is also the lesson that Paul and Barnabas so earnestly impressed upon their hearers: “Through many tribulations”—or temptations—“we must enter the kingdom of GOD.” Some believe that Tertullian is instead referring here to Matthew 19:23: “A rich man will enter the kingdom of heaven with difficulty.”

**From the bath,** that is, after the bath.

**One must fast.** Just as Christ began his fast not before but [Col. 1204A] after baptism.

**Of salvation.** He means here the feast of Easter, which immediately follows Holy Saturday, formerly designated in a special manner as the day for solemn baptism (the preceding chapter). For the paschal jubilation is itself a mutual congratulation upon our salvation, which we, dead in Adam but rising with Christ to a new life, have recovered. Behold, therefore, the most ancient origin of paschal greetings, formerly a token of mutual charity among Christians, which the cunning spirit of this age customarily envelops in deceitful formulas of words and empty ceremonies.

Moreover, the sorrow of fasting is less suited to paschal joy, and accordingly the Church wished fasts to be excluded from these solemnities and indeed from the entire paschal season. Furthermore, the Church ordained that no Sunday should be darkened by fasting, since it wished that day also to be consecrated to the memory of the risen Christ. This is evident from canon 15 of Saint Peter of Alexandria, who flourished toward the end of the third century, [Col. 1204B] after approving the fasts on Wednesdays and Fridays, he says: “But as for the Lord’s Day, it is a joyful day, because Jesus Christ rose on that day.” What, moreover, of the fact that at the Council of Gangra, held in A.D. 370, canon 18, we find that even an anathema was pronounced upon those who, under the pretext of a more austere life, fasted on Sunday? This should not appear surprising, since they were regarded as sharing the practice of the Manichaeans and Priscillianists, who established Sunday as a regular day of fasting. Hence Saint Augustine, in Epistle 86 to Casulanus, when treating of the Saturday fast, etc., calls fasts of this kind “a great scandal” and “horrible.”

**Against him,** namely, against the tempter, the devil.

**With divine provisions,** namely, the manna that rained daily from heaven (Exodus 16:4ff.).

**Than God’s.** Namely, when that ungrateful and gluttonous people repeatedly lamented: “Who will give us meat to eat? . . . Our soul is dry; our eyes behold nothing but manna” (Numbers 11: [Col. 1204C] 4, 6).

**The water,** that is, his baptism in the Jordan.

**Of God.** Junius rightly judged this word redundant.

**By the word.** “Man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of GOD” (Matthew 4:4). This saying had been transferred here by Christ from Deuteronomy 8:3, where Moses, recalling to the people God’s blessings and the afflictions sent upon them in the desert, says: “He afflicted you with want and gave you manna as food . . . so that he might show you that man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of GOD.” This is that figure of Israel from which our author said above that Christ turned the reproach back against the devil.

**To be struck down.** An elegant metaphor. Properly speaking, those are said to be “struck down” whose larger veins of the neck—which are otherwise customarily called *lisae*, and in Greek *σφραγῖτιδες*—are cut. Thus Celsus, book IV of *On Medicine*, chapter 1, says: “The *lisae*, according to Donatus, are the larger veins of the throat; [Col. 1204D] when these are severed, an animal immediately dies.” Hence *elisum* means something dead from such a cause. We likewise say of others carried off by a violent death that they were “struck down,” as Suetonius says in *Caligula*, chapter 26: “More than twenty Roman knights were struck down during that disturbance.” Cicero also uses “struck down by illness” in book V of the *Tusculan Disputations*.

**Birthday.** For it is “the bath of regeneration,” in accordance with Christ’s words to Nicodemus: “Unless a person is born anew, he cannot see the kingdom of GOD” (John 3:3). A person’s first birthday, therefore, is from his mother with respect to the body; the second is from GOD with respect to the soul, which, having died through sin, is reborn through baptism. These are they who were born “not of blood,” etc., “but BORN OF GOD” (John 1:13).

**Mother.** The Church may fittingly be understood here: “Jerusalem above, who is OUR MOTHER” (Galatians 4:26).

**Brothers.** “But we, BROTHERS, [Col. 1205A] like Isaac, are CHILDREN of promise” (ibid., verse 28).

**Possessions.** Not temporal possessions only, but especially the spiritual things necessary or wonderfully conducive to sustaining this new life.
