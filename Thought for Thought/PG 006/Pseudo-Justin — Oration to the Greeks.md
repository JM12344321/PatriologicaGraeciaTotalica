Do not think, Greeks, that I abandoned your customs without reason or careful judgment. I found nothing in them holy or pleasing to God. Your poets’ compositions are monuments of madness and self-indulgence. Anyone schooled by the teacher you prize above all others is worse off than any human being.

They tell, first, how Agamemnon joined his brother’s inflamed self-indulgence, madness, and uncontrolled desire; how he agreed to sacrifice his daughter and threw all Greece into upheaval so that he could recover Helen, abducted from a leprous shepherd. When the war had made captives of others, Agamemnon himself became Chryseis’s captive and, over Briseis, took up hostility against Thetis’s son. And Achilles himself—

—the man who crossed a river, destroyed Troy, and mastered Hector—became Polyxena’s slave, was defeated by a dead Amazon, stripped off his divine-made weapons, dressed in wedding clothes, and became a victim of love-magic in Apollo’s temple. Odysseus, the Ithacan son of Laertes, made a profession of wickedness in the name of virtue. His voyage past the Sirens proves he lacked sound judgment: he could not think to block his ears.

Ajax, Telamon’s son, bearer of the seven-oxhide shield, lost the contest over Achilles’ armor to Odysseus and was overcome by madness. I do not want this as my education. I do not aspire to a “virtue” that would make me accept Homer’s myths. The whole rhapsody, from beginning to end—the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*—is `[OCR: ΥύνὴΔ]`.

Hesiod wrote *Works and Days* after Homer; who could assent to his foolish *Theogony*? It says—though the OCR damages the opening—that Cronus, Uranus’s son, overthrew his father, seized power, and then chose to devour his children because he feared the same fate. Zeus, however, hidden away by the Curetes, imprisoned his father and, according to the myth, apportioned—

—he killed a lion and the many-headed hydra, slew an inexhaustible wild creature, brought down man-eating birds, hauled Cerberus up from Hades, and cleared Augeias’s great dung-wall; he killed fire-breathing bulls and a doe, won golden fruit, killed a venomous serpent, Achelous (the reason is too shameful to state), and the guest-murderer Busiris; he crossed mountains to get water that spoke, so the tale goes. Yet this performer of so many great exploits was, like a child, beaten with cymbals by satyrs. Conquered by desire for a woman, he delighted in being beaten on the buttocks by a laughing Lydian. In the end he could not remove Nessus’s shirt, built his own pyre, and died.

Hephaestus ought to stop envying Ares: old and lame, he was disliked, while Ares, young and handsome, was loved. Thus, Greeks, your gods are condemned by their lack of restraint, and your heroes prove unmanly, as your dramatic stories display: Atreus’s crimes, Thyestes’ adulterous beds, the abominations of Pelops’ line, Danaus killing from jealousy and becoming drunkenly childless, and the Furies preparing Thyestes’ feast. Procne still mourns in bird-form, while her tongue-cut sister, the Athenian woman, keeps chirping. Why rehearse Oedipus’s torments—Laius’s murder, marriage to his mother, and the mutual killing of his brothers who were also his children?

I hate your festivals too: their boundless excess, seductive pipes summoning people to ecstatic dancing, lavish perfumes [OCR-uncertain wording] and garlands. With this huge heap of evils—

—you blot out shame, intoxicate your minds through self-indulgence, and habitually engage in unholy, frenzied sexual unions.

Let me add this: Greek, why are you angry at your son if, initiated into Zeus’s cult, he plots against you and violates your marriage? Why call that man your enemy when you worship a god who does the same? Why condemn your wife’s sexual license while honoring Aphrodite with temples? If someone else said these things, you could dismiss them as a baseless charge instead of truth. But your own poets sing them, and your own stories proclaim them aloud.

`Ξ.` `[OCR: damaged section marker.]` Come, Greeks: share an incomparable wisdom. Let the divine Word educate you; learn to know the imperishable King. Recognize these heroes—not as figures who make slaughter among peoples. He does not ask from us physical strength, beauty, or pride in birth; he asks for a pure soul fortified with holiness. The divine Word continually governs us, and the ordinances of our King—divine deeds entering the soul through the Word’s power—are a trumpet of peace to a soul at war, a banishment of terrible passions, a lesson that extinguishes living fire. This education does not make poets, philosophers, or formidable rhetoricians; it makes mortals immortal and humans divine, lifting them from earth to realms beyond Olympus.

`ταθετε` `[OCR: damaged imperative]`: be educated; become as I am, for I too once was as you are. The divine quality of this education and the power of the Word captured me. Just as `[OCR: ἐπααιὰλς, comparison damaged]` causes something to creep from its den and drives away a dreadful serpent, so the Word expels from the soul’s innermost recesses the terrible passions of sensation—first desire, from which every evil—
