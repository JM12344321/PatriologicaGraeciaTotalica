## CHAPTER IV

Now, as regards that more necessary opinion of yours, insofar as it concerns your very condition [Col. 0613B], we affirm that you remain after life has been brought to its close, and that you await the day of judgment; and that, according to your deserts, you are destined either for torment or for refreshment, each everlasting.

[Note: [Col. 0613C] “Insofar as,” Junius.]

[Note: [Col. 0613D] “And according to his deserts,” etc. The author of the book *On Faith*, p. 43: “Each one of us will have an individual resurrection according to the deserts of his faith and life, whether to refreshment or to burning.” See the comments on that passage of Septimius: “Even for a slight offense, the delay of resurrection having been paid,” p. 338. RIG.—This is a passage in support of the merits of works, of which there are several elsewhere. Here again, indeed, is an obsolete word: *passionalis*, “liable to suffering.” PAM.]

To undergo these things, your original substance must necessarily return to you, together with the material and memory of the same human being, because you could experience neither evil nor good without the flesh’s capacity for suffering, and there could be no rationale for judgment without the presentation of the very person who merited the suffering imposed by judgment.

That Christian opinion, although far more honorable than the Pythagorean, which does not transfer you into beasts; although fuller than the Platonic, which restores to you also the endowment of the body; although more welcome than the Epicurean, which defends you from annihilation—nevertheless, because of its own name, it is reckoned merely vanity, stupidity, and, as they say, presumption [Col. 0614A].

[Note: [Col. 0613C] “More serious.” Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0613C] *Suum* is absent from Latinus and Rigaltius; *solum*, Latinus and Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0613D] “Is reckoned presumption.” Resurrection was described by this name among the gentiles. For Christians, honored with this glorious title, were called “presumptuous.” Thus Apuleius, book IX, tore at a Christian woman: “Then, despising and trampling upon the divine powers, in place of an established religion she feigned, with sacrilegious presumption, a God whom she proclaimed to be the only one,” etc. LAC.]

But we are not ashamed if our presumption is shared with you. For first, when you remember someone who has died, you call him “poor wretch”—not, surely, because he has been snatched away from the good of life, but because he has already been assigned to punishment and judgment.

[Note: [Col. 0614B] “Not as one snatched away from the good of life,” Latinus.]

[Note: [Col. 0614C] “Because” is absent from Junius; “as one snatched away from the good,” Rigaltius.]

At other times, however, you call the dead “secure.” You thereby acknowledge both the hardship of life and the benefit of death. Moreover, you call them secure whenever you go outside the gate to the tombs, making a funeral offering on your own behalf with provisions and delicacies, or return from the tombs the worse for drink.

[Note: [Col. 0614C] “Outside the gate.” It was formerly provided and forbidden at Rome, even by the laws of the Twelve Tables, that no one should be buried or cremated within the city. Hence Juvenal, Satire 1:

> I shall try what may be permitted against those  
> Whose ashes are covered by the Flaminian and Latin Ways.

LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0614C] “And *mattyae*,” Junius.]

[Note: [Col. 0614D] “With provisions and *matteae*.” It is certain that *matteae* here means those funeral banquets customarily held at tombs; but it appears that *mattyis* should be read. For in Athenaeus Ματτύη πᾶν τὸ πολυνελὲς ἕδεσμα (“*Mattya* means any sumptuous dish”), that is, *mattya* means any sumptuous dish. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0614D] “You go to the tombs.” He says that those who went outside the city to the tombs to make funeral offerings “went away.” RIG.]

But I demand your sober opinion. You call the dead “poor wretches” when you speak from your own mind, when you are far away from them. For at their banquet you cannot reproach them with their lot as though they were present and reclining at table with you; you must flatter those on whose account you live more festively [Col. 0614B].

[Note: [Col. 0614C] “Their” is absent from Rigaltius; Latinus adds “when.”]

[Note: [Col. 0614C] “More lavishly,” Ciacconius.]

Do you therefore call someone who feels nothing a poor wretch? What of the fact that you curse him as though he could feel, when you recall him under the sting of some grievance? You pray that the earth may lie heavy upon him and that his ashes may suffer torment in the lower regions.

[Note: [Col. 0614D] “You pray that the earth may lie heavy.” Pliny, book XI, where he speaks of the Earth: “Whose divine power, last of all, may we in our anger never invoke as heavy upon those who are now no one—as though we did not know that she alone never grows angry with humanity.” “Those who are now no one” means the dead. Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* I: “I speak of the dead, who are no one.” RIG.—This formula, “May the earth lie heavy upon you,” is proverbial, as is the other near the end of this chapter, “He has now departed and must return,” which even now remains in common German speech. PAM.]

Likewise, on the favorable side, when you owe someone gratitude, you pray for refreshment for his bones and ashes, and desire that he may rest well in the lower regions. If there is no capacity for suffering in you after death, if there is no persistence of sensation, if, in short, you yourself are nothing once you have left the body, why do you lie against yourself, as though you could suffer something further? Indeed, why do you fear death at all, if there is nothing for you to fear after death, in that respect in which nothing is to be experienced after death?

[Note: [Col. 0614C] I would prefer: “If you are nothing, what is there for you to fear after death?”]

[Note: [Col. 0614D] “In that respect in which nothing is to be experienced after death?” This particle always signifies a part or place, as in “on what side,” “in what place.” Therefore: Why do you fear death if nothing is to be feared after death, in that place or respect in which nothing is to be experienced after death? LAC.]

For even if it can be said [Col. 0615A] that death is feared not because it threatens anything, but because it cuts off the benefit of life, nevertheless, since at the same time you also escape the far more numerous hardships of life, you dispel the fear by gaining the weightier share; and the loss of good things is no longer to be feared, since it is compensated by another good, namely peace from hardships.

[Note: [Col. 0614C] “It can be said,” Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0615C] “Anything further,” Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0615C] “Escape,” the Venetian edition.]

[Note: [Col. 0615C] “By that gain,” Junius.]

That which frees us from everything fearful is not itself to be feared.

[Note: [Col. 0615D] “It is not to be feared,” etc. St. Chrysostom, Homily 21 *To the People*, teaches that nothing in death is to be feared, since we are tending toward a better and more desirable life, καὶ πάσης ἀπηλλάγμενην ἀνωμαλίας (“and freed from every vicissitude”). So too Seneca, Epistle 102: “Therefore look fearlessly upon that decisive hour: it is the last not for the soul, but for the body.” LE PR.]

If you fear departing from life because you know it to be very good, then certainly you ought not to fear death, which you do not know to be evil. But since you do fear it, you know it to be evil. Yet you would not know it to be evil—and therefore would not fear it—unless you knew that there was something after death that makes death evil and causes you to fear it.

Let us now leave aside the natural form of the fear of death. No one should fear what he cannot escape. I engage the question from the other side: the hope of a happier state after death. For a desire for fame after death is innate in almost everyone [Col. 0615B]. It would take too long to recount the Curtii and the Reguli, or the Greek men whose innumerable eulogies celebrate their contempt for death for the sake of posthumous fame.

[Note: [Col. 0615D] “It would take too long to recount.” The history of the Curtii and of Regulus, who met death for their country, is too well known to require fuller treatment. He afterwards called fame “posthumous” metaphorically, meaning fame that endures after death. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0615D] “The Curtii.” This is the man who devoted himself to the gods of the dead for the salvation of his country. For when the earth had sunk into an immense chasm in the middle of the Forum, and the Romans had received the answer that the breach would not close unless one of the nobles cast himself into it, Curtius sacrificed his life for his country. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0615C] “Eulogies,” Junius.]

Who today does not strive so earnestly to ensure that his memory will be frequented after death, preserving his name either by works of literature, by the simple praise of his character, or by the ambitious magnificence of his very tomb?

[Note: [Col. 0616D] “Preserve his name.” Virgil, *Aeneid* VII, has almost the same concerning Caieta, Aeneas’s nurse:

> And now your honor preserves your resting place, and your name marks your bones  
> In great Hesperia—if that is any glory.

LE PR.]

How, then, can the soul today aspire to something that it wishes to possess after death, and prepare so earnestly for what it will use after its departure, if it knows nothing of what comes afterward?

[Note: [Col. 0615C] “The soul,” Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0615C] “Which,” others.]

But perhaps people are more certain of sensation after your departure than of a resurrection at some future time, for presuming which we are branded presumptuous. Yet this too is proclaimed by the soul. For if someone asks about a person long since dead as though he were alive, the ready answer that comes immediately to hand is: “He has now departed, and must return” [Col. 0615C].

[Note: [Col. 0615C] Rigaltius adds “about.”]

[Note: [Col. 0616D] “He has now departed.” The ancients said “he has lived,” “he has departed,” or “he has departed to the majority,” in place of “he is dead”; and in Festus too *abitio*, “departure,” is used for death. LE PR.]

## CHAPTER V

These testimonies of the soul are as simple as they are true; as commonplace as they are simple; as universal as they are commonplace; as natural as they are universal; as divine as they are natural. I do not think [Col. 0616A] they can appear frivolous or insipid to anyone who reflects upon the majesty of nature, from which the soul’s authority derives. As much as you grant to the teacher, so much you will adjudge to the pupil. Nature is the teacher, the soul the pupil. Whatever the former has taught or the latter has thoroughly learned was delivered by God, who is, of course, the teacher of the soul’s own teacher.

[Note: [Col. 0615C] “I think,” Rigaltius.]

What the soul can divine concerning its first institutor, it is for you to assess from the soul that is within you.

[Note: [Col. 0616C] “Is within you,” Rigaltius.]

Feel her, who causes you to feel! Reflect upon her as a prophetess in presentiments, a diviner in omens, and one who foresees events. Is it astonishing that, being given by God to humanity, she knows how to divine? It is equally astonishing if she knows him by whom she was given. Even when deceived by the adversary, she remembers her author, his goodness, his decree, her own end, and that of the adversary himself. Is it therefore astonishing that, being given by God [Col. 0616B], she sings the same things that God has given his own people to know?

[Note: [Col. 0616C] “And” is absent from the Paris edition.]

But one who has not thought that eruptions of the soul of this kind constitute a teaching of nature, secretly entrusted to a consciousness both connatural and inborn, will instead say that, through opinions scattered widely among the people, the practice of published writings has established and virtually hardened this manner of speaking into a habit.

[Note: [Col. 0616C] “Outburst,” Wouwer.]

[Note: [Col. 0616C] “And” is absent from Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0616C] “Secretly entrusted,” Wouwer.]

[Note: [Col. 0616C] “Published,” Junius.]

Certainly the soul is earlier than the written word, speech earlier than the book, sensation earlier than the pen, and the human being himself earlier than the philosopher and the poet.

[Note: [Col. 0616D] “Certainly the soul is earlier than the written word.” Since this passage calls the soul’s utterance “Tradition” a little later, it can very well be applied to the authority of ecclesiastical Traditions, since these precede all books and the pen, that is, all literature. PAM.—“The soul is earlier than the written word.” Antony the Great, according to Athanasius in his *Life*, employed the same argument against certain philosophers. LAC.]

Are we then to believe that before literature and its dissemination human beings lived mute, without utterances of this kind? Did no one speak of God and his goodness, no one of death, no one of the lower regions? Speech, I suppose, went begging—or rather could not exist at all—because even then those subjects were absent without which, even today, speech cannot be [Col. 0616C] happier, richer, or wiser. This would mean that things now so readily available, so constant, so close at hand, and in some fashion born upon the very lips, did not exist in former times, before letters sprouted in the world and before Mercury, I suppose, had been born.

And from where did the very system of letters obtain the ability to know and disseminate in spoken use things that no mind had ever conceived, no tongue uttered, and no ear received [Col. 0617A]?

[Note: [Col. 0616C] “Did it obtain? From where did it know?” Junius.]

But since the divine Scriptures, which are in our possession or that of the Jews—into whose wild olive tree we have been grafted—precede secular literature by far more than merely some modest period, as we have demonstrated in the proper place to establish their credibility, then even if the soul borrowed these utterances from writings, it must surely be believed to have borrowed them from ours, not from yours. For earlier writings are more potent in instructing the soul than later ones, which were themselves waiting to be instructed by those earlier writings.

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Into whose olive tree we have been grafted from the wild olive,” Latinus and Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Were waiting to be instructed.” That is, they were awaiting it. RIG.]

Even if we concede that the soul was instructed from your writings, the tradition nevertheless belongs to its first and principal origin; and whatever you happened to receive from our writings and hand on is wholly ours. Since this is so, it matters little whether the soul’s consciousness was formed by God [Col. 0617B] or by the writings of God. Why then do you wish, O human being, to maintain that these things came forth from the human opinions of your writings and entered the callousness of common usage?

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Into the callousness of common usage.” He means custom, which through long use has drawn over itself, as it were, a callus.]

## CHAPTER VI

Believe, therefore, your own writings; and from our records believe all the more in the divine writings; but from the judgment of the soul herself believe nature in the same way. Choose which of these you regard as the more faithful sister of truth.

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Faithful,” Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Regard as the sister of truth.” That is, which comes nearer to the truth, τῆς ἀληθέιας ἀδελφήν (“the sister of truth”). LE PR.]

If you doubt your own writings, neither God nor nature lies. That you may believe both nature and God, believe the soul; thus it will come about that you believe yourself as well. She is certainly the soul whom you value in proportion to how much she makes you what you are; to whom you belong entirely; who is everything to you; without whom you can neither live nor die; and for whose sake you neglect God.

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “As highly,” Junius.]

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “For whose sake you neglect God.” That is, you fear becoming a Christian lest, together with us, you lay down your soul—that is, your life—for the name of Christ. RIG.]

For since you fear becoming a Christian [Col. 0618A], confront her: why, while worshiping another, does she name God? Why, when she identifies spirits that are to be cursed, does she call them demons? Why does she call heaven to witness, yet use the earth in imprecation? Why does she serve in one place, but appeal to an avenger in another? Why does she pass judgment on the dead? Why does she possess the words of Christians, whom she wishes neither to hear nor to see? Why did she either give those words to us, or receive them from us? Why did she either teach or learn them?

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Why does she call heaven to witness?” Macrobius, book III, chapter 9, and Servius on *Aeneid* III note that the ancients, according to the character of the deities, sometimes looked downward and sometimes upward. *Contestari* means to summon someone openly as a witness. Cicero, *Against Verres*: “Calling gods and human beings to witness.” Cicero likewise uses *contestata* in *On Divination*: “For whom would authority, attested and sealed by the most illustrious records, not move?” *Detestor*, however, means “I curse” and “I abominate.” LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0617D] “Why does she serve in one place, but appeal to an avenger in another?” He alludes to what he said above in chapter 2: “Finally, in the very temples you implore God as judge.” Here, however, he suggests that God is invoked by them not merely as judge but also as avenger—that is, as redeemer and champion—as our Reynardus explains more fully in chapters 8 and 9 when citing this passage in connection with the laws of the Twelve Tables. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Nor,” Rigaltius.]

Hold suspect the agreement of proclamation amid so great a disagreement of conduct.

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “You have,” Junius and Rigaltius.]

You are foolish if you assign utterances of this kind to the Latin language alone, or to Greek, languages considered closely related to one another, and on that basis deny the universality of nature.

[Note: [Col. 0618C] “Which are considered closely related to one another.” Pliny writes that the fact that the ancient Greek letters were almost the same as those that are now Latin is evidence of this. PAM.]

The soul does not fall from heaven for Latins and Argives alone. Among all nations, “human being” is one name: there is one soul but various voices, one spirit but various sounds; each nation has its own language, but the substance of language is common.

[Note: [Col. 0617C] “Among all nations there is one human being but a various name,” Junius and Rigaltius. “Among all nations there is a various name,” Wouwer.]

God is everywhere, and God’s goodness everywhere; the demon is everywhere, and the curse upon the demon everywhere; the invocation of divine judgment everywhere; death everywhere, and consciousness of death everywhere, and testimony everywhere [Col. 0618B].

[Note: [Col. 0618C] “God is everywhere,” etc. “He is present everywhere and fills all things,” Ρὁ πανταχοῦ παρὼν καὶ πάντα πληρῶν (“present everywhere and filling all things”; the anomalous initial Ρ is preserved as recovered), says St. Chrysostom, Oration 2 *Against the Jews*. LE PR.]

Every soul, by her own right, proclaims what we are not permitted even to mutter. Deservedly, therefore, every soul is both guilty and a witness: guilty of error to the same degree that she is a witness to truth. And she will stand before the courts of God on the day of judgment, having nothing to say:

“You proclaimed God, yet did not seek him; you abominated demons, yet worshiped them; you appealed to God’s judgment, yet did not believe it existed; you anticipated punishments in the lower regions, yet took no precautions against them; you understood the Christian name, yet persecuted the Christian.”

[Note: [Col. 0618C] “Nor,” Wouwer and Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0618D] “And she will stand before the courts of God,” Rhenanus; and *stabilis*. Nothing should be changed. “Before the courts of God” means before the palaces of God, unless perhaps the expression relates to sheep and *caulas Dei*, “the folds of God,” should be read; nevertheless, nothing should be changed. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0618C] “Having nothing to say, but hearing it said: ‘You proclaimed God,’” Junius.]

# Book to the Martyrs

[Col. 0619]

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIANUS TO THE MARTYRS

[Note: [Col. 0619B] This book—or rather address—to Confessors, whom he calls “Martyrs” according to the ancient manner of speaking, was written around the year 197, in the judgment of the distinguished Naesselt. Tertullian was then still wholly Catholic, and it was approximately the time when Severus had launched an inquiry against those who had followed the parties of Niger and Albinus, as these words of Tertullian himself suggest (*To the Martyrs*, chapter 6): “In this matter, indeed, let even the present times serve you as evidence of how many persons, and of what kind, undergo deaths unexpected in view of their birth, rank, physical condition, and age, for the sake of a human being—either at his own hands, if they acted against him, or at the hands of his adversaries, if they stood for him.”

This book is an ardent exhortation to the Martyrs. In it he vigorously stirs and encourages those who were held in chains for the sake of the Christian religion to constancy in the faith and cheerfulness in enduring the squalor of prison and the punishments threatening them. Above all, however, he urges them to peace—the peace that those who did not possess it from the Church sought from the martyrs.

[Col. 0619C] At the beginning he praises the devotion of the faithful, who supplied bodily nourishment to the Martyrs in prison. He then exhorts the Martyrs themselves not to wish to grieve the Holy Spirit, but to preserve him in such a way that they maintain peace and concord with one another—the peace which those who did not possess it in the Church customarily entreated from the Martyrs in prison. “And therefore you ought, for that very reason, to possess, foster, and preserve it among yourselves, so that perhaps you may also be able to confer it upon others” (ibid., chapter 1).

From Tertullian’s words learned men conclude that he composed the present treatise while in communion with the Catholic Church. For after becoming a Montanist he held all these things in derision, as is clear from *On Modesty*, chapter XXII, and *On Fasting*, chapter XII.

In order to give the Martyrs courage to endure the squalor of prison, Tertullian proposes that the world itself is more truly a prison; and immediately he enumerates the benefits flowing from their detention in prison, arguing thus: “You have been separated from the world itself—how much more from the age and its affairs? … You do not see alien gods; you do not encounter their images; you do not participate, by the very fact of mingling with them, in the nations’ solemn days; you are not lashed by foul odors; [Col. 0620B] you are not struck by the shouting of the spectacles or by the cruelty, frenzy, or shamelessness of those celebrating them; your eyes do not fall upon places of public lust. You are free from scandals, temptations, evil recollections, and now even from persecution. Prison affords the Christian what the wilderness afforded the Prophets.… Let us remove the name ‘prison’ and call it ‘retirement.’ Although the body is enclosed and the flesh detained, all things lie open to the spirit. Wander in spirit, stroll in spirit, setting before yourself not shaded tracks or long colonnades, but that road which leads to God. As often as you walk upon it in spirit, so often you will not be in prison. The leg feels nothing in the stocks when the mind is in heaven” (ibid., chapter II).

Afterwards he attempts to rouse the Martyrs still further to patience and constancy by an argument drawn from the obligation, contracted in baptism, of serving as soldiers in God’s service. “Let prison now,” he says, “be troublesome even to Christians. We were summoned to the army of the living God at the very time when we responded to the words of the oath. No soldier comes to war with luxuries, nor does he proceed from the bedchamber to the battle line.… [Col. 0620C] Even in peace they learn by labor and hardships how to endure war.… Everything is achieved through sweat, lest bodies and spirits take fright when passing from shade to sunlight, from sunlight to the sky” (ibid., chapter III).

Finally, so that the Confessors may not allow themselves to be overcome by the severity of tortures, he adduces the examples of gentile men and women who bravely endured torture and death from the motive of vain glory alone (ibid., chapters IV and V). In the judgment of the very learned Dupin, nothing is more forceful in this brief address or better suited to move the emotions (Dupin, *Bibliothèque des auteurs*, vol. I).

In treating this subject, Tertullian was afterwards imitated by the blessed Cyprian in his book *On the Exhortation and Praise of Martyrdom* and in his letters to the Martyrs and Confessors.

Moreover, almost all learned men believe that Septimius addressed this discourse to martyrs held captive in some town under Carthaginian jurisdiction, or even in Carthage itself, on account of these words: “Finally, it awaits the judgments not of the Proconsul but of God” (Tertullian, *Treatise to the Martyrs*, chapter II). For the *Notitia Imperii* makes very frequent mention of the Proconsul of Africa. LUMP.]

## SUMMARY

With the fifth persecution under Severus now immediately threatening, Septimius wrote this short work, in which he exhorts the martyrs to constancy in the faith and cheerfulness in undergoing the squalor of prison and the decrees of the judges. Above all, however, he invites them to the peace that those who did not possess it in the Church sought from the martyrs.

## CHAPTER ONE

Among the provisions for the flesh, blessed martyrs-elect, which both our lady mother the Church supplies to you in prison from her own breasts and individual brethren from their own resources, receive something also from us that may serve to nourish the spirit as well.

[Col. 0620A]

[Note: [Col. 0620C] “Blessed martyrs.” That is, “worthy of praise.” Tertullian adorns the martyrs with this epithet, as shortly afterwards, “First of all, therefore, blessed ones,” and below in *To the Martyrs*. He also applied it above to Eleutherius, the Roman pontiff and martyr, after his death. Cyprian repeatedly calls them “most valiant and most blessed,” and in one place “most valiant and most faithful.” RHEN.]

[Note: [Col. 0620A] Others omit “elect” as superfluous.]

[Note: [Col. 0619A] “Works,” Rhenanus, Franeker, and Paris.]

For it does no good for the flesh to be fattened while the spirit goes hungry. Indeed, if that which is weak is cared for, that which is weaker must likewise not be neglected.

[Note: [Col. 0619A] August Neander emends “weaker” to “stronger.”]

I am not so important a person as to address you; nevertheless, even the most accomplished gladiators are exhorted from a distance not only by their own trainers and overseers, but also by any uninstructed and superfluous persons, since suggestions shouted even by the crowd itself have often proved useful.

First of all, therefore, blessed ones, do not grieve the Holy Spirit (Eph. IV, 3), who entered the prison with you. For if he had not entered with you then, you would not be there today. And therefore take care that he remain there with you, so that he may lead you from there to the Lord.

Prison is indeed the devil’s house, in which he confines his own household. But you entered prison for this purpose: that you might trample upon him even in his own house. For you had already encountered and trampled upon him outside. Let him not therefore say: “They are in my domain; I shall tempt them with contemptible splinters”—with passions or dissensions among themselves [Col. 0621A].

[Note: [Col. 0621B] “Hatreds,” Franeker; “fastings,” Rhenanus; for “contemptible,” Junius prefers “useful”; Rigaltius restored “splinters.”]

[Note: [Col. 0621B] “Privations,” Rhenanus.]

[Note: [Col. 0621B] “They are in my domain; I shall tempt them with contemptible hatreds.” Pamelius attests that this was the Vatican manuscripts’ reading. But no Christian will agree that hatreds existed among the martyrs themselves, and contemptible hatreds at that. Moreover, if Tertullian related that there were hatreds among the martyrs, he certainly added a futile and frivolous reproach concerning passions and dissensions. For what hatreds exist without passions and dissension? Indeed, Satan’s stratagem would have been exceedingly feeble: “The martyrs who have been driven into prison are in my domain; I shall therefore tempt them with contemptible hatreds.” Whom does he not tempt every day with hatreds of this kind—even when they are not in his domain, even outside prison? I therefore do not see how the Vatican reading can be defended.

In editions published before Pamelius the text read: “I shall tempt them with contemptible fastings, privations, or dissensions among themselves.” But no satisfactory sense can be derived from that either. For what sane person ever spoke of “contemptible fastings”? Or why should we suspect any fasting here, amid the provisions for the flesh which our lady mother the Church was supplying to these very martyrs in prison from her own breasts? This is stated most explicitly at the beginning of this work, and it also sufficiently refutes Rhenanus’s interpretation, which applies the word “privations” to a scarcity of provisions. Therefore, neither do those pre-Pamelian editions solve the difficulty.

Whoever copied the Dijon manuscript pointed toward the source of the truth. For there the reading is: “I shall tempt them with contemptiblesplinters, privations, or dissensions among themselves.” Let us separate what has wrongly been joined, and we have the author’s genuine reading: “I shall tempt them with contemptible splinters.”

Tertullian had heard that among the martyrs there were certain seeds, not of hatred, but of apostasy and dissension. Some perhaps argued that the authority of the Scriptures did not prohibit them from using money paid to extortioners to avert their chains and death itself. He therefore exhorts them to take particular care that Satan not devise plots against them, turning over in his mind the following thoughts: “I once tempted human beings when they were not in my domain—namely, when they were in paradise—and tempted them with the most excellent fruit of a certain divine tree. Those people today, who are in my domain—namely, in prison—I shall also tempt, but with contemptible splinters.”

It is as though he said: “Formerly, when humanity was tempted in paradise, a temptation of great magnificence had to be employed—namely, the desire for the fruit that would make gods out of human beings. But now I shall tempt those placed in my domain, since they are in prison and the kingdom of darkness, with trifles and rubbish: for example, fear of some momentary distress, or the desire to prolong for a few years a life that dies day by day. Finally, I shall set them against one another and harass them with dissensions.”

These are Satan’s readily available weapons, lying close at hand, as though made from worthless brushwood and the combustible splinters of trees. *Scidiae* or *scidia*, whence also *discidia*, “divisions,” are σχίδακες, κλάσματα ξύλων (“splinters, fragments of wood”), cheap cuttings from felled trees. Vitruvius, book II, chapter 1: “They stop up the gaps with splinters and mud.” RIG.]

Let him flee from your sight; let him shrink back and hide in his lowest depths, benumbed like a serpent charmed or smoked out. Let him not fare so well in his own kingdom that he sets you against one another; rather, let him find you fortified and armed with concord, because your peace is war against him.

Some who did not possess that peace in the Church were accustomed to entreat it from the martyrs in prison. And for that reason too you ought to possess it among yourselves, foster it, and preserve it, so that perhaps you may be able to confer it upon others as well.

[Note: [Col. 0622C] “That peace some,” etc. These words must first be understood of peace or reconciliation with the other Christians and with God. For the custom had grown up that those detained in prison by the tyranny would give certificates, upon request, to believers undergoing penance; with the aid of these they obtained the peace of God and entered into communion with the other brethren.

These statements also contribute very greatly to the authority of indulgences, whose perhaps excessive facility Cyprian once criticized. In the last century the Council of Trent, convened against the heresy that had sprung up under the pretext of abuses concerning indulgences, confronted both the heresy and the abuse. It confronted the heresy by defining that it belongs to the Church to grant indulgences—for the blessed Paul once granted them to Alexander and Hymenaeus. It confronted the abuse by desiring that moderation be applied in granting them, in accordance with the ancient and approved custom of the Church, etc.; Session 25, Decree on Indulgences. It thereby refers us to the ancient canons of councils, such as the many canons of Ancyra, Neocaesarea, Carthage, Agde, etc. LE PR.]

## CHAPTER II

Let the other impediments of the mind have accompanied you only as far as the threshold [Col. 0621B] of the prison, as did your parents. From that point you were separated from the world itself—how much more from the age and its affairs?

Do not let the fact that you have been separated from the world dismay you [Col. 0622A].

[Note: [Col. 0621B] “From the world” is absent from Rigaltius.]

For if we reflect that the world itself is more truly a prison, we shall understand that you have gone out of prison rather than entered one. The world possesses deeper darkness, which blinds the hearts of human beings. The world puts on heavier chains, which bind human souls themselves. The world breathes forth fouler impurities: human lusts. Finally, the world contains more defendants—namely, the entire human race. In short, it awaits the judgments not of the Proconsul but of God.

[Note: [Col. 0622B] “Prisons,” Rigaltius.]

[Note: [Col. 0622B] “Imbues,” Semler.]

[Note: [Col. 0622D] “In short, it awaits the judgments not of the Proconsul but of God.” *Sustinet* means “awaits.” RIG.—Permit us to doubt the interpretation of the learned men who have won some reputation for themselves in this field. Almost all of them believe that the captives were held in some town under Carthaginian jurisdiction, or even in Carthage itself, because of the word “Proconsul”; for, they say, the *Notitia Imperii* makes very frequent mention of the Proconsul of Africa. Yet no one is unaware that magistracies were changed according to the emperors’ pleasure. LE PR.]

You may therefore consider yourselves, blessed ones, perhaps transferred from prison into a place of safekeeping. It has darkness, but you yourselves are light. It has bonds, but you are free for God. A foul odor is breathed forth there, but you are an odor of sweetness. A judge is awaited, but you are going to judge the judges themselves (Wis. III) [Col. 0622B].

[Note: [Col. 0622B] Rhenanus adds “concerning the judges.”]

Let the person who sighs for the fruit of this age be sorrowful there. Even outside prison, the Christian renounced the age; in prison, he renounces prison too. It makes no difference where you are within the age, since you are outside the age [Col. 0623A]. And if you have lost some of life’s pleasures, it is a commercial transaction to lose something in order to gain greater things.

[Note: [Col. 0623C] “You have lost,” Junius.]

I say nothing yet about the reward to which God invites the martyrs. Meanwhile, let us compare the very modes of life of the age and of prison, and see whether the spirit does not gain more in prison than the flesh loses. Indeed, through the Church’s care and the brethren’s love-feast, the flesh does not even lose the things that are its rightful due; and the spirit gains in addition those things that are always beneficial to faith.

You do not see alien gods; you do not encounter their images; you do not participate, by the very fact of mingling with them, in the nations’ solemn days; you are not lashed by foul odors; you are not struck by the shouting of the spectacles or by the cruelty, frenzy, or shamelessness of those celebrating them; your eyes do not fall upon places of public lust. You are free from scandals [Col. 0623B], temptations, evil recollections, and now even from persecution.

Prison affords the Christian what the wilderness afforded the prophets (Mark I). The Lord himself more frequently dwelt in retirement, so that he might pray more freely and withdraw from the age (Luke IV). Finally, it was in solitude that he displayed his glory to his disciples (Matt. XVII; Mark IX; Luke II; 1 Peter 1).

Let us remove the name “prison” and call it “retirement.” Although the body is enclosed and the flesh detained, all things lie open to the spirit. Wander in spirit, stroll in spirit, setting before yourself not shaded tracks or long colonnades, but that road which leads to God.

[Note: [Col. 0623C] “Shaded tracks.” With trees arranged in a long row on either side. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0623C] “Setting before yourself,” Franeker and Paris.]

As often as you walk upon it in spirit, so often you will not be in prison. The leg feels nothing in the stocks when the mind is in heaven.

[Note: [Col. 0623D] “The leg feels nothing in the stocks.” Here *nervus* is a kind of wooden device pierced by many holes, which tyrants used to torture the martyrs, so that their legs, forced apart and dislocated, would be broken. This kind of punishment is called ξύλον (“wood,” here a wooden instrument of restraint) by certain Greeks, but by many of the more ancient writers κᾶλον (“wood,” likewise referring to that instrument of restraint); the two terms signify the same thing. Scaliger also taught me that defendants at Athens were called ἐγκαλοσκελεῖς (“those whose legs were confined in the stocks”). LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0623C] “When the hand,” Semler.]

The mind carries the whole person about and transfers him wherever it wishes (Matt. VI): “Where your heart is, there also will your treasure be” [Col. 0623C]. Therefore let our heart be where we wish to have our treasure.
