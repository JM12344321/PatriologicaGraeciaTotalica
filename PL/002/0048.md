Nothing is without an origin, except God alone. Just as origin necessarily takes precedence in establishing the condition of all things, so it must also take precedence in their examination, so that their condition may be established [Col. 0468D]. For you cannot discern what sort of thing something is unless, once you have learned whence it comes, you are certain that it exists. [Note: [Col. 0467D] “Once” (*cum*) omitted by Junius.]

And therefore, having been brought by the order of this little work to the present subject, I too demand from Marcion the origin of the apostle Paul—as some new disciple, a hearer of no one else, who meanwhile believes nothing except that nothing is to be believed rashly. For whatever is believed without acknowledgment of its origin is believed rashly. I am all the more justified in reducing this inquiry to a matter of careful concern, since the man affirmed to me as an apostle is one whom [Note: [Col. 0467D] “In the register of the apostles.” Here *album* means a catalogue [Col. 0468D] of the apostles; for among the jurists an *album* [Col. 0469D] is a register, or a section containing the names of the decurions, whence elsewhere *proscriptio*, in *Herennius* 10, Digest, *De decurionibus et filiis*. In much the same sense Dionysius, book LV, called the roll of senators λεύκωμα τῶν βουλευόντων, “the register of those serving in the senate,” and Suetonius spoke of the register of cithara-players in *Nero*, chap. 20. LE PR.] I do not find in the register of the apostles [Col. 0469A] in the Gospel.

Then, on hearing afterward that he was chosen by the Lord when the latter was already at rest in heaven, I take this as a kind of lack of foresight, if Christ did not know beforehand that Paul would be necessary to him, but only after the office of apostleship had already been organized and dispatched upon its work decided, as an afterthought rather than by forethought, that he should be added—by necessity, so to speak, rather than by choice.

Therefore, shipmaster of Pontus, if you have never received stolen or illicit goods [Note: [Col. 0469D] “Into your light vessels.” An *acatus*, either ἄκατος or ἀκάτιον, is a kind of vessel propelled by sails rather than oars. LE PR.] into your light vessels; if you have never diverted or adulterated any cargo at all, then, as one presumably still more cautious and trustworthy in matters concerning God, I should like you to disclose to us under what bill of lading you received the apostle Paul. Who stamped him with the mark of his title? Who consigned him to you? Who placed him aboard, so that you may produce him with confidence? Otherwise he may be shown to belong to the one who has produced all [Col. 0469B] the documentary evidence of his apostleship.

“He himself,” you say, “professed himself to be an apostle” (Gal. 1:1), and indeed “not from men nor through a man, but through Jesus Christ.” [Note: [Col. 0469D] Certainly anyone can make a declaration concerning himself. Tertullian is speaking of the declaration made before the public records. For we “profess” when we voluntarily declare something publicly and before the records, as when we declare merchandise so that the state may not be defrauded in the payment of duty. Hence in the Pandects Marcian calls merchandise upon which no duty has been paid *merces improfessas*, “undeclared goods.” The ancients likewise registered before the public records children born to them; this provided both certain and ready proof of age if any doubt of this kind should arise. In this sense Seneca elegantly speaks of “declaring a debt,” something candidates and defendants were compelled to do, *De Beneficiis*, book VI. Livy speaks of “declaring grain,” book IV, decade 1, where the commonly printed copies incorrectly read “by compelling them to confess the grain.” RHEN.]

Certainly anyone can make a declaration concerning himself. But his declaration is authenticated by another’s authority: one person writes it, another subscribes it, another seals it, and another enters it into the public records. No one is both his own declarant and his own witness. Besides this, you have surely read (Luke 21:8) that many will come saying, “I am Christ.” If there is one who falsely claims to be Christ, how much more readily might someone proclaim himself an apostle of Christ?

I am still acting in the character of a disciple and inquirer, so that from this point I may both blunt your faith, since you have no means of proving its source, and put your impudence to shame, since you lay claim to him but do not accept the source from which you could substantiate your claim. Let Christ be another’s [Col. 0469C], let the Apostle be another’s, provided that they cannot be proved except from the documentary record of the Creator. [Note: [Col. 0469D] “Thus … thus,” according to others.] For [Note: [Col. 0469D] “Not,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] Genesis long ago also promised Paul to me.

For among those figures and prophetic blessings concerning his sons, Jacob, when he had turned to Benjamin, said (Gen. 49:27): “Benjamin is a ravening wolf; in the morning he will still devour, and in the evening he will give food.” For he foresaw that Paul would arise from the tribe of Benjamin: a ravening wolf, devouring in the morning—that is, in his early years devastating the Lord’s flock as a persecutor of the churches—and afterward giving food in the evening—that is, as his life was already declining, nourishing Christ’s sheep as the teacher of the nations. [Note: [Col. 0470D] “When he had said,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.]

Likewise, Saul’s initial savagery in persecuting David, followed by his repentance [Col. 0470A] and satisfaction toward the man who returned good for evil, foreshadowed nothing other than Paul in Saul, according to their tribe, and Jesus in David, according to the Virgin’s lineage.

If these mysteries of the figures displease you, at least the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 9) have handed down to me this account of Paul, which you too must not deny. From that source I demonstrate that the persecutor became an apostle, “not from men nor through a man”; from that source I am also led to believe him; from that source I exclude you from defending him. Nor do I fear your saying, “Then you deny the apostle Paul?” I do not blaspheme the man whom I defend. I deny him in order to compel you to prove him. I deny him in order to establish that he is mine.

Either, if you look to our faith, accept the things that establish it; or, if you appeal to your own, produce the things that lay its foundation. Either prove that what you believe exists, or, if you do not prove it, how [Col. 0470B] do you believe? Or what sort of believer are you, opposing the very source by which alone what you believe is proved to exist?

You now have the Apostle from my possessions, just as you have Christ: the Apostle is as much mine as Christ is. [Note: [Col. 0470D] “Here too we shall fight within the same lines.” He gladly refers proverbially to “lines,” as in “back to the lines” and “to draw lines beforehand.” RHEN.] Here too we shall fight within the same lines. At the very outset we shall invoke the preliminary objection that an apostle who is denied to belong to the Creator—and indeed is brought forward against the Creator—must teach nothing, understand nothing, and will nothing in accordance with the Creator; and, above all, that he must proclaim another God with a firmness equal to that with which he broke away from the Creator’s law.

For it is not plausible that, while turning people away from Judaism, he would not at the same time show them toward faith in which God he was turning them, since no one could pass away from the Creator without knowing to whom he had to pass. If Christ had already revealed another God, the Apostle’s testimony would necessarily follow [Col. 0470C], both so that he might not be regarded as the apostle of a God other than the one Christ had revealed, and because the one who had already been revealed by Christ could not lawfully be concealed by the Apostle. But if Christ had revealed nothing of this sort concerning God, then it was all the more necessary that he be revealed by the Apostle, since by then he could not be revealed by another; and such a god would undoubtedly not be worthy of belief if he had not been revealed even by the Apostle.

We have laid this foundation so that from this point onward we may declare that we shall likewise prove that the Apostle circulated no other God, just as we have proved that Christ did not—proving it, of course, from Paul’s own epistles. The fact that these have likewise been mutilated even in their number will already have to be prejudged from the established character of the heretic’s Gospel.

## CHAPTER II

[Note: [Col. 0470D] “The principal epistle against Judaism,” etc. This had crept from the margin into the text; we have restored it to the [Col. 0471D] margin, both here and everywhere below. It is, in fact, the title of this chapter: “The Epistle to the Galatians,” to which we have added “from the chapter.” Yet the phrase “the principal epistle against Judaism” bears against modern heretics, who pervert the meaning of the Apostle’s words and do not hesitate to cite the words of this epistle, chapter 4, not against Judaism but against the Church’s observances of feast days. PAMEL.]

We too acknowledge that the epistle which instructs the Galatians is the principal epistle against Judaism (Gal. 1) [Col. 0471A]. For we embrace that entire abolition of the old law as a dispensation proceeding from the Creator himself, just as we have already discussed often in the course of this work with regard to the renewal foretold by the prophets of our God.

For if the Creator promised that the old things would cease, evidently because new things were to arise, while Christ, when he marked the time of this distinction—“The Law and the Prophets were until John”—set in John the boundary between the two orders, so that from that point the old things ended and the new began, then necessarily the Apostle, under Christ revealed after John, invalidates the old things but confirms the new. In this way he advances the faith of no God other than the Creator, with whom the passing away of the old things had also been foretold.

Therefore both the destruction of the Law and the building up of the Gospel support my position [Col. 0471B] in this epistle as well. They concern that presumption of the Galatians by which they presumed that Christ, as belonging to the Creator, must be believed in while the Creator’s law remained intact—for it would still have seemed incredible that a law should be abolished by its own author.

But if they had heard of an altogether different God from the Apostle, they would of course have known of their own accord that they must withdraw from the law of the God whom they had abandoned when they followed another. For who would wait any longer to be taught that he ought to follow a new discipline after he had received a new God? Rather, because the same divinity that had always been known in the Law was proclaimed in the Gospel, but the discipline was not the same, the entire issue in dispute was whether the Creator’s law ought to be excluded by the Creator’s Gospel in the Creator’s Christ. Take away this issue, and the question becomes pointless.

If the question were pointless, and everyone already recognized of his own accord [Col. 0471C] that through faith in another God he must depart from the Creator’s order, the Apostle would have had no subject matter for teaching so emphatically what faith itself would spontaneously have dictated.

Therefore the whole intention of this epistle teaches nothing other than that departure from the Law [Note: [Col. 0471D] “Cessation,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür; *dicessionem*, Pamelius.] which proceeds from the Creator’s dispensation, as we shall further demonstrate. Likewise, if he brings forward no mention of any new God—something he would have done nowhere more appropriately than in this subject, so as to set forth, in the single and wholly sufficient definition of a new divinity, the reason for dismissing the Law—it is evident in what sense he writes:

“I marvel that you are so quickly being transferred from him who called you into grace, to another gospel”: another in manner of life, not in religion; in discipline, not in divinity. For the Gospel of Christ ought to call them away from the Law to grace, not away from [Col. 0471D] the Creator to another God. No one had moved them away from the Creator, so that they might seem to be transferred to another Gospel precisely while being transferred to the Creator.

Moreover, by adding that there was in fact no other Gospel, he confirms as belonging to the Creator the Gospel whose existence he defends. For [Col. 0472A] the Creator too promises a Gospel, saying through Isaiah (Isa. 40:9): “Ascend the high mountain, you who bring good news to Zion; lift up your voice in your strength, you who bring good news to Jerusalem.” Likewise, in the person of the apostles (Isa. 52:7): “How timely are the feet of those who proclaim peace, who proclaim good things”—surely proclaiming them to the nations as well, since he also says (Isa. 42:4), “In his name the nations will hope,” that is, in Christ’s, to whom he says, “I have appointed you as a light to the nations.”

Suppose [Note: [Col. 0471D] Perhaps *esto*, “suppose,” should be read.] there is also a Gospel of the new God you desire, and that this was the one defended by the Apostle. There are now, then, two Gospels belonging to two Gods, and the Apostle will have lied when he said that there was no other at all, since there is another. He could have defended his Gospel by demonstrating its distinct character, rather than by defining it as the one Gospel.

But perhaps [Col. 0472B], to escape from this difficulty, you will say that this is why [Note: [Col. 0471D] “Here I see,” Latinius.] he added: “Even if an angel from heaven should proclaim a different gospel, let him be accursed”—because he knew that the Creator too would proclaim a gospel. Once again, then, you entangle yourself. For this is precisely what binds you fast. To affirm two Gospels is not the act of one who has already denied that there is another.

Yet the meaning is clear from his placing his own person first: “But even if we, or an angel from heaven, should proclaim a different gospel.” For this was said by way of example. Otherwise, if he himself was not going to proclaim a different gospel, then surely neither was an angel. Thus he named an angel in order to show that men were all the less to be believed when neither an angel nor an apostle was to be believed. He was not referring the angel to the Creator’s Gospel.

From there, as he runs through the course of his conversion from persecutor to apostle, he confirms the text of the Acts [Note: [Col. 0472D] “Of Acts” omitted by Junius.] of the Apostles, in which [Col. 0472C] the very subject of this epistle is also recognized: certain persons had intervened [Note: [Col. 0472D] “Certain persons had intervened,” etc. That is, certain persons had come between and opposed them. RHEN.] who said that circumcision was necessary and that the law of Moses must be observed. The apostles were then consulted about this question, and by the authority of the Spirit reported that burdens must not be imposed upon men which their fathers themselves had been unable to bear.

Now if the Acts of the Apostles agree with Paul in this matter as well, the reason why you reject them is already apparent: namely, because they preach no God other than the Creator and no Christ belonging to any other than the Creator, since even the promise of the Holy Spirit cannot be proved to have been fulfilled from any source other than the documentary record of Acts.

It is surely not plausible that Acts should agree with the Apostle in one respect, when they set forth his history in accordance with his own testimony, yet disagree in another when they announce the Creator’s divinity in Christ; for Paul did indeed follow the apostles’ preaching in every respect [Col. 0472D], since he received from them the rule for teaching the abolition of the Law. [Note: [Col. 0472D] “As though Paul had not followed the apostles’ preaching in every respect,” Pamelius.]

## CHAPTER III

Finally, he writes (Gal. 2–3) that after fourteen years he went up to Jerusalem to obtain the support of Peter and the other [Col. 0473A] apostles, so that he might compare with them the rule of his Gospel, lest for so many years he had run, or was still running, in vain—that is, if in any respect he was proclaiming the Gospel outside their rule. To such an extent had he desired [Note: [Col. 0473D] “He desired,” Latinius.] to be approved and confirmed by them.

But if at any time you wish [Note: [Col. 0473D] “But if those whom you sometimes wish,” Latinius.] the apostles to be understood [Note: [Col. 0473D] “To be understood,” Latinius.] as more closely allied with Judaism, since [Note: [Col. 0473D] Rhenanus, Semler, and Oberthür add “however.”] he says that not even [Note: [Col. 0473D] “Not even” omitted by Latinius.] Titus was circumcised, he now begins to show that the question of circumcision alone, arising from their continuing defense of the Law, was agitated by those whom for this reason he calls false and intruding [Note: [Col. 0474D] “Introduced,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] brothers. They were pressing for the establishment of nothing other than the continuance of the Law, undoubtedly from an unimpaired faith in the Creator, and were thus perverting the Gospel—not by an interpolation of Scripture [Note: [Col. 0473D] “Interpolation of Scripture.” An interpolator, or corrupter, is a heretic who distorts, truncates, diminishes, or adds to Holy Scripture. LE PR.] through which they might fashion Christ as belonging to the Creator, but by retaining [Col. 0473B] the old discipline so that they might not exclude the Creator’s law.

“Therefore,” he says, “because of the intruding false brothers who slipped in to spy upon our liberty, which we have in Christ, so that they might reduce us to slavery, we did not yield in submission even for an hour.”

Let us attend both to the meaning itself and to its cause, and the corruption of the text will become apparent. He first says: “But not even Titus, who was with me, although he was a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised”; then he adds, “because of the intruding false brothers,” and the rest. He plainly begins to give the reason for an action contrary to the preceding statement, showing why he did it—an action he would neither have done nor explained if the circumstance for which he did it had not occurred.

Finally, I should like you to say: if those false brothers had slipped in to spy upon their liberty, would they have yielded in submission? I think not. Therefore they did yield [Col. 0473C], because there were persons [Note: [Col. 0474D] “Would they not have yielded in submission? I think they would. Therefore they had yielded, because there were persons,” the Wouwer manuscript.] on whose account they had to yield. For this was appropriate to an immature faith still hesitating over observance of the Law, when the Apostle himself was uncertain whether he had run, or was running, in vain.

The false brothers who were spying upon Christian liberty therefore had to be frustrated, lest they carry it off into the slavery of Judaism before Paul knew that he had not run in vain, before his predecessors gave him the right hand of fellowship, and before, on their authorization, he undertook [Note: [Col. 0474D] “Discharged,” Latinius.] the duty of preaching among the nations.

He therefore necessarily yielded for a time. This is consistent with his reason for circumcising Timothy and bringing men with shaven heads into the temple, events recorded in Acts (Acts 16; 21). They are so true that they agree with the Apostle when he declares (1 Cor. 9:20) that he became a Jew to the Jews in order to gain the Jews, and behaved as one under the Law on account of [Col. 0473D] those who were under the Law. Thus too, on account of those intruding men [Note: [Col. 0474D] “Those introduced men,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.], he finally became all things to all people [Col. 0474A], so that he might gain them all.

If they demand that these matters too be understood from this passage, no one will doubt [Note: [Col. 0474D] “No one will doubt,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] that Paul was a preacher of that God and Christ whose law, though excluding it, he nevertheless admitted temporarily as circumstances required—a law which would have had to be cast off immediately if he had brought forward a new God.

It is therefore fitting that Peter, James, and John gave Paul the right hand of fellowship and made an agreement concerning the distribution of their duties: Paul was to go to the nations, and they to the circumcision, provided only that they remembered the poor. This too was in accordance with the law of the Creator, who sustains the poor and needy, as was proved in our examination of your Gospel.

Thus it is established that the question concerned the Law alone, since the dispute reveals what it was appropriate to preserve from the Law. [Note: [Col. 0473D] “But he rebuked Peter for not walking with a straight foot.” In the Greek the word is ὀρθοποδοῦντα, “walking straight.” Nevertheless, concerning that rebuke one should note what Eusebius has in his *History*, book [Col. 0474D], chap. 12, where, on the authority of St. Clement, he says that this Cephas was someone other than blessed Peter: ἕνά φησί γεγονέναι τῶν ἑβδομήκοντα μαθητῶν, ὁμώνυμον Πέτρῳ τυχόντα τῷ ἀποστόλῳ, “He says that this man was one of the seventy disciples who happened to bear the same name as the apostle Peter.” LE PR.]

But he rebuked Peter because he was not walking with a straight foot according to the truth of the Gospel. Certainly he rebuked him; yet for no other reason than his inconsistency in eating, since he varied his practice according to the identity of those present, fearing those who were of the circumcision. He did not rebuke him for any perversion concerning the divinity, on account of which he would also have resisted others to their faces, since he did not spare Peter himself over the lesser matter of an ambiguous manner of life. But how do the Marcionites want this to be believed?

For the rest, let the Apostle proceed in denying that a man is justified by works of the Law, but rather by faith—yet by faith in the same God to whom the Law also belongs. For he would not have labored to distinguish faith from the Law if a difference in the divinity itself, had it existed, would automatically have distinguished them. Rightly, then, he did not rebuild the things he had destroyed.

The Law, however, had to be destroyed from the time when John’s voice cried out in the wilderness (Luke 3): “Prepare the ways of the Lord,” so that ravines might be filled, hills and mountains lowered, and crooked and rough places made straight and level—that is, [Col. 0474C] the difficulties of the Law changed into the facilities of the Gospel.

He also remembered that the time spoken of in the Psalm had now come (Ps. 2): “Let us break their bonds away from us and cast off their yoke from us.” This was from the time when “the nations raged, the peoples meditated vain things, the kings of the earth took their stand, and the rulers gathered together as one against the Lord and against his Christ,” so that man might now be justified by the liberty of faith, not by the slavery of the Law: “Because the righteous man lives by faith” (Heb. 10:38).

And if the prophet Habakkuk foretold this (Hab. 2:4), you have the Apostle confirming the Prophets, just as Christ did. Faith, therefore, in which the righteous man will live belongs to the same God whose is the Law by which the worker is not justified.

Likewise, if there is a curse in the Law but a blessing in faith, you find both set forth by the Creator: “Behold,” he says (Deut. 11:26), “I have set before [Col. 0474D] you a curse and a blessing.” Marcion cannot claim the distinction for himself. Although it is a distinction between things, it is not therefore a distinction between authors [Col. 0475A], since both things are set forth by one author.

Why Christ became a curse for us is evident from the Apostle’s own teaching, as is how strongly this supports our side—that is, in accordance with faith in the Creator. For because the Creator pronounced, “Cursed is everyone who is suspended on a tree,” Christ will not therefore appear to belong to another God and for that reason already to have been cursed by the Creator at that time in the Law. And how could the Creator have cursed him beforehand if he did not know him?

Why should it not be more fitting for the Creator to have surrendered his own Son to his own curse than for that god of yours to have subjected his son to the Creator’s curse—and indeed on behalf of a man who belonged to another? Finally, if this seems savage in the Creator’s treatment of his Son, it is equally savage in your god. But if it is rational in your god, it is equally so in mine—and more so in mine.

For it would more readily be believed that the one who had once set both a curse and a blessing before man had provided a blessing for man through Christ’s curse [Col. 0475B] than that a god who, according to you, had never declared either should have done so.

“We have therefore received the spiritual blessing through faith,” he says—the faith by which, according to the Creator, the righteous man will live. This, then, is what I say: faith belongs to the God to whom the established form of grace through faith belongs.

But when he also adds, “For you are all sons of faith,” what heretical industry erased above becomes evident [Note: [Col. 0475D] Namely, what heretical industry erased above: the mention of Abraham. Tertullian indicates that Marcion erased several preceding verses both at the beginning of this chapter and before the words already quoted. He repeats this in the following chapter, and not only concerning the mention of Abraham but also concerning the statement, “Nevertheless, he does not reject or add to a human covenant.” PAMEL.]: namely, the mention of Abraham, in connection with which the Apostle affirms that through faith we are sons of Abraham, and in accordance with which mention he here too described us as sons of faith.

Otherwise, how are we sons of faith? And of whose faith, if not Abraham’s? For if Abraham believed God and it was reckoned [Note: [Col. 0475D] “Is reckoned,” Latinius.] to him as righteousness, and he thereby deserved to be called the father of many nations, while we [Col. 0475C], moreover, by believing God, are justified in the same way as Abraham and likewise attain life, just as the righteous man lives by faith, it follows that above he pronounced us sons of Abraham in his capacity as the father of faith, and here sons of the faith through which Abraham had been promised as the father of the nations.

By the very fact that he was calling faith back from circumcision, was he not seeking to establish as Abraham’s sons those who believed while preserving the integrity of their flesh, just as Abraham had believed? Finally, the faith of one God cannot be admitted according to the pattern established by another God, so as to reckon believers righteous, cause the righteous to live, and call the nations sons of faith. All this belongs to the God [Note: [Col. 0475D] “His” is absent from the Paris manuscript.] with whom it is already known beforehand under the same mention of Abraham, since Marcion is refuted by the very meaning of the passage.
