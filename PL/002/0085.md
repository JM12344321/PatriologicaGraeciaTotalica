And these points I have advanced, as it were, from the common pattern of the human condition in support of [Note: [Col. 0806C] “Supports,” Rhenanus, Semler.] the flesh. Let us now also consider, from the distinctive pattern of the Christian name, how great a prerogative this substance—frivolous and filthy though it is—possesses with God. Indeed, it might have sufficed for it that no soul whatsoever can attain salvation unless it has believed while it is [Note: [Col. 0806C] “Is” omitted by others.] in the flesh: so true is it that the flesh is the hinge of salvation. In short, [Note: [Col. 0806C] “Concerning which,” Rhenanus.] when the soul is enlisted for God, it is the flesh itself that makes it possible for the soul to be enlisted. Thus [Note: [Col. 0806C] “But also,” Rhenanus, Pamelius.] the flesh is washed so that the soul may be cleansed of stains; the flesh is anointed so that the soul may be consecrated; the flesh is signed so that the soul too may be fortified; the flesh is overshadowed by the laying on of hands [Col. 0806B] so that the soul too may be illuminated by the Spirit; the flesh feeds upon the body and blood of Christ so that the soul too may be fattened upon God. Rigaltius.] Therefore those whom their works unite cannot be separated in their reward.

For even the sacrifices pleasing to God—I mean the soul’s struggles: fasts, late and dry meals, and [Note: [Col. 0806D] “The filth that attends this observance.” Elsewhere he spoke of the horror of ashes and the roughness of sackcloth. To these they also added the following expressions from Lucilius: filthiness, lack of bathing, lack of washing, and neglect. Rigaltius.] the filth that attends this observance—the flesh supplies at the cost of its own discomfort. [Note: [Col. 0806C] “Is supplied,” Rhenanus.] Virginity too, and widowhood, and [Note: [Col. 0806D] “Modest concealment within the privacy of marriage.” He means the intimacies of marriage themselves, which in book II *To His Wife* he says are, among the saints, the duties of sex and ought to be conducted modestly and temperately, with due regard for the necessity itself, as though under the eyes of God. Rigaltius.] modest reserve within the privacy of marriage, and acquaintance with it only once, are offered to God from the good things of the flesh. [Note: [Col. 0806C] *Adolantur*, Rhenanus, Semler.]

Come now: what do you think of the flesh when, [Note: [Col. 0806D] “For faithfulness to the name.” We have noted more than once that Tertullian and blessed Cyprian commonly use “the name” for “the Christian name.” So also in chapter 40, “because of punishments for the name”; the expressions are characteristically Tertullianic. On those varied and unheard-of penalties, Eusebius should be consulted. Is it not then most blessed and most glorious, [Note: [Col. 0807D] “Which is able to obey so great a debt before Christ the Lord.” This is how the copies read, and I do not think anything should be changed, although Ciacconius preferred *pariare*, “to balance the account.” A debtor obeys a creditor who calls upon him when he makes satisfaction. Rigaltius.] since it can obey so great a debt before Christ the Lord, [Note: [Col. 0807C] “Be equal,” Latinus; “balance the account,” Ciacconius.] so that this alone remains its debt to him: that it has ceased to owe him—bound all the more precisely because [Note: [Col. 0807C] “In that,” Pamelius, François.] it has been discharged?

## CHAPTER IX

Therefore, to retrace the flesh which God fashioned with his own hands in the image of God, which he animated from his own breath in the likeness of his own vitality, which he placed over his whole creation to inhabit, enjoy, and rule it, which he clothed with his mysteries and disciplines; whose purity he loves, whose chastisements he approves, whose sufferings he values for himself—shall this flesh, belonging to God in so many ways, not rise again? [Col. 0807B] Far be it, far be it [Note: [Col. 0807C] “Far be it” only once, Rhenanus.] that God should abandon to eternal destruction the work of his hands, the object of his creative care, the sheath of his breath, [Note: [Col. 0807D] “The queen of his creation.” By “creation” he means the act of creating. For God created the human being as king of everything on earth and in the sea. Rigaltius.] the queen of his creation, the heir of his liberality, [Note: [Col. 0807D] “The priest of his religion”: one who offers praise and thanks to God. Rigaltius.] the priest of his religion, the soldier of his testimony, [Note: [Col. 0807D] “The sister of his Christ,” because it is akin and of one stock with the flesh of Christ—not as most of those heretics against whom the author contends supposed, namely, that Christ’s flesh was of a different nature, whether phantom-like, celestial, animal, or spiritual. Their heresies were discussed in the preceding book. Lac.] the sister of his Christ!

We know God to be good; [Note: [Col. 0807D] “We learn that he alone is supremely good from his Christ,” etc. God has been recognized as supremely good through works [Col. 0808D] of grace. This is also said against the Marcionites, who distinguished two gods, an evil creator-god and a good god, and said that the latter was the God to whom Christ belonged and whose goodness was made known through Christ. Lac.] from his Christ we learn that he alone is supremely good. [Note: [Col. 0807C] “His” absent from Rhenanus, Semler.] He who commands love, after love of himself, toward one’s neighbor, will himself also do what he has commanded: he will love the flesh, which is his neighbor in so many ways. Though it is weak (Matt. 26:41), strength is perfected in weakness (2 Cor. 12:9); though feeble, none desire a physician except those who are ill (Matt. 22 [sic]); though dishonorable, [Note: [Col. 0808D] “But upon the more dishonorable we bestow greater honor.” When it is necessary to name such parts, we are accustomed first to express respect. Why do you reproach the flesh with those conditions which await God, which hope in God, which are honored by him, and which he assists? I would venture to say that, had these things not befallen the flesh, [Col. 0808A] goodness, grace, mercy, and every beneficent power of God would have had no occupation. [Note: [Col. 0807C] “Would have been vacant,” others.]

## CHAPTER X

You retain the Scriptures by which the flesh is darkened; retain also those by which it is made illustrious. You read when [Note: [Col. 0807C] “When,” Rhenanus.] the flesh is cast down: direct your eyes also to when [Note: [Col. 0807D] “When,” Rhenanus.] the flesh is raised up. [Note: [Col. 0808C] “Revealed,” others.] “All flesh is grass” (Isa. 41 [sic]); Isaiah did not pronounce this [Note: [Col. 0808C] “This” absent from Pamelius, François.] alone, but also: [Note: [Col. 0808D] “All flesh shall see the salvation of God.” Below, chapter 59, these same Scriptures are cited in almost the same words. It is read according to the Septuagint in place of what blessed Jerome translates simply as “and all flesh together shall see”; Leo de Castro, on Isaiah, shows elegantly that both readings come to the same thing. Le Prieur.] “All flesh shall see the salvation of God” (*ibid.*).

In Genesis, God is marked as saying (Gen. 6): “My Spirit shall not remain in these people, because they are flesh”; but he is also heard through Joel (Joel 2): “I shall pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh.” Nor should you recognize the Apostle from only one manner of writing, in which he frequently goads the flesh. For although he denies that anything good dwells in his flesh; although he affirms that those who are in the flesh cannot [Col. 0808B] please God, because it lusts against the spirit; and if he makes any other statements of this sort, so that not the substance of the flesh but its conduct is dishonored—we shall indeed say elsewhere that nothing ought properly to be reproached against the flesh except as a rebuke to the soul, which subjects [Note: [Col. 0808C] “Submits,” others.] the flesh to itself as its servant. Yet meanwhile [Note: [Col. 0808D] “Paul is also in those writings”; that is, Paul’s voice is also heard in those testimonies of Scripture. Rigaltius.] Paul is also in those writings when he bears the marks of Christ in his body; when he forbids [Note: [Col. 0808C] Rhenanus and Semler add “the body.”] our body, as God’s temple, to be defiled; when he makes our bodies members of Christ; when he admonishes us to bear and magnify God in our body.

Accordingly, if the disgraces of the flesh exclude its resurrection, why should its dignities not rather introduce it? For it is more consonant with God to restore to salvation what he has at times rejected than to surrender to destruction what he has also [Col. 0808C] at some time [Note: [Col. 0808C] “At some time” lacking in Rhenanus.] approved.

## CHAPTER XI

Thus far concerning the commendation of the flesh against its enemies, who are nevertheless its greatest friends. For no one lives more carnally than those who deny the resurrection of the flesh. By denying its [Note: [Col. 0808C] “Its” lacking in Rhenanus, Semler.] punishment, they also despise its discipline. Of them the [Note: [Col. 0808D] “The Paraclete through the prophetess,” etc. He calls the spirit of Montanus the Paraclete, to which the author had already adhered when he wrote this treatise. Prisca, elsewhere [Col. 0809C] Priscilla, whom the author mentions *Against Praxeas* and in the book *On Fasts*, was a prophetess of Montanus. Lac.] Paraclete also spoke lucidly through [Col. 0809A] the prophetess Prisca: “They are flesh, and they hate the flesh.”

If so great an authority fortifies the flesh—an authority able to plead so powerfully for its claim to salvation—must we not also review God’s own might, power, and freedom of action: whether he is great enough to be able to rebuild and restore the tabernacle of the flesh after it has fallen apart, been devoured, and been carried away [Note: [Col. 0809C] “Removed,” Junius.] in any conceivable manner? And has he promulgated any examples of this sovereign right of his [Note: [Col. 0809D] “In the public record of nature”: in nature’s public tablets. The author himself, in *On the Crown*: “Do you therefore seek God’s law? You have this common law in the public record of the world, in the natural tablets, to which the Apostle too is accustomed to appeal.” Rigaltius.] in the public record of nature, [Note: [Col. 0809C] “Had promulgated,” Rhenanus; “may promulgate,” others.] lest there be any who perhaps [Note: [Col. 0809D] “Lest some perhaps still do not know how to know God.” The Pithoean manuscript reads “thirst.” This form of expression is familiar to Septimius. Those who do not yet know God thirst to know him, and therefore do not know him. So too: “Nevertheless, they thirst for an interpretation of his very simplicity.” Rigaltius.] still do not know [Note: [Col. 0809C] “Thirst,” Rhenanus, Semler; “know,” Pamelius, François.] how to know God, who must be believed in according to no other rule than that he be believed able to do all things?

Certainly, among the philosophers you have those who maintain that this world is unbegotten and unmade; [Note: [Col. 0809D] “Among the philosophers you have those who say that this world is unbegotten.” The saying “Nothing comes from nothing” is commonplace; from it Aristotle, *Physics* I and *On the Heavens*, thinks that the world has existed and will exist eternally. But if one attends to the true and genuine explanation of that principle, God said: “I am who I am”; it must therefore be inferred that all things flowed forth from that existing God and that the world was created. Some think Aristotle later reconsidered this opinion. Le Prieur.] but far better is the position that almost all the heresies, admitting that the world was born and made, ascribe its creation to our God. Therefore trust [Note: [Col. 0809C] “He will trust,” Rigaltius, Venice.] that he brought forth this whole universe from nothing, [Col. 0809B] and you know God by trusting that God possesses such power.

For some, too weak to believe this first point, prefer to hold that the universe was brought into being by him out of an underlying matter [Note: [Col. 0809C] “Of the whole,” Wouwer manuscript.]—according to the philosophers. [Note: [Col. 0809D] Plato, as Athanasius attests in *On the Incarnation of the Word*, at the beginning, and Epiphanius, [Col. 0810C] *Heresy* 6, said that God fashioned the world from pre-existent and unmade matter. Yet Marsilio Ficino, [Col. 0810D] in his compendium of Plato’s *Timaeus*, chapters 9 and following, attempts to excuse him from this error and claims that Plato meant only that matter was made by God more immediately than other things. Lac.] But even if this were held to be true, since he would nevertheless be said to have brought forth, through the reformation of matter, substances entirely different and forms entirely different from what the matter itself had been, I would defend no less that he brought them forth from nothing, if he brought forth things that had not existed at all.

[Note: [Col. 0810D] “For what difference does it make,” etc. The author does not say this dogmatically, since he does not dare despise the settled laws of philosophy approved by the Fathers—as, on the matter under discussion, Gregory, homily 20 on Ezekiel. Lac.] For what difference does it make whether something is brought forth from nothing or from something, provided that what did not exist comes to be? For even not to have existed is to have been nothing. Conversely, to have existed is not to have been nothing. Now, even if there is a difference, both alternatives nevertheless support me. For whether God fashioned all things out of nothing, he will also be able to produce out of nothing the flesh reduced [Col. 0809C] to nothing; [Note: [Col. 0809C] “Produced,” Pamelius, Rhenanus.] or whether he formed [Note: [Col. 0810C] “Moderated,” Rhenanus, Semler.] other things out of matter, he will also be able to summon the flesh out of whatever else it has been absorbed into.

[Note: [Col. 0810D] “And surely one who made it is competent to remake it.” The Manichaeans, Cerdonians, and Marcionites denied that re-creation or resurrection, although creating from nothing seems far more difficult than remaking. Le Prieur.] And surely one who made it is competent to remake it. How much greater a thing it is to have made than to have remade, [Col. 0810A] to have given a beginning than to have restored it! Thus you should believe that the restoration of the flesh is easier than its original formation.

## CHAPTER XII

Look now also at the actual examples of divine power. Day dies into night and is buried everywhere in darkness; [Note: [Col. 0810D] “The glory of the world is laid in mourning.” He means the sun, which Orpheus calls κόσμου τὸ περίδρομον ὄμμα, “the roaming eye of the world,” and Proclus of Lycia in hymn 1 calls ἀλλὰ Θεῶν ἄριστε, πυριστεφὲς, ὄλβιε δαῖμον, “but you, best of gods, fire-crowned, blessed divinity.” Le Prieur.] the glory of the world is laid in mourning; every substance is blackened; all things grow squalid, silent, and numb; everywhere there is a cessation of business. [Note: [Col. 0810C] The Pithoean manuscript adds “a repose of things.”] Thus the lost light is mourned; and yet, once again, with its splendor, its endowment, and its sun, that same light—undiminished, whole, and complete—revives for the entire world, killing its own death, the night; tearing open its own tomb, the darkness; becoming its own heir, until night too revives with its own equipment.

For the rays of the stars are rekindled, which the morning blaze had extinguished; the absent constellations are brought back, [Col. 0810B] which the temporal division had removed; the mirrors of the moon are adorned anew, [Note: [Col. 0810C] “Are given back,” Rhenanus.] which the monthly reckoning had worn away; winters and summers revolve again, [Note: [Col. 0810C] Rhenanus and Semler insert “and.”] springtimes and autumns, with their powers, characteristics, and fruits. Indeed, the earth too is instructed by heaven to clothe the trees after their stripping, to color the flowers anew, to spread the grasses again, to bring forth the same seeds that have been consumed, and not to bring them forth before they have been consumed.

A wondrous order! The plunderer becomes the preserver: that she may restore, she takes away; that she may keep, she destroys; that she may make whole, she damages; that she may even increase, she first consumes. For she restores things richer and more cultivated than those she annihilated—truly, destruction at interest, injury at usury, damage at a profit. Let me say it once for all: the whole creation is recurrent. Whatever you encounter has existed before; whatever you lose, there is nothing that does not exist again. [Col. 0810C] All things return to their condition once they have departed; all things begin once they have ceased. They are therefore ended so that they may come to be; nothing wholly perishes except unto salvation.

Thus this entire [Note: [Col. 0810D] “This revolving order of things.” From the perpetual alternation of things, their continual flux and revolution, he demonstrates the resurrection. Indeed, from that sequence of natural things the wiser philosophers had an inkling of it, such as Plato in the *Timaeus* and *Phaedo*, and Seneca, Epistle 35: “The death which we so greatly fear and accuse interrupts life; it does not take it away. One will come again who will restore these days to the light.” The same in Epistles 40, 92, and 102, and *To Marcia*, [Col. 0811D] chapter 26. Le Prieur.] revolving order of things is a testimony to the resurrection of the dead. [Col. 0811A] God prescribed it through works before he did through writings; he proclaimed it through powers before he did through words. He sent nature ahead to be your teacher, intending afterward to send prophecy, so that, as nature’s pupil, you might believe prophecy more readily; so that, when you hear, you might immediately admit what you have already seen everywhere [Note: [Col. 0811C] “Certainly,” François.]; and so that you might not doubt that God also raises the flesh, since you know him [Note: [Col. 0811C] “Who,” Rhenanus, Junius.] to be the restorer of all things.

And surely, if all things rise again for the human being, for whom they were provided—and moreover not for the human being apart from the flesh—how can the flesh itself perish completely, when it is for the flesh and for its sake that nothing perishes?

## CHAPTER XIII

If the universe insufficiently prefigures the resurrection; if creation signifies nothing of the sort, because its individual elements are said not so much to die as to cease and are thought not to be reanimated but [Col. 0811B] re-formed, then accept the fullest [Note: [Col. 0811C] “Clearest,” others.] and firmest specimen of this hope. For it is an animate creature, subject both to life and to death. I mean that bird peculiar to the East, famous for its uniqueness and extraordinary in its succession: [Note: [Col. 0811D] “Which willingly performs its own funeral and renews itself.” Clement, *To the Corinthians*, relates it thus: when the phoenix senses that it is near death, it constructs for itself from frankincense, myrrh, and other aromatics of this sort a σηκόν, “enclosure” (Pliny: “nest”); soon afterward the bird, laid within it, dies. From the dead flesh there is born first, as it were, a little worm, which then becomes a fledgling. In this, he says, an image of the resurrection is given us by God. Rigaltius.] it willingly performs its own funeral and renews itself, departing and succeeding itself through an end that is also a birth; once again a phoenix where now there was none; once again itself, which no longer existed; another and yet the same.

What could be more explicit and more significant for this cause? Or what other matter possesses such a proof? God speaks even in his Scriptures: [Note: [Col. 0811D] “‘For he shall flourish,’ he says, ‘like the phoenix.’” ὡς φοῖνιξ means “like a palm tree,” Ps. 12 [sic]. The author was deceived by the ambiguity of the Greek word. Semler.—Septimius is accustomed thus to interpret Scripture very broadly for his purpose. Accordingly, here he wished the ambiguous word *phoenix*, which may denote either bird or tree, to be understood of the phoenix bird, and he adapted the verb “shall flourish” to the resurrection. Rigaltius.] “For he shall flourish,” [Note: [Col. 0811C] “You shall flourish,” Rhenanus; “he shall flower forth,” others.] he says (Ps. 91:13), “like the phoenix”—that is, out of death, out of burial—[Note: [Col. 0811D] “So that you may believe that the substance of the body can be exacted even from fires.” These are indications that, in his account of the phoenix, Tertullian followed reports handed down in different forms by different authors, selecting those which he saw [Col. 0812C] as most useful to his argument. Clement’s epistle had related that the phoenix was reborn from its decomposing, not its cremated, flesh. Therefore, to confirm the restoration of bodies even in that kind of death in which hope of restoration seemed especially extinguished, he turned [Col. 0812D] to those who had written that a new phoenix was restored from the ashes of the one cremated. “So that you may believe,” he says, “that the substance of the body can be exacted even from fires.” He joins this to the cited verse of the Psalm, as though the psalmist had in mind the story of the cremated phoenix. Rigaltius.] so that you may believe that the substance of the body can be exacted even from fires. The Lord pronounced that we are worth more than many sparrows: if we are not also worth more than phoenixes, that is nothing great. But [Note: [Col. 0811C] “Indeed,” Junius.] are human beings to perish once for all, [Col. 0811C] while the birds of Arabia are secure in their resurrection?

## CHAPTER XIV

[Col. 0812A]

With such outlines of divine powers before us—God operating through parables no less than speaking through them—let us now come also to his actual edicts and decrees, for it is by these that we are chiefly arranging this division of our subject matter. We began with the dignity of the flesh: whether, after it has fallen apart, salvation belongs to it. We then proceeded to the power of God: whether it is great enough and accustomed to confer salvation upon a thing that has fallen apart. Now, if we have established both, I would have you inquire also into the cause: whether there is any cause worthy enough to vindicate the resurrection of the flesh as necessary, and indeed as in every way owed by reason. For there remains the objection that, although the flesh is capable of being restored and although the divinity is competent to restore it, [Note: [Col. 0811C] “And,” Rhenanus, and so Junius.] a cause for the restoration must nevertheless take precedence.

Accept, then, the cause, you who learn concerning [Note: [Col. 0811C] “Because, I think,” Junius.] God that he is [Note: [Col. 0812D] “As supremely good as he is just.” He strikes at the Marcionites, who did not unite justice and goodness in one God but, dividing the divine substance into two gods, called one good, the other evil and just. Theodoret reported this error of Marcion, together with its arguments, in book V *On the Divine Decrees*. Lac.] as supremely good [Col. 0812B] as he is just: supremely good from his own nature, just on account of ours. For if the human being had not sinned, he would have known God solely as supremely good, from the property of his nature; but now he also experiences him as just, from the necessity of the case [Note: [Col. 0812C] François and Pamelius add “his own.”]—yet supremely good even in this very fact, while also just. For in displaying justice by assisting the good and punishing the evil, he furnishes both verdicts for the good: on the one side avenging this good, on the other rewarding that good. [Note: [Col. 0812D] “But you will learn more fully in the controversy with Marcion.” He refers the reader to the books he wrote against Marcion. Rhenanus.] But in the controversy with Marcion you will learn more fully whether this constitutes the whole nature of God.

Meanwhile, such is our God: rightly a judge, because Lord; rightly Lord, because author; rightly author, because God. Hence that nameless god of the heretics is rightly not a judge, since he is not God; [Note: [Col. 0812C] “Lord,” the Ursinus and Wouwer manuscripts.] rightly not Lord, since he is not the author. I no longer know whether he is God, [Note: [Col. 0812D] “I no longer know whether he is God,” etc. The sense appears to be: “I do not know whether the god of the heretics is God, since he is not the author, although the true God—that is, our God—is known to be the author; nor is he Lord, although he ought to be if he were the author.” Rhenanus.] seeing that he is neither author, as God is, nor Lord, as the author is. [Note: [Col. 0812C] “As Lord … as author” absent from the Ursinus and Wouwer manuscripts.]

Therefore, if it is most fitting for [Note: [Col. 0812C] Wouwer inserts “concerning.”] God and Lord and [Note: [Col. 0812C] Rhenanus inserts “not.”] author to ordain judgment for the human being on this very question—whether he has taken care to acknowledge and observe [Col. 0813A] [Note: [Col. 0813C] “Should take care,” Rhenanus, Semler.] his Lord and author, [Note: [Col. 0812C] “And his author” absent from the Wouwer manuscript.] or not—then surely [Note: [Col. 0813C] “Accordingly,” Semler, Rhenanus; “this also,” Pamelius.] the resurrection will discharge the judgment. This will be the whole cause—or rather, the necessity—of the resurrection: [Note: [Col. 0813D] Namely, the ordaining of judgment, which is most fitting for God. Thus it must be read, since “to ordain judgment for the human being” precedes, and below: “Finally, this will be the rationale of the judgment ordained for the final end.” Rhenanus.] namely, the ordaining of judgment, which is most fitting for God. In considering its arrangement, determine whether divine adjudication presides over both substances of the human being that must be judged—the soul as well as the flesh. For whatever is fittingly judged is also appropriately raised.

We say, first, that God’s judgment must be believed full and perfect, as now the final judgment and thereafter perpetual; that it is also just in this respect, since it is deficient in nothing; and also worthy of God in this respect, since, after so great a measure of his patience, it is full and perfect. Consequently, [Note: [Col. 0813C] Pamelius and François add “perfect.”] the fullness and perfection of judgment can consist only in the presentation of the whole human being. The whole human being, moreover, comes forth from the union [Col. 0813B] of both substances, [Note: [Col. 0813C] “Assembly,” Rhenanus.] and must therefore be presented in both, since it is the whole who must be judged. Unless whole, surely the human being will not have lived. As he lived, therefore, so must he be judged, since he must be judged concerning the life he lived. For life is the cause of judgment and must be accounted for through as many substances as those through which it was lived.

## CHAPTER XV

Come now: let our adversaries sever the interweaving of flesh and soul first in the conduct of life, so that they may then dare to sever it also in the recompense of life. [Note: [Col. 0813D] “Let them deny the partnership of works,” etc. The reasoning of this chapter is derived from what is good, equitable, and fitting, and all who wrote on the resurrection of the flesh after Tertullian employed it. Lac.] Let them deny the partnership of deeds, [Note: [Col. 0813D] “Works,” others.] so that they may justly be able to deny the reward as well. Let the flesh have no share in the verdict if it had no share in the cause. Let the soul alone be recalled if it alone departs. [Note: [Col. 0813D] “It alone departs.” He does not take “depart” to mean “perish altogether,” for the immortal soul persists, but “depart from this life,” which depends upon the ministries of the flesh. Lac.]

Yet the soul no more departs alone than it alone completed the course from which it departs—[Col. 0813C] I mean this life. Indeed, so far is the soul [Note: [Col. 0813D] “Alone,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] from passing through life alone that we do not even exclude thoughts, though solitary and though not brought to realization [Note: [Col. 0814C] “Disposition,” Rhenanus.] through the flesh, from fellowship with the flesh. For whatever the soul does in the heart is done in the flesh, with the flesh, and through the flesh. This particular part of the flesh—the soul’s citadel—even the Lord censures on account of wicked thoughts: “Why do you think evil things in your hearts?” (Matt. 9); and: “Whoever has looked upon a woman to lust after her has already committed adultery in his heart” (Matt. 5). Thus even a thought without deed and [Col. 0814A] without accomplishment is an act of the flesh.

But even if the ruling faculty of the senses, which is called the *hegemonikon* (ἡγεμονικόν, “governing faculty”), has its appointed seat in the brain, or in the central division between the eyebrows, or wherever [Note: [Col. 0814C] “Wherever” only once, Rhenanus, Semler.] philosophers please, the flesh will be the whole thinking-place of the soul. [Note: [Col. 0813D] “The flesh will be the whole thinking-place of the soul.” Tertullian calls *cogitatorium* the place in the body, or the part of the body, where the soul thinks. Rigaltius.] The soul is never without the flesh for as long as it is in the flesh. It does nothing apart from that without which it does not exist.

Inquire further whether thoughts too are administered [Note: [Col. 0814C] “Are administered,” Rhenanus, Semler.] through the flesh, seeing that through the flesh they are discerned externally. Let the soul turn something over within itself: the countenance produces an indication. [Note: [Col. 0814D] “Judgment,” Rhenanus, Semler.] The face is the mirror of every intention. [Note: [Col. 0814D] “The face is the mirror of every intention.” The affections of the mind are commonly observable in the countenance, so that sadness, cheerfulness, love, hatred, and other emotions are readily judged from the face; hence the grammarians derived *vultus* (“countenance”) from *velle* (“to will”). Cicero himself also said in *Against Piso*: “The eyes, eyebrows, forehead—in short, the whole countenance—are a kind of silent speech of the mind.” Le Prieur.] Let them deny partnership in deeds to that whose partnership in thoughts they cannot deny.

They themselves enumerate the transgressions of the flesh: therefore, as a sinner, it will be liable to punishment. We, however, also set against these the virtues of the flesh: therefore, as one that has done well, it will also be entitled to reward. [Col. 0814B] And although it is the soul that acts and impels toward everything, obedience belongs to the flesh. It is impermissible to believe God either an unjust judge or an ineffectual one: unjust, if he bars the partner in good works from rewards; ineffectual, if he exempts the partner in evil works from punishments—since human adjudication is considered more perfect precisely insofar as it also calls the assistants in every deed to account, neither sparing them nor begrudging them the right to share with its authors in the fruit either of punishment or of favor.
