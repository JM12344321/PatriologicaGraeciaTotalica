EXTENDERE (“to stretch out”), of dogs, when, throwing themselves forward on their feet in hope of prey, they loosen the joints of the body and uncoil their coils. I, 313 A, “Which may stretch them toward the destruction of the eyes.” I, 1279 B, “Let us stretch forward to what lies ahead.” [Col. 1301C] I, 1319 B, “Those women enlarge for themselves both the beauty that has been given and seek out what has not been given.” I, 676 B, “They extend the diligence of their observance.”

EXTERMINIUM (“destruction”). II, 612 A, “The destruction of the city of Jerusalem,” that is, its devastation.

EXTIMARE. II, 82 B, “He assigned touch to the hands,” that is, he located the sense of touch—the outermost or *extimus* sense—in the hands, and indeed in their extremities, for the fingertips perceive and sense differences of touch most acutely.

EXTRA (“outside”). I, 512 A, “A Christian is called faithful even outside,” that is, toward outsiders and by strangers—by the Gentiles.

EXTRANATURALE (“outside nature”). II, 721 B, “Sleep is certainly not outside nature.”

EXTRANEUS (“alien to,” “removed from”). I, 446 B, “Although we are found in some place removed from the disturbances of the crowds,” that is, although we involve ourselves in no turbulent affairs, nevertheless, when the empire is shaken, we too are shaken, and some disaster can strike us; thus we suffer together with you. It is often construed with the genitive. I, 1317 B, “From whom you profess yourself [Col. 1301D] alien.” I, 1250 A, “No one alien to patience can accomplish any work pleasing to the Lord.” II, 727 A, “But who is so alien to humanity?”

EXTREMUS (“extreme,” “utmost”). II, 708 A, “Because nothing is fuller than that which is most extreme.” I, 387 A, “Yet the very last of them.”

EXUL (“exiled”). I, 1247 A, “But exiled from the happiness of freedom and joy.”

EZECHIEL. II, 790 C, “We do indeed read in Ezekiel of that cow which gave birth and did not give birth.” Since nowhere in the prophet Ezekiel is there any mention of the words cited here by the author, commentators rightly question how this passage is to be explained. Pamelius and others think it is what appears in Job II: “The cow gave birth and was not deprived of its offspring.” Junius suspects that it was taken from some apocryphal writing—unless perhaps one understands [Col. 1302A] Ezekiel the tragic poet, who is cited by Clement of Alexandria in *Stromata* II and frequently by Eusebius in his books *On the Preparation for the Gospel*.

## F

FABALE (“bean field”). II, 702 A, “Pythagoras, however, taught his disciples that they must not even pass through bean fields.” That precept of Pythagoras was observed even at the risk of life. “Do not touch beans” (*Χυάμων μὴ θιγγάνειν*), writes Iamblichus in chapter 31, where he relates that certain disciples of Pythagoras, fleeing the soldiers of the tyrant Dionysius, happened upon bean fields. Mindful of the decree and unwilling to violate it, they stopped, not daring at all to pass through the bean fields, but preferred to be slaughtered and buried there.

FABRICARE (“to make”). I, 1261 C, “How shall we make friends for ourselves from mammon?”

FACERE (“to go,” “to journey”). II, 1038 B, “Whither Hammon makes his way from Libya to him.” Hyginus relates that Hammon came from Africa to Liber; Liber, moreover, is Osiris [Col. 1302B] among the Egyptians. Others read *vasit*, “went.”  
“To be useful”: II, 1045 B, “This garment has something that may be useful.” The meaning of the entire passage is: Let the cloak exhibit no signs of a man who has passed over to better things; nevertheless, this garment possesses a sphere in which it can display its usefulness.  
II, 227 A, “Because we also make sons, although we beget them.”  
II, 399 B, “He who did not permit us to produce children,” Greek *τεκνοποιεῖσθαι* (“to beget children”).  
II, 618 B, “To serve as a soldier before becoming a man.” Tertullian published the same thought in this form in *Against Marcion* III, chapter 13: “Nature nowhere permits this: to serve as a soldier before living.”  
II, 143 A, “Those who kill only the body, but do nothing to the soul.”  
II, 792 B, “So that it may make a conclusion from the common preface,” that is, furnish the function of a common preface, doing what a common preface customarily does.

FACILIS (“ready,” “inclined”). II, 538 B, “He is readily disposed toward fables,” that is, he receives them gladly. II, 647 B, “Certainly Socrates was more readily driven by a different spirit,” that is, rather and more certainly. “For the ancients,” Donatus says, “used *facile* in the sense of ‘certainly.’”

[Col. 1302C] FACILITAS (“facility”). II, 52 C, “Facility in devising and equipping errors.” This must be understood in an active sense, for a pliant disposition and a compliant arm that accomplishes what it is instructed to do.

FACINUS (“deed”). II, 692 A, “To which the entire deed is drawn.” The other reading, “to which the whole flock is drawn,” is preferable. See PECUS.

FACTIO (“faction,” “party”). II, 468 A, “I shall now set forth the affairs of the Christian party myself.” I, 478 A, “For which there is complaint concerning factions.” II, 152 A, “Of the Valentinian faction.”

FACTITAMENTUM (“fabrication”). II, 680 A, “They are seen to have been understood concerning fabricated things.”

FACTITATIO (“making”). II, 226 B, “Or is this too reckoned in the making of man?”—that is, in his creation or formation.

FACTITATOR (“maker”). II, 177 B, “To the makers of idols.”

FACTITIUS (“artificial”). I, 651 A, “But not even care of an artificial body, as though it surpassed God’s formative art.”

FACTURA (“making,” “creature”). II, 652 B, “It is therefore admissible both that *factura* (‘a making’ or ‘creature’) be called ‘being generated,’ and that this be used for ‘being inherent.’” *Capit* here means “it is permissible, it is lawful.” He uses *factura* of the soul where elsewhere he would say “creation.” See INESSE.

[Col. 1302D] FACULA, a diminutive of *fax* (“torch”). I, 461 A, “With a very few small torches.” II, 1027 C, “How will the oil of your lamp be able to suffice both for you and for me?” II, 135 A, “Who had not prepared oil for their lamps.”

FAMA (“report,” “reputation”). II, 1030 C, “Under public report because of devotion to the woof.” Junius prefers *infamatae*: that is, either “made infamous” or “bruited by report, publicized,” as in Livy: “He orders the door to be barred for the purpose of bringing the matter into ill repute.”

FAMILIA (“household,” “circle”). II, 554 A, “The circle of Phosphorus cries *pheu!* (φεῦ, ‘alas!’),” that is, the followers of the rhetorician, his *συνόντες, οἰκεῖοι ἐταῖροι* (“associates, intimate companions”). II, 1252 A, “Persecuting the Name together with his household.” Rhenanus, from the manuscripts: “Persecuting the Name, his household.” By “the Name” he means the Christian name, and by “his household,” namely Christ’s, he means Christians.

FAMOSITAS (“infamy”). I, 654 B, “With the goods of dignities taken away upon some rock of infamy.”

[Col. 1303A] FAMULARE is the Greek *δουλοῦν*, “to reduce to servitude.” I, 400 B, “He might enslave the very elements.” I, 476 A, “For the glory of enslaving liberty.” II, 224 A, “So that from it living creatures may be made servants for my use.” II, 862 A, “But having served God.” II, 868 A, “Liberating what had been enslaved.”

FAMULATORIUS (“servile,” “domesticated”). II, 705 B, “Those among the domesticated kinds must be subdued,” that is, among those kinds of animals which are kept for service.

FAMULUS (“servant”). II, 707 B, “All things are servants of man.”

FARSUS, for *fartus* or *farctus* (“stuffed”). II, 971 A, “For some time stuffed with every bath.” Likewise II, 1042 A, “A gaping opening stuffed with crimes.”

FARSURA (“stuffing”). II, 781 B, “The remaining stuffing,” a Tertullianic word for *fartura* or *farctura*.

FASCINARE (“to bewitch”). II, 755 A, “Let that old woman too be silent, lest she bewitch the boy,” that is, lest she chant his fate over the boy and turn it to good effect through incantations.

FASTIDIBILIS (“liable to be despised”). II, 707 B, “Despicable things among the gifts, which not even the worst would fear.”

FASTI (“calendar”). II, 364 D, “Of those who defend circumcision and [Col. 1303B] the Jewish calendar.” Thus Rigault reads; in Pithou’s copy, *fastus* (“pride”) is read.

FASTIDIOSUS (“disdainful”), one who spurns love. I, 359 A, “And Cybele sighs for the disdainful shepherd.”

FASTIDIRE (“to disdain”). I, 1265 A, “If, having first been disdained, we shall not be objects of disdain in our vengeance.” Rigault excellently restores: “If we have harmed the one who disdains us by retaliatory disdain.” We are disdained by an adversary who hates us; if in turn we hate and disdain him, we have now answered the disdainful man with disdain, repaid evil for evil, and failed to observe the Lord’s command. Here Septimius said “to harm one who disdains” just as he says “to know one who is born” in his book *On the Exhortation to Chastity*.

FASTIDIUM (“disdain”). II, 289 A, “Things released by God and free from disdain,” that is, animals which are free and released from the law, because God did not love them equally but rejected and disdained them.

FASTIGIUM (“eminence”). II, 803 A, “The eminence of the Craftsman ought to have been reconsidered,” that is, his majesty, dignity, and greatness. [Col. 1303C] I, 420 A, “But how vain it is to ascribe the eminence of the Roman name to the merits of religiosity.” II, 920 A, “That he might restore to himself the eminence of an Apostle.”

FATIGABILIS (“liable to fatigue”). II, 703 C, “Liable to fatigue even from climbing stairs.”

FATUM (“fate”). II, 714 A, “Since it is now enrolled even under fate,” that is, numbered and truly included among those things in which inheres that hidden power of preserving the union of body with soul. For this is what they called “fate” and “the fated periods of life,” says Macrobius, *On the Dream of Scipio* I, chapter 13. II, 718 A, “When on the final day the Fates are summoned to write.” II, 683 C, “And the fate and necessity of providence.” I, 428 A, “Jupiter himself is subject to fate.”

FERALIS (“deathly”). I, 1309 A, “In the deathly workshops of the accursed mines.” For condemned persons were sentenced to labor in the mines, and Christians often among them. Concerning these, see Cyprian, book III, letter 25.

FERIA (“weekday”). II, 956 A, “The fourth and sixth weekdays,” that is, Wednesday and Friday.

[Col. 1303D] FERETRUM (“trophy-frame”). I, 1270 C, “What a trophy-frame God constructed for the devil in that man,” that is, what a trophy God erected over the devil. For here *feretrum* is not a *νεκροφόρειον* (“bier”) on which a dead man is carried out, as Rigault maintains, but a triumphal apparatus upon which the weapons of the defeated were carried around in procession. Thus he continues: “What a standard,” etc.

FERRE (“to set against”). I, 647 A, “Let us set all the remaining qualities of those very things against all the things of God.” He says: Let us set the things of the spectacles against the things of God. “The things of God” means divine or Christian discipline. *Contra ferre* means to set against, oppose, compare, bring into conflict, or collate.

FERRUM (“iron”). I, 1328 A, “As from those scars of a body born for iron.” He calls the body “born for iron” because, as soon as it is born, it is wounded with iron: the lowest part of infants’ ears is pierced.

FIBULA (“clasp”). II, 221 B, “For moreover the very word ‘but,’ like a clasp belonging to a connective particle, has been attached to link the narrative,” that is, a conjunction or connective particle [Col. 1304A] clasping and binding the discourse together. A hypallage. II, 850 C, “The word ‘man’ is in a certain sense the clasp of two interwoven substances.” II, 93 B, “Or even loosen the clasp for voluntary sins”: a proverbial expression meaning “open a door or window.” II, 1011 D, “Who would not know that he reluctantly loosened the clasp of this good?”

FICTRIX (“sorceress”). II, 815 A; 1044 B, “And yet a cup—I do not say a poisoned one, etc.—but sorceresses, or chief priests of Cybele.” Le Prieur thinks *frictricis* should be read, and thus Tertullian would have been speaking of cups from which those shameful female tribades or eunuchs drank with a kiss. But Rigault’s reading from the Pithou manuscript is better: *fictricis*, *φαρμακίδος* (“of a sorceress”), a witch who fashions waxen images. Ursinus’s book reads *fascinatoris* (“of an enchanter”). What follows shows that *fictricis*, not *fictrices*, must be read.

FIDELIS (“faithful,” “believer”). II, 908 C, “From whatever instructor they come, whether spiritual or merely a believer.” II, 920 A, “But not all believers are Apostles.” II, 56 A, “Who is a catechumen and who a believer is uncertain.”

[Col. 1304B] FIDELITER (“faithfully”). II, 928 A, “Quite deliberately, and above all faithfully,” that is, as befits a Christian.

FIDENTIA (“confidence”), in a favorable sense. I, 416 A, “They build up confidence in our very hope.” Cicero also used this word in the same sense.

FIDES (“faith,” “assurance”). II, 1040 B, “Let custom yield allegiance to time, nature to God.” Salmasius and others read *debet* (“owes”). *Fidem dare* is to follow, obey, or serve. II, 206 A, *Bona fide*, that is, “to speak seriously and plainly.” II, 543 A, “If you inquire in good faith.” II, 686 B, “I grieve in good faith.” II, 138 B, “For which their suffering would give assurance, etc.,” that is, trustworthy proof and testimony. II, 281 A, “Now not even the sacrament of his faith,” that is, baptism. I, 677 B, “If we say after faith has been sealed,” that is, after baptism. II, 986 C, “Faith too possesses familiarity with certain names.” Here by “faith” he means what elsewhere he calls a sect, philosophy, discipline, or religion—namely the Christian one.

[Col. 1304C] FIDUCIATUS (“having pledged security”). I, 694 A, “Borrowing money from pagans, having given pledges as security,” that is, *ὑποθέμενοι* (“having pledged”); and *fiduciare* is *ὑποτίθεσθαι* (“to pledge as security”) in an old glossary.

FIGERE (“to fix”). II, 1043 B, “And to fix with the point” means to gaze keenly, with the eyes intent and as though fixed upon the object: *ἀτενὲς ὁρᾷν* (“to gaze intently”). Those clad in garments foreign to natural moderation, which drive men awry from an upright condition, openly expose themselves to view—or rather to ridicule—and seem to wish to turn everyone’s eyes upon themselves. Yet the sort of inspection or spectacle they court is joined with reproach and insult toward those who wear them, so that it is reckoned not to their praise but as a fault. To this is added: “To point out with the finger, to betray with a nod.” II, 815 B, “Is it then also a matter of fixing a judgment upon little vessels and instruments?”—that is, asserting it fearlessly and steadfastly.

FIGULARE (“to mold like a potter”). II, 578 A, “Thus the demiurge molds man.” II, 920 B, D, n. 14, “When he had molded man.”

FIGULATIO (“molding”). II, 690 C, “Nor to be compacted and brought forth along with the molding of the flesh.” II, 801 B, “They concede the entire molding [Col. 1304D] of man to our God.”

FIGURA (“figure”). II, 146 B, “To whom rather would he have disclosed the figure of his utterance?”—that is, the mysteries and sacraments of his discourse, when, namely, the meanings depart from their literal expressions. II, 339 C; 620 B, “Babylon is a figure of the city of Rome.” II, 460 C (see note [c], 459 D), “By saying, ‘This is my body’: that is, ‘This is a figure of my body.’” II, 461 B, “But that you may also recognize the ancient figure of blood in wine.” I, 1205 C, “That figure of bodily medicine foretold spiritual medicine.” II, 89 A, “But if they were figures of us”—namely, the temple, ark, tabernacle, etc. II, 489 A, “For he shows that Christ would arise in the figure of a flower.”

FIGURARE (“to represent figuratively”). II, 44 A, “For it properly represents the distinction between doctrines,” that is, teaches it through a figure. II, 811 A, “If the universe insufficiently figures the resurrection.” II, 462 A, “He who then figured wine as blood.”

FIMBRIA is a headband confining the ends of the hair. II, 913 A.

[Col. 1305A] FILIUS OMNIUM (“everyone’s son”), that is, one born from an uncertain union held in common. I, 312 A.

FINIRE (“to end”). II, 143 B, “If he is to be ended by beasts,” that is, if a man condemned to the beasts must undergo death and end his life. II, 966 B, “So that this observance may all the more be ended at the sixth hour of the day,” that is, fixed or determined in advance.

FINIS (“end,” “limit”). II, 207 B–C, “About to punish those whose end is contrary to evil,” that is, about to punish those who have proposed evil to themselves as their end, contrary to his command. II, 271 C, “The limit of malignity will be found,” that is, the highest degree of malignity.

FLABELLARE (“to fan”). II, 1043 A, “But when it is fanned into passion, the heat now comes from a conflagration of glory.”

FLABRUM (“breeze”), a calmer and lighter wind. II, 1033 A, “While the breezes likewise changed.”

FLAGRUM (“whip”). I, 301 A, “Or of those still breaking whips,” that is, those who have only just been manumitted, upon whose backs remain fresh marks of whips broken in the beating.

FLAMMEUM (“bridal veil”). II, 588 A, “For the torch and the bridal veil.” The *flammeum* was a veil with which brides were covered, so called because the wife of the Flamen habitually used it, since she was not permitted to divorce. Suetonius discusses the *flammeum* in *Nero*, chapter 38; Martial, books II and XII; Juvenal, *Satire* VI.

[Col. 1305B] FLAMMEUS (“flame-colored”). I, 1322 A, “With a flame-colored head they take very bad—indeed the worst—omens for themselves,” that is, as though they were foreshadowing and portending for themselves the flame of eternal fire and infernal Gehenna. So Cyprian; and Jerome to Laeta: “Do not dye the hair red and thereby portend something for it in the fires of Gehenna.”

FLATURALIS (“pneumatic,” “pertaining to breath”). II, 663 A, “Because they were not constructed with organs of breath,” that is, with organic parts belonging to breath.

FLERE (“to weep”). II, 708 B, “He wept in vain unto destruction,” that is, he repented in vain.

FLORERE (“to bloom”), of purple: II, 1036 B, “Indeed, deeper than every shellfish-dye with which necks bloom”; of the sun and moon: I, 334 A, “The lights had bloomed,” that is, excelled in splendor. Compare Lucretius, book IV, verse 451. II, 562 A, “Each one bloomed with whatever was best and most beautiful.” Irenaeus: [Col. 1305C] *Ὅπερ εἶχεν ἐν ἑαυτῷ κάλλιστον καὶ ἀνθηρώτατον συνενεγκαμένους* (“bringing together whatever each possessed in himself that was most beautiful and flourishing”).

FLUERE (“to flow,” “to become lax”). II, 1042 B, “He had flowed away from the male.” It signifies a boxer relaxed into the softness of feminine endurance, smeared with ointment and luxuriant in clothing.

FLUSTRUM (“a calm at sea”). Festus: “*Flustra* are so called when the waves do not move in the sea.” II, 1033 A, “Tempered by sea-calms.”

FLUXILIS (“fluid”). II, 578 A, “For fusibility and fluidity are qualities of liquid.”

FOENERARE (“to repay with interest”). I, 1260 A, “They demanded tooth for tooth and repaid evil with evil at interest.” II, 700 A, “And elsewhere they repaid paganism more abundantly.”

FOETURARE (“to carry as a fetus”). II, 578 B, “And thus carried as a fetus,” that is, borne in the womb, *κυοφορηθέν*.

FOLLICULUM (“fetal membrane”). II, 786 B, “Through that umbilical cord, as though it were the offshoot of its membrane.” He calls *folliculum* what Hippocrates calls *ὕμενα, τὴν περιέχοντα τὴν γόνην* (“the membrane containing the offspring”). Galen says that the *chorion* is described by these words. The fetal covering; Hesychius: *κάλυμμα τὸ συγγενόμενον ἐκ τῆς κοιλίας* (“the covering formed together with it from the womb”). [Col. 1305D] The Latins called it the *secundae* (“afterbirth”). Within these are veins through which, as through roots, blood is transmitted to the fetus’s navel; therefore Septimius says that what he calls the umbilical cord is an offshoot of the membrane. Servius notes at *Georgics* III that *follicula* denotes the womb: “‘In the genital field,’” he says, “means ‘in the female membrane,’ which they call the womb, as Pliny also teaches; for it was formerly called *folliculus*.”

FOLLICARE (“to puff up”). II, 1037 B, “Puffing,” that is, inflated like a bellows.

FOLLIS (“bag,” “skin”). I, 532 A, “Pound the bag of Anaxarchus.” Thus he calls his little body. For when he was being pounded like barley-groats, he professed that nothing else was happening to him than what happens to barley: namely, that the divine inner particle, the vigor of the mind, remained unharmed, while only the perishable outer bag was ruptured and scattered.

FORCEPS (“clamp”) is an iron—or rather wooden—instrument [Col. 1306A] shaped like the pincers commonly called tongs. The boss of the toga was fastened with these clamps so that they might constrain the arrangement of its folds and keep them in place. II, 1046 A, “And let him entrust the entire fabrication of the gathered boss to restraining clamps.”

FORIS (“outside”). I, 458 A, “Some outside, others within,” that is, those who have one thing on their lips and another in their hearts. II, 22 A, “And always outside,” that is, outside the Church and alien to God was what the nations held in common.

FORMA (“form,” “rule”). II, 1033 B, “But by swimming out again *in forma*, the world changed again,” etc. One should read *informa* as one word, for *informi* (“formless”), just as *elinguus* is used for *elinguis*; others read *infima* (“the lowest things”). Others read this whole passage: “By swimming out again he changed the lowest things; again another world, the same one changes,” etc. I, 309 A, “From the form of all mysteries,” that is, from their rule. II, 45 B, “According to both forms”: one, the succession of bishops; the other, succession in kinship of doctrine. II, 654 B, “Which is not by the form of corporeal things,” that is, by their law or nature. I, 681 A, “Give the form [Col. 1306B] according to which you wish to be dealt with,” that is, choose the rule according to which you wish to be treated. II, 475 B, “That faith belongs to the God to whom belongs the form of the grace of faith.”

FORMARE (“to suborn”). I, 436 A, “You were suborned by those same spirits.” Above he said “modulated” and “subordinated.”

FORS (“perhaps,” “possibly”). II, 703 B, “Perhaps I should find animals among these kinds as well.”

FORUM (“press-vat”). I, 461 B, “As though in the vat of a winepress.” Properly, among Latin authors *forus* or *forum*, from *ferre* (“to carry”), denotes a wine vessel into which the gathered grape is carried, whence it passes into the empty jar, as Varro says in *On Agriculture* I, chapter 34. From this comes *fora*, very large vessels into which grapes are brought and from which wine is usually poured into jars. Columella, book XI, chapter 2, and book XIII, chapter 18. Among later writers, however, *forum* is *ληνός*, the basin of a winepress.

FOVELA (“comfort”), refreshment. II, 657 A, “Incorporeality is immune from punishment and from comfort.”

FOVERE (“to comfort”). II, 817 A, “For even now souls are tormented and comforted in the lower regions.” He alludes to the rich miser’s request, when he asked for a drop of water from Lazarus, who was being comforted in Abraham’s bosom. From this arose another paradox of Tertullian, who thought that the souls of the saints were comforted and rejoiced in Abraham’s bosom, or paradise, or beneath the altar, until the day of judgment. On this, see *On the Soul*, chapters 7, 9, 55, 56, and 58.

[Col. 1306C] FRATERNITAS (“brotherhood”). II, 908 B, “Because the brotherhood readily receives virgins.” The passage is obscure; it nevertheless seems possible to refer it to almsgiving, through which Christian brothers willingly received virgins.

FRAUDARE (“to deprive”). II, 1034 B, “With a single camp deprived,” concerning Cambyses’ army, covered over and buried by immense heaps of sand in the Libyan Syrtes. I, 494 A, “By which we abstain from defrauding another.”

FREQUENTIA (“multitude”). II, 246 B, “He presented it to the multitude,” that is, published it to the general public. I, 390 A, “Most of the multitudes of the mountains.” He means wild beasts, numerous in the mountains and their inhabitants, such as lions, bears, and so forth.

FRICTRICES, lewd and abominable women; Greek *τριβάδες, ἀπὸ τοῦ τρίβεσθαι ἐναλλὰξ ὀλισβῷ* (“tribades, from rubbing against one another by means of an artificial phallus”). II, 1044 B.

[Col. 1306D] FRIGERE (“to be cold”). I, 465 A, “We who are cold toward every ardor for glory and dignity”; opposed to *calere* (“to be hot”), II, 247 A.

FRIGIDUS (“cold”), of images of the gods, which in this respect also differ in no way from the dead, from whom vital heat is absent. I, 343 A.

FRONS (“front”). II, 947 B, “It was therefore fitting that the whole form of the common discipline should be displayed upon its own front.” Without doubt he calls the bishops, presbyters, and deacons the “front” of the Church. The form—that is, the tablets—of the whole ecclesiastical discipline, he says, ought to be displayed and hung upon the front of the Christian Church, so that the edict which everyone would follow might thus stand in advance. Rhenanus, however, takes the expression proverbially, so that “to be displayed upon its own front” means to be presented freely and openly, not under compulsion.

FRUCTIFICATIO (“fruiting”). II, 458 C, “For if the fruitings of little trees provide a sign of summertime.”

FRUCTUS (“fruit”), enjoyment. II, 457 C, “Concerning the same coming of the Son of Man and its enjoyment.”

[Col. 1307A] FRUSTRA (“deceptively”). II, 1034 A, “The field acts deceptively,” that is, it deceives and frustrates those hoping for fruit.

FRUSTRARI (“to evade”), to mock, evade, or evade and slaughter. I, 372 A, “A hired criminal for evading beasts,” because the greatest part of that daring art consisted in eluding the attacks of beasts. See NOXIUS. II, 381 B, “He has vainly frustrated John’s order.” II, 556 A, “But when wisdom had struggled after impossibilities, it had been frustrated,” that is, deceived. Junius conjectures *frustra erat* (“it was in vain”).

FRUSTRATORIUS (“frustrating,” “deceptive”). II, 731 B, “How much more vain and deceptive.”

FRUTICARE (“to sprout”). II, 552 B, “What had sprouted to the Father’s glory,” that is, had been brought forth like a shoot. II, 825 A–B, “Thus, even if it sprouts into recognition of the sacrament.”

FUGARI (“to be driven”). II, 1045 A, “They are driven into Bellona’s frenzies,” that is, they are seized with madness in Bellona’s temple; hence the name *Bellonarii*.

FUGERE (“to flee”). II, 112 C, “He who fled would fight again.” Rhenanus thinks that a Greek verse had been placed in the text: *ἀνὴρ ὁ φεύγων καὶ πάλιν μαχήσεται* (“The man who flees will also fight again”). [Col. 1307B] Long ago, when Demosthenes fled from the battle in the Chersonese in which the Athenians were defeated while fighting Philip, king of the Macedonians, he was accustomed to reply with this little verse to those who reproached him for that shameful flight and for throwing away his shield.

FULIGO (“soot”). II, 1041 B, “They smear their eyes with soot,” that is, with antimony, that black powder. See PULVIS.

FUMUS (“smoke”). II, 991 A, “It emerges new, now no longer from the mud of seed, not from the smoke of desire, but from water and the pure Spirit.” Wouwer conjectures *fimo* (“dung”); by pursuing similarity of sound, however, he corrupts antithesis, Tertullian’s most characteristic device. For just as pure water is set against the “mud of seed”—that is, liquid mixed with the filth of earth—so the pure Spirit is set against the turbid and dense “smoke of desire.” Although Tertullian fashioned this expression with reference to the pure Spirit, the comparison of desire and foul lust with smoke, which envelops men’s minds and blinds them with darkness, is nevertheless not foreign to the teachings of ancient sages.

[Col. 1307C] FUNAMBULUS (“tightrope-walker”). II, 1000 B, “Come then, you tightrope-walker of modesty and chastity.”

FUNDERE (“to spread out”). II, 1041 A, “To spread out a matron’s robe,” that is, to wear a fuller robe. The *stola* was a woman’s garment which men generally could not wear without disgrace—

FUNERARE (“to dispose of in death”). II, 737 B, “Therefore they say that it should not be disposed of even by fire.”

FUNESTARE (“to make deathly,” “to defile”). II, 810 A, “The glory of the world is made deathly; every substance is blackened.”

FUNGI (“to perform”). II, 1032 B, “It performs”—supply: “that function of changing the garment.” Pamelius interprets it “it gradually perishes”; others read *stringitur* (“it is drawn tight”). II, 426 A, “Thus I shall knock nowhere else than where I have performed my function,” that is, where and upon whom should I knock, except upon the very one who calls me and instructs me by this exercise of his calling? A rather obscure metalepsis. II, 87 B, “Not otherwise … for one who has completed ‘the man,’” Greek *τὸν ἄνθρωπον τετεληκότι*, that is, *τὰ ἀνθρώπινα* (“human affairs”). A Hellenism.

FUNGUS (“swelling”). I, 655 B, “And swellings of the ears.” *Fungi* are tubers or swellings arising from blows and the collection of blood, the name being transferred from diseases of trees, [Col. 1307D] concerning which see Pliny, *Natural History* XVII, chapter 24.

FUNIS (“rope”). II, 355 B, “A rope of controversy must also be drawn concerning the inscription.” “To draw a rope” is a proverbial expression for disputing with someone. It is derived from a children’s game in which they pulled a hempen rope and continued pulling until either they broke it or one of the two, releasing his grip upon it, fell on his buttocks. II, 597 A, “With a contentious rope drawn by turns.” II, 984 C, “But it is not fitting to stretch such a contentious rope any further in opposite directions by alternately drawing upon the authority of the Scriptures.” II, 843 B, “I do not now wish to draw the matter out with the rope of controversy.”

FURIAE (“frenzies”), the madness of Bacchants. I, 306 A, “Although you have now sacrificed your frenzies to the Italian Bacchus.” II, 987 A, “But the remaining impious frenzies of lusts.”

FURTIVUS (“secret”). II, 750 C, “How often, likewise, when the body is afflicted, the soul seeks out a secret joy for itself.”

FURUNCULUS (“boil”). II, 315 A, “A wolf or a boil does not remain hidden.”

## G

[Col. 1308A]

GALATICARI (“to Galatianize”). II, 973 A, “Therefore, in observing their times and days and months and years, we plainly Galatianize,” that is, we imitate the Galatians, rave like the Galatians, Judaize like the Galatians. Similarly, from the fraudulent character of the Cilicians, the comic poet Pherecrates coined the verb *ἐγκιλικίζειν* (“to Cilicianize”) to signify the deceits and impostures of the pagan gods: *ἀεί ποθ᾽ ἡμῖν ἐγκιλικίζουσ᾽ οἱ θεοί*—“the gods are always somehow imposing upon and mocking us in Cilician fashion.”

GALATICUS (“Galatian-red,” scarlet). II, 1045 A, “An overlay of Galatian red” is a cloak dyed with Galatian scarlet. Pliny, *Natural History* IX, chapter 41.

GALERUS (“cap”). The *galerus* of matrons who were priestesses of Ceres was an arrangement of added hair made in the form of a cap, with which matrons especially covered the head. II, 1045 A, “Because of the mark of the fillet—not *vitae*, ‘life’—and the privilege of the cap.”

GARAMANTES. II, 903 A, “Or the feathers of the Garamantes.” The Garamantes were peoples of Africa, very much given to robbery, as Pliny relates in *Natural History* V, chapter 6. In order [Col. 1308B] to appear more savage and inspire greater terror among Africans, they equipped themselves with the feathers of the most terrifying animals they could find, of the kind the same author describes in book VIII, chapter 21. Junius prefers “or the crests of the Garamantes,” that is, twisted braids of hair shaped like the crest on soldiers’ helmets or those of Samnite gladiators, whom Martial mentioned in book I, epigram 3: “And the Ethiopians with hair differently twisted”; for the Garamantes were their neighbors.

GEHENNA. I, 520 A, “If we threaten Gehenna, which is a subterranean treasury of hidden fire reserved for punishment.” I, 1247 A, “Consider Gehenna in your heart.” II, 437 A, “Because after it follows the oven or furnace of Gehenna.”

GEMONIUS (“Gemonian”). II, 590 B, “Through truly Gemonian steps.” These were at Rome on the Aventine, in the thirteenth region of the city, near the Bean Portico. They were also called the Gemonian Stairs, upon which the bodies of condemned persons were piled and exposed to public view until they were dragged with a hook into the Tiber. Tertullian had this infamous place in view when he said, “one fruit after another.”

[Col. 1308C] GENEROSITAS (“noble birth”). II, 478 A, “And that the noble birth of Christianity, in Abraham’s son born of the free woman, possesses the mystery of allegory, just as the legal servitude of Judaism does in the son of the slave woman.” II, 658 B, “That they may judge the noble birth of their offspring from the boldness of the pupils”—concerning the eagle.

GENICULATIO (“kneeling”), a bending of the knees. I, 703 C, “When have droughts not also been driven away by our kneelings and fasts?” I, 1222 B, “One must pray with fasts, kneelings, and vigils.”

GENIMINA (“offspring”). II, 718 A, “They profess their offspring as candidates of demons.”

GENS (“nation”). II, 246 C, “The most savage nations were the Sarmatians, Geloni, Massagetae, and the entire race of wagon-dwelling peoples.”

GENTICUS (“national,” “tribal”). II, 718 B, “For a tribal act of devotion.” Tacitus similarly used the expression “tribal custom” in *Annals* VI.

GENTILITUS (“nation by nation”). II, 1040 A, “Part of which is inhabited nation by nation,” that is, by particular nations.

GENUINI (“molars”), the back teeth which grind food. II, 662 B, “Bring out your molars.” II, 1042 A, “Molars shadowed among the forelocks.”

[Col. 1308D] GINATEM. II, 234 B, “The middle *ginatem*, as they say.” These words yield no meaning; accordingly, the reading presented by all the manuscripts must be restored: “of the middle *agina*, as they say,” namely, a movement so evenly balanced that it inclines in neither direction and neither side outweighs the other. “For,” as Festus teaches, “the *agina* is the hole into which the beam of the balance is inserted and in which it turns.”

GLUTEN (“glue”). II, 988 B, “The letters themselves are our bonds.”

GOMPHUS, Greek *γόμφος*, “nail.” I, 340 A, “We place our necks before lead and glue and nails.” Tertullian enumerates three materials employed about the heads of gods when these were set upon statues: lead, used in images made of gold, silver, tin, and lead; glue, used in wooden images and those fashioned from clay; and nails, used in colossal statues, where the head was fixed to the neck with iron nails.

GRADUS (“position,” “stance”), of time. I, 391 A, “Through two stages,” [Col. 1309A] namely past and future.  
II, 22 B, “Look: I now voluntarily withdraw from that position.” II, 512 A, “So that now Marcion too may withdraw from his position.” II, 993 B, “I shall now withdraw from that position for a little while.” All are metaphors taken from athletes already engaged in combat.  
I, 436 A, “Which I must necessarily meet from the same stance,” taken from gladiators, who, when about to decide the contest with an antagonist, plant their stance; retreat to evade blows; advance again; take their stand upon the same spot; and resist as they go to meet him. The meaning is: “for which it is necessary to use the same argument and weapon.”  
II, 260 A, “When by this position we drive God out,” that is, when we take this power—the power of creating—away from God.  
II, 377 A, “Until I drive you, Marcion, from your position,” that is, until I convict you of falsehood and weaken your arguments.  
II, 654 C, “Unless I drive out the position of this definition,” that is, unless I show that neither the definition nor the reality can stand. All these expressions are drawn from wrestlers and signify refuting an adversary and defeating him in the contest of disputation.  
II, 797 B, “For everyone cast down or moved from the position of the hope he had adopted,” [Col. 1309B] that is, one who, induced by others’ persuasions, has now abandoned the hope upon which he relied.  
II, 368 A, “But from here we now enter upon another position,” that is, to enter a new contest—or at least a new manner of contest—or, once one matter has been disproved and shaken, to prepare oneself to overthrow another.  
II, 321 A, “From this point, finally, I join battle.” II, 599 A, “Let us therefore join battle.” II, 609 C, “Let us therefore join battle on this point”: *κατάρχεσθαι τῆς μαχῆς* (“to begin the battle”), to open the contest of disputation, to approach the matter more closely.  
II, 452 B, “But I direct myself toward the position of another question,” that is, I prepare myself to examine another question.  
II, 363 C, “And here I could now plant my stance.” II, 495 A, “And accordingly we rightly plant this stance”; and in many other places. The metaphor, drawn from athletes engaging in a contest, means vigorously defending a position once adopted and persisting in it, or “pressing one’s argument,” as Cicero says; or dwelling upon the careful weighing of a single matter without making any irrelevant digressions.  
II, 971 B, “You have therefore moved from your position by departing from tradition, since you observe things that have not been established.”  
[Col. 1309C] I, 433 A, “When challenged to sacrifice, we bar the approach of treachery”—others read “on behalf of the faith”—“with our conscience”; that is, we steadfastly resist and struggle against it, and close every entrance into our minds.  
II, 29 A, “This position, therefore, we bar above all,” that is, this is the first objection we make and remove from the contest.  
II, 248 A, “But another treatise will maintain this position against the heretics,” that is, will undertake the defense of this matter.  
II, 324 A, “I would have made these preliminary moves, as though still from the first position.” Before the proper contest with an antagonist, gladiators were accustomed to make preliminary displays for the sake of exhibiting their skill and entertaining the spectators. Thus it means to introduce at the outset light and almost playful, comparatively weak arguments, before the true strength of the arguments is brought forth.  
II, 1023 B, “But I shall now also run down into this position,” that is, I shall also prepare myself to investigate this matter.  
II, 470 B, “At the very position we shall invoke the prescription.”  
II, 159 A, “Let this also have been said by me concerning the third rank,” that is, the person [Col. 1309D] third in order. See also column 157 C.  
II, 164 C, “To show the third rank in the Paraclete.”  
II, 433 B, “If those things accrue to the kingdom, they must be restored in the second rank; the second rank belongs to him whose the first also is.”

GRAECARI (“to Greekize”), to imitate Greek customs. II, 1048 A–B, “Unless even the breeze is not at all Greekized.” Barthius, in his *Adversaria*, interprets it: “is cultivated in the Greek manner.”

GRAECATUM (“in the Greek manner”). II, 1045 A, “And sandals flatter Aesculapius in Greek fashion.”

GRAECIA (“Greece”), the shamelessness of the utterly mendacious Greeks. II, 1037 A, “You will immediately laugh at the audacity and ‘Greece’ of the name.” Others read “the exceptional audacity of the name”; others, “the name now Greek”; Turnebus, “and the charm of the name.” II, 972 B, “Throughout the Greeces,” that is, throughout the various provinces of Greece in which various councils were convened.

GRAMA, Greek *λήμη*, a defect of the eyes, as Festus teaches—that is, rheum of the eyes. II, 578 A, “And mud from the rheums and eye-discharges of wisdom.”

[Col. 1310A] GRANDIS (“great,” “grand”). I, 452 B, “A grand duty indeed, to bring hearths and marriage-beds out into public.” II, 1032 B, “Suitable indeed for grander fables.”

GRATIA (“grace,” “claim”). II, 131 B, “For martyrdom does not contend with idolatry over some evil common to both, but over its own grace”; that is, the contest is not between two evils as to which is more powerful, but martyrdom contends for its own grace, its own good, which liberates from idolatry. II, 309 A, “Is the Egyptians’ complaint therefore greater, or the Hebrews’ claim?” The “claim” is what was owed to them and what they had earned—that is, their wages.

GRATIOSITAS (“attractiveness”). II, 255 B, “And I wished to beat back the very natural attractiveness of novelty.”

GRATUITUS (“gratuitous,” “without consequence”). II, 726 A, “Finally, good deeds too are gratuitous in dreams, and offenses are without danger.”
