[Col. 0964B] This principal form of disciplining one’s diet can now also establish a presumption concerning the lesser practices of abstinence, according as they too are useful or necessary in their measure. For the exclusion of certain foods is a partial fast. Let us therefore examine the novelty or futility of xerophagies—that is, dry diets—to see whether in these too there is not a practice of religious discipline as ancient as it is effective.

I return to Daniel and his companions, who preferred a diet of vegetables and a drink of water to the royal dishes and wine-jars, and thereby became more handsome—so that no one need fear even for his little body’s appearance—and, moreover, spiritually cultivated. For God gave the young men knowledge and understanding in every branch of learning, and to Daniel understanding in every utterance, in dreams, and in all wisdom (Dan. 1:17), by which he might also know this very thing: by what means an understanding of mysteries could be obtained from God.

Finally, in the third year of Cyrus, king of the Persians, when he had fallen into reflection upon a vision, he adopted another form of humiliation:

> “In those days I, Daniel, was mourning for three weeks. I ate no pleasant bread; neither flesh nor wine entered my mouth; I did not anoint myself with oil, until the three weeks were completed.”

When they had elapsed, an angel was sent and addressed him as follows:

> “Daniel, you are a pitiable man; do not be afraid, because from the first day on which you applied your soul to reflection and humiliation before God, your word was heard, and I came in response to your word.”

[Col. 0965A] Thus the wretched condition and humiliation of xerophagies dispel fear, turn God’s ears toward us, and make us masters of hidden things.

I return also to Elijah. Although ravens had been accustomed to satisfy him with bread and meat, why afterward, near Beersheba in Judaea, when he had been awakened from sleep, did that angel undoubtedly offer him only bread and water? Had the ravens failed, which might have fed him more generously? Or would it have been difficult for the angel to seize some servant from a royal banquet somewhere, together with a fully furnished dish, and transfer him to Elijah, just as a reapers’ meal was supplied to the hungry Daniel in the lions’ den? Rather, it was necessary to establish an example teaching that in a time of distress, persecution, or any sort of exigency, one must live by xerophagies.

With such a diet David gave expression to his confession: he ate ashes as though they were bread—that is, bread dry and foul as ashes—and mixed his drink with weeping, plainly in place of wine (Ps. 101:10). For abstinence from wine also has its claims to distinction: it both vowed Samuel to God and consecrated Aaron. For Samuel’s mother said:

> “He shall drink neither wine nor intoxicating drink” (1 Reg. 1:15);

for such was also the manner in which she herself prayed to God. And the Lord said to Aaron:

> “You shall drink neither wine nor strong drink, you and your sons after you, whenever you enter the tabernacle or go up to the altar, and you shall not die.”

So truly will those die who minister in the Church without sobriety. In the same way he reproaches Israel shortly afterward:

> “And you gave my consecrated ones wine to drink.”

This restriction of drink, moreover, is a part of xerophagy. Yet wherever abstinence from wine is either required by God or vowed by a human being, there we should understand that restriction of food is also implied, setting the pattern for the drink: as the food, so the drink. It is not likely that anyone would sacrifice half his appetite to God, sober in his water but drunken in his food.

Whether the Apostle too knew xerophagies—he who had undergone greater things, thirst and hunger and many fasts, and who rejected drunken bouts and revelries—the case of his disciple Timothy is sufficient proof. On account of Timothy’s stomach and frequent infirmities, he advised him to use a little wine, from which Timothy had abstained not by institutional rule but by devotion—although habit would have been better for his stomach. [Col. 0966A] By the very fact that he discouraged abstinence from wine under the compulsion of necessity, he commended it as worthy of God (cf. Lev. 10:9; Amos [sic] 15:13; 2 Cor. 11:27; Rom. 13:13; 1 Tim. 5:23).

## CHAPTER X

They likewise denounce our stations—our appointed fasts—as unworthy, and some of them, because they are continued into the evening, under the charge of novelty. They say that this duty too should be undertaken voluntarily and not prolonged beyond the ninth hour, following, of course, their own custom. But as regards the question of their being imposed, I shall answer once for all the relevant cases. For the moment, concerning the particular issue belonging to this form of observance—that is, the measure of its duration—we must first demand of them whence they derive this new rule which they prescribe for terminating stations.

If it is because Peter and those with him are said to have entered the temple for prayer at the ninth hour, who will prove to me that they were observing a station on that day, so that the ninth hour may be interpreted as the conclusion and discharge of the station? Indeed, you would more readily find that Peter went up to the upper parts at the sixth hour to pray because it was time to take food, so that the sixth hour might all the more be fixed as the conclusion of this duty, which he seemed about to complete after prayer.

Moreover, since in the same narrative of Luke the third hour is also shown as an hour of prayer, when those who had been initiated by the Holy Spirit were regarded as drunk; and the sixth, when Peter went up to the upper parts; and the ninth, when they entered the temple—why should we not understand that, while the unrestricted liberty of praying always, everywhere, and at every time remains entirely intact, these three hours nevertheless had greater solemnity in divine prayers, since they are especially distinguished in human affairs, divide the day, mark off its business, and are publicly sounded?

Daniel’s example also recommends this interpretation, for he prayed three times a day—plainly by setting apart certain hours, and none other than those which were distinguished and afterward apostolic: the third, sixth, and ninth. Accordingly, I shall say that Peter, following the ancient custom, observed the ninth hour, praying for the third time in the duty of the final prayer.

I say this because of those who think they are acting according to Peter’s rule, though they do not understand it; not as though we rejected the ninth hour, at which we most often complete our stations on Wednesday and Friday, but because, in the case of observances kept by tradition, we ought all the more to provide a worthy rationale in proportion as they lack the authority of Scripture, until they are either confirmed or corrected by some heavenly gift.

> “And if there is anything you do not understand, the Lord will reveal it to you” (Phil. 3:15).

Therefore, after setting aside the Paraclete, the confirmer of all these things and guide of all truth, I ask whether among you a less worthy rationale is advanced for observing the ninth hour, so that this same rationale may be attributed even to Peter, if he was then observing a station. For it is derived from the Lord’s departure. Although that departure ought always to be commemorated without distinction of hours, we are nevertheless then more deeply bound to him, in accordance with the very designation “station”; for soldiers too, although never forgetful of their military oath, are more especially obedient when on station-duty.

Accordingly, the discipline of abstinence must be observed until that hour at which the world, darkened from the sixth hour onward, performed its mournful office for the Lord at his death, so that we too may then return to gladness when the world also recovered its brightness.

If this savors more strongly of Christian religion because it more fully celebrates Christ’s glory, I can equally establish from the same sequence of events the fixed point of a late station: namely, that we should fast until evening, awaiting the time of the Lord’s burial, when Joseph carried away the body he had requested and laid it in the tomb. Hence it is even irreligious to refresh the servants’ flesh before the master’s.

But I would concede this only in response to the challenge of their arguments, repelling conjectures with conjectures—though, I think, with more trustworthy ones. Let us see whether antiquity too affords us any such support.

In Exodus, did not that posture of Moses, as he fought against Amalek with prayers and persevered until sunset, constitute a late station? Do we suppose that Joshua son of Nun ate lunch on that day when, as he fought the Amorites, he commanded even the elements to keep station?

> “The sun stood still in Gibeon, and the moon in Aijalon. The sun and moon stood still at the command, until the people took vengeance upon their enemies; and the sun stood still in the middle of heaven and did not proceed toward its setting and the end of a single day.”

No such day existed before or afterward—plainly one so prolonged—“that,” Scripture says, “God should listen to a human being,” one, to be sure, equal to the sun in remaining so long at his duty, his station even longer than a late one.

Certainly Saul too, when engaged in battle, expressly imposed this duty (1 Reg. 14:24):

> “Cursed is the man who eats bread before evening, until I take vengeance upon my enemy.”

None of all his people tasted, and the whole land did not eat. God, moreover, granted such authority to the edict of that station that Jonathan, Saul’s son, although unaware that the fast had been fixed to last into the evening, was immediately convicted by lot of an offense because he had tasted honey; and only with difficulty was he delivered from danger through the people’s entreaty, for he had become guilty of appetite, however innocent.

Daniel too, in the first year of King Darius, while fasting in sackcloth and ashes and making confession to God, says:

> “And while I was still speaking in prayer, behold, the man whom I had seen in the vision at the beginning, flying swiftly, drew near to me at about the hour of the evening sacrifice.”

This will be a late station, which, fasting until evening, sacrifices to God a richer prayer.

## CHAPTER XI

[Col. 0968B] I believe, however, that all these things are unknown to those who take offense at our practices, or are perhaps known only from reading and not from attentive consideration. This accords with the greater mass of the unlearned among, of course, that most glorious multitude of the Psychics.

For this reason we have proceeded through each separate form—fasts, xerophagies, and stations—so that, as we review the preparation furnished by both Testaments and show how greatly the duties of refusing, reducing, or delaying food are beneficial, we may blunt those who disparage these practices as useless. Likewise, by showing at the same time the rank they have always held within religious discipline, we may refute those who denounce them as novel: for what has always existed is not new, and what is useful is not empty.

There is also this consideration before us: some of these duties, having been commanded by God to a human being, established a law; others, having been offered by a human being to God, fulfilled a vow. Yet even a vow, when accepted by God, creates a law for the future through the authority of the one who accepted it; for by approving what had been done, he thereby commanded that it should be done thereafter.

Here, accordingly, the controversy of the opposing side is introduced under another form when they say: “It is either false prophecy, if a spiritual voice established these solemn observances, or heresy, if human presumption devised them.” Yet in censuring the very form through which the ancient observances too ran their course, and in turning back upon us arguments that opponents of the ancient observances might have raised against them, they will be obliged either to reject those ancient observances too or certainly to accept these current ones—especially since the latter, whoever their institutor may be, whether spiritual or merely faithful, tend toward the same God as the ancient observances.

For both heresy and false prophecy will undoubtedly be judged by a difference of divinity among all of us who are champions of the one God, the Creator, and of his Christ. I therefore defend this side without distinction, offering them whichever position they wish to take.

“It is the spirit of the devil,” you say, O Psychic. How, then, does it impose duties belonging to our God and require them to be offered to none other than our God? Either contend that the devil cooperates with our God, or let Satan be regarded as the Paraclete.

But you affirm that it is a human Antichrist; for in John heretics are called by this name. How, then, did whoever he is arrange in our Christ these duties toward our Lord, when Antichrists too have come forth against our Christ in relation to God? On which side, therefore, do you think the spirit is confirmed among us: when it commands, or when it approves what our God has both always commanded and always approved?

Yet once again you plant boundary-stakes for God, in discipline just as in grace, in solemn observances just as in spiritual gifts—as though his duties had ceased just as his benefits had, and so you deny that he still imposes obligations because here too “the Law and the Prophets were until John.” It remains for you to abolish him altogether, since as far as lies in you he is so wholly inactive.

## CHAPTER XII

For already in this kind of observance too, “made rich and filled, you reign” [Col. 0970A] (1 Cor. 4:8), accusing yourselves of no sins to be worn away by fasts, needing no knowledge of revelations to be wrested forth through xerophagies, and fearing no battles of your own to be dispersed by stations.

Even if the Paraclete had fallen silent after John, we ourselves ought especially for this purpose to have become prophets to ourselves. I no longer mean merely for appeasing God’s anger, or obtaining his protection or grace, but for fortifying ourselves beforehand against the condition of the last times, by imposing every form of ταπεινοφρόνησις (*humble self-abasement*). For prison must be learned beforehand; hunger and thirst must be practiced; endurance of both starvation and a straitened diet must be acquired, so that a Christian may enter prison in the same condition in which he would have emerged from it.

He will not be going there to suffer punishment, but discipline; not the world’s torments, but his own duties. Thus he will go forth from confinement to the contest all the more confidently, already worn away, having nothing left of the flesh, so that the tortures too may have no material upon which to work; armed only in dry skin, hardened like horn against the claws; having already sent ahead the sap of his blood, as though it were the soul’s baggage, while the soul itself now hastens onward, having through frequent fasting already learned to know death close at hand.

It is plainly your practice to furnish taverns in prisons for martyrs whose constancy is uncertain, lest they miss their customary fare, grow weary of life, or be scandalized by the new discipline of abstinence—a discipline which not even that former martyr of yours, Pristinus, no Christian, had touched. For a while, taking advantage of lax custody, you stuffed him with every bath as though it were better than baptism, every retreat of luxury as though it were more private than the Church, and every enticement of this life as though it were worthier than eternal life. By these things, I suppose, you so bound him not to wish to die, and finally, on the very day of his trial, so enervated him—having first medicated him in broad daylight with spiced wine as though with an antidote—that, when merely tickled by a few claws, for that was all his drunkenness perceived, he could no longer answer the governor when asked which Lord he confessed.

And thus, already wrenched away from this confession, having nothing but hiccups and belches, he defecated in the very act of his denial.

That is why those who preach the discipline of sobriety are false prophets; that is why those who observe it are heretics! Why, then, do you hesitate to believe in Apicius as the Paraclete whom you deny in Montanus?

You lay down as a prior objection that the solemn observances belonging to this faith were established by the Scriptures or by the tradition of the elders, and that nothing further is to be added to its practice, because innovation is unlawful. Stand your ground at that position, if you can.

> **Note [Col. 0969C].** “Going forth from confinement to the contest, worn away.” *Abusus* means almost consumed and exhausted by long use. If the manuscript evidence afforded any help, I would prefer *obesus*, in the sense in which an ancient poet cited by Gellius used *obesus* for “thin and slender.” —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 0969C].** “Hardened like horn against the claws.” Claws were instruments of torture. At first, as the name suggests, they were claws cut from animals; later they were iron implements made in their shape. With these they scraped—or, as some said, “wrote upon” or “engraved”—the victims’ sides. Septimius therefore says that skin made dry and horny by fasting would be more resistant to such tortures. —RIG.

> **Note [Cols. 0969C–0969D].** “Having already sent ahead the sap of his blood.” That is, having sent it ahead to God, to whom he himself is already hastening in his entirety. Thus elsewhere he spoke of a “wife sent ahead” and “children sent ahead,” meaning those who had already departed from this world. —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 0969D].** “As though it were the soul’s baggage.” He uses “baggage” in the military sense. A Christian emaciated by many fasts has already sent this part of himself—his blood—to the Lord as his baggage, vessels, and equipment. How severe this Paraclete-inspired teacher is with those exceedingly austere fasts! Yet I marvel that in bloodless and horny tissues there is so much wit, charm, and elegance. —RIG.

> **Note [Cols. 0969D–0970C].** “It is plainly your practice to furnish taverns in prisons for martyrs whose constancy is uncertain.” At the beginning of the book *Against the Valentinians*, he says that the love of the brethren is readily given; and in the book *Against Praxeas*, that the greater part of believers are always simple and uneducated. Here, therefore, he seems to reproach people of this kind for the offices of most generous charity bestowed upon that impostor Peregrinus, who was eventually recognized and expelled from Christian communion, as Lucian relates. Septimius also mentions him among pagan examples in the book *To the Martyrs*. —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 0970C].** “To furnish taverns.” Lucian: συνεκάθευδον μετ’ αὐτοῦ, διαφθείροντες τοὺς δεσμοφύλακας, εἶτα δεῖπνα ποικίλα εἰσεκομίζετο—“They slept there with him, bribing the prison guards; then varied dinners were brought in.”

> **Note [Col. 0970C].** “Martyrs whose constancy is uncertain”—those whose faith had been less thoroughly tested and not sufficiently proved.

> **Note [Col. 0970C].** “Lest they miss their customary fare”—lest they long for what was customary and familiar to them; so that they might live more luxuriously in prison than at home.

> **Note [Cols. 0970C–0970D].** “Lest they be scandalized by the new discipline of abstinence, which not even that former martyr of yours, Pristinus, no Christian, had touched.” We think one should read “your former one”—that is, one who was once yours—just as at the beginning of the book *Against Praxeas* he said “a former teacher.” Many details in Lucian’s account of Peregrinus fit the history Septimius narrates here. Lucian reports that for a time this man was famous and highly esteemed among the Christians; that because of discourses about Christ which he delivered throughout the cities of Syria, he was arrested and thrown into prison; that there he was cared for with the greatest diligence by the Christians’ charity and zeal, with dinners of every kind brought in; and that he was soon released from prison through the governor’s clemency. Later, after being expelled from Christian fellowship for certain offenses, he traveled through various cities, philosophizing with some renown. Finally at Athens, driven mad by a craving for fame, he summoned a crowd, delivered a lengthy speech on contempt for death, leapt into a blazing pyre, and was seen no more. Septimius says that this martyr, whatever his character, was a Psychic—that is, a Catholic—but not a Christian, because under torture he had denied Christ. Lucian writes that his Peregrinus was cast into prison because he revered Christ, τὸν μέγαν ἐκεῖνον ἄνθρωπον ἐν Παλαιστίνῃ ἀνασκολοπισθέντα—“that great man who was crucified in Palestine”; that the Christians immediately hurried to him as brothers and provided every kind of consolation; and indeed that, after bribing the guards, they remained with him and spent the night in the prison. —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 0971C].** “Stuffed,” Semler.

> **Note [Cols. 0971C–0971D].** “Having first medicated him with spiced wine as though with an antidote.” He was stupefied with myrrh-flavored wine and fortified in advance by the myrrh. In the account of the martyrdom of Fructuosus, Augurius, and Eulogius, we read that a spiced drink was offered to them by the charity of the brethren. When advised to take the mixed drink, they replied that it was not yet the hour for breaking the fast. It was then the fourth hour of the day, and a Wednesday; on that weekday they were observing their customary station even in prison. Moreover, those who, at the very onset of martyrdom, took such care of their bodies that they would feel neither suffering nor torture seem to have made a mockery of martyrdom. For what steadfastness or glory belongs to a person stupefied into insensibility by intoxication from a medicated drink? —RIG.

For this Pristinus, stuffed with excessive wine and food, denied Christ when torture was applied, and in the very act of denial, amid hiccups and belches, his perfidious bowels having been loosened, he did what Lucius did in the Milesian tale when he drove the executioners away from his donkey—or what the dying emperor Claudius did, speaking through the part by which, as Seneca says, he spoke more readily. But how did he deny Christ if in his drunkenness he could produce nothing but hiccups and belches? He appears to have denied Christ because through drunkenness he deprived himself of the consciousness of martyrdom and the glory of a splendid confession. —RIG.

## CHAPTER XIII

For behold, I now bring a countercharge against you. Apart from Easter, you fast outside those days on which the Bridegroom was taken away; you introduce the half-fasts of stations; and at times you live on mere bread and water, as each person sees fit. Finally, you reply that these things are to be done voluntarily, not by command.

You have therefore abandoned your position and gone beyond tradition, since you perform observances that have not been established. What sense does it make for you to allow to your own discretion what you do not grant to God’s command? Shall human will be permitted more than divine power?

I remember that I am free in relation to the world, not to God. With me, voluntarily performing a duty for the Lord stands on the same footing as his imposing it: I owe him not only obedience but courtship; the former I render at his command, the latter at my own discretion.

It is well, moreover, that bishops too are accustomed to command fasts for the whole people. I do not mean deliberately for the purpose of gathering contributions, as is your means of profit, but sometimes because of some cause for ecclesiastical concern. Accordingly, if at a human edict all of you together practice ταπεινοφρόνησις (*humble self-abasement*), how do you censure among us the very unity of fasts, xerophagies, and stations? Unless perhaps by our assemblies we are violating senatorial decrees and imperial mandates directed against associations.

When the Holy Spirit preached in whatever lands and through whatever persons he chose, he foresaw impending ecclesiastical trials or worldly scourges and, as Paraclete—that is, Advocate—commanded the remedies furnished by such duties for the purpose of entreating the Judge. Suppose for the moment that this was done merely to train us in the discipline of sobriety and abstinence: because we receive him, we necessarily observe what he then established.

Look at the Jewish calendars, and you will find nothing novel: whatever practices have been commanded to the fathers, all subsequent posterity preserves by hereditary religious observance.

In addition, throughout the regions of Greece, councils drawn from all the churches are held in designated places. Through these, all the more important matters are dealt with in common, and the very representation of the entire Christian name is celebrated with great reverence. And how worthy a thing this is: with faith presiding, to assemble from every direction unto Christ!

> “Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell together in unity” (Ps. 102 [sic]).

You hardly know how to sing this except when dining with a great many others. Those assemblies, however, first performed their work through stations and fasts; they know how to grieve with those who grieve, and only then to rejoice together with those who rejoice.

If we too, in different provinces yet mutually represented in the Spirit, perform these solemn observances for which the word then present provided authority, that is the law of the sacrament.
