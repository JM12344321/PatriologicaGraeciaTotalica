### 11

Nor are we accused under this name merely of abandoning the common religion, but also of introducing a monstrous superstition. For, as some of you have dreamed, an ass’s head is our god. Cornelius Tacitus gave rise to this suspicion. In the fourth book of his *Histories*, [Col. 0577B] where he arranges his account of the Jewish war, he begins with the origin of that people and, having argued as he pleased both about that origin and about the name of their religion, relates that the Jews, while on an expedition through desolate places and suffering from a lack of water, escaped by employing as guides to a spring

[Note: [Col. 0577D] Havercamp rightly directed that this be written instead of the common reading, “from springs”; cf. *Apology* 16.]

some wild asses, which were thought to be seeking water after feeding. Thus, out of gratitude for this, the Jews worshiped the image of a similar beast. From this, I suppose, it was presumed that we also, as relatives of the Jewish religion, are initiated into the same image.

Yet the same Cornelius Tacitus—that most loquacious purveyor of lies—forgetting his own assertion, relates later that Pompey the Great, after defeating the Jews and capturing Jerusalem, entered the temple, searched it, and found no image there. Where, then, would that god have been? Surely nowhere more than in so memorable a temple, especially one closed to everyone except the priests, who would have no reason to fear an outsider.

But why should I defend myself, when for the present I profess a provisional admission of everything that can equally be transferred to you? [Col. 0577C] Let our god be believed to have some asinine personage: will you really deny that you have the same things among yourselves? Indeed, you worship entire asses together with their Epona, and every beast of burden, herd animal, and wild beast, which you similarly consecrate along with their stalls. And perhaps you charge us with this as a crime because, among the worshipers of them all, we worship only the ass. But whoever asserts that we are priests of the cross will be our fellow devotee.

### 12

The essential character of a cross is that it is a sign made of wood. Among yourselves also you worship what is of the same material, though with an effigy upon it. Although yours has a human figure, so too does ours have its own proper form. Let the outlines be disregarded, provided that the essential character is one; let the form be disregarded, provided that the wood itself is the god’s body. [Col. 0577D–0578A] But if a difference arises on this point, how far removed from the upright post of a cross are Attic Pallas and Pharian Ceres, who are represented without form by a rough stake and by a mere shapeless little piece of standing wood?

[Note: [Col. 0577D] **A part of the cross, and indeed the greater part, etc.** Crosses are left to us, crosses are fixed up for us; yet from this the greatest honor redounds to us. Every piece of timber fixed upright in a straight position is a part of a cross, and indeed its greater part. But the entire cross is charged against us, including, of course, its crossbeam and that projecting seat.

[Note: [Col. 0578D] **Including that projecting seat.** The seat of the cross was a shorter stake driven into the middle of the upright. Ἐν τῷ μέσῳ πηγνύμενον ξύλον ὡς κέρας, καὶ αὐτὸ ἔξεχον, ἐφ` ὧ ἐποχοῦνται οἱ σταυρούμενοι (“A piece of wood fixed in the middle like a horn and itself projecting, upon which those being crucified sit astride”), says Justin in his *Dialogue Against Trypho the Jew*. You are, indeed, the less excusable because you have dedicated a mutilated and truncated piece of wood, while others have consecrated one complete and fully constructed.

For the rest, your religion of the complete cross is itself complete, as I shall demonstrate. You are also unaware that this origin came to your gods from that gibbet. For every image—whether carved from wood or stone, cast from bronze, or produced from any other richer material—must first be preceded by the sculptor’s hand. The modeler first sets up a wooden cross, because the position of our body itself contains the silent and hidden line of a cross: the head rises above, the spine extends straight, the shoulders slope . . . If you set a man upright with his hands extended, you have made the image of a cross. [Col. 0578B]

Upon this beginning, then, and as it were upon this framework, clay is laid over it, gradually completing the limbs and constructing the body; and the form that was chosen for the clay is imposed upon the cross within. From there, by compass and leaden molds, the preparation of the image is transferred into marble, earthenware, bronze, or whatever material it was decided should become a god. From the cross comes the clay, and from the clay the god: in a certain fashion the cross passes through the clay into the god. You therefore consecrate the cross from which the consecrated object begins.

The following may serve as an illustration. From the kernel of an olive, the stone of a peach, or a grain of pepper placed beneath the earth, a tree rises into branches, foliage, and the form of its kind. If you transplant it, or use one of its branches to produce another shoot, to what will that which arises from the stock be attributed? Surely to that grain, stone, or kernel. For since the third stage will be ascribed to the second, and the second equally to the first, the third is thus carried back to the first through the second. [Col. 0578C]

There is no need to argue the matter longer, since by a natural rule every kind whatsoever refers its lineage to its origin; and to the degree that a kind derives its lineage, its origin is implicated in that kind. If, therefore, in the lineage of your gods you worship an origin in crosses, this will be the kernel and primordial seed from which the forests of your images are propagated.

Now to manifest examples:

[Note: [Col. 0578D] Rigaltius: *At manifesta*; Verias and Havercamp: *ad manifesta*.]

you venerate Victories as divinities, and indeed as divinities the more august because they are the more joyful. What better raises them upon a framework

[Note: [Col. 0578D] Havercamp: *contignatione*, “framework.”]

than crosses, which are in a manner the internal structure of trophies? [Col. 0578D–0579A] Thus in its Victories the religion of the camp also worships crosses: it adores standards, swears by standards, and prefers the standards to Jupiter himself. Yet that display of images and all that ornamentation of gold are merely necklaces upon crosses. Likewise, in the *cantabra* and banners, which the military guards with no less sanctity, those hanging cloths are the garments of crosses. You are ashamed, I suppose, to worship unadorned and naked crosses.

### 13

Others, certainly with more refinement, think that the sun is the Christian god, because it has become known that we pray toward the region of the east

[Note: [Col. 0579D] **That we offer prayer toward the east.** It is most certainly true that Christians were accustomed to offer their prayers facing east. St. Jerome on chapter 6 of the prophet Amos: “In the mysteries we first renounce him who is in the west and who dies for us together with our sins; and then, turned toward the east, we enter into a covenant with the Sun of Justice and promise that we will serve him.” Profane people and Gentiles, who were wholly without the truth, likewise turned toward the east, but because they falsely believed the rising sun to be their god. or celebrate the day of the Sun with rejoicing. Do you do any less? Do not many of you, affecting an act of worship, sometimes move your lips toward the sunrise even when adoring the heavenly bodies? It is certainly you who have also admitted the Sun into the register of the seven days and selected its day above the others: on that day you either abstain from bathing, postpone it until evening, or devote yourselves to leisure and feasting. Yet in doing this you yourselves deviate from your own religions toward those of strangers. [Col. 0579B] For Jewish are the festivals, Sabbaths, “pure meal,” Jewish rites of lamps, fasts with unleavened bread, and prayers upon the shore—all of which are certainly foreign to your gods. Therefore, to return from my digression: you who reproach us with the sun and its day should acknowledge your proximity to us. We are not far removed from your Saturn and your Sabbaths.

### 15

More ass-bedded gods are found among you. If we meet on equal terms in the matter of gods, it follows that there is no difference between us in sacrifice or sacred rite either, so that the comparison may be completed under another category.

Do we propitiate or initiate by infanticide? ### 16

Yet what less do you do—indeed, what do you not do in greater measure? Is it a small thing that you gape after human entrails, merely because you devour people alive and full-grown? Is it a small thing that you lick human blood because you draw forth blood that is yet to be? Is it a small thing that you feed upon an infant because you drain an entire premature infant?

We have reached the hour of the lamps, the service of the dog, and the devices of darkness. At this point I fear that I must yield. For what comparable thing shall I find among you? But now praise the prudent design of our modest incest: we have contrived a counterfeit night so that we might defile neither daylight nor true night; [Col. 0581A] we have thought that even earthly lights ought to be spared; we also deceive our own conscience, for whatever we do willingly we only suspect ourselves of doing!

Your own incestuous acts, by contrast, enjoy their freedom in every daylight and every night, under the consciousness of the entire sky. And what turns out still more happily for you is that, although you openly mingle in incest beneath the witness of the whole heavens, you alone remain ignorant of it; whereas we, even in darkness, can recognize our crimes.

Ctesias indeed reports that the Persians freely unite with their mothers, both knowing what they do and feeling no horror. That the Macedonians too openly do what they approve is evident. For when Oedipus first entered their stage with his eyes put out, they greeted him with laughter and mockery. The tragic actor, dismayed, removed his mask and said, “My lords, have I displeased you?” The Macedonians answered: “No; you yourself performed beautifully. The exemplar reads: “After sentence had been passed for the punishment of the Senate.” This is plainly corrupt. For what could that mean: “a tragedy after sentence had been passed for the punishment of the Senate”? And why does he mention Fuscianus, prefect of the City, if punishment had been decreed by the Senate? Indeed, nothing here was punished either by the Senate or by the prefect of the City.

I therefore establish first that the preceding passage should be punctuated thus: “Hence such streams of plots flow to mimes and comedies. Hence this also—recently a tragedy of the following kind—broke forth”: that is, this stream of a comic plot which had previously been such a tragedy. He then begins to narrate the tragedy, a plainly pitiable event:

“Under Fuscianus, prefect of the City, after sentence had been passed for the punishment of an accused man”—some criminal, obviously, while the populace was hurrying to the spectacle—“a child’s journey beyond the door, occasioned by the accidental negligence of his attendants, was diverted by passersby, and he disappeared from his home.”

That this must be understood of a boy six years old or thereabouts is made probable even by the fact that he remembered having disappeared in childhood, then, his appearance having changed with age, having been brought back to Rome and, when already a young man, having been consigned to chains.

The story should therefore proceed thus: “His journey beyond the door, occasioned by the accidental negligence of his attendants, was diverted by passersby, and he disappeared from his home.” In the same manuscript codex there follows: “A petty Greek had seized him from the threshold—or ‘entering,’ in the Greek manner,” so that this final word, “entering,” appears to be an interpretation of what preceded, “from the threshold.” If we have established this, it will at the same time be established that there is no reason by which the words we have enclosed in brackets, so that they may be removed, can be defended. The story should therefore continue thus: “A petty Greek had seized him, in the Greek manner. Thereafter, his appearance having changed with the age he spent in Asia,” etc. Finally, he may have said “his age in Asia” to mean the period of life passed in Asia.]
