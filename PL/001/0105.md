Casaubon, moreover, identifies three men named Apicius, all infamous for the vice of gluttony (Casaubon on Athenaeus, bk. II, ch. 6, p. 22 ff.). In the preceding dissertation (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 23, art. 4), we cited more than once the treatise on cookery published under the name of Apicius. You will not need great effort to persuade us that this is the man of whom Tertullian speaks.

[Col. 0985B] But whoever he may have been, no gentile ever said that a sect founded by anyone deserved hatred solely because of his name, or because of the names of the others whom we have reviewed, unless it had first been established that there was something evil in it. The gentiles, therefore, ought not to have hated the Christians either because of their own name or because of the name of their founder, unless they had first ascertained that they were wicked. Yet we have already shown that those same gentiles neither knew, nor wished ever to know or investigate, whether the Christians did anything that ought to be pursued with hatred and punishment.

On the contrary, however, it was entirely unknown to no one that Christians were immediately condemned merely under their designation, or solely on account of their name, by the proceedings and judgments of provincial governors before whom they had been brought. For, Tertullian said, we now call upon your own records—that is, the records of the judges—as witnesses that Christians are condemned by them for this name alone: [Col. 0985C] “You who preside each day over the trial of prisoners,” that is, of those committed to prison, “who conclude the indictments by your sentences” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 44, p. 496), or examine the accusations against defendants: has anyone among all of them been found to be an assassin or murderer? Any purse-snatcher (*ibid.*; cf. Festus, s.v. *Manticularius*), that is, one who stole money stored in other people’s purses? Any robber of bathers, or, as he says elsewhere, a thief of the baths, who stole the clothes or silver of people while they were bathing? Yet certainly all these were pagans (Apuleius, *Apology* 1, near the end), and among them you found no true follower of the Christian religion. “For it is not with our people,” Tertullian continues, “but with yours that the prison is always seething” (Plautus, *Captivi*, act IV, scene 2), and it is entirely filled with your criminal gentiles. [Col. 0985D] “It is with your people, too, that the mines are always sighing” (Tertullian, *De Fuga*, ch. 13)—namely, with those who, having been convicted of crimes, were compelled to dig in the mines. Finally, “it is with your people that the beasts are always fattened; it is from your people that the providers of public shows always feed their herds of criminals” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 44, p. 497). The providers of public shows, as we have already observed elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 13, art. 1; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 27, art. 1), were those who presented gladiatorial spectacles. They fed and maintained criminals who, after being condemned to the beasts, had to fight them. If the men were defeated, the victorious beasts were then fattened upon their flesh.

Accordingly, no Christian belonged to their number [Col. 0986A] because he had been thrown into prison or condemned to the beasts for any crime, but solely because of his name, which contained absolutely nothing evil. For Tertullian openly and emphatically declares: “No one there is a Christian unless it is for this alone, because of his name; or, if he is also guilty and convicted of something else”—that is, of some capital crime—“he is now no Christian” (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*), but an apostate and deserter from the Christian religion. Nothing, therefore, was more unjust or more contrary to divine and human laws than to condemn innocent Christians, never heard or permitted to defend themselves, solely because of their name, and to torture and kill them with punishments of every possible severity.

## CHAPTER XVIII

### How absurdly the gentiles objected that certain laws of their own bound them to harass and kill the Christians, although they themselves so often violated even the more sacred laws with impunity

[Col. 0986B]

### ARTICLE I

#### By what arguments Tertullian shows that no law or authority can compel any person to worship a god whom he knows to be false, or deprive anyone of the freedom to embrace the religion he believes to be true

The gentiles, driven into the greatest straits, sought refuge in the authority of their laws as though in their final stronghold. “We are bound,” they said, “by our laws, which command that Christians be harassed [Col. 0986C] and tortured even to death. In order to torture them, therefore, it is enough for us to know that they really are Christians; there is no further need to inquire who or what sort of people they are” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 3, p. 282). But Tertullian replies that this argument is empty and utterly absurd, and addresses the gentiles themselves as follows: “If through your laws you consider it lawful and necessary to condemn Christians without hearing or defending them, you profess violence and an unjust domination from the citadel” (*ibid.*); “from the citadel,” he says—that is, perhaps, from the elevated summit of the state itself (*ibid.*, ch. 1, p. 259), as he had said at the beginning of this book. For one who condemns any accused person without hearing his case and orders him to be killed is undoubtedly not discharging the office of an equitable judge, but exercising a most unjust domination and an intolerable tyranny.

[Col. 0986D] Yet, to break the obstinacy of the gentiles completely, he draws another argument from the fact that they granted every individual the freedom to worship any god whatever except the one true God. For after saying that one god was freely and lawfully worshipped by one person and another by someone else, he presses them as follows: “Take care that it does not contribute to an indictment of irreligion to take away freedom of religion and forbid the choice of a divinity, so that I may not be permitted to worship whom I wish, but am compelled to worship whom I do not wish” (*ibid.*, ch. 24, p. 418). Religious worship, he says, must be free and voluntary, and no one can ever be compelled by any person or any law to embrace a religion which he knows to be false: “For the performance of a divine rite,” as he later said, “requires a willing mind” (*ibid.*, [Col. 0987A] ch. 28, p. 435). If you ask for his reason, he will give you this: “It is a matter of human right and natural liberty that each person worship what he has thought proper; another person’s religion neither harms nor benefits anyone else. Nor is it the part of religion to compel religion, which ought to be undertaken voluntarily, not by force, since sacrifices too are demanded from a willing mind. Thus, even if you compel us to sacrifice, you will render no service to your gods. For they will not desire sacrifices from the unwilling, unless they are contentious. But God is not contentious” (the same author, *Ad Scapulam*, ch. 2).

You certainly hear with what weighty arguments our Author shows that it is contrary not only to human but also to natural right for anyone ever to be compelled by any force to adopt a false religion or the worship of a false god. For the religion of any person neither benefits [Col. 0987B] nor harms another, who is made neither better nor worse by his neighbor’s good or evil.

Next, God does not desire sacrifices from one who is unwilling and resistant, unless he is contentious—that is, unless he wishes to sow disputes and quarrels. But such a God can by no means be the true God. Indeed, Tertullian further says: “No one will wish to be worshipped by an unwilling person, not even a man” (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 418). For pretence and wicked hypocrisy are plainly to be condemned when someone outwardly feigns worship of one whom inwardly he despises and detests.

Moreover, such is certainly human frailty that a person offends the true God again and again. Yet he cannot appease Him except by a free and sincere conversion and repentance of mind, or by a sacrifice performed with the fullest freedom. [Col. 0987C] Therefore, without the greatest injustice, no one can compel me to sacrifice to Jupiter or to any other god whose favor or anger toward me I do not care about—much less if I have learned with absolute certainty that he was a man and no longer exists.

Tertullian presses the point further: “Let one person,” he says, “worship God, another Jupiter; let one stretch suppliant hands toward heaven, another toward the altar of Faith; let one, if that is what you think, count the clouds while praying, another the ceiling panels; let one dedicate his own soul to his god, another the soul of a goat” (*ibid.*). Ought not all these persons either to be tolerated or condemned by an equal right, without one rather than another being deprived of the liberty to worship whom he wishes?

But these words of Tertullian are not altogether clear and transparent, and there may perhaps be room to doubt whether [Col. 0987D] he is speaking only of the gentiles or of them together with the Christians, as many have thought. What if he should be said to have designated both groups and, in addition, the Jews? For he could have written of Christians and Jews: “Let one worship God”—that is, the one and almighty God—and of the pagans: “another Jupiter,” as no one fails to understand. Of the same Christians he could also have said: “Let one stretch suppliant hands toward heaven.” For, as we have seen (above, ch. 14, art. 3), they prayed to God with outstretched hands. Although a pagan might pray to his gods in a similar manner, Tertullian nevertheless seems to contrast him with the Christian when he says in the same place: “But let another”—namely, a gentile—“similarly stretch his hands toward the altar of Faith.” Our manuscripts read [Col. 0988A] *of Phidias*, but undoubtedly through a copyist’s error. Perhaps *of Fidius* would be a more tolerable reading, whom the gentiles, as Ovid sings, believed to be Jupiter’s son and a god (Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. VI, § 4). Yet *of Faith* is correct, for Faith had a temple on the Capitol as a goddess, as we have observed elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 866).

The subsequent words of our Author seem to concern the Jews: “Let one, if that is what you think, count the clouds while praying.” For Juvenal sang of these same Jews:

> Some, whose lot was to have a father who revered the Sabbath,  
> Worship nothing but the clouds and the divinity of heaven.  
> (Juvenal, *Satires* 16, v. 916.)

But why did Tertullian add: “if that is what you think”? Did he not wish by these words to designate those same Jews whom [Col. 0988B] the gentiles falsely believed to worship both the clouds and the heavens? Tertullian contrasts with him another, a gentile, who worshipped the ceiling panels—namely, of his temples—as we shall show below. He continues: “And let another,” he says—suppose, a Christian—“dedicate and consecrate his own soul to his God”; but, by contrast, “let another,” a pagan, “dedicate the soul of a goat to a false god.” For Virgil sings:

> Therefore we shall duly render Bacchus his proper honor  
> With ancestral songs, and bring platters and sacrificial cakes;  
> And, led by the horn, the sacred goat will stand at the altar.  
> (Virgil, *Georgics*, bk. II, v. 393.)

And Martial:

> Harmful to the vine, at your rose-decked altars, Bacchus,  
> Stood the goat, about to die, a victim welcome to your rites.  
> (Martial, bk. III, epigram 24.)

Yet what prevents Tertullian from attributing all these things [Col. 0988C] not to Christians and Jews, but to gentiles alone? Did not Menoeceus, Codrus, and Curtius, whom we discussed in the preceding dissertation after Lactantius (Lactantius, bk. III, ch. 12, p. 370; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 10, art. 4), dedicate their souls to the gods? Aristophanes, moreover, portrays Socrates praying to and invoking the clouds (Aristophanes, comedy *Clouds*, act I, scene 3). The gentiles too prayed to their gods with hands raised toward heaven, as has been said. Tertullian therefore appears rather to be speaking of them alone, and his argument would thereby acquire greater force. “Quite right,” you will say, “were it not that he contrasts with Jupiter God, doubtless the true God, whom he afterward again names in the singular.” But what prevents him from having contrasted with Jupiter, the greatest god of the gentiles, any other god, whether of the greater or the lesser [Col. 0988D] nations? In whatever way his words are understood, he quite rightly concludes from them that the gentiles, contrary to every law and principle of justice, deprived Christians of the ability to worship the true God. For under their rule everyone else was permitted to worship not only Jupiter, but any false and ridiculous god whatever, with the sole exception of the one true God. With the same indulgence with which these impious men, in their cruelty, took from Christians the liberty to honor the true God, they allowed, in every city and province, as will be said below, any person to worship and adore whatever god he pleased, even if that god was wholly unknown to others.

But we now seem to hear someone whispering in our ear that Tertullian has there wandered completely from the road, [Col. 0989A] since by these arguments he seeks to prove that everyone ought to be granted the freedom to worship whatever god he wishes, whether true or false, and to choose a religion at his own discretion. Did Tertullian, then, truly lose his senses—he who, as we have seen, strives with all the powers of his intellect to demonstrate that the Christian religion alone is true and must be professed, while all the others are false and must be rejected; who plainly and thoroughly maintains that it is the greatest wickedness for a Christian to deny or even conceal his religion; who heaps every praise upon our martyrs precisely because they preferred to be tortured and killed by any of the most dreadful torments rather than burn three grains of incense to false gods, and because with altogether marvelous constancy they shed their blood in defense of their faith and religion? Had he thought otherwise, he would plainly have wasted his labor, and without any purpose, in composing his *Apologeticum* and other similar [Col. 0989B] books. His arguments, therefore, have no other aim than to demonstrate that no one can be compelled to abandon the true religion and embrace a false one, and that the gentiles were most unjust because they granted everyone freedom to worship any god, even one unknown to others, while depriving only the Christians, who worshipped the true God, of that freedom.

But someone else will ask us why Tertullian asserts so emphatically that religion is voluntary, when elsewhere he explicitly said: “It is proper for heretics to be compelled to their duty, not enticed. Their hardness must be overcome, not persuaded” (Tertullian, *Scorpiace*, ch. 2). Let him ask Rigaltius, however, how our Author wishes the hardness of heretics to be overcome and them to be recalled to their duty, [Col. 0989C] and he will hear: not by force or bodily coercion, but by the spiritual weapons of divine Scripture and by divine authority. And indeed Tertullian himself continues as follows: “And surely that will be adjudged sufficiently excellent which is proved to have been established and commanded by God.” Accordingly, he refutes and conquers them there with these weapons.

We may add that the case of heretics and that of gentiles is not the same. For whatever you may say ought to be determined concerning heretics, who acknowledged the true God but fell away from the Church through their errors and crimes, it was nevertheless never lawful for the pagans, as Tertullian rightly says, to drive Christians by torments and tortures to the veneration of false gods. [Col. 0989D] For no one is driven—or indeed even led—into error without guilt. But nothing prevents heretics from being recalled by legitimate authority to the truth which they abandoned.

### ARTICLE II

#### How foolishly the gentiles pleaded, when torturing and destroying Christians, that they were bound by certain imperial laws, although Lycurgus’ laws had themselves been amended—whereupon he brought about his own death by starvation—and other laws had been abrogated by the Romans, especially the Julian and Papian laws and those ordering debtors to be cut into two parts; and what those laws were

No one can ever be bound by any law, except a most unjust one, to worship a false god. [Col. 0990A] Yet, as Tertullian rightly says, “an unjust law has no claim to honor” (Tertullian, *Ad Nationes*, bk. I, ch. 6, p. 566), nor has it ever bound the conscience of any person. Christians, therefore, could not be compelled by any human law to worship fictitious gods. With what effrontery, then, could the gentiles object that they were bound by certain laws of their emperors to torture and kill Christians, when they could not deny that very many laws had been enacted by men of even greater authority—as we have already seen in the preceding dissertation (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 28, art. 1)—in accordance with human weakness and the circumstances of their times, but that for this very reason they boldly and with impunity amended, despised, and trampled upon them? How could they have denied something known to everyone, not only through Cicero’s most certain testimony (Cicero, *De Legibus*, bk. I, p. 328, line 5 [Col. 0990B] ff.), but also through the constant experience of every age? It was therefore hardly necessary for Tertullian to prove it by various examples, and by far more than was appropriate. Nevertheless, because, as they say, excess does no harm, he assembled a great many laws enacted by various legislators and afterward either amended or rendered obsolete by their peoples, so that at last he might utterly silence the gentiles, who constantly and ad nauseam invoked against the Christians the authority of the laws enacted concerning them. We have therefore considered it our duty to follow all his footsteps and, to the best of our ability and in our customary manner, to elucidate the particular statements he made concerning the ancient laws of the gentiles, their authors, amendment, and abrogation.

He begins with the most ancient legislator, Lycurgus, [Col. 0990C] whose laws, he says, when amended by the Lacedaemonians, inflicted such grief upon their author that in retirement he passed judgment upon himself by starvation; or, as elsewhere he said in different words conveying the same meaning, “he chose *apokarteresis*” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 4, p. 286; and ch. 46, p. 512)—that is, he died by voluntary hunger and starvation. Why, I ask you, did the Lacedaemonians amend these laws unless because they judged them imperfect and neither useful nor suitable to themselves? Pausanias recorded that he took refuge in a certain village named Alpium after one of his eyes had been struck out by Alcander, who had disliked his laws (Pausanias, bk. III, p. 100). Valerius Maximus recounts this somewhat more fully in the following words: “Even the laws which Lycurgus devised for the public welfare could not protect him from encountering hostile [Col. 0990D] citizens. For he was frequently attacked with stones, was once expelled in a public frenzy, was even deprived of an eye, and at last was driven from his very homeland” (Valerius Maximus, bk. V, ch. 3, foreign example § 2). But both authors were silent about the kind of death by which he ended his life. Aelian writes that when Lycurgus wished to imbue the Lacedaemonians with justice, his eye was put out either by a stone hurled from ambush or by a blow from a staff (Aelian, *Varia Historia*, bk. XIII, ch. 23), as various writers had reported. More pertinent to our subject, however, he expressly cites Ephorus as a witness that Lycurgus died of hunger in exile. But Aelian did not say there whether this hunger was voluntary. Our Author, therefore, follows Plutarch more closely, for Plutarch explicitly writes that Lycurgus bound [Col. 0991A] the kings, senate, and citizens by oath to observe his laws diligently until he returned from Delphi (Plutarch, vol. I, *Life of Lycurgus*, p. 57). And so that they might not be released from the religious obligation of their oath, *ἐτελεύτησεν ἀποκαρτερήσας* (“he died by starving himself”); he caused his own death by starvation. Yet this did not prevent the Lacedaemonians from despising and abolishing not only their oath, but also his laws.

Our Author then passes to the Romans and addresses them in these words: “Do not you too every day, as experience illuminates the darkness of antiquity”—that is, taught by your own experience—“lop and cut down that whole old and squalid forest,” or infinite and obscure multitude of ancient laws, “with the new axes of imperial rescripts and edicts,” that is, with new rescripts of the emperors (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 4, p. 286)? [Col. 0991B] In the edition of Rigaltius and those following it, however, the reading is “clear and cut the soil”; but by what authority neither he told us, nor can we ascertain anything except that this reading conflicts with all the manuscripts and even with the Puteanus copy which he had before him. We are nevertheless aware that Tertullian wrote elsewhere: “He undertook to cultivate everything, to weed it and clear the soil, so that from it, as though from shoots and slips, peoples might be planted from peoples and cities from cities throughout every region of the world” (the same author, *De Pallio*, ch. 2). But who has ever been permitted to transfer a word from one book of an author into another in defiance of the protest of all the manuscripts? Whichever way one reads, our Author’s meaning is sufficiently clear: the bristling forest of ancient laws was cleared and pruned by new imperial edicts, through which many things were uprooted like [Col. 0991C] harmful weeds. The gentiles therefore boasted in vain, and with such clamor, of the authority of their laws against the Christians.

To demonstrate this point further, Tertullian immediately added: “Did not Severus, the most resolute of emperors, only yesterday, after so venerable an old age, set aside the utterly futile Papian laws, which compel the begetting of children before the Julian laws permit marriage to be contracted?” (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 4, pp. 286–287). He clearly teaches there that these Julian laws were amended by the Papian laws, but that both were abrogated by the emperor Severus. There were various Julian laws, so called after Julius Caesar. The Papian—or, more properly, Poppaean—laws took their name from Papius and Poppaeus, suffect consuls in the time of Caesar Augustus. Everyone readily concedes that Tertullian designates the Julian law concerning the marriage of the [Col. 0991D] orders. But Herold frankly confesses that he does not sufficiently understand how the Papian law compelled children to be begotten before the Julian law ordered marriage to be contracted (Herold, notes on Tertullian), while Antonio Agustín acknowledges that the matter is unknown to him (Agustín, *De Legibus*, ch. 18). Yet it is certain that, as Dio (Dio, *Roman History*, bk. IV, p. 256), Cornelius Tacitus (Tacitus, *Annals*, bk. III, before the middle), and Suetonius report (Suetonius, *Life of Augustus*, § 34), this Julian law was revised by Caesar Augustus more severely than the others. For besides the rewards proposed for those who had begotten children, he imposed heavier penalties upon the unmarried and celibate, penalties which he was later compelled to mitigate because of the disturbance raised by those who resisted. [Col. 0992A] Suetonius further adds: “When he perceived that the force of the law was being evaded by the immaturity of betrothed girls and frequent changes of marriages, he shortened the period for having a betrothed and imposed a limit upon divorces” (Suetonius, *ibid.*). Can it not be inferred from the words “the immaturity of betrothed girls” that, because of the multitude of those previously destroyed in civil and other wars, the emperor Augustus decreed that maidens should marry even when they were not yet sufficiently grown or mature for a husband? If that is Suetonius’ meaning, the following appears to be Tertullian’s: the Poppaean law enacted that maidens be given in marriage at an earlier age, before they could have contracted it under the Julian law. And indeed we learn from Dio that the same Augustus decreed that a betrothal agreement should be void if the marriage were postponed beyond two years—that is, as [Col. 0992B] Dio himself says, the maiden whom a man wished to betroth should be ten years old and the marriage should not be deferred beyond two years (Dio, *Roman History*, bk. LIV, p. 609). But if a girl could be betrothed and marry in the tenth year of her age, she was therefore compelled, as Tertullian says, to bear children even though under the Julian law she could enter into marriage only in her twelfth year. See, if you please, the annotations of Gothofredus (Gothofredus, *Codex Theodosianus*, p. 226 ff.) and others on the third law concerning betrothals; and consider, I ask you, whether this interpretation of Tertullian’s words, or some other that you may devise, pleases you more.

[Col. 0992C] There can be no doubt, however, that the emperor Severus abrogated that part of both the Julian and Poppaean laws and assigned a time at which maidens might marry at a mature age. For Tertullian asserts that this was done “yesterday,” that is, in his own time. Furthermore, we learn from Marcian that something in the Julian laws was amended by the same Severus (*Digest*, bk. XXIII, title 2, law 19). Elsewhere we showed (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. 2, ch. 18, art. 3, p. 1153) that Constantine the Great removed the penalties which those Julian and Poppaean laws had decreed against the unmarried and childless. But these developments occurred after the time of our Tertullian.

Finally, Rigaltius thinks that Tertullian called these laws “utterly futile” because Christians, believing that the end of the ages was imminent and yearning for mortal life to be exchanged for eternal life, not unreasonably regarded it as vain and foolish to think about begetting children. [Col. 0992D] But we have seen that those same Christians were accustomed to pray for a delay of this end. We shall therefore be more readily persuaded that our Author said this from his zeal for chastity and virginity, virtues which Christians cultivated with as much ardor as the gentiles held them in contempt and scorned them.

Tertullian then immediately continues: “In former times there were laws that men adjudged debtors should be cut into parts by their creditors. Yet by public consent this cruelty was afterward erased” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 4, p. 287). This was one of the most ancient laws, first included in the Ten and afterward in the Twelve Tables, which, according to Livy, amid the immense heap of laws accumulated one upon another, “are the source of all public and private law” [Col. 0993A] (Livy, bk. III, §§ 34–35). Aulus Gellius will teach you what that law was and in what words it was framed: in one place he gives its beginning (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, bk. XV, ch. 13, p. 821), and elsewhere transcribes the whole of it as follows:

> FOR AN ACKNOWLEDGED DEBT AND FOR MATTERS ADJUDGED AT LAW, THIRTY DAYS SHALL BE ALLOWED. THEREAFTER THERE SHALL BE A LAYING ON OF HANDS. THE DEBTOR SHALL BE BROUGHT INTO COURT. UNLESS HE SATISFIES THE JUDGMENT, OR SOMEONE COMES FORWARD IN COURT TO GUARANTEE IT FOR HIM, THE CREDITOR SHALL TAKE HIM AWAY WITH HIM. HE SHALL BIND HIM EITHER WITH A THONG OR WITH SHACKLES WEIGHING FIFTEEN POUNDS, NOT LESS; OR, IF HE WISHES, HE MAY BIND HIM WITH HEAVIER ONES. IF THE DEBTOR WISHES, HE SHALL LIVE AT HIS OWN EXPENSE. IF HE DOES NOT LIVE AT HIS OWN EXPENSE, THE PERSON WHO HOLDS HIM BOUND SHALL GIVE HIM A POUND OF SPELT A DAY. IF HE WISHES, HE MAY GIVE MORE. ON THE THIRD MARKET DAY THEY SHALL CUT HIM INTO PARTS. IF THEY CUT MORE OR LESS, IT SHALL BE WITHOUT FRAUD.  
> (*Ibid.*, bk. XX, ch. 1, p. 1101 ff.)

[Col. 0993B] Fulvius Ursinus reproduced this same law, though with various readings (Fulvius Ursinus, notes on the cited book of Agustín).

Under this law, therefore, those burdened with debt were first granted a period of thirty days in which to find the money. Then they were bound and thrust into prison. On the third market day, or after another sixty days, they paid the penalty with their lives; and if there were several creditors, the debtor’s body was cut up and its several parts given to the several creditors. “Certainly nothing,” says Gellius, “is more savage than this, unless—as the facts themselves indicate—so great a cruelty of punishment was threatened with the intention that it should never be reached. For we now see many men assigned and bound because the worst men despise the punishment of bonds. I have neither read nor heard that anyone was cut apart in ancient times, since the savagery of that punishment [Col. 0993C] could not be despised” (Gellius, *ibid.*). You clearly see that this is the very law which Tertullian asserts was rendered obsolete and rejected by public consent. Quintilian too confirms this most plainly in the following words: “Some things are not praiseworthy by nature, but are permitted by law; thus, under the Twelve Tables, it was lawful for a debtor’s body to be divided among his creditors, but public custom rejected that law” (Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria*, bk. III, ch. 6).

[Col. 0993D] But if among the laws of the Twelve Tables—which even boys were once required to learn as the best of all laws, as Cicero says (Cicero, *De Legibus*, bk. II, p. 341, line 27)—there was something not only unworthy of praise, as we have shown elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 26, art. 1), but altogether savage as well, how much more might this be true of other laws enacted by men solely for their own advantage! For no antiquity and no dignity of a legislator commends a law, but equity alone. No law, therefore, is more unjust than one by which a Christian is condemned solely because of his name, without his case ever being heard.

### ARTICLE III

#### An examination of how freely the gentiles violated the laws restraining excessive expenditure on dinners, household furnishings, and clothing; what those laws were, and what “hundred-as dinners” were; how boldly they overthrew other laws, especially that by which a patrician possessing ten pounds of silver was expelled from the senate; who that man was, and who were the freedmen who, while still breaking their scourges, scorned similar sumptuary laws

[Col. 0994A] Tertullian continues his argument. Against the gentiles, who raged against the Christians under the pretence of certain laws, he objects that they themselves were no more bound by those laws than the gentiles were by sumptuary laws which, with great equity and prudence, restrained expenditure on dinners, household furnishings, and clothing (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 4, p. 288), or, as he himself likes to say, “held expenditure and ostentation in check” (the same author, ch. 6, p. 205). Yet they had utterly overthrown those laws with such boldness that they could give Tertullian no answer when he asked them: “What has become of those laws which commanded that no more than one hundred asses be entered as the expenditure upon a dinner, and that no more than one hen be served, and one that had not been fattened?” Aulus Gellius relates that he had read in the miscellanies of Ateius Capito a decree of the senate by which the leading men of the state [Col. 0994B] were ordered to swear that at the Megalensian Games they would spend no more than one hundred and twenty bronze coins, or asses, on each dinner, apart from vegetables, spelt, and native wine; and that no more than one hundred pounds’ weight of silver should be used at a banquet (Gellius, *Attic Nights*, bk. II, ch. 24). The Fannian law then provided that no more than one hundred asses should be spent during the Roman Games, the Plebeian Games, the Saturnalia, and certain other days; thirty asses on ten other days in each month; and finally ten asses on all remaining days. Later, the Licinian law permitted an expenditure of two hundred asses at weddings and thirty asses on other days, apart from fruit and vegetables. But afterward, when these laws had been effaced by neglect and age and many were gluttonously consuming their ample inheritances, the dictator Sulla enacted the Cornelian law, by which permission was granted to spend thirty sesterces upon a dinner on certain solemn feast days, and no more than three on other days. [Col. 0994C] In addition, the Aemilian and Antian laws further prescribed the expense of dinners and the kind and quantity of food. Finally, by the Julian law, which was enacted during the reign of Augustus, as we have said, these permitted expenses were greatly increased, yet in such a manner that the surge of unbridled luxury was restrained within certain limits. All these matters, explained by Macrobius (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, bk. III, ch. 17) after Aulus Gellius (Aulus Gellius, *loc. cit.*), plainly establish and confirm the judgment of our Tertullian.

Nor did they show greater concern for the Fannian law already praised by us, which forbade more than one hen, and one not fattened, to be served at a dinner. [Col. 0994D] Pliny mentions this law and explains in the following words how the gentiles began to undermine it: “The people of Delos began to fatten hens. From this arose the pestilent practice of devouring plump birds basted in their own fat. I find this first singled out among the ancient restrictions concerning dinners, already in the law of the consul Gaius Fannius, eleven years before the Punic War: that no bird should be served except one hen, and one that had not been fattened” (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. X, ch. 50, p. 442)—*altilis* meaning plump and fattened, a term Juvenal uses without qualification (Juvenal, *Satires* 5, v. 117). “This provision was afterward carried over and passed through all the laws, and a means of evading them was discovered by feeding cockerels too upon food soaked in milk; prepared in this way, they are judged much more agreeable” (Pliny, *loc. cit.*). Concerning these [Col. 0995A] sumptuary and dietary laws, much more is said by Paulus Manutius (Paulus Manutius, *De Legibus Romanis*, ch. 18) and Antonio Agustín (Antonio Agustín, *De Legibus*, bk. I, ch. 18), whom we cited in another dissertation, where we heard Arnobius arguing similarly from these laws after Tertullian (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 9, art. 1).
