The first merit of this edition is the editor’s diligence and care; its second is that it presents, judiciously selected, those contributions which learned men had offered in various places since Gronovius’s time for polishing, elucidating, and even obscuring Minucius; its third is that the editor follows a modest and exact critical method, which, in accordance with the precepts of the great Ernesti, is occupied more with defending and explaining the received reading than with exercising conjecture; and its final merit is that it repeats briefly and summarily—yet without loss of clarity—the sound observations of others and, in general, takes the greatest care over style. Ernesti’s preface instructs that the reading of sacred writers should be joined to that of the earlier Greek and Roman writers. **SECOND LINDNER EDITION.** The distinguished Lindner published Minucius at Langensalza after further revision, octavo. In this new edition, following the example of the most learned men Gesner and Heyne, he judged that variant readings, scholars’ conjectures, and matters pertaining solely to textual criticism (κρίσις) should be separated from the rest, lest they weary readers who have little taste for such things. He then brought back beneath the text the annotations which he had formerly relegated to the indexes, and which few or no readers sought there. He examined each point more carefully, and wherever he had erred he did not hesitate candidly to admit it. In place of Baudouin’s dissertation, sufficiently well known and readily available in our lands, he also supplied the letter of the distinguished van Hoven, *On the True Age of Minucius*, and appended to it several brief excursuses which would, however, have exceeded the proper limits of notes.

## § II. Literary Annals of Cyprian

[Col. LVII]

## THIRD CENTURY

### Year 246

**CYPRIAN**, having professed himself a Christian from the year 243 and having devoted two years vigorously to sacred literature, made his beginning as a writer on behalf of his faith in the letter *To Donatus*. (Morcelli; see below, col. 142.)

247–248.

He first wrote the short work *On the Vanity of Idols*. (Pearson, Morcelli.)—Many hold the opinion that, while still a presbyter, he wrote the three books of *Testimonies against the Jews*, and likewise the book *On the Dress of Virgins*. (Morcelli, Maran.)

249.

When the Decian persecution arose at the beginning of his episcopate, Cyprian is believed to have written Epistle LXVI (Maran); Epistle LXII (Morcelli); Epistles LXI and LXV (Pearson).

250.

While the persecution was raging, the Cyprianic Epistles III and IV appear to have been written, as well as thirteen mentioned in Epistle XV, of which seven survive, namely V, IX, X, XII, XIII, and XIV, sent to Rome with XV; then the letters of Cyprian and of the confessors of Carthage and Rome, in approximately the following chronological order: XIX, XX; XVII, XVIII, XXII; XXI; XXIII, XXV; XXIV, XXVII; XXVIII, XXIX; XXXI, XXVI; XXXII, XXX; XXXVII; VIII; XVI, XXXV, XXXIII, XXXIV, VII, VI, XXXVI, XXXVIII, XXXIX; to which perhaps Epistles XVI and XXXV should be added. (Maran, Morcelli.)

251.

To this year are assigned Epistle XI, while Cyprian was still in exile; the book *On the Lapsed*, after his return; the delegates’ Epistles LXI, XLII, XLIII, and XLIV; then the book *On the Unity of the Church*; thereafter Epistles XLV, LXVIII, XLVI, L; XLIX, XLVII, and LI. (Maran, Morcelli.)

252.

At the beginning of the year, Epistle LII (Maran, Pearson); a little later LV (Morcelli); between these, the short work *On the Lord’s Prayer*; around Easter, Epistle LIII; then Epistles LIX and LXIV, followed by LV, LVI, LIV, and LVII (Maran, Morcelli); at the end of the year, LVIII and LXXXI. (Maran, Pearson, Morcelli.)

253.

The book *To Demetrianus* (Morcelli); *On Works and Almsgiving* (Maran, Morcelli); Epistle LX (Maran).

254.

Epistles LXIX, LXVII, and LXVIII. (Maran, Morcelli, Pearson.)

255.

Around autumn, Epistle LXX, then LXXI. (Maran.) The Seventh Council of Carthage, otherwise called the Third, and Firmilian’s Epistle, according to Morcelli’s opinion.

256.

Shortly after Easter, Epistles LXXII, LXXIII, LXXIV, and LXXVI; the aforesaid council, together with Firmilian’s Epistle, according to the opinion of Dom Maran; as winter approached, Epistles LXXV and LXIII. (The same.) The books *On Patience* and *On Jealousy and Envy*. (Maran, Pearson, Morcelli.)

257.

[Col. LVIII]

Epistles LXXVII, LXXVIII, LXXIX, and LXXX; the book *An Exhortation to Martyrdom*. (Maran, Morcelli.)

258.

Epistles LXXXII and LXXIII, shortly before his martyrdom.

## FOURTH CENTURY

### Years 300–328

**LACTANTIUS**, *Divine Institutes*, book V, chapter 1:

> “Cyprian, then, stood forth as the one preeminent and illustrious writer, both because he had won great renown through the profession of rhetoric and because he composed a very large number of works, admirable in their kind. For his talent was fluent, abundant, agreeable, and—what is the greatest virtue of discourse—clear, so that one cannot determine whether he was more ornate in expression, more successful in exposition, or more powerful in persuasion. Yet beyond his words he cannot please those ignorant of the mystery, because the things of which he spoke are mystical and designed to be heard by the faithful alone. Consequently, he is commonly ridiculed by the learned men of this age to whom his writings have happened to become known. I myself heard a certain decidedly eloquent man who, by changing one letter, called him ‘Coprianus’—from the Greek word κόπρος or κοπρία, in Latin *dung*, as though ‘dung-like’—as one who had devoted an elegant intellect, suited to better things, to old women’s tales.”

**EUSEBIUS**, in the *Chronicle*:

> Κυπριανὸς, Καρθαγένης ἐπίσκοπος, τῷ ὑπὲρ Χριστοῦ μαρτυρίῳ τελειοῦται.
>
> “Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, is perfected through martyrdom for Christ.”
>
> “Cyprian, first a rhetorician, then a presbyter, and finally bishop of Carthage, is crowned with martyrdom.”

353–368.

**HILARY**, *Commentaries on Matthew*:

> “Cyprian, a man of holy memory.”

373–390.

**PACIAN**, Epistle 2:

> “Cyprian suffered amid the concord of all, in the peace shared by all, and in the company of confessors; he confessed the faith repeatedly through successive persecutions, was tormented by much laceration, and at the last was given the saving cup to drink.”

328–391.

**ST. GREGORY NAZIANZEN**, Oration XVIII, otherwise XXIV:

> “These are the things which we had to say concerning Cyprian, and I hardly know whether it is necessary to say more; for even if we were to extend our discourse at length, we could nevertheless offer nothing that would in any respect correspond to his virtues and to the esteem in which each person holds him.”

Paris edition, p. 440 and following.

400.

**PRUDENTIUS**, Hymn XIII:

> Cease weeping for so great a righteous man: he possesses heaven’s realms,  
> Yet no less hovers over earth, nor does he withdraw from this world.  
> He expounds, speaks, treats, teaches, instructs, and prophesies.  
> Nor does he govern Libya’s peoples only: from the rising

## FIFTH CENTURY

[Col. LIX]

### Years 382–420

**ST. JEROME**, *Catalogue of Illustrious Men*:

> “Cyprian the African at first taught rhetoric with distinction; thereafter, at the persuasion of the presbyter Caecilius—from whom he also acquired his cognomen—he became a Christian and distributed all his property to the poor. Not long afterward, having been chosen as a presbyter, he was also appointed bishop of Carthage. It is superfluous to compile an index of the works of his genius, since his works are clearer than the sun. He suffered under the emperors Valerian and Gallienus, during the eighth persecution, on the same day as Cornelius at Rome, though not in the same year.”

*Letter to Paula*:

> “Blessed Cyprian advances sweetly and calmly, like the purest fountain; and although he is wholly engaged in the exercise of the virtues and beset by the hardships of persecutions, he did not discourse upon the divine Scriptures.”

*Commentary on Jonah*, chapter 3:

> “Let us set before ourselves Blessed Cyprian, who was formerly a defender of idolatry and attained such distinction in eloquence that he even taught oratory at Carthage; who at last heard Jonah’s message and, being converted to repentance, attained such virtue that he publicly preached Christ and bent his neck to the sword for him.”

430.

**ST. AUGUSTINE.**—No one is unaware of the great veneration with which St. Augustine regarded this great bishop of Carthage, how highly he valued his works, and how many excellent lessons and instructions he found even in all that the holy Martyr wrote concerning the repetition of Baptism. It is enough merely to say that St. Augustine loved the eloquence of the divine Cyprian, but at the same time asserted that it was exceedingly difficult to attain the same excellence (book IV, *On Christian Doctrine*, chapter 14). Elsewhere, however, he says:

> “The authority of Cyprian does not alarm me” (book II, *On Baptism*), “because Cyprian’s humility restores me; we certainly know the great merit of Cyprian, bishop and martyr.”

> “No tongue would suffice for the praises of Cyprian, that revered bishop and venerable martyr, not even if he himself were to praise himself” (*Sermon 313 on St. Cyprian*).

> “I could in no way believe that Cyprian—a Catholic bishop, a Catholic martyr, and one who humbled himself before everyone in proportion to his greatness, that he might find grace before God—especially in the holy council of his colleagues, put forward anything other than what he carried in his heart” (book III, *On Baptism*, chapter 3).

> “Following, in accordance with our weakness, the authority of the Catholic Church, of which he is himself an eminent and most illustrious member, let us resolve the question against heretics and schismatics” (book VII, *On Baptism*, chapter 1).

> “Even Pelagius himself, the heresiarch of these men, certainly mentions with due honor the most blessed Cyprian, rendered most glorious also by the crown of martyrdom, who was exceedingly well known not only to the African and Western churches but also to the Eastern churches, as his fame proclaimed him and his writings were disseminated far and wide” (Augustine, book IV, *To Boniface*, chapter 8).

387–465.

[Col. LX]

**ST. MAXIMUS OF TURIN** has two homilies for the feast day of St. Cyprian: one, LXXIX, cols. 255–258; the other, LXXX, cols. 259–260; Rome edition, 1784.

492–496.

**ST. GELASIUS**, Roman pontiff, in his decree *On Ecclesiastical Writings*: “The short works of Cyprian, martyr and bishop of Carthage,” t. IV of Labbe’s *Collection of Councils*, cols. 12, 62; Hardouin, t. II of the *Collection of Councils*, col. 942; and Mansi, t. VIII, col. 161 and following. The works of this Father are indeed listed in the Gelasian Decree among those rejected by the Roman Church as apocryphal: “The apocryphal short works of Thascius Cyprian,” says the author of this Decree. Yet, apart from other considerations which clearly prove that the quoted words were added to Gelasius’s Decree by some outsider, this is most powerfully demonstrated from Gratian; for Gratian lists the works of the divine Cyprian not among the apocryphal books, but among the books approved by the Church. See Tillemont, note 52, *sur S. Cyprien*, t. IV, p. 33.

## SIXTH CENTURY

### About the Year 550

**PROCOPIUS**, book I of the *Vandalic War*:

> Κυπριανὸν ἅγιον ἄνδρα μάλιστα πάντων οἱ Καρχηδόνιοι σέβονται, καὶ αὐτῷ νεών τινα λόγου πολλοῦ ἄξιον, πρὸ τῆς πόλεως ἱδρυσάμενοι παρὰ τὴν τῆς θαλάσσης ἠϊόνα, τά τε ἄλλα ἐξοσιοῦνται, καὶ ἄγουσιν ἐνιαύσιον ἑορτήν, ἣν δὴ Κυπριανὰ καλοῦσι.
>
> “The Carthaginians venerate Cyprian, a holy man, above all others; and having erected for him, outside the city beside the seashore, a shrine highly worthy of mention, they both render him exceptional honors in other respects and celebrate an annual festival which they call the *Cypriana*.”

562.

**CASSIODORUS**, *Divine Readings*, chapter XIX:

> “The most blessed Cyprian, with cultivated speech flowing like oil into every sweetness: a distinguished declaimer and an admirable teacher. How many who wavered did he not keep from falling? And those who had fallen he strengthened by his most steadfast preaching; he himself led confessors to martyrdom. And that he might not prove inferior to his own preaching, he himself also, by the Lord’s favor, was adorned with the crown of martyrdom.”

**ENNODIUS**, bishop of Ticinum:

> The heart, tongue, understanding, and dignity  
> Of Cyprian, prophet and martyr,  
> Proclaim his death through suffering.  
> Death’s blow bestowed life;  
> Light won by the sword flashes forth.  
> He was resplendent in his words  
> And rich in matchless speech;  
> The wave of a torrent’s flood,  
> The barbed shafts driven against the horn,

# MANUSCRIPTS

## FOURTH–SEVENTH CENTURIES

**Paris Manuscript I.** In the Royal Library, under no. 712 of the Latin Supplement; formerly at Saint-Germain under nos. 185–186; previously preserved in the Coislin Library. It was perhaps received from Italy, as the Benedictine monks conjecture. It is in nearly square quarto format, composed of parchment leaves, and elegant ones at that, though wrinkled with age; divided into two columns enclosed between two vertical rules; written in a Roman uncial hand, neat, full, not very large, and somewhat angular, [Col. LXI] with letters not distinctly separated and rounded; with very fine strokes, the lower shafts cut obliquely, but the upper ones straight across; with uncials, in red and sometimes touched with lead, for the first three lines of each book; but for titles and, at the end, in the word *explicit*, alternately red and black. It is distinguished by no punctuation, virtually no abbreviated, conjoined, or corrupted words, and no page marks except on the last folio of the gatherings, where it is also signed in uncials. The letters are of moderate size and written with a finer pen; the lower shafts scarcely descend; in T the transverse stroke, and in G and L the lower strokes, appear to have vanished; at the end of a line only *b.* occurs for *-bus*; elsewhere *ae* is written as *a e*; *Dms*, *Dmi* for *Dominus*, *Domini*; *q.* for *-que*; and ΧΡΙ, in Greek letters, for *Christi*. Although all have attended to these suitable and consistent characteristics, some have assigned the manuscript to the fourth or fifth century, others to the sixth or seventh.

The following words are written on the first folio in Dom Jean Mabillon’s own hand: “A manuscript of approximately the seventh century.” He then records both what it contains and what is missing. Missing above all are all the epistles; the beginning of the book or epistle *To Donatus*; the entire book *On Jealousy and Envy*; the beginning of the book *To Fortunatus*, supplied in the Council by a recent hand; in the Council of Carthage, the opinions of the bishops from 53 to 69; the beginning of the book *In Praise of Martyrdom*; and book III of the *Testimonies against the Jews*.—Mabillon adds:

> “Then follows the Psalter in Greek and Latin, in such a way that the Latin version occupies the facing page, in uncial script, without accents or breathings; it begins at Psalm XVIII, the earlier part of which is missing, and ends in LXXII, the latter part of which is absent.”

But through the destructive misfortune of recent times, and perhaps the fire at the Library of Saint-Germain, this Psalter has perished entirely. Cf. *Nouveau Traité de Diplomatique*, t. III, p. 55, note 1; *Paléographie universelle*, by D. Champollion-Figeac and Aimé Champollion, Paris, published by Firmin Didot, 1840.

**Orléans Manuscript**, in uncial letters, in the Public Library; formerly at Saint-Benoît of Fleury: the books *On Mortality* and *On the Sacrament of the Cup*. (Haenel.)

1. Andrea of Aleria had copied from the Paris manuscript the short works of Cyprian which he published in the first Roman edition.

2. Latino Latini had at his disposal, for Paolo Manuzio’s Roman edition, the very ancient Verona Manuscript, which we discussed in its proper place. He did not state explicitly which manuscripts he subsequently used in his second revision.

3. Pamelius used manuscripts of Affligem Abbey, not far from Brussels; an English manuscript belonging to John Clement, who had sent him certain corrections from it; a manuscript of Blandijnberg Abbey near Ghent, called St. Peter’s, which was burned by the sectarians a little later; and a fragment of a manuscript belonging to Louis Carrion, in which several short works of Cyprian were contained.

He also used the printed copy belonging to Johann Canchius of Utrecht, with corrections in the margin drawn from various manuscripts; a manuscript of St. Pantaleon’s at Cologne, which he calls the Cologne manuscript; corrections by Johann Costerius written in the margin of Canchius’s copy; a manuscript of Dunes Abbey in Flanders which survived the sectarians’ fire; a manuscript of the Englishman John Harris, from which he had received corrections by no means to be regretted; a manuscript of Marchiennes Abbey, not far from the University of Douai; and a manuscript of St. Nicholas outside the walls of Tournai.

4. Rigault cites the variant readings of the Royal, Benevento, Corbie, Naples, Reims, Verona, and de Thou manuscripts, Nicolas Le Fèvre’s book, and the Pomposa copy.

5. Finally, the Oxford editors employed the following manuscripts: four Bodleian manuscripts; four belonging to Isaac Vossius; two from New College, Oxford; one from Lincoln College there; one from Pembroke College, Cambridge; one from the Lambeth Library; one from the Arundel Library; one from the York Library; one from the Westminster Library; one from the Salisbury Library; and one from the Cottonian Library. They likewise used collations of a manuscript of Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, and of another London manuscript, made by Thomas Gale; likewise of another Salisbury manuscript by the bishop of St. Asaph. They used collations of the de Thou manuscripts from the monasteries of Mont-Saint-Michel, St. Victor in Paris, St. Maurice at Tossat, and St. Peter at Corbie, prepared at Paris by the Benedictine Fathers; collations of several manuscripts preserved in the Vatican, Verona, and Benevento, as well as of the king of France’s manuscript, through the diligence of Dom Robert Rigby. Account was also taken of the manuscripts used by Jacques Pamelius, Goulart, Nicolas Le Fèvre, Latino Latini, etc.

6. Baluze not only reexamined all the assistance derived from manuscripts in previous editions, but also consulted several new ones, which Maran briefly enumerates in the Prolegomena, p. CXLVII. Baluze had written the variant readings of many of them into his books; but because those books were too quickly dispersed, it was impossible to gather complete information about them.

# EDITIONS OF CYPRIAN

The editions of Cyprian may conveniently be divided into seven classes. The first is to be extended from the year 1471, from the tentative beginnings of the Roman *editio princeps*, to the Rembolt edition; the second from the Rembolt edition to Erasmus’s; the third comprises the edition of Erasmus, so often reprinted; the fourth was established by Pamelius; the fifth by Rigault; the sixth by Fell; and the seventh by Baluze. From that point down to our own edition, based jointly on Baluze and Oxford, there extends a twofold line of editions, some of which must be referred to Baluze and others to Fell.

> The masters Conrad Sweynheym and Arnold Pannartz  
> Printed many such works together at Rome.  
> Pietro with his brother Francesco, both de’ Massimi,  
> Contributed to this work the home it desired. 1471.

It is clear from Andrea of Aleria’s letter that this edition of St. Cyprian preceded the edition of the sacred Bible, the printing of which the craftsmen had been forced to interrupt for lack of larger paper. He reports that he had copied these epistles from an ancient Paris manuscript while residing in that city for the sake of his studies. This letter of his—in which he included an explanation of many words used by St. Cyprian which, as he himself says, “on reading through them rapidly, he believed required some interpretation for the sake of certain beginners in literary studies”—together with the index immediately following it, occupies slightly fewer than three folios. Audiffredi sharply corrects Lair, stating that he himself had handled four copies in Roman libraries, whereas Lair had said that, apart from the Crevenna copy, he had seen no other and that one was not easily encountered. It is listed in the catalogue of the Chigi Library.

1471.

**FIRST VENICE EDITION**, commonly called the **SPEYER EDITION**. Venice, by Wendelin of Speyer.

> **Note [Col. LXV].** It may be useful to note briefly an error which had occupied a great many books on this subject in the preceding century and which has since been repeatedly refuted and then revived: namely, that this edition appeared at Speyer, the craftsman’s name occasioning the deception. Yet this error should no more be charged against those who in the preceding age compiled the literary history of these writers than it should be reproached against the editors themselves—that is, those who had the good fortune, scarcely granted to the former scholars, to handle these old editions personally. Among their number is Reinhart of Altdorf, editor of Cyprian’s *Epistles* in 1689, who inspected both editions—the Roman and the Venetian—lent to him from the Nuremberg Library, yet speaks of them no differently than if he had learned of them in a dream, calling the latter the Speyer edition and saying that its craftsman was “a certain Wendelin.” The English editors similarly, and Baluze following them, always call this the Speyer edition. Baluze’s error, moreover, will readily find a reader all the more indignant when, fresh from reading what Maran accurately taught on this subject at the beginning of that edition, he encounters the error. I should also like to know what that “old Venetian edition” may be which Baluze repeatedly cites alongside the Speyer edition. Cf. the notes on the *Testimonies against the Jews*, p. 596. **SCHOENEMANN.**

Folio. The *Epistles of St. Cyprian*. At the end:

> The reader addresses Wendelin, the craftsman from Speyer, who restores the Epistles of Blessed Cyprian to the light.
>
> 1471.

Although I would not prove from the words “restores to the light,” as does the learned editor of the 1776 *Catalogue of the Crevenna Library*, quarto, t. I, p. 59, that this edition is later than the Roman edition, the following may nonetheless serve as a more certain argument on this occasion: whereas the Roman edition identifies the manuscript from which it was printed, the Venetian edition says nothing of any similar aid and, by that very silence, testifies that it reproduces a previously printed old edition. [Col. LXVI] A weightier consideration is the agreement of the text in the two editions, which, according to the same learned scholar, is so great that—apart from certain typographical errors which Wendelin must appear to have removed from the Roman edition—the Venetian reproduces it exactly.

> **Note [Col. LXVI].** Extraordinary are the things that Debure relates concerning this edition in his *Bibliographie instructive*—more accurately, *in need of instruction*: “It earned the distinction of being the edition which M. Baluze chose as the basis for preparing the edition he gave us of this Father of the Church, and he held it in quite exceptional esteem. It has the advantage of containing many original passages which that author was obliged to suppress or alter in his own edition.” **SCHOENEMANN.**

It is thought to surpass the Roman edition in the beauty and elegance of its workmanship. Like nearly all editions of this period, it lacks page or folio numbers, catchwords, and signatures. Before the text stand two folios containing an index.
