## CHAPTER XLIX

**ARGUMENT.—It is therefore absurd that the same thing should be praised and admired in philosophers, but hated and persecuted in Christians. Even if their opinion be false, since it nevertheless conduces to upright living, it ought to be assigned to ridicule rather than punishment. Regulus, lest one man should live in exchange for many enemies, suffered crosses throughout his entire body: what a brave man, a victor even in captivity [Note: [Col. 0532C] “What a brave man, a victor even in captivity!” That is the praise assigned to him by Cicero in *On Duties* III.99. There the history of his ill-fated courage is related: “When Marcus Atilius Regulus, during his second consulship, had been captured in Africa through an ambush—Xanthippus the Spartan serving as commander and Hamilcar, Hannibal’s father, as commanding general—he was sent under oath to the Senate, on the condition that, unless certain noble Carthaginian prisoners were returned, he himself would return to Carthage. When he arrived at Rome, he saw the appearance of advantage, but judged it false, as the event makes clear. It consisted in remaining in his native country, living in his own home with his wife and children, regarding the calamity he had suffered in war as a common hazard of military fortune, and retaining the rank of consular dignity. When Anaxarchus was being pounded with a pestle like barley groats [Note: [Col. 0532A] Thus the Fulda manuscript, Ald., Rig., and Hav.; others: “to destruction.”], he said: “Pound, pound [Note: [Col. 0532C] “Anaxarchus, when like…” Famous is the saying of Anaxarchus in Laertius, Book IX. When he was being pounded [Col. 0532D] by Nicocreon σιδηροῖς ὑπέροις (“with iron pestles”) with iron hammers because Nicocreon had heard that he himself deserved to be served up as a meal, the philosopher is said to have declared with full courage:

> πτίσσε τοῦ Ἀναξάρχου θύλακον, Ἀνάξαρκον δὲ οὐ πτίσσεις (“Pound Anaxarchus’s sack; you are not pounding Anaxarchus.” The recovered second spelling of the name, Ἀνάξαρκον, appears corrupt.).

“Pound Anaxarchus’s little sack, for you are not pounding Anaxarchus.” LAC.], the sack of Anaxarchus [Note: [Col. 0532D] “The sack of Anaxarchus.” Elegantly expressed: for while being pounded like barley groats or barley, he likewise declares that nothing else is happening to him than happens to barley—namely, that the divine inner particle, the vigor of the mind, remains unharmed, while only the perishable husk is broken and scattered. For this is what he called his little body, within which that element was in no way afflicted. Marcus Antoninus calls it τὸ ἔνδον ἐγκεκρυμμένον (“the thing hidden within”) in Book X; Iamblichus, θεῖος ἄνθρωπος (“the divine man”); Stobaeus, in his *Physical Extracts*, τὸ ἡγεμονικὀν μέρος (“the governing part”); and finally Philo Judaeus, in *On the Preliminary Studies*, ἄνθρωπος ἐν ἀνθρώπῳ, κρείττων ἐν χείρονι, ἀθάνατος ἐν (“a man within a man, the better within the worse, the immortal within”) [Col. 0533B] θνητῷ (“the mortal”). To these should be added the incomparable commentary of Isaac Casaubon on Persius, Satire V.129:

> But if within, and in the diseased liver,  
> Masters arise.

From this one understands Paul’s highly learned expression when he writes in Romans 7:24, Τίς με ῥύσεται ἐκ τοῦ σώματος τοῦ θανάτου τούτου (“Who will deliver me from this body of death?”), as well as the words read in verse 23. After the example of this Anaxarchus, *sack* and *little sack* were commonly used for the mortal body. I have seen examples in Lindenbrog’s notes on Virgil’s *Catalecta*, p. 301; Elmenhorst’s notes on Arnobius, p. 66; and Lydius’s *On Military Affairs*, p. 77. To these may be added the following passage from Gregory Nazianzen, *On the Different Ways of Life* [Col. 0533C]: “Yet Gregory will no longer be a companion to the Emperor as he was before, gratifying his belly somewhat, reclining sad and silent among the banqueters, scarcely drawing breath, and eating in a servile fashion.” HAV.]; for you are not pounding Anaxarchus [Col. 0533A] [Note: [Col. 0533C] “For you are not pounding Anaxarchus.” The same Gregory of Cygnus, in his poem *On Humility of Soul*: “Or shall anyone behold the pounding of Anaxarchus’s hands in the mortar, as though he were not present, while he nevertheless ordered his own skin-bag to be pounded violently, as though he himself remained unpounded, so that he might be seen to be no one [reading uncertain]?” Concerning Zeno, Diogenes Laertius—to whom he attributes this—writes:

> Ἤθελες, ὧ Ζὴνων, καλὸν ἤθελες, ἄνδρα τὑραννον

   Κτείνας, ἐκλῦσαι δουλοσύνας Ἐλέαν

“You desired, O Zeno—you desired a noble thing: by killing the tyrant, to free Elea from slavery.”.
