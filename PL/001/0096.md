Very great, therefore, was the power not merely of exorcists but of all Christians over demons, [Col. 0890B] by which they very frequently cast them out of the bodies of pagans.

But if anyone should inquire whence, or by what name, they possessed this power, Tertullian will answer him: “All our”—that is, Christians’—“dominion and power over those demons derives its force from the naming of Christ,” or from pronouncing Christ’s name when they adjured the demons, “and from the recollection of those things which they expect to befall them from God through Christ the arbiter” (the same, *Apologeticum*, ch. 23, p. 410). Tertullian therefore rightly concludes from this: “The demons, fearing Christ in God and God in Christ, are subjected to the servants of God and of Christ” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 23, p. 410). But how did they fear them? “Through fear, as wicked servants do,” he says (ibid., ch. 27, p. 432), as we read in our manuscript codices. They therefore feared Christ and his disciples with servile fear. “And fear,” he continues, “breathes hatred”; or, as Minucius says, “through fear they secretly sow hatred of us” [Col. 0890D] (Minucius, *Octavius*, p. 255). The demons, therefore, were both subject to Christians and afraid of them, and for that reason hated them. Why should they not be afraid, since, as our author further says, “they are subdued by them, succumb to their own condition, and entreat at close quarters those whom they attack from afar” (Tertullian, ibid., ch. 27, col. 432)? But if they entreat them, fear them, hate them, and are subjected to them, then, when adjured by them, they surely did not lie; rather, as we have already said, they truly confessed that they really were demons and by no means gods, and that there is only one God. Moreover, our author says that so genuine a confession and so public a testimony customarily made people Christians and established and confirmed the truth of the Christian religion (ibid., [Col. 0891A] col. 23, p. 26). But we have treated these matters in our earlier dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 20, art. 3; *on Arnobius*, ch. 3, art. 1; *on Lactantius*, ch. 9, art. 2; ch. 16, art. 1; ch. 20, art. 7; ch. 33, art. 4).

But as we now hasten, in our customary manner, to explain other matters, someone may perhaps delay us and ask why our author said that the demons were “pre-condemned because of their wickedness,” and afterward added: “Their desperate condition, resulting from their pre-condemnation, counts as a consolation the enjoyment of their malignity in the meantime, owing to the postponement of their punishment” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 22, col. 404; ibid., ch. 27, p. 432). “What,” he will ask, “is this pre-condemnation of the demons, this postponement of punishment, and this consolation of enjoying their malignity?” For Tertullian himself had stated no less clearly, as we observed, that it is gathered from the sacred [Col. 0891B] Scriptures that the race of demons, together with the founders and prince of their kind, is not merely pre-condemned but condemned absolutely. Yet although the condemnation of the demons is certain, they do not yet suffer all the punishments due for their crimes. For even in the sacred Scriptures themselves they are said to have reproached Christ the Lord: “Have you come here to torment us before the time?” (Matt. 8:29). Augustine, however, explained this more clearly from the epistle of the apostle Peter: “That certain angels sinned,” he says, “and were thrust down into the lowest parts of this world, which is like a prison to them, and that they are reserved there for their final condemnation on the day of judgment, the apostle Peter shows most plainly, saying that God did not spare the angels who sinned, but, thrusting them into the prisons of infernal darkness, delivered them to be kept for punishment at the judgment” (Augustine, *City of God*, book X, [Col. 0891C] ch. 33, p. 293; 2 Peter 2:4). Yet this postponement and delay of the infernal darkness and of the fire reserved for them was a consolation of some sort in their pre-condemnation and desperate condition. For in the meantime they enjoyed their malignity, by which, as we have seen, they either induced Christians to worship themselves, or false gods, or caused those who refused to be tortured with the most cruel torments, even to death. But we have discussed elsewhere this consolation of theirs and the deferment of their punishment (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 20, art. 5).

# CHAPTER XI

## On the restoration of dead human beings to life, the final judgment of God, the eternal punishments of the wicked and rewards of the righteous, and the receptacles of souls before the last judgment

### ARTICLE I

#### By what arguments Tertullian proved that all dead human beings can be restored to life and will in fact be restored; whether souls stripped of their bodies, and without the bodies’ ministry, can do, suffer, grieve, and rejoice in anything

[Col. 0891D]

We must now pass to other doctrines of the Christian religion which Tertullian asserts and defends in his books against the pagans. One of these, and one worthy of our attention, is his constant teaching, supported by various weighty arguments, that absolutely all dead human beings are to be restored to life at the end of the world. [Col. 0892A] His first argument is drawn from the infinite power of God: “He who,” he says, “fashioned this great body of the world from what had not existed”—that is, from nothing (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 520)—or, as our codices and those printed before Rigaltius have it, “set it in place,” or created it, can certainly himself raise human beings “from the death of vacancy and emptiness”—that is, from death, or from emptiness and the deprivation of life—to a second life. For they constitute only one part of the world, which, it is agreed, “is animated by the spirit that animates all things.” Thus Rigaltius and later editors present this text of Tertullian. But our codices and the older printed editions read more correctly: “animated by the spirit that animates all souls.” Our author’s argument is indeed drawn, as they say, from the greater to the lesser, or from the whole, which [Col. 0892B] was made, to the part, which can therefore be remade. The world, he says, was animated or created by the Spirit of God, the animator of all souls; therefore that same Spirit of God can animate human beings again after their death—human beings animated not by the spirit of all, but only by their own—and restore them once more, not from nothing as before, but from destruction and from a life previously lost, to a second and identical life.

Moreover, this very power of God to raise dead human beings is demonstrated clearly by Tertullian not only from the creation of the entire world but even from the creation of the human being alone. For before he was created, the human being was nothing; otherwise he would remember having existed at least at some time. But if he was created by God from nothing, he can certainly be recalled from death by the same power and will of God [Col. 0892C] (ibid.). For no greater power is required for the first creation than for renewal and restoration. Nor should you inquire too curiously and importunately how this can be done: “For explain, if you can,” says Tertullian, “the manner in which you were made, and then ask in what manner you will be made” (the same, ibid.). For by the very power by which you were first made, you will afterward be remade. Accordingly, if you do not doubt the first making, you ought much less to doubt the second: “For surely,” as he further says, “you will more easily be made what you once were, since it was equally not difficult for you to be made what you once were not” (ibid.). We have heard Minucius Felix, Lactantius, and others argue in a similar manner in the foregoing dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 7, art. 2; and *on Lactantius*, ch. 29, art. 4).

[Col. 0892D] But if this lies entirely within God’s power, nothing remains to be proved except that he wills it and has signified that he truly wills it. Yet, as our author says, God “has ordained and proclaimed” this (Tertullian, ibid.), not merely once but repeatedly in the sacred Scriptures of the Old and especially of the New Testament. Nor is there any need to gather here their testimonies, which are everywhere readily available to all and which no one who has touched those Scriptures even lightly can possibly fail to know. Their authority, moreover, is most certain, as we demonstrated above (above, ch. 6, art. 3 ff.). It is therefore established that God both can and wills that absolutely all dead human beings be restored to the former condition of their life; and consequently there will certainly be [Col. 0893A] a universal return of them to life.

Tertullian presses the point further and demonstrates by another argument that it is altogether necessary for all dead human beings someday to return to life. The whole argument is contained in these words: “Certainly, because the purpose of restoration is the appointment of judgment, the same person who had existed must necessarily be presented, so that he may receive judgment according to his good or contrary deserts” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48). He therefore teaches that there is absolutely no room for doubt that all human beings must present themselves at God’s final judgment at the end of the world. But this cannot happen unless they return to life and receive from God a final sentence, and either reward or punishment, according to their good or evil deserts.

Our author, however, presses this argument much more fully [Col. 0893B] in his book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, where he argues as follows: “This will be the whole cause—indeed, the necessity—of the resurrection: namely, the appointment of judgment, most fully consonant with God. From the ordering of that judgment, consider whether divine adjudication must indeed preside over both substances of the human being that are to be judged, the soul as well as the flesh” (the same, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 14). For a human being certainly acts well or badly not with the soul alone but also with his body. Such is the partnership of the soul with the body that “in the flesh, with the flesh, and through the flesh is done whatever is done in the heart” (ibid., ch. 15), that is, in the soul. Indeed, even if the soul sometimes thinks by itself, this thought of its is scarcely ever without some interaction with the body: “For if the soul turns anything over,” says Tertullian, “the countenance supplies an indication; the face [Col. 0893C] is the mirror of every intention” (ibid.).

There is no need at present to investigate whether he truly believed that the soul can do absolutely nothing without interaction with the body and without its senses. For we have already said something about this opinion, and an opportunity to discuss it will arise immediately. For now, therefore, let it suffice to note that very many acts of the soul, as no one denies, are performed not without the ministry of the body and the senses. The human body is joined to the soul in such a way that it is certainly a participant in at least many good or evil deserts. The whole human being, therefore—that is, his body and soul—must be presented at the final and most equitable judgment of God, so that they may receive either punishment or reward for everything done in this mortal life.

Nor does our author allow anyone to object to him that the human [Col. 0893D] soul alone enjoys free choice of the will, while the body is driven into every action as an instrument. For the body is not an instrument foreign to the soul, but is so united to it that, just as it participates in the soul’s actions, so too it participates in its deserts. After the end of the world, therefore, it must be restored to life so that, by God’s just judgment, it may be punished or rewarded according to its deserts.

The third argument by which Tertullian endeavors to prove the future resurrection of the human being is not without difficulty and will appear false to many. For he argued in this manner (the same, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, p. 520): “Souls and bodies will therefore be presented again, because the soul alone cannot suffer anything without stable matter”—that is, the flesh. Can the soul, then, [Col. 0894A] suffer absolutely nothing without the flesh? “Yes,” someone will say, “Tertullian indeed believed this.” For in another book he addresses the soul in these very words (the same, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, ch. 4): “We affirm that you remain after the completion of life, await the day of judgment, and, according to your desert, are destined either for torment or for refreshment, each of them everlasting. To endure these, your former substance, and the matter and memory of the same human being, must necessarily return to you, because you can experience neither evil nor good without the flesh’s capacity for suffering, and there is no ground for judgment without the presentation of the very person who merited the suffering imposed by judgment.” Thus he affirms—not merely once, nor doubtfully, but insistently—and repeats that the soul can feel absolutely nothing without association with its body. He considered this [Col. 0894B] so true that he concludes from it that the dead human body will therefore return to life, because without it the soul can do nothing good or evil.

But withhold your assent for a little while, if you can. I would first have you turn your attention to what the same Tertullian argued in his books *On the Soul* and *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, where he treats expressly and more fully both the dead body’s return to life and the soul’s capacity for acting and suffering. In the former book he asserts most explicitly and firmly that the soul, separated from the body, either suffers pain or is filled with joy. For all human souls remain in the underworld after death, “and, whether you will or not,” he says, “both punishments and refreshments are already found there.” Then, when someone objects that the soul must await the resurrection of the flesh, its partner in works, before it can grieve [Col. 0894C] or rejoice, he answers without hesitation that this is not necessary. For, as he puts it, “from its own resources it has enough for either category of suffering.” And certainly the soul by itself, without the ministry of the body, is sometimes tormented by anger or hatred; and sometimes, even while the body is afflicted, it seeks a secret joy for itself, as he confirms by the examples of Mucius, Zeno, and others (ibid.). Conversely, it sometimes grieves, is oppressed, and is tormented while the body remains unharmed. But if in this life, while bound to the body, the soul by itself is affected by joy or grief, how much more so when, after death, it is separated from the body and awaits the final judgment? Finally, some of its acts occur without interaction with the flesh, such as thoughts alone and bare acts of will, which divine judgment takes into account [Col. 0894D] (the same, *On the Soul*, ch. 17). From these considerations he draws the following conclusion: “Therefore it is most fitting that the soul, even without waiting for the flesh, should be punished for what it committed without the flesh’s participation; so too, for pious and benevolent thoughts in which it did not need the flesh, it will be refreshed without the flesh.” He taught the same things, and several others, in his book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, where he expressly demonstrates that human beings are altogether to be restored to life.

“What then,” you will ask, “did Tertullian contradict himself so absurdly?” But if you are willing to keep your judgment still suspended, you will see that he himself removed this apparent contradiction, although it is by no means slight. For in the same book *On the Resurrection* he repeats what he had said, [Col. 0895A] namely, that “souls are now tormented or comforted in the underworld, although naked, although still exiled from the flesh” (the same, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 17). But because some might object that it can be inferred from this that the soul is thus sufficient unto itself in its “capacity,” as he calls it, “for suffering and sensation,” and therefore does not need the flesh to be presented again, he responds to them as follows (ibid.):

> On the contrary, it will need the flesh to this extent—not because it cannot feel anything without the flesh, but because it must also feel together with the flesh. For insofar as acting is concerned, it is sufficient from its own resources; and so too for suffering. For acting, however, it is sufficient from its own resources only to a lesser degree. From its own resources it can merely think, will, desire, and determine; but to accomplish anything it awaits the service of the flesh. Thus for suffering also it demands the service of the flesh, so that through it it may suffer as fully as it was unable to act fully without it. Therefore, for those things for which it is sufficient from its own resources, it meanwhile pays the penalty—desire, thought, and will. [Col. 0895B] Moreover, if these were sufficient for the fullness of deserts, so that deeds were not also required, the soul would be entirely sufficient for the completion of judgment, being judged for those things in the doing of which it alone had been sufficient. But since deeds also are bound to deserts, and deeds are carried out through the flesh, it is no longer sufficient for the soul without the flesh to be comforted or tormented for the works of the flesh, even though it has members of its own; for these are correspondingly insufficient for it to feel fully, just as they are insufficient for it to act perfectly. Therefore, in whatever manner it acted, in that manner it also suffers in the underworld, tasting judgment first, just as it first introduced the offense; yet it awaits the flesh, so that through it it may also receive recompense for the deeds whose conception it entrusted to the flesh.

We have transcribed this entire argument [Col. 0895C] and response of our author, although it is rather long, because it not only completely resolves the difficulty proposed to us, but also yields several observations about the nature of the soul which should not be passed over without comment.

He therefore teaches that the naked soul, without partnership with the body, is truly either tormented or comforted in the underworld. Because, however, it could be objected to him from this that the soul is not a purely spiritual substance but a bodily one, or, as he himself says, possesses a “corporeity of its own,” he was unwilling to examine this more difficult question, lest he stray too long from his subject. Meanwhile, he concedes, though he does not expressly admit, that the soul has some kind of body and certain members peculiar to its own kind. Yet he asserts most explicitly that the soul has acts of its own which do not [Col. 0895D] depend upon the body at all, such as thoughts, acts of will, and desires. If, moreover, these acts—to note the point in passing—are altogether spiritual, he could much less deny that the soul itself is spiritual.

Since these are acts of the soul alone, it possesses from its own resources not only the capacity to act and suffer, but also that by which it can merit either reward or punishment. Once released from the body, therefore, it can and ought to be affected by either punishment or reward according to its deserts. For so long as it is united to the body, it always sins or earns merit first. After death, therefore, it must necessarily be affected by the punishments or rewards due to it, alone and before the body’s return. Tertullian was therefore convinced that the soul, once the bonds [Col. 0896A] of the body have been broken, suffers, is tormented, and is tortured. But because in this life it also committed many sins together with its body, it must therefore be reunited to its body, restored to life, so that it may be punished and tormented according to its deserts.

Once these points have been explained in this way, the alleged contradiction in Tertullian disappears. For he rightly said both that the soul truly suffers something without the body and that it can suffer nothing. Where he says that it can suffer nothing, the words “fully and perfectly” must be understood—that is, to the extent merited by its sinning together with the body. For with that with which it sinned, it must certainly also be punished. Tertullian therefore rightly proves from this the future restoration of the dead body to life, because the soul alone cannot suffer, namely, the full and perfect punishment which it jointly merited by sinning with the body.

[Col. 0896B] If you permit us to add anything further to these observations, we shall note that Ambrose, in the appropriate place, as well as Athenagoras, Minucius Felix, and Lactantius clearly proved by this very same argument—drawn from the punishments or rewards due to a human being who acts well or badly in this life—that the same human being will return to life; indeed, Lactantius also used it to demonstrate the immortality of our soul, as we have shown in our dissertations on their books (Ambrose, book II, *On Faith in the Resurrection*, § 12, p. 1147; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 475; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 24, art. 3, and ch. 29, art. 5; *Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 7, art. 2).

### ARTICLE II

#### By what examples, by what oracle of the Pythia, and by what philosophical opinion concerning the transmigration of souls Tertullian shows that all dead human beings whatsoever are to be restored to their former state of life, and how he answers the objections advanced

[Col. 0896C]

Although Tertullian demonstrated most clearly by the foregoing arguments that any human beings whatsoever can, by the will of almighty God, be raised from death, however incurred, he nevertheless endeavors to confirm this with several examples. Yet we must not conceal that these examples, since they are not entirely parallel, cannot furnish a perfect and in every respect complete demonstration of human resurrection. They can at least fittingly confirm that the pagans deny in vain that human beings can be restored to the former state of a life that has passed.

[Col. 0896D] Having offered these observations against more captious critics, we must hear Tertullian himself (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 520): “An example of human resurrection,” he says, “has also been marked out by God himself as testimony for you. The light, slain each day, shines again, and the darkness in turn withdraws and succeeds; the stars, having died, revive.” In our manuscript copies and the printed editions earlier than Rigaltius, the reading is *vivescunt*—that is, as is clear from Pliny, “they become alive” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book XVII, ch. 10, p. 344). Our author continues: “The seasons begin when they end; fruits are consumed”—our manuscripts and printed codices have “are brought to completion”—“and return. [Col. 0897A] Seeds certainly spring up more fruitfully only after being corrupted and dissolved. All things are preserved by perishing; all things are re-formed from destruction” (Tertullian, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 12). He pursues these points with greater verbal splendor in another book; Minucius Felix does so more briefly, and Theophilus of Antioch more fully, as we have explained elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 7, art. 2).

Our Tertullian concludes his argument in these words (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 43): “You, human being—so great a name, if you understand yourself and how great you truly are, learning even from the inscription of the Pythia that you are lord of all things that die and rise again—will you die for the purpose of perishing?” He therefore says that the human being’s condition is no worse than that of the other things whose lord [Col. 0897B] he is, which die and rise again. The human being, therefore, has a far stronger claim than they do both to die and to rise again.

But someone will ask what that oracle of the Pythia cited by him was. Some answer that it is what he reports in that very passage: “Lord of all things that die and rise again.” Yet they have offered no argument proving that this was pronounced by the Pythia. Furthermore, he did not say in these words that he was reporting an oracle of the Pythia, but that from the oracle of the Pythia a human being could learn that he was lord of all things that die and rise again (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1170). To us, therefore, that oracle of the Pythia appears to be none other than this celebrated saying of Apollo: “Know yourself,” which we have discussed elsewhere. Tertullian, moreover, seems deliberately to have added [Col. 0897C] “even from the inscription of the Pythia,” because, as we noted in the same place, it had been inscribed upon the temple of Pythian, or Delphic, Apollo. “Why then,” you will ask, “did Tertullian write *Pythiae* (‘of the Pythia’), and not *Pythii* (‘of the Pythian’), the latter being a name by which Apollo was sometimes called?” If *Pythiae*, rather than *Pythii*, is to be read in his text, we shall answer that it is called “of the Pythia” because the Pythian prophetess customarily prophesied in that temple. For, to omit other authorities, this is expressed in the following verse of Lucretius (Lucretius, book V, v. 113):

> The Pythia, who speaks from Phoebus’ tripod and laurel.

Tertullian proceeds and, in order to break and ever more decisively refute the obstinacy of the pagans, presses them with the following *ad hominem* argument, as it is called. It is certain, he says, that they placed confidence in any philosopher [Col. 0897D] who attempted, through certain arguments proposed as ingeniously as elegantly, to defend Pythagoras’s empty opinion concerning the transmigration of souls. Indeed, none of them, deterred by the jibes of the biting Laberius, who mocked it, abstained from eating all animal flesh, “lest perhaps,” Tertullian further says, “he dine upon beef from some ancestor of his”—that is, lest in eating beef he should eat the soul of some ancestor, which he believed had migrated into the body of an ox (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 42). Tertullian did not think that so ridiculous an opinion needed to be refuted here (ibid.): “For,” he says, “we shall need many jokes and leisure if we wish to sport with this question of who might seem to be re-formed into what beast [Col. 0898A].” Rigaltius indeed reads thus; but our manuscript codices and the printed editions older than his have: “We shall also need many passages,” from which arguments to refute this error can be drawn, *ex otio*, or *otio* (“with ease”), just as Pliny wrote *ex facili* for *facile* (“easily”) (book III, epistle 3). Yet to many, *jocis* (“jokes”) will doubtless seem the preferable reading, since this utterly ridiculous opinion of Pythagoras and his followers ought to have been exploded with jokes rather than with more powerful arguments. The noun *jocis* is not unreasonably thought to accord better with the verb *lascivire* (“to sport”) than *locis* (“passages”) does (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 42). Nevertheless, shortly before this he had said that any philosopher could be overwhelmed by an immense weight of arguments if he affirmed, as Laberius says, that [Col. 0898B] according to the opinion of Pythagoras “a man is made from a mule, a snake from a woman” (the same, *On the Pallium*, ch. 1). These are verses of that poet of mimes, whom he mentions elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 7, art. 1), published by Crinitus, as we noted elsewhere:

> And now from a mule they make a man, from a woman a snake,  
> And from diverse things diverse other things.

Various writers, especially Cicero, Suetonius, both Senecas, Aulus Gellius, and Macrobius, frequently mention this Laberius, of whom Horace, among others, sang:

> Yet in conceding this, I would not grant the rest as well; for should I  
> Admire both the mimes of Laberius and his beautiful poems?

But such was the madness of the pagans and such their fury against [Col. 0898C] Christians that the Christian opinion, as our author rightly observes in another book, “although more honorable than the Pythagorean, which transfers you into beasts; although fuller than the Platonic, which restores to you also the endowment of the body; although more serious than the Epicurean, which defends you from destruction—nevertheless, solely because of the name ‘Christian,’ is accounted vanity, folly, and, as it is called, presumption.” Nor was it enough for the pagans to reject and scorn this Christian position, which was better than the others; rather, “the Christian who defends it will be driven out by the populace with stones rather than merely with boxing gloves”—these are further words of Tertullian in the *Apologeticum* (the same, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 42). But instead of *caestibus* (“boxing gloves”), our codices read *coetibus* (“assemblies”); that is, he will be expelled from meetings and gatherings. Tertullian indeed argues, and not badly, [Col. 0898D] from the greater to the lesser, as they say. The Christian, he says, who asserts that doctrine concerning the human being’s future return to life, will not only be expelled from the assemblies of the pagans, but, what is worse, will be overwhelmed with stones. Yet if *coestibus* is not very well written in Rigaltius’s edition, still worse is the reading *coedibus* (“slaughters”) found in the older editions, since slaughters are more cruel than stoning. We have treated the opinions of Pythagoras, Plato, and Epicurus noted by our author in our dissertations on the books of Minucius Felix and Lactantius (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 7, art. 1; and *on Lactantius*, ch. 34, art. 1).

Since Tertullian had attempted to support the human being’s return to life with the proposed examples of light and darkness, the stars and [Col. 0899A] seasons, fruits and seeds (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 45), the pagans argued from this: “Then it will always be necessary to die and always to rise again.” Yet this is plainly absurd. But first, Tertullian replies: “If the Lord of things had so ordained, you would experience, however unwillingly, the law of your condition” (ibid.). That is, because the true and omnipotent God is lord of all things, and consequently of death and life, he can certainly raise a dead human being to life not merely once, but as often as he wills. If, therefore, he had decreed various returns to life for the human being, the latter would experience that law unwillingly. Tertullian said *ingratis*, that is, involuntarily and against one’s will. For our author himself often used this word in that sense; indeed Cicero himself says: “What he does not owe must be extorted from him against his will and unwillingly.” And elsewhere: “They will gladly keep silent about what they can, but they will say unwillingly what necessity requires” (the same, ch. 4, col. 5; *Against Hermogenes*, ch. 19; *Against the Valentinians*, ch. 26, p. 300; Cicero, first speech *For Quinctius*, p. 9, line 2; and *Against Verres*, book IV, p. 163, line 15). Do you see that the words “gladly” and “unwillingly” are opposed to one another?

Secondly, Tertullian answers: “But as matters now stand, God has ordained no otherwise than he has proclaimed” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, p. 43)—namely, in the sacred Scriptures, as we noted above, where he proclaims that he has ordained for the human being to die once and rise once.

Finally, he answers that human beings therefore rise again so that, [Col. 0899C] “throughout the immeasurable perpetuity of eternity, they may receive what they have merited, whether evil or good, in this life” (ibid.). They must therefore be restored to life once. Afterward there will be neither death nor resurrection, but eternal life.

He next also meets the objections of those who customarily asserted that it was impossible for the very same human body—submerged in the deep and immeasurable sea, reduced to ashes, devoured by beasts and sometimes by human beings—ever to rise again: “For nothingness itself belongs to God, to whom the whole also belongs” (ibid.). For since God created all things from nothing, and is lord both of an existing thing and of a non-existing thing and of nothingness, he can certainly both make and remake anything whatsoever, in whatever manner pleases him. He therefore rightly concludes from this: “Wherever you have been dissolved, whatever material has destroyed, swallowed up, abolished, or reduced you to nothing, he will restore you” [Col. 0899D] (ibid.). But we have discussed this subject more fully in our preceding dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, ch. 7, art. 2; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 28, art. 4).

### ARTICLE III

#### At what time dead human beings will be raised to life and present themselves at God’s final judgment, and how Tertullian uses the pagans’ opinions concerning Minos and Rhadamanthus to confirm that everyone’s evil and good deserts will then be examined

After Tertullian had proved, by the weighty arguments we have set forth, the future return to life of every [Col. 0900A] dead human being, he immediately shows when all human beings are to rise again. “The Reason,” he says—that is, doubtless, the Word and Speech of God—“fashioned the world out of mutually opposing elements” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 48, col. 43), that is, elements contrary to one another; or, as he said in another book, “out of diverse substances, so that they might subsist in unity” (the same, *On the Pallium*, ch. 2). This same Reason willed time to flow from the beginning of this world and continually pass away until it reaches eternity, which stands opposite it. But between infinite and immeasurable eternity and finite time, it interposed a middle boundary, at which “the age” (*Apologeticum*, passage cited)—that is, the entire duration of this world—would end and infinite eternity begin. “At that final boundary of time and first boundary of eternity,” he says, [Col. 0900B] “the whole human race will be restored, in order to settle the account of whatever good or evil it has merited in this age” of its mortal life, “and thereafter to pay it throughout the immeasurable perpetuity of eternity” (ibid.). Thus the text stands in Rigaltius’s and the old editions and in our manuscript codices. But in some printed editions *deprehendendum* (“to be detected”) is read instead of *dependendum* (“to be paid”), while in others the reading is *exinde dependendum* (“thereafter to be paid”). In still others, moreover, this verb is omitted entirely. Nor does it appear especially necessary if the verb *expungendum* is taken here by Tertullian, as elsewhere and by other writers, to mean “to be paid in full.” But if he uses it to mean the rendering by human beings of the sum of their works, with their accounts to be reckoned and settled, then the verb *dependendum* must be retained, so that his meaning here is: absolutely all human beings will be restored so that [Col. 0900C] all the accounts of their good and evil deserts may be reckoned, examined, and settled, and they may then, throughout all eternity, pay and discharge what they merited during this life. Suetonius, moreover, appears to have used the verb *expungo* in this second sense when he wrote of the emperor Claudius: “When he reviewed the records of the panels” (Suetonius, *Life of Claudius*, § 15)—that is, as some have observed, when he arranged and reckoned which members of the panels were to be retained among the number of judges. To this point they also refer these words of Papinian: “A man censured and restored in connection with desertion is deprived of his pay for the period during which he was a deserter” (*Digest*, book XLIX, title 16, law 15, *On Military Affairs*).
