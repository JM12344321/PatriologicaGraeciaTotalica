The first author who presents himself to us is Pliny, cited just above, where he discusses places from which the seas had withdrawn and islands that had emerged [Col. 1069A]: “The famous islands of Delos and Rhodes,” he says, “are recorded as having emerged long ago. Later, smaller ones emerged: Anaphe beyond Melos; Nea between Lemnos and the Hellespont; Hanelo between Lebedos and Teos; Thera and Therasia among the Cyclades, in the fourth year of the 135th Olympiad; and, among the same islands 130 years later, Hiera, also called Automate” (Plin., lib. II ibid., cap. 87, pag. 236). To this may also be added Ammianus Marcellinus, where he investigates the causes of earthquakes: “Brasmatiae,” he says, “which violently heave up the ground, thrust forth enormous masses, as Delos, Hiera, and Anaphane emerged in Asia” (Marcell., lib. XVII, cap. 7, pag. 456). Do you see that Delos and Rhodes are mentioned in these passages, just as they are by Tertullian himself? Is there not therefore room to suspect that, from the other two islands, Hiera and Anaphe, as Pliny writes [Col. 1069B], or Hiera and Anaphane, as Marcellinus writes, the corrupt word *Hierannapen* was produced by ignorant copyists? We shall, however, readily yield to anyone who offers a better suggestion.

There remains the last island named by Tertullian: Co, or Cho, as in our manuscripts and in the already cited first book *To the Nations*; or Coro, as in the old editions; or Coon, as in Heraldus’s edition. Pamelius, however, thinks that it should be corrected to Coo, because we read in Eusebius’s *Chronicle*: “On the island of Cos very many things collapsed in an earthquake” (Chron. Euseb., ad. 38 ann. August.). Perhaps not altogether wrongly, for Strabo mentions the island Κῶς (Cos) (Strab., lib. II Geogr., pag. 124, et lib. X, pag. 489), as do others. But some have advised us that the text of Eusebius’s *Chronicle* reads “in [Col. 1069C] Cyprus,” and they endeavor to confirm this by Seneca’s testimony. Finally, some have suspected that Tertullian wrote Cea, and they think that this emendation can be supported by these words of Pliny: “More than thirty miles were suddenly torn away from the island of Cea, carrying off a great multitude of people” (Plin., lib. II Natur. Hist., cap. 92, pag. 239). Would this conjecture not gain some additional strength from the fact that the same Pliny later observes that Cos was called Cea by Staphylus (Ibid., lib. V, cap. 9)? But this will doubtless be established more securely if it can be proved that Tertullian had Pliny’s books in view.

He certainly appears to have been aiming at Pliny when he said: “Plato records that a land greater than Asia or Africa was taken away by the Atlantic sea” (Tertullian., Apologet., cap. 40, pag. 481). And again in another book: “Plato records that an Asia or Africa of greater extent was submerged in the Atlantic sea” (lib. I ad Nation., cap. 9, pag. 571). For we have seen that the same Pliny wrote: “It carried lands away altogether—first of all, where the Atlantic sea now lies, over an immense extent, if we believe Plato” (Plin., lib. II Hist. natur., cap. 90, pag. 238). The passage of Plato cited by both is found in his *Timaeus* (Plato., Tim., tom. III, pag. 25), as we have observed elsewhere (Dissertat. in Arnob., cap. 6, art. 3). Yet even if Tertullian had never thought of Pliny when writing these things, this nevertheless confirms no less strongly what he repeats elsewhere, that a Libya or Asia was leveled beneath the Atlantic sea.

But let us follow him still further, unless you think it has already been sufficiently proved by so many examples [Col. 1070A] that Christians were not the cause of public calamities, and unless you are weary of having it proved by still others. He proceeds and says: “An earthquake drank up the Corinthian Sea” (Tertullian., lib. de Pall., cap. 2); and in the book *To the Nations*: “The Corinthian Sea was carried away by an earthquake.” Some think that he was alluding there to the destruction of the cities Helice and Bura, concerning which Pliny again says: “The sea swallowed Helice and Bura in the Corinthian Gulf, traces of which are visible in the deep” (Plin., lib. II Natur. Hist., cap. 92, pag. 239). If you desire more, consult Strabo (Strab., lib. I Geogr., pag. 59), Pausanias (Pausan., lib. VII, pag. 212 et 231), Seneca (Senec., lib. VI Natur. Quaest., cap. 23, pag. 893), and, passing over others, Ovid, who sang:

[Col. 1070B] If you seek Helice and Bura, Achaean cities,

You will find them beneath the waters; and even now sailors

Are accustomed to point out the sunken towns with their lofty walls.

(Ovid. *Metamorph.* lib. XV, v. 293, etc.)

But a little earlier he composed these verses, not indeed about cities but about the lands named by our Author:

I myself have seen what once had been most solid earth

Become sea; I have seen lands made from the sea;

And marine shells lay far from the ocean,

And an ancient anchor was found on the highest mountains.

See, if you please, what we noted on Arnobius in the preceding dissertation (Dissertat. in Arnob., cap. 6, art. 3). But someone will object that these things do not suffice for Tertullian’s purpose unless it is shown by some argument that the Corinthian Sea was, as he says, carried away and drunk up by an earthquake. Yet Callisthenes, already cited by [Col. 1070C] us after Seneca, will fully satisfy him, for he teaches that cities and lands can easily be swallowed by overflowing waters when air, slipping through hidden openings, shakes the earth (Senec., lib. VI natur. Quaest., cap. 85, pag. 895). For then a chasm is formed in which everything is consumed. Hence the same Seneca said again: “A violent convulsion engulfed the two cities Helice and Bura.” But Bura and Helice were cities of the Peloponnesian, or Corinthian, isthmus, which could not have been submerged by the waters without the Corinthian Sea being swallowed in a similar event. Yet it was not swallowed in its entirety, but produced the double sea of which Ovid says:

And the isthmus that separates the waters of the twin sea.

(Ovid. epist. 12, v. 104.)

[Col. 1070D] And Lucan:

. . . As the slender isthmus

Which cuts through the waves and separates the twin sea,

And does not permit the straits to meet; should the land recede,

The Ionian Sea would break against the Aegean. . . .

(Lucan. lib. II, v. 99.)

Moreover, what Tertullian added is far easier for everyone to understand: “The violence of the waves cut Lucania away from Italy and banished it under the name of Sicily” (Tertullian. *Apologet.* cap. 40, pag. 481). In another book, however, he speaks at somewhat greater length: “Once a side of Italy, battered by the Adriatic and Tyrrhenian seas, was intercepted through its middle and left Sicily as its remnant; while that entire region of rupture, forcing back the contentious meeting of the seas into narrow confines, gave the sea a new defect—not that of spewing out shipwrecks, but of devouring them” (Idem, [Col. 1071A] lib. de Pall. cap. 2). These things have indeed been handed down to memory by many geographers, poets, and others. Ovid, in the same passage cited a little above, sang of Zancle, one city of Sicily:

. . . Zancle also is said to have been joined

To Italy, until the sea swept away their boundaries

And thrust the land back with an intervening wave.

(Ovid., lib. XV *Metamor.* v. 290.)

Lucan, whom we have also commended, sang the same thing more than once concerning the whole of Sicily (Lucan. lib. II, v. 543, et lib. III, v. 58 et seqq.), as did Virgil in these verses:

These places, once convulsed by violence and vast destruction—

So greatly can the long passage of time alter things—

Are said to have burst apart, when previously both lands

Had been one; the sea came violently between them and with its waves

Cut the Hesperian side away from Sicily, and flows

[Col. 1071B] Between fields and cities drawn apart by a narrow channel.

(Virgil., lib. III *Aeneid.*, v. 414 et seqq.)

After citing these verses, Seneca added: “Thus, by this inundation celebrated by the greatest poets, Sicily was cast away from Italy” (Senec., lib. IV natur. Quaest., cap. 29, pag. 898). See Pliny (Plin., lib. II natur. Hist. cap. 87, pag. 235), Statius (Stat., lib. III *Thebaid.* et lib. I *Sylvar.*), Silius Italicus, Cluverius, and others.

Why say more? Before Saturn and all the other gods of the gentiles there occurred the cataclysm which, as Tertullian says, destroyed the whole world, or, as Plato thought, only the level country (Tertullian. *Apologet.*, cap. 40, p. 481). More briefly in the book *To the Nations*: “A cataclysm destroyed the whole world” (lib. I ad Nation. cap. 9, p. 571); but more fully in another book: “Even now,” he says, “the sea’s shells [Col. 1071C] and trumpets travel abroad upon the mountains, desiring to prove to Plato that even the heights were afloat” (Idem, lib. de Pall. cap. 2). No one fails to understand that he is speaking there of the universal flood described in the sacred book of Genesis (Genes., cap. VI et VII). He censures Plato there because Plato thought that the waters of this flood had not reached the highest mountain peaks, to which, as he fancied, a great many shepherds fled and, unlike the others, did not suffer shipwreck (Platon. tom. II, lib. III de legib. pag. 677; et tom. III Tim. pag. 22). Our Author refutes this false opinion from the fact that shells and trumpets were found upon mountains—or *buccina*, as Pliny says, who observes in the same place that this is a smaller kind of shell shaped like a horn or trumpet (Plin., lib. IX natur. Hist. cap. [Col. 1071D] 36, pag. 340, et cap. 33, pag. 328). But a little earlier we cited Ovid, who testifies that shells and an anchor had been found on the highest mountains. Yet at the time of that universal flood Saturn and the other gods afterward invented by the gentiles did not yet exist, as is clearly demonstrated by the cities founded by them, or in which they were born and died—or, as the book *To the Nations* says, were born, remained, and were buried (Tertullian. loc. cit.). We have discussed these matters, and the origin of the gods and of idolatry, in the preceding dissertations, and something more will be said below.

## ARTICLE III

### On the disasters that were caused before the beginnings of the Christian religion by heavenly fire and by human arms; on Sodom and Gomorrah, which were burned, and their land, where fruits attempt to grow and then turn to ashes when touched; on Volsinii and Pompeii, or the Tarpeians, consumed by fire; on the disaster at Cannae, after which Hannibal measured the slaughter of the Romans by a bushel of rings taken from them; and on the Capitol occupied by the Senones

[Col. 1072A]

Tertullian passes to other disasters caused by heavenly fire and by human arms, and says: “A fiery rain burned Sodom and Gomorrah” (Tertullian. *Apologet.* cap. 40, p. 482), before the Israelites migrated from Egypt into Palestine, and consequently many centuries before the Christians, who arose from them, came into the world. The destruction of both these cities is confirmed by the most certain testimony of Holy Scripture [Col. 1072B] (Genes. cap. XIX, v. 24). But even if that testimony were lacking, an unmistakable indication of that disaster would be supplied by the land itself, which, to use Tertullian’s favored manner of expression, “still smells of burning; and if any fruit of the trees there attempts to appear so far as the eyes are concerned, it nevertheless turns to ashes when touched” (Tertullian., loc. cit.). So indeed it reads in our manuscripts and in others. Some, however, wrongly read “arise and are seen.” For there is an ellipsis, and one must understand there: “attempts to ripen.” In another book, however, he says: “Impiety deserved rains of fire. Sodom survives to this day, and no Gomorrah; everything is ashes, and the neighboring sea, together with the soil, lives a death” (Idem, lib. de Pall. cap. 2). Josephus, however, relates that the remnants of the divine fire were still visible there, together with the outlines of five towns and ashes reborn from fruits which, although resembling edible fruit, dissolve into smoke and ashes when touched by the hands [Col. 1072C] (Joseph. lib. V de Bello Judaico. cap. 5, pag. 892). Augustine even asserts that this is corroborated by the authority of gentile writers (August. lib. XXI, de Civit. cap. 3, pag. 629). If anyone wishes one of them to be cited, we can readily supply Cornelius Tacitus, whose words are these: “Not far from there”—that is, from the Dead Sea—“are plains said once to have been fertile and inhabited by great cities, but to have been burned by bolts of lightning; traces remain, and the ground, scorched in appearance, has lost its productive power. For everything that springs up of itself or is sown by hand, whether it grows into grass or flower or reaches its customary form, black and empty, vanishes as though into ashes” (Tacit. lib. V *Histor.* paulo post init.). But as a gentile man ignorant of Holy [Col. 1072D] Scripture, he suspects that this failure of the fruits to mature arises from the exhalation of the lake, by which the air spread over the land is first corrupted and then corrupts the land. Solinus erred no less, for he similarly attributes this effect of divine vengeance to the heavenly sun alone, by which he relates that this land was struck:

“At a great distance from Jerusalem a grim gulf opens, which the black earth, reduced to ashes, attests was struck from heaven. Two towns are there, one named Sodom and the other Gomorrah. Although the fruit produced near them has the appearance of ripeness, it cannot be eaten. The mere envelope of its outer skin confines sooty ash within; when pressed even by a light touch, it exhales smoke and collapses into drifting dust” (Solin. *Polyhist.*, cap. 35, pag. 65).

[Col. 1073A] Tertullian afterward says: “Nor were Tuscany and Campania at that time complaining about Christians, when fire from heaven overwhelmed Vulsinii, and fire from its own mountain overwhelmed Pompeii” (Tertullian. *Apologet.*, cap. 40, p. 485). Again, in another book: “From a cloud of this kind Tuscany also burned ancient Vulsinii, while Campania—so that it might place greater hope in its mountains—lost Pompeii” (lib. de Pallio, cap. 2). But these passages are not without their difficulty. For in the book *To the Nations* we read: “Fire from heaven overwhelmed Vulsinii, and fire from its own mountain overwhelmed the Tarpeians” (Idem, lib. I ad Natio. cap. 9. pag. 54). Those who are called Tarpeians there are called Pompeians in the other two books, although Tertullian appears to be speaking about the same peoples. But Pompeii was at no slight distance from the Tarpeians, who inhabited the Tarpeian, one of Rome’s seven hills [Col. 1073B]. Virgil sings of this hill:

Hence he leads him to the Tarpeian seat and the Capitol,

Golden now, but once rough with woodland thickets.

(Virgil., lib. VII *Aeneid.* v. 347.)

And again later:

Manlius, guardian of the Tarpeian citadel, stood

Before the temple at its summit and held the lofty Capitol.

(Ibid. v. 652.)

There is no need to cite Ovid (Ovid. lib. I *Fastor.*, v. 79) and others on so well-known a matter.

Pompeii, however, was a town on the coast of Campania: “Not far,” says Pliny, “with Mount Vesuvius overlooking it and the river Sarnus washing its shores” (Plin. lib. III natur. Hist. cap. 4. pag. 327). Pomponius Mela supports Pliny in these words [Col. 1073C]: “Within sight of Mount Vesuvius are Pompeii, Naples, and Puteoli” (Mel. de Sit. orb. lib. II. cap. 4, § 65). Strabo (Strab. lib. V Geogr., pag. 247) and other geographers relate the same things. Seneca, moreover, described not only the location of this city but also its destruction:

“We have heard that Pompeii, a celebrated city of Campania, at which the Surrentine and Stabian shores meet on one side and the Herculanean shore on the other, while the sea, led in from the open, surrounds it, subsided in an earthquake, and that all the adjoining regions were afflicted. This earthquake occurred, indeed, in the winter, on the Nones of February, in the consulship of Regulus and Virginius” (Senec., lib. VI natural. Quaest. cap. 1. pag. 881).

Finally, Cornelius Tacitus uses fewer words where he writes of the emperor Nero: “A large [Col. 1073D] part of Pompeii, a celebrated town of Campania, collapsed in an earthquake” (Tacit., lib. XV *Annal.* long. post init.). But these accounts do not entirely serve Tertullian’s purpose, for he asserts that the destruction of this city occurred not by an earthquake but by fire, and not during Nero’s reign but before the time of Christ. Therefore, if anyone demonstrates that he was speaking not of the town of Pompeii but of the Tarpeian citadel, destroyed by heavenly fire, we shall not protest greatly. But observe, I ask you, that the city of Pompeii was situated upon another mountain than Vesuvius. Yet if it was at some time burned by flames erupting from that Mount Vesuvius, before the Christian religion was proclaimed, could this not have been noted by our Tertullian as an extraordinary event?

[Col. 1074A] There is less difficulty concerning Vulsinii, or Volsinii, whose people he elsewhere called the Volsinians (Tertullian. *Apologet.* c. 24, p. 421), as we shall say below (Infr. cap. 27. art. 2). For Juvenal sings of them:

Who fears, or ever feared, the fall of chilly Praeneste,

Or of Volsinii, set among wooded ridges?

(Juvenal., satir. 3, v. 181.)

Their town is called Οὐλσίνιον (Volsinium) by Strabo. Pliny records in precisely as many words that it was overwhelmed by fire, as Tertullian says: “Volsinii, the most renowned town of the Etruscans, was burned by lightning” (Plin., lib. II natur. Hist., cap. 52. pag. 207). He is silent there, however, as to when this occurred; but nearly everyone is persuaded that both authors must be understood to refer to the same fire, which occurred before the rise of the Christians.

[Col. 1074B] Tertullian presses the matter still further and, by another example very well known to all, challenges the gentiles as follows: “No one at Rome yet worshiped the true God when Hannibal, at Cannae, measured his slaughter by the bushel through the rings of the Romans” (Tertull. *Apologet.*, c. 40, p. 485). For who does not know of the battle of Cannae, after which the victorious Hannibal, as Livy relates, ordered the gold rings to be poured out in the vestibule of the senate-house? Their heap was so great that some authors say that, when measured, they filled three and a half bushels. The report nearer the truth maintained that there was no more than one bushel (Liv., lib. XXIII, § 12). But even if there had been only one bushel, it undoubtedly suffices for these rings to have been measured by a bushel, as our Author says. Pliny, however, writes that three bushels of rings were sent by Hannibal to Carthage (Plin., lib. XXXIII natur. Hist., cap. [Col. 1074C] 1. pag. 12), and Augustine says the same (August., lib. III de Civit. cap. 19).

But here we cannot remain silent about the fact that, in Rigault’s edition, contrary to the authority of our manuscripts and of the old editors, *per* was omitted from Tertullian’s text and *caede sua* was written. As to his reason, Rigault was entirely silent. For what reason could he have had? Is Tertullian’s style not sufficiently evident there, and is this not his meaning: after his victory Hannibal did not count individually the number of Romans killed in battle, but measured it by a bushel through their rings? Hence Augustine, already commended by us, says:

“He sent three bushels of gold rings to Carthage, so that they might understand that so much Roman rank had fallen in that battle that it was more readily comprehended by measurement than by number; and from this it was thought that the slaughter of the remaining multitude—necessarily more numerous in proportion as it was lower in rank, namely the men who lay without rings—should be estimated rather than counted” [Col. 1074D] (August., loc. cit.).

But do not begrudge the effort of consulting this book of Augustine, where, like our Author, he argues against the gentiles from this and other disasters.

Another Roman disaster was assuredly more ancient, but no less celebrated and well known to all, when, as Tertullian again writes, “the Senones occupied the Capitol” (Tertullian. *Apologet.*, cap. 40), or, as we read in our manuscripts, “had occupied it.” But what, you will say, does he mean when he says, “occupied”? Do not Livy (Liv. lib. V, § 41 et seq.), Augustine (August., lib. II [Col. 1075A] de Civit. cap. 22. pag. 51), and certainly a great many others consistently assert that the Capitol was by no means stormed by the Senones, but only Rome was captured by them? Nothing is more certain. Consequently, the verb “had occupied” is understood there to mean “had kept under siege.” Yet at that time Saturn and the other gods of the gentiles, as Tertullian says, were worshiped by all, both besiegers and besieged. Christians, therefore, who did not then exist, could not have been the authors either of this calamity or of the others that we have related thus far.

## ARTICLE IV

### How clearly Tertullian shows that Christians were not the authors of public calamities, since, when these threatened, they implored the mercy of the true God through the harsh labors of repentance; but that the gentiles were, since, while drunkards and while the baths, taverns, and brothels remained in operation, they sacrificed rain rites to Jupiter, proclaimed barefoot processions to the people, and awaited clouds from paneled ceilings

[Col. 1075B]

When the gentiles asked who, if not the Christians, the most bitter enemies of the gods, could be the cause of public disasters, Tertullian replies that responsibility for them must be cast upon the gentiles themselves alone (Tertul. *Apologet.* c. 40, p. 487). For, he says: “The human race has always deserved ill of God,” the true and only God, for two reasons. First, “as neglectful of him whom, although it understood him in part, it did not seek” (Ibid.); that is, although by the light of nature alone it had at least partially recognized that he truly existed and that there could be no other God, it did not trouble to investigate further who he was, and consequently did not render to him the duties and honors that were his due [Col. 1075C].

Second, that same human race also invented other gods for itself to worship. Therefore, by no longer “seeking the master of innocence” (Ibid.) and the avenger of crimes, but abandoning him, it plunged headlong into idolatry and every kind of wickedness. Thus the gentiles found God—whom they could have had as their gracious protector by acknowledging him and worshiping him as was fitting—angered by their crimes. It was not the Christians, therefore, who always worshiped the true God with due service and pious works, who were the cause of public calamities, but the ungrateful race of the gentiles themselves, which spurned him and most grievously offended him through idolatry and other crimes. Tertullian reinforces this entire argument by what Christians and gentiles respectively did when a disaster had arisen or was imminent [Col. 1075D]. For when, as he says, “the summer and winter seasons suspend their fruitful rains,” as we said a little above (Ibid.), and the rains especially desired; and “the year is a cause for anxiety,” whether sterility is feared for it—or, as he himself says elsewhere, “when the sky is stunned and the year grows dry” (Tertull., *Apolog.* loc. cit. et lib. de Jej., c. 16)—then Christians, by fasts and the other most severe labors of repentance which we discussed above (Supr., cap. 14. art. 1 et 2. cap. 15. art. 1), wrested an answer from God’s infinite mercy by a violence pleasing to him. This indeed brought it about, as he rightly and deservedly observes (Tertullian. *Apologet.* cap. 40), that from the time when Christians tempered the iniquities of the age by holiness of life and these pious works, the troubles by which people had formerly been afflicted and tormented were much [Col. 1076A] lighter.

By contrast, when some particularly frightening calamity was imminent or had arisen, the gentiles provoked the true God by feasting, drunkenness, fornication, adultery, and the impious invocation of false gods. “For then,” Tertullian says in addressing them, “having eaten every day and about to dine immediately, while the baths, taverns, and brothels remain in operation, you sacrifice rain rites to Jupiter” (Ibid.). These are his exact words in our manuscripts, except that they read *cauponiis*, a term used by the historian Justin, though he added *artibus* (Justin. lib. I). We read the same in the old editions of the same *Apologeticum*, except that they have “having worked in taverns”; but other editions read better, “while the taverns remain in operation,” that is, supplying their service [Col. 1076B]. Rigault, however, citing no manuscript and doubtless acting entirely on his own authority, completely deleted the preceding words: “having eaten every day and about to dine immediately.” Is an editor, then, permitted to correct—or rather corrupt—his author’s text without offering any reason? For Tertullian contrasts the Christians with the gentiles and shows the very different ways in which each group sought to avert an impending public calamity. “Then,” he says, the Christians, “begrimed with sackcloth and ashes,” took no food throughout the entire day. The gentiles, by contrast, did not wait for the hour of dinner, but tasted food beforehand, being about to dine more lavishly in the taverns. Afterward they were refreshed by the pleasure of the baths and wallowed in every kind of fornication in the brothels.

They also provoked the true God by another crime of idolatry [Col. 1076C]. For, having rejected him, they “sacrificed rain rites to Jupiter.” According to Festus, an *aquilicium*, or *aquaelicium*, is so called when rainwater is drawn forth by certain rites, as once occurred—if the account is believed—when the *manalis* stone was led into the City (Fest. cap. 20. de verbor. Signif. ad v. Aqualicium). If you ask what that *manalis* stone was, the same Festus will answer you: “They called *manalis* a certain rock that stood outside the Capene Gate beside the temple of Mars. When, because of excessive drought, they dragged it into the City, rain immediately followed; and because it made waters flow, they called it the *manalis* stone” (Idem. ad. v. Manualis). Thus, during a drought, when they dragged this stone from the temple of Mars, they performed rites for Jupiter—doubtless Jupiter Pluvius, or Pluvialis, who is called ὑέτιος (“Rain-bringing”) by Pausanias (Pausan. lib. II. pag. 61. lin. 44), and ὄμβριος Ζεύς (“Rainy Zeus”) [Col. 1076D] by others. Hence Tibullus sang the following of Egypt:

Because of you, your land asks for no rains,

Nor does its parched grass supplicate Jupiter Pluvius.

(Tibull. lib. I, eleg. 7. v. 25 et 26.)

In addition to this, the gentiles proclaimed barefoot processions to the people during that time of drought. For they had a custom of approaching the temples barefoot on certain festive or solemn days. Ovid celebrates the Vestalia in these verses:

By chance I was returning during the festival of Vesta, by the way

Where the New Way is now joined to the Roman Forum.

I saw a matron descend this way with bare foot.

I was silently astonished and halted my step.

(Ovid., lib. VI *Fastor.* § 9.)

After the body of the deceased emperor Augustus was burned [Col. 1077A] in solemn procession, “the leading men of the equestrian order,” as Suetonius relates, “wearing tunics, ungirded, and barefoot, gathered the remains and deposited them in the mausoleum” (Sueton. lib. II, in Vit. August. § 100). Some have further thought that Juvenal had this rite in view where he sang the following about the Galli, priests of the Mother of the Gods:

. . . Then, naked and trembling, she will burst forth

Across the proud king’s entire field upon bloodied

Knees. . . .

(Juvenal. satir. 6, v. 466.)

Seneca likewise committed this to writing: “When someone shaking a sistrum lies at another’s command, when someone skilled in cutting his own arms makes his forearms and shoulders bleed with poised hand, when some woman crawls along the road on her knees and howls” (Senec., lib. de Vit. beat., cap. 27, [Col. 1077B] pag. 640). But here, like Juvenal, he discusses those who proceeded on their knees rather than those who went barefoot into the temple to pray to their gods. The cited words of Ovid therefore apply more closely to Tertullian’s purpose.

Thus proceeding barefoot, the gentiles “came to the Capitol,” Tertullian says—that is, to the temple of Capitoline Jupiter—and there “sought a rain-bearing sky,” and awaited “from the paneled ceilings” a cloud heavy with fruitful rains: that is, from the paneled ceiling of the very temple in which they wrongly believed their supreme Jupiter dwelt.

Although our Author said there that, during this very time of calamity, no crime of intemperance or lust was omitted by them, he nevertheless testifies [Col. 1077C] in another book that some displayed a certain appearance of repentance, not only going barefoot but also laying aside the insignia of their offices. For in another book he says: “When the sky is stunned and the year grows dry, barefoot processions are proclaimed, the magistrates lay aside their purple, turn the fasces backward, prescribe a prayer, and repeat the sacrifice” (Tertull. lib. de Jejun. cap. 16).

But through these works of idolatry they could plainly obtain nothing either from their gods, who did not exist, or from the true and omnipotent God, who alone is the giver of every good. Who, then, does not now understand how true and certain is the conclusion of our Tertullian when addressing the same gentiles: “You, therefore, are the burdensome ones in human affairs; you are guilty of public misfortunes; you are always the enticers of evils, among whom God is despised [Col. 1077D] and statues are worshiped” (Idem. *Apologet.* cap. 41. pag. 488). Some editions read: “always the summoners.” For in the height of impiety you worship false gods and statues and reject the true God, who deservedly chastises impious and criminal people with public calamities. The gentiles themselves, therefore, truly were the authors of public calamities, and certainly not the Christians, who, worshiping the true God, could assuredly obtain from him what they requested.

## ARTICLE V

### An examination of the reasons given by Tertullian why God permits Christians to be afflicted by public calamities, and why those reasons cannot apply to the gentiles

Overwhelmed by the most grievous weight of so many and such powerful arguments [Col. 1078A], the gentiles, with astonishing obstinacy, were never willing to yield, but endeavored in every possible way to turn against the Christians the weapons that the Christians had hurled at them. If Christians, they said, are not truly the authors of public disasters, why does their God, if he is omnipotent and just, so calmly permit them to be tormented, harmed, and oppressed by the same calamities as other guilty and criminal people?

Tertullian replies that this weapon was being hurled back by the utterly leaden and blunt hand of purblind men who were entirely ignorant of the eternal dispositions of the supreme God (Tertull. *Apologet.* cap. 41). For at the supreme judgment after the end of the world, God will distinguish the good from the wicked and will treat them according to their deserts, with either everlasting punishments or rewards. In the meantime, however, he willed the advantages and disadvantages of this life to be common to all [Col. 1078B], “so that,” Tertullian says, “all might experience, through an equal participation,” either his gentleness or his severity, and might love the former and fear the latter (Ibid.). Public calamities, therefore, served as a reminder of duty to Christians, but as punishment for crimes to the gentiles. Nor could the latter insist that this was wrongly invented by the Christians themselves. “For these things,” he continues, “we have learned from God himself” (Ibid.); that is, from the sacred books of the Old and New Testaments (Matth. cap. V. v. 45, et cap. XIII. v. 49, et cap. XXV. v. 32 et seqq.), whose supreme authority he had demonstrated, as we saw above (Supr. cap. 8. art. 1 et 5).

Second, he replies that Christians were not afflicted by any adversities in this world, but rather rejoiced in them (Tertull. loc. cit.), since they had nothing more pleasing or dearer than to depart from this world so that they might more quickly attain [Col. 1078C] that eternal happiness which they knew with absolute certainty had been promised to them, and to no others, by the most certain divine oracles. The gentiles, however, could never similarly weaken the force of this argument, which applied to them alone. For if their gods oppressed the world with public calamities because of the Christians, their enemies, those gods allowed their own worshipers to be involved in the same disasters only at the cost of ingratitude. “They ought,” as Tertullian rightly says, “to have separated them from what the Christians deserved” (Tertull. ibid.). After presenting the reasons that we have expounded in favor of those same Christians, he addresses the gentiles in these words:

“But if all your evils come upon you from those whom you worship, on our account, why do you persist in worshiping beings so ungrateful and unjust, who ought rather to have assisted and defended you, to the distress of the Christians”—our manuscripts read “in the distress”—“whom they ought to separate from what the Christians deserved?” [Col. 1078D] (Ibid.).

You see that these latter words are repeated twice. Rigault therefore removed them entirely from his edition as having been added out of place, contrary to the authority of all the manuscripts, whether printed or handwritten. But what, I ask you, prevented Tertullian, while arguing against the gentiles, from repeating, after the reasons adduced to prove it, the same words with which he had stated his thesis?

But these matters, someone will say, are not so important as knowing how he could infer that, if the gods of the gentiles possessed any sense of gratitude and justice, they ought to have separated the gentiles [Col. 1079A] from the Christians in public calamities. But he correctly infers this because the gentiles had no certain reason whatever to hope that, after departing from this life, they would receive from their gods—who did not exist—an eternal reward for their virtues. Christians, however, as we have said, most certainly expected that reward from their omnipotent God. The gentiles therefore ought not to have worshiped these gods if they were persuaded that they were being afflicted by them with public calamities because of the Christians. For if those gods could confer nothing after death upon their own worshipers, who in this life had suffered public calamities because of others, they were utterly ungrateful and unjust—or certainly powerless, and consequently altogether devoid of divinity. Tertullian therefore rightly concludes that the worshipers of these gods, and certainly not the Christians, were the authors of public calamities [Col. 1079B]. Elsewhere we have clearly shown (Dissert. in Arnob. cap. 6. art. 4 et seqq.) how this accusation, repeated by the gentiles as often as it was foolishly, was refuted by Arnobius, and how Minucius Felix and Lactantius demonstrate that the arguments of those who attempted thereby to undermine divine providence were utterly feeble and futile (Dissert. in Minut. cap. 6. art. 3, et in Lactan. cap. 14. art. 3). But let us now proceed to the final argument of those same gentiles against the truth of the Christian religion.
