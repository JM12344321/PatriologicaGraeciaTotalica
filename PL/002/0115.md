Mothers and fathers rise again into the restored light,

High-hearted youths, boys, and unmarried maidens,

And aged men who have died stand there with spirits now living;

The world resounds with groaning at the wailing of infants.

Peoples of such varied kinds will come from the lowest regions:

The hosts of the East, those whom the furthest land beholds,

Those who inhabit the middle regions beneath the sloping vault of heaven,

[Col. 1093C] And those who possess the Riphaean heights beneath the frozen constellation.

All are present, each terrified of his end: the tenant farmer,

The rustic, and the Atreus-born king who has laid aside his royal diadem;

The rich man, mingled with the poor, is equal within the throng.

A profound trembling is everywhere; then the world groans with prayers,

And the peoples, stretching out their hands, are stupefied amid their cries.

## CHAPTER VII

The Lord himself sits there, radiant with sublime light,

And, mighty in every power, flashes like fire;

He shines from the heavenly seat upon his lofty throne,

Surrounded by martyrs, by a gleaming multitude of men,

And attended by his chosen prophets,

Whose radiant bodies shine in snow-white robes.

Now the priests too stand there in shining garments,

[Col. 1094A] Wearing bright-red crowns upon their distinguished brows;

And all, humbly upon their knees, worship reverently.

*Hagie, hagie, sancte Deus*: “Holy, holy, holy God”—all have one voice.

Here the Lord will command the twofold people to stand before him;

He orders the wicked to be separated according to their number,

While addressing with gentle words those who followed his commands.

He bids them, clothed in strength now that death has been conquered,

To dwell forever in the never-extinguished air of light;

To pass continually through the ever-flourishing, ancient kingdoms,

Through the promised riches, through the ever-pleasant lawns,

And to live perpetual life in a glorious body.

## CHAPTER VIII

There is a place in the regions of the East beloved by the Lord,

Where the light is clear and radiant and a more wholesome breeze breathes,

Where day is eternal and time unchanging;

[Col. 1094B] It is a region set apart for God, exceedingly rich in plains,

And supremely blessed, situated at the axis of the cloudless realm.

The air there is joyful and destined always to remain in light,

And a gentle, favoring wind breathes life-giving currents.

The earth, with its exceedingly fertile soil, bears all things abundantly;

Flowers breathe fragrance in the meadows, and purple radiance

Blends everything in the fields with a generous, brilliant light.

One flower joyfully clothes another in the mantle of its own brightness;

Here the dewy countryside is variegated with abundant seed,

And snow-white fields ripple with rosy flowers.

Throughout those regions of more blessed air, it cannot be known

Which flower is more beautiful, or which surpasses the others in distinction.

Lilies such as these never grow in flower-bearing gardens,

Nor do such lilies blossom in our fields;

Nor do their blooms grow so red the moment a rose opens to the breeze,

[Col. 1094C] Nor is Tyrian purple so deeply dyed with crimson.

A splendid jewel shines among the colored stones;

[Note: [Col. 1093D] “From here the *prasinus* gleams,” etc. Pliny calls it *prasius*, or, as the Author does, *prasinus*, one of the class of green gemstones, Book XXXVII, chapter 8; but *prasinus* signifies the color green. On the carbuncle and the emerald, see above, Book II, *Against Marcion*, chapter 10, no. 64.]

From here the *prasinus* gleams; from there the carbuncle blazes,

And the great emerald grows green with grassy light.

Here too cinnamon grows upon fragrant shoots,

[Note: [Col. 1094D] “Here too cinnamon grows upon fragrant shoots, and luxuriant amomum blazes amid thick foliage,” etc. Lactantius, in his poem *On the Phoenix*, seems to have imitated the Author not only in the rest of this chapter, but especially in this passage, where he wrote:

> Then cinnamon, and the fragrance of amomum breathing from afar,  
> He gathers. . . .

On cinnamon, or cinnamomum, see Pliny, Book XII, chapter 19; on amomum, chapter 13 of the same book. We have emended “the flourishing branches,” etc., in place of “the living branches,” just as, several lines later:

> And Crete’s medicinal gift gives off a lovely fragrance,

in place of “the lovely things give off fragrance”; here he alludes to the various kinds of Cretan herbs whose use in medicine is especially important, since no venomous animal is born on that island.]

And luxuriant amomum blazes amid thick foliage.

Here lies the uncreated gold of radiant light,

And lofty groves occupy the sky in the season of flowering,

While exceedingly abundant produce weighs down the flourishing branches.

India has not spread out groves comparable to these;

The pine does not raise such dense crowns upon the mountain,

Nor does the tall-tressed cypress have shade so richly curled;

Nor does any branch blossom better when springtime comes.

Here black fir trees flourish upon their lofty summits,

[Col. 1095A] And these forests alone are eternally green and free from hail.

No leaves fall, nor is any flower absent at any season.

A crimson flower blooms there too, like the purple of Tarsus.

This flower, I believe, is the rose: both its redness and its fragrance are keen;

Such beauty it displays in its petals, such perfume it breathes.

A tree stands there, most beautiful with new blossom and fruit,

And the blessed trees grow thick with life-giving produce.

Rich honey blazes within the green cane,

And drinkable milk flows in full streams.

Whatever grows green in that holy earth breathes life,

And Crete’s medicinal gift gives off a lovely fragrance.

A spring there flows in a gentle course through peaceful fields;

From it four rivers irrigate the divided lands.

The garden, I think, is always in spring, clothed with flowers;

No cold from a wintry constellation disturbs its breeze,

[Col. 1095B] And it refreshes the fertile earth with a better breath.

There is no night there; the stars keep their darkness away.

Anger and treachery are absent, as is dreadful desire;

Fear is shut out, and cares are driven from the threshold.

Here the wicked man, an exile, has departed to the regions he deserves;

He is never permitted to touch the forbidden groves.

There ancient faith rests in its chosen abode;

Life stands rejoicing in an eternal covenant,

And secure salvation exults in the tranquil fields,

Destined always to live and always to remain in light.

Here dwells every devout person, every cultivator of justice and goodness,

Everyone who with a sincere mind feared God who thunders,

Who honored his parents with sacred devotion,

And lived the remainder of his life always without crime;

Or who comforted a needy friend with faithful resources,

[Col. 1095C] And brought aid to one who was poor and grievously afflicted;

The helper of the poor and protector of orphans,

Who rescued the innocent and assisted those oppressed by accusation;

Who gave guests the precious bounty of his generous table,

Conducted everything in a godly manner and performed the prescribed acts of devotion;

Who harmed no one and never coveted what belonged to another.

All, exulting there in divine praises

And exhorting one another, raise their voices to the stars;

Singing psalms, they celebrate thanksgiving to the Lord amid their joys,

And will travel the guiltless road with messengers accompanying them.

## CHAPTER IX

After the Lord has bestowed these blessed gifts

And likewise sent the righteous into the eternal kingdoms,

Then comes the pitiable multitude, mourning its crimes.

They all pour forth groans with tears now dried up,

[Col. 1095D] And bear witness to their deeds with repeated howlings;

They tremble at the flames and the standing weapons appointed for their deserts.

The angelic host rebukes them and forbids them to pray,

And forbids them to pour forth belated cries by shouting.

With pardon withdrawn, they are cast into the lowest depth.

O wretched people, how often was the divine majesty made known to you!

You heard the peals; you saw the lightning of heaven;

You experienced continual rains, the fury of winds and hail!

How often did the serene periods of night and day

Make the seasons fruitful with the beautiful gifts of God;

The roses bloomed in spring, summer did not lack its harvest,

Autumn poured forth soft fruits of many kinds,

[Col. 1096A] And harsh winter, though it was, shattered the ice-bound olive trees!

God bestowed everything, and his goodness did not fail.

The earth trembled before God; the sea hung upon his voice;

The frightened river recoiled, leaving its sands dry;

And every created thing throughout creation acknowledges God.

You wretched people denied the Lord of heaven and earth,

And often—horrible to tell—drove away God’s heralds;

Indeed, you killed the righteous with cruel slaughter,

And deceit always clung to you after crime.

You will therefore reap the fruit of your wicked sowing.

You know that God exists, and yet you are accustomed to mock him.

You will pass into the deep darkness of fire and sulfur,

Destined to suffer blazing wrath amid just torments—

[Note: [Col. 1095D] Perhaps *justis*, “just,” would be better.]

God commands your bones to descend to eternal punishment,

To pass beneath the blaze of raging Gehenna without end,

[Col. 1096B] To be driven as a mass through the swirling pools of flame.

He commands the elements to be transformed into a threatening fire,

And all the fire of heaven to descend from the clouds.

## CHAPTER X

Then ravenous Tartarus is enclosed in swiftly spreading fire,

And within it the flame surges in stormy waves,

While the whole earth, overturning, mingles the embers together.

The furrow is fiery, sharp teeth turn upon them,

And for all the years a fiery torrent will be armed against them.

The conflagrations roar against the world with Tartarean violence—

[Note: [Col. 1096D] Should it not rather be *impete*, “with violence”?]

There they are scorched in the heat as they rush headlong;

From here they flee, and from there they are driven back in swift course.

The rage of the savage flame confronts those who flee,

And now at last they recognize the punishment due for their crime.

[Col. 1096C] Now there is the wandering land possessed by no inhabitant,

Hemmed in at the furthest extremity of the sea and beneath the profound boundary,

Where the sun, its ray having completed its course, divides the world,

And where the stars are hidden away after traversing the heavens.

The upper air swells, its light blocked by black darkness;

At the furthest boundary, fiery streams stand pooled in hidden channels.

The region’s nature is fiery for those marked out for punishment,

And its marsh, glowing with infernal heat, is dreadful.

Punitive crime thunders within its horrifying furnaces;

The flame seethes and is swept from the broad whirlpool,

Rushing into the instruments of torture, while amid savage billow and abyss

Turbid sand is mingled with the muddy bottom.

Here the captive multitude of the wicked will be sent, groaning—

Sin destined to burn, the host of the criminal body.

Great lamentation is there, grief accompanied by bellowing,

[Col. 1096D] Frantic gnashing, and then the howling of those in pain.

Flames will rise far away, and limbs will resound;

The fire will blaze up, and groaning will be borne into the air.

Then the human progeny guilty of evil hangs in torment

And will begin to stretch its palms toward heaven.

Then it will wish to know the Lord whom it had not wished

To know before, when there was a season in which knowing him would have profited them.

There each person will confess the savagery of his life,

His unjust hands and the crimes of his cruel mind,

And he will shudder at the impious deeds he set in motion during his life.

First of all, everyone who previously thought wrongly of God,

And worshiped wandering stones under the name of a fabricated deity,

[Col. 1097A] Consecrated altars with blood-bearing images,

Feared painted figures that had no voice,

Revered the insubstantial shadows of a false divinity,

Or was immediately led astray by any wicked error;

Anyone who was an adulterer or had killed his children with the sword;

Who raged as a robber, or by deceit put off his dependents,

Or showed too little kindness in his disposition;

Whoever stained his hands with human blood,

Or secretly mixed poison for another’s death;

Or clothed his heart in the false dye of criminality,

Rejoiced in another’s misfortune and in unjust gain;

Everyone who committed any wickedness belonging to the catalogue of evils—

A vast heat and bitter fire will torture him.

All will pay the penalty in an exceedingly bitter death.

Thus a very great multitude of mourning human beings will lie there.

## CHAPTER XI

[Col. 1097B]

How often did the holy Prophets once foretell this,

And, repeatedly admonished by the breath of God, declare what was to come!

No one—alas for our misery!—no one offered his ears.

But almighty God wished his rewards to be known,

As well as the threats contained in the law through many such events.

He determined this by sending many who proclaimed

Divine words; he orders them, awakened, to come forth from their tombs

With him, when he himself had risen after breaking open his tomb.

Many indeed were astounded that the tombs had opened

And that bodies long dead could be seen in the clear light.

Marveling at their devout discourses and gracious sayings,

They stretch their palms toward the stars at the sound of that very voice,

And give thanks to God and to Christ the victor for such things.

It is certain that they did not then return to the silent tombs

[Col. 1097C] Or remain any longer enclosed within the earth’s womb;

But the remaining multitude now rests in the lowest sleeping-places

[Col. 1098A] Until, when the appointed time is complete, that great day

Arrives. All of you, acknowledge now the true Lord,

[Note: [Col. 1097C] “All of you, acknowledge now the true Lord.”—We correct *sua cernere luce*, “to behold in his light,” in place of *suam cernere lucem*, “to behold his light.” In his customary manner, the Author uses *quam* in the sense of *antequam*, “before,” when he adds:

> . . . before the end of the age  
> Overwhelms the unwary and that final day closes  
> Unknowing eyes;

[Col. 1098C] in what follows there, we emend *mortis quia lege tenetur*, “because he is held by the law of death,” in place of *qui*. We begin a new period at “What opportunity remains, keep watch over your precious salvation,” although the word *pretium*, “opportunity/value,” is corrupt unless it is understood in place of *precum*, “of prayers.”]

Who alone enables this soul to behold in his light,

And who can likewise subject it to punishments in Tartarus,

And to whom all power over life and death lies open.

God is able—for willing is sufficient for him—and by speaking he fulfills

The deed, and absolutely nothing can hinder him by standing in his way.

He alone is mine, in whom I believe with my inmost senses.

But since every course is concluded by death,

Let whoever exists today consider everything within his own mind.

And therefore, while life remains, while it is possible to behold the light,

While it is possible to change one’s life—before the end of the age

Overwhelms the unwary and that final day closes

Unknowing eyes, because each person is held by the law of death—

What opportunity remains, keep watch over your precious salvation,

[Col. 1098B] And run the good race readily and with assurance.

Wash away your committed sins by devout rites,

And shun the all-too-restless storms of the world.

Then enter the straight paths and hold fast to holy things.

From this point onward, drive wicked crime away with joyful hearts;

Let guilt long congealed within the breast be washed clean,

And blot out the abominable stains of the evil you have committed.

Appease the fearsome God with unceasing prayers.

Let all the worst mortal things yield to the good things that live,

And now preserve a new life without crime.

Let your minds begin to grow accustomed to things good and true,

And readily offer your voices in divine praises.

Thus all better things will glow brightly for you who are devout;

Thus too you will receive the gifts of heavenly life,

And with God you will live forever through the long age,

[Col. 1098C] Beholding the golden joys of the starry kingdom.

# GENESIS, BY AN UNCERTAIN AUTHOR  
*(C, G, S)\**

[Col. 1097D]

In the beginning the Lord created heaven and earth.

[Note: [Col. 1097D] *Locavit*, “established,” in the Victorinus manuscript.]

For the earth was formless and hidden beneath the flood,

And boundless God moved over the vast waters,

While chaos and black darkness obscured everything.

When he commanded these to be separated from their axis, he said:

“Let there be light,” and everything shone in a radiant world.

[Note: [Col. 1097D] “Radiant”: so Pamelius after Fabricius. Morel and the Victorinus manuscript read *clari*, “of the radiant world.”]

When the Lord had completed the works of the first day,

He established the bright vault amid the newborn clouds.

The boundless sea receives the shores swept by its waters,

[Note: [Col. 1098D] “Wandering,” Baluze from the Victorinus manuscript.]

Drawing into itself the manifold rivers with powerful currents.

The golden light of the third day uncovered the face of the lands;

The dry ground was soon designated by the name “earth.”

Flowering shoots rose upon the windswept fields,

[Col. 1098D] And fruit-bearing branches together bent down their arms.

The fourth day brought forth the moon together with the lamp of the sun

And fashioned the stars radiant with trembling light.

He gave these elements as signs to the world placed beneath them,

That through their varied risings they might indicate the seasons as they changed.

On the fifth day, however, the flowing rivers received fish,

And birds of many kinds spread their wings in the air.

[Note: [Col. 1098D] “Their bodily wings,” Morel and the Victorinus manuscript.]

The sixth day sent ice-cold serpents gliding into coils

And scattered whole herds of quadrupeds across the fields;

He commanded them all everywhere to increase through manifold

Generation, to wander over the boundless lands, and to graze.

When divine power had established these things by command,

Seeing that worldly affairs lacked a ruler,

[Col. 1099A] He declared: “Let us make man exactly according to our

Countenance, like ourselves, that he may rule throughout the whole world.”

[Note: [Col. 1099D] “In our countenance,” Morel and the Victorinus manuscript.]

And though he could have fashioned him with a single word,

He himself nevertheless deigned to shape him with his holy right hand,

And from his divine breast breathed spirit into the insensible breast.

After he saw him fashioned in a form as though it were his own—

[Note: [Col. 1099D] “Fashioned in the image of the Craftsman,” the Oxford editor and Baluze.]

He judges it improper for him to endure corrosive cares alone.

[Note: [Col. 1099D] Baluze conjectures *non patitur*, “he does not permit,” in place of *metitur*.]

At once he lulls his eyes with a moistening sleep,

So that a woman might be formed more gently from the extracted rib

And one substance might strengthen their limbs through their twofold union.

She is also given the name of life, which is called Eve.

For this reason, children customarily leave their parents

And cleave to their spouses once households have been established.

Then, on the seventh day, God rested at the completion of his works,

Establishing as sacred the joys of the age to come.

[Note: [Col. 1099D] “Consecrated for the joys of the age to come,” the Victorinus manuscript and Baluze.]

[Col. 1099B] At once, when the throngs of living creatures had been presented in order,

He assigned to every individual the name that remains with it,

Adam being skilled through the knowledge bestowed by the Lord.

[Note: [Col. 1100D] Morel and Pamelius read “wisdom.” The Victorinus manuscript reads: “Adam, through the wisdom bestowed upon him.”]

God deigns to address him, and Eve joined to him as well:

“Increase by manifold offspring through the times to come,

So that both heaven’s vault and the full earth may be filled with your progeny;

And, as my heirs, pluck the various fruits

Which the groves and fields produce from the rich turf.”

When he had explained these things, Paradise was arrayed as a joyful court

And beheld the light of the sun for the first time.

Among these things grew a tree bearing death-dealing fruit,

Producing the conjoined taste of life and death.

Through the middle of the dwelling a river flowed in a pure course,

Watering the distinguished gardens with its liquid waves

And dividing into four channels from its surging source.

[Col. 1099C] The Phison, exceedingly rich in gold, flows with golden waters

And polishes conspicuous gemstones in its roaring flood.

One is called the *prasinus*; another is the blazing carbuncle.

Clear in its shallows, the river grazes the land of Evilat.

After it, Gaeon glides by and enriches the Ethiopians.

The third is the Tigris, joined to the pleasant Euphrates,

Furrowing Assyria separately with its swift stream.

Here Adam was placed as guardian and craftsman with his faithful wife,

And was instructed by the Thunderer in these words:

“Do not fear to pluck together the permitted fruits

Which the uncultivated grove has produced upon its leafy branches.

Take care not by chance to gather the harmful evil

That grows green from the twofold sap separated according to its functions.”

Meanwhile, deep night held their eyes no less blind,

And no garment yet covered their newly fashioned limbs.

Amid these abodes and gardens of mellow berries,

[Col. 1099D] Foaming and surpassing the animals in cunning intelligence,

A serpent crawled silently on its cold coils,

Turning livid lies over in its biting mind.

It assailed the tender heart within the woman’s breast:

“Tell me, why do you fear the blessed produce of the tree?

Surely God consecrated all the familiar fruits, did he not?

If you should desire to pluck sweet nourishment from them,

A golden world will return from the star-bearing pole.”

[Col. 1100A] She refuses and fears to touch the forbidden branches;

But nevertheless her heart is overcome by weak judgment.

At once, when she bit the now-ripened fruit with her snow-white teeth,

The clear sky, stained by no cloud, shone upon her.

Then that taste, planting allurement within her honeyed throat,

Compelled her to carry the gift to her husband, who was unaccustomed to it.

As soon as he took it, with night wiped away, their bright,

Clear eyes flashed in the shining world.

Therefore, when each looked upon the other’s naked body

And they saw their shameful parts, they shaded them with fig leaves.

It happened that, when the Lord’s sun was already declining toward its setting,

They recognized his sound and fled trembling toward out-of-the-way places.

Then the Lord of heaven addressed sorrowful Adam:

“Tell me, where are you dwelling now?” He answered him as a suppliant:

“O great Lord, I tremble in my fearful heart

At your words; naked, I am afflicted by chilling fear.”

[Col. 1100B] Then the Lord said: “Who gave you the harmful fruit?”

“This woman handed it to me, saying that her eyes had suddenly

Been flooded with shining daylight, that a limpid radiance

Had shone upon her, together with the sun and the stars of heaven.”

Immediately God’s wrath terrified the troubled Eve,

As the Most High sought the author of the forbidden deed.

Thereupon she revealed the following: “I accepted the enticements

Of the speaking serpent, through deceitful fraud and flattering entreaty;

For, weaving his words together with vipers’ venom,

He told me that these fruits were sweeter than all others.”

At once the Almighty condemned the dragon’s deeds,

Commanding that the hateful monster be a spectacle to all,

That it should henceforth crawl with its breast spread upon the ground and bite

Only the earth, while war remained for every future age

[Col. 1100C] Between human understanding and the serpent itself as it glided;

That, with its head cast down, it should crawl prone behind men’s feet,

So that man might tread on it whenever it glided close to attack.

The woman, wretchedly deceived by fraudulent speech,

Is commanded to bear children through harsh suffering

And to endure servitude in devotion to her husband.

“But you, to whom the judgment of your wife appeared true—

She who yielded, overcome by the merciless dragon—

Will lament wretched toil through long ages.

For in place of a crop of wheat, there will rise for you

The thistle and the *paliurus*, thick with sharp thorns,

So that, with weary entrails and a sorrowful heart,

The anxious search for nourishment may cause you many sighs,

Until, at the setting hour of approaching death,

You are returned to the recumbent earth from which you bear your body.”

[Col. 1100D] These things done, the Lord gave the trembling pair the hardships of life

And, casting them down, expelled them far from the consecrated gardens.

He placed them facing away and barred them out with fire in their midst,

In which a cherub whirls with exceedingly swift heat,

While its burning point boils fiercely and rolls forth flames.

Lest their limbs stiffen in sluggish cold,

The Lord sewed for them skins torn from the bodies of livestock,

Covering their naked limbs with warm garments.

Therefore, when faithful Adam now perceived himself

[Col. 1101A] To be a husband through their marital union, he bestowed upon his wife

The name of beloved mother. Soon made the father

Of two offspring, he named them with differing designations:

One had the name Cain, and Abel was joined to him.

[Note: [Col. 1101B] “When two offspring had been begotten,” the Victorinus manuscript.]

The latter’s care was to tend harmless sheep,

But the other turned the earth with a curved plow.

When these continually brought their gifts to the Thunderer,

They gave dissimilar fruits according to the prompting of their minds.

The former offered what had been sown in the fertile clods;

But the other devoted himself through a gentle lamb,

Bearing unblemished entrails in his hand and snow-white fat.

At once he found favor with the Lord, who regarded his devout vows.

Because of this Cain grew white-hot with chilling anger.

God deigned to address him and spoke as follows:

“Tell me: if you live uprightly and perceive what is harmful,

[Col. 1101B] Can you not remain pure by refraining from crime?

[Col. 1102A] Cease destroying your brother with malicious intent;

He will bow his neck to you, as to a lord.”

Yet, not broken even by these words, he led his brother into the fields,

And when he encountered him in the deserted grass,

He crushed his innocent throat, breaking it with both hands.

[Note: [Col. 1101B] Morel reads *fraudant*, “they defraud.”]

The Lord, observing this deed from high heaven,

Asked where upon earth Abel was dwelling.

Cain denied that he had been appointed his brother’s keeper.

God said to him: “Does not the voice of his blood,

Sent to me, resound and ascend to heaven’s lofty vault?

[Note: [Col. 1102B] Morel reads *Nonne*, “Does not?”]

Know, then, what will remain for you because of so great a crime:

For the earth, now soaked with your brother’s blood,

Accursed and hateful to you, will reject the seeds entrusted to it

And will produce no crop from the plants it takes up,

So that you will be sluggish, your limbs striking together with violent trembling.”

# SODOM, BY AN UNCERTAIN AUTHOR  
*(C, G, S)*

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 1101B]

Almighty God had already destroyed the period of the primeval age

With the avenging Flood and all its waters,

Which heaven poured upon the earth and the surface of the sea spewed forth.

Thereafter he pledged not to decree punishment through watery

Destruction whenever the atmosphere brought on winter storms;

To restrain the rains, he had appointed the many-colored bow

That marks the clouds with a purple and green border.

## CHAPTER II

[Col. 1101C]

But the renewed impiety of human beings, together with their second race,

Sprang up again as a new age of evil,

Now allotted not rains but fires for its destruction.

Thus the land of Sodom deserved to be burned with fiery

Showers and to foreshadow the signs of the future end.

[Note: [Col. 1101D] Fabricius and Pamelius read “flames”; Cauchius, “bundles”; the Victorinus manuscript, “fountains”; Morel, “torches”; but Baluze reads “showers” from the Colbertinus manuscript.]

There unbridled lust, the enemy of modesty,

Was as good as law, though a forewarned guest would flee from it,

Choosing sooner to seek death either among the Scythians or at the altars of Busiris,

To be slaughtered in sacrifice and to pour forth—here the transmitted wording “the Bebrycian cross” is corrupt—

And to satisfy the Libyan wrestling grounds;

Or sooner to assume new forms through Circe’s potions

Than to lose his violated sex among the Sodomites.

They assailed heaven with odiousness: unions mingled

Incestuously with like, the nature of the race rebelling

[Col. 1101D] Against itself, and the name of man violated together with his body.

But at the appointed time God, having surveyed everything,

Patiently judges the unjust when the age of their crime

Has not been compelled by any force to grant their wrath a respite.

Now the avenging day was at hand. Two youths in form, sent

From the angelic host—though both were spirits—

[Note: [Col. 1101D] Two manuscript codices read “two in angelic form.”]

Carried the divine commands in the Lord’s service

[Col. 1102B] And entered Sodom. There was Lot, a foreign settler

Of devout stock, wise and a cultivator of justice.

He alone remembered God; a useful tree is accustomed

To lie hidden in savage forests, as though a guest because of its fruit.

[Note: [Col. 1101D] Others read “savage trees.”]

As he sat then before the gate—the heavenly inhabitants had scarcely approached

The walls—although unaware that they were divine, he voluntarily

Called them over, addressed them, and honored them with a father’s respect.

He invited them into his home when they were preparing to lodge far away.

[Col. 1102C] Prevailing through lengthy entreaties, he satisfied his friends

With the precious fare of a devout table and with upright services lovingly rendered.

Night had given rest; the next light had dispelled the darkness,

And Sodom clamored at the doors, assembled for an obscene act.

[Note: [Col. 1101D] Baluze, from the Colbertinus manuscript, reads “with the law of shameful things”; [Col. 1102D] Victorinus, “to be defiled according to law”; Oxford, “a mass of ropes.” The transmitted line is uncertain.]

Lot pleaded against them: “Young men, do not now let your concerted lust

Inflame you and lead you to defile youth itself.

To what progeny does this indulgence summon you? To what seed, squandered in vain?

No souls enter marriage in that fashion—not those inhabiting the haunts of beasts,

Not those returning to their stalls, nor those gaping beneath the waters,

Nor those that hang near the clouds by the measure of their wings,

Nor those that move across the earth with bodies stretched out.

Every kind has its kind of intercourse; but woman alone is wife

To all, and no one ever has any mother but a woman.

Now, if you deem it right to ravage youthful chastity,

Inside I have twin daughters of marriageable age.

[Col. 1102D] Their virginity swells in flower, already ready for harvest,

A desire worthy of men, which your pleasure may carry off.

As their father, I surrender them and will pay the price of grief for my guests.”

At this the mad crowd said: “Who are you, of what importance, and from where,

That you should lord it over us and establish laws—

A foreign settler correcting the Sodomites?” It redoubled its fury:

[Note: [Col. 1102D] Is *rector*, “ruler,” to be read? Baluze. The Victorinus manuscript reads *conrector*; the Colbertinus manuscript, *non rector*. Shortly afterward Baluze, from Victorinus, and Morel read “so that it might heap up”; Oxford reads “and it heaps up.”]

[Col. 1103A] “Now, therefore, you yourself will pay for what our desire intended

For your daughters and for your guests as well. One man is enough for all.”

Word and deed came together; the raging crowd did not delay.

Just as, when a torrent is driven by winter’s violence,

It is carried with one onset into the courses of all the streams,

[Note: [Col. 1103D] “Into the courses”: so Baluze from the Colbertinus manuscript; others read merely “the courses.”]

[Note: [Col. 1103D] Baluze, from the Victorinus manuscript, reads “streams”; others read “all.” For “is carried,” the Victorinus and Colbertinus manuscripts read “is borne.”]

If any tree there is struck by the swift waters,

It will not remain long. It delays as much as its root permits,

Its leafy crown lingering, until, having lost the soil beneath it through erosion,

It hangs from its undercut trunk

And, uncertain, postpones its certain fall now this way, now that.

No differently did Lot, amid the crowd’s whirling frenzy,

Sway, now almost overcome. But divine power

Came to his aid: the angelic youths snatched him from the threshold

And restored him to the house. The mad crowd there

Was punished with dreadful blindness as the first sign of its penalties.

[Note: [Col. 1103D] Morel and two manuscript codices read, “Meanwhile they punish them with blindness,” except that Victorinus has “their eyes.” Baluze thinks *interdiu*, “during the day,” should be read.]

## CHAPTER III

[Col. 1103B]

Then they revealed God’s decrees: a fitting punishment was about to come

From heaven upon Sodom, while Lot himself had deserved salvation

By right of his justice. “Why, then, do you not prepare

To hurry in flight from this place and likewise lead out whatever

Family you have? We are now bringing destruction upon the city.”

Lot swiftly addressed his sons-in-law, but the hard minds of the men

Disdained to believe the warning and mocked his fear.

Soon, at the time when light attempts to mount above the darkness

And heaven’s face is two-colored by night and day,

The youths pressed to lead the Chaldaean family

Out of Sodom and to grant salvation to the righteous household:

“Come now, Lot, arise and take your wife,

And your twin daughters, and depart from here as an exile.

[Col. 1103C] Escape Sodom before its punishment.” They also led the trembling family

By their kindly hands. “And I give you these further commands:

Preserve your life, Lot. Do not by chance desire to turn

Your gaze backward behind you, or halt your steps once begun.

Hurry to the mountain.” Lot feared that with his slow pace

He could not rescue himself upon the heights before the wrath of heaven

Overwhelmed him. He therefore began to request another refuge:

A small city he had seen opposite him.

“To this place,” he said, “I direct my flight; it scarcely rises within its walls,

[Note: [Col. 1103D] The Colbertinus manuscript reads, “the flight, if indeed by their hands.”]

And is neither distant nor large.” They favored his request

[Note: [Col. 1103D] The Colbertinus and Victorinus manuscripts read “It is for me: favor my plea,” except that Morel reads “show favor.”]

And likewise brought about the salvation of that city itself.

Hence the name Segor for the village: a foreign word made it known.

Then Lot entered Segor; at the same time the sun rose—

That sun was indeed the final one for Sodom—and, blazing, it brought

Conflagration upon the city, for it had armed all its rays with fire.

[Col. 1103D] A rival darkness followed, attempting to enclose the light

And to weave clouds into the confused sky.

Smoking clouds gathered; a new rainstorm burst forth;

[Col. 1104A] The upper air seethed with sulfur and blazing flames,

And the scorched ether crackled with liquid fire.

From this event fiction derives its reputation from truth within falsehood:

The sun’s offspring, they say, desired his father’s chariot,

But the weightless boy was unable to restrain the proud horses

Of fire; the world burned, and then the charioteer was snatched away

By a thunderbolt, while his sisters were transformed through their forbidden mourning.

Let the Eridanus see whether any poplar grows white there,

Or whether any feathered bird, more melodious with age, is found.

Here they mourn the miracle of a form transformed in another way.

For Lot’s wife and companion—alas!—then again a woman

Unable to endure a command, at the divine rumblings from heaven

Alone turned her presumptuous eyes backward, to no avail.

She would not live to tell what she had seen; there and then,

[Col. 1104B] Changed into brittle salt, she stood as her own tomb,

Her own image preserving her form without a body.

[Note: [Col. 1103D] The Colbertinus manuscript reads “preserving it in flesh”; Morel reads “form” in place of “her form.”]

Indeed, she still endures in naked station beneath the open air,

Neither dissolved by decay under the rains nor demolished by the winds.

Moreover, if any visitor mutilates her form,

She immediately restores the wounds from her own substance.

It is also said that, living now in another body, her sex

Discharges the fertile monthly blood in its accustomed manner.

Sodom is nowhere; nowhere do its impious

Walls shine. Every house, bereft of its owners, is thus nowhere.

The whole region is a funeral pyre: here embers with black horror,

And there gray ashes amid decay, mark the conflagration.

That former wildness perished, which Lot, looking upon it—

[Note: [Col. 1104D] “That former *feritas* perished.” This is an obscure verse. If *faritas* is understood in the sense of “fortitude,” the sense will be established in accordance with the poem, so that *feritas* is said to derive from *ferendo*, “enduring.” The word in that sense is now obsolete. PAMELIUS. The Oxford editor believes that the corrupt passage can be emended and completed as follows:

> That former appearance perished; beholding it, Lot  
> Is said to have abandoned dealings with his devout brother.

BALUZE.]

No one plows the pitch-black, sooty clods in vain.

[Col. 1104C] Even if any half-ruined acres there attempt to produce

The joyful wealth of autumn, they readily promise to the eyes

The finest things—pears, peaches, and every kind of apple—

Until they are plucked; for as soon as it is touched, the fruit, betraying itself,

Dissolves into ash and becomes an empty ember.

[Note: [Col. 1104D] Two manuscript codices read “the ash of an empty fruit.”]

Thus, then, with heaven and earth alike buried,

Not even the sea lives there; the stillness of that sea is death.

No living creature stirs its waves in gasping coils.

[Note: [Col. 1104D] The Colbertinus manuscript and Morel read “because it gives life to none.” Fabricius and the Oxford edition read “and the mullet gives it life.”]

It never breathes a sigh from its native south wind

And cannot bring forth from its abyss any race

Covered with scales, smooth skin, or shell,

Whether a curled shell or one closed with a double hinge.

Only bitumen there falsely resembles the sea’s own produce:

The soot of the scorched sea. Lively heat,

Cooking beneath the standing waters, tempers it through the heat

[Col. 1104D] Of sulfur and salt and makes the marine substance adhere to the earth as pitch.

At its own season the rich poison of the burned

Water is carried in, and a skin is woven over the black surface with foam.

[Col. 1105A] Those whose business is to capture this merchandise approach.

They tilt their light skiffs by shifting their weight to the sides,

So as to induce the vein of bitumen, once taken up, to climb aboard.

For, lifting itself of its own accord, it will swim to the edges

Of the cargo boat and repay the generous service with its value—

Unless it touches a cloth worn by a woman during her monthly period.

Behold, another portent floats upon the sea born from the catastrophe:

Everything cast into it floats. Nature’s power assigned

[Col. 1106A] To sinking bodies has withdrawn. Finally, if, as a test,

A light-bearing wick is set upright—the nostril, as it were, by which the flame breathes—

And its tip sails upon the surface, if flame is extinguished by flame,

The shipwrecked destruction of light will pass beneath the waters.

These punishments of Sodom and Gomorrah, marked out for all ages,

Will teach unjust nations, whose hard hearts have abandoned

[Note: [Col. 1106A] Baluze and the Colbertinus manuscript read “hardened hearts”; [Col. 1106B] Morel and the Victorinus manuscript read “hard bodies.” Shortly afterward the Colbertinus manuscript reads “to revere the laws.”]

The fear of God, to observe the laws sent from heaven

And to look to the one Lord of all things.

# TO A SENATOR WHO TURNED FROM THE CHRISTIAN RELIGION TO THE SERVICE OF IDOLS  
*(C, G, S)*

## ARGUMENT OF THE BOOK *TO A SENATOR WHO TURNED FROM THE CHRISTIAN RELIGION TO THE SERVICE OF IDOLS*, BY JACOBUS PAMELIUS

[Col. 1105B] He severely rebukes the senator because, although formerly a Christian and a consul, he did not fear to become a priest of Isis, or of the Mother of the Gods; he therefore exhorts him to come to his senses at least in old age. Moreover, since certain features here too are Tertullianic, I preferred to ascribe the work to Tertullian rather than to the blessed Cyprian.

## CHAPTER I

When I saw you once again serving various vain

Rites and being held by your former error,

I was astounded at the warning; because you have always loved poems,

[Note: [Col. 1105D] “I was astounded at the warning,” etc. This expression is not only Cyprianic—elsewhere he said, “the mind was astounded”—but also Tertullianic, as can be seen above in the book *On the Judgment of the Lord*, chapter II, where one reads:

> Many indeed were astounded that the tombs had opened.

We have, moreover, corrected *monitus*, “at the warning,” in place of *motus*, “moved.”]

[Col. 1105C] I hastened to write verses, answering you in verse,

So that I might rebuke you for preferring darkness to light.

For who could endure your believing that the Great Mother

Can be called a goddess, and again thinking that she should be worshiped,

When vile infamy brands her worshipers?
