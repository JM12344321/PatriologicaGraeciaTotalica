## ARTICLE V. How clearly Tertullian proved the antiquity of the books of Moses from the age of Moses, who was contemporary with Inachus and preceded Danaus by several centuries, as well as the fall of Troy and Homer; with a discussion of Manetho, Berossus, Iromus or Hieronymus, and Josephus

[Col. 0795D]

Certainly no one will deny that the antiquity of the books of Moses can be proved in no clearer way than from the age of Moses himself. Accordingly, relying upon the testimony of ancient writers, Tertullian explains in the following words when Moses lived and whom he preceded: “He was,” he says, “contemporary with Inachus of Argos; by almost four hundred years—for seven years are wanting—he preceded Danaus, himself regarded among you as most ancient; by about a thousand years he preceded the fall of Priam; I might even say, by more than five hundred years, Homer” (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 19). First, therefore, he teaches [Col. 0796A] that Moses was contemporary with Inachus. But in the first volume of our *Apparatus* we showed that this was the common opinion of ancient writers down to Eusebius, an opinion which some have also attempted to defend in the present century (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 385, 531, 836, 909, and 1342). Our Author confidently affirms that he had many guarantors and patrons of his opinion. And lest anyone should think that he asserted this without evidence, he names these very persons individually and by name, as we shall see shortly.

Moreover, supported by the authority of these writers, he asserts that Moses preceded Danaus by 393 years. But consider, I ask, whether Tertullian drew this from Josephus, whom he there cites after the other writers. For after Josephus [Col. 0796B] had recounted the chronology of the Egyptians published by Manetho, he concluded it with these words: [non-Latin text omitted]. “Those who were called the Shepherds, that is, our ancestors, after being delivered from Egypt, inhabited this province three hundred and ninety-three years before Danaus came to Argos, although the Argives regard him as most ancient” (Joseph., Book I *Against Apion*, p. 1041). In copying the text of Theophilus of Antioch, therefore, who cites the same Manetho, a scribe doubtless seems to have erred when he wrote that the Hebrews departed from Egypt [non-Latin text omitted] three hundred and thirteen years [Col. 0796C] before Danaus came to Greece (Theoph., Book III *To Autolycus*, p. 131). For Theophilus, as is evident from what has been said, had doubtless written [non-Latin text omitted], three hundred and ninety-three. But it was easy for a not sufficiently skilled scribe to write [non-Latin text omitted] in place of [non-Latin text omitted]. Nevertheless, we shall await the judgment of others concerning this conjecture. Yet from these words of Theophilus you will readily understand why Tertullian called Danaus most ancient among the pagans.

Then he continues: “Moses,” he says, “preceded the fall of Priam, that is, the destruction of Troy, by approximately one thousand years” (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 19). Here again he follows in the footsteps of Josephus, who asserts that the departure of the Hebrews from Egypt was so ancient [Col. 0796D] [non-Latin text omitted] “that it preceded the Trojan War by nearly one thousand years” (Joseph., Book I *Against Apion*, p. 1042). Both added the adverb “approximately,” and certainly not without reason. For, as we have seen elsewhere, opinions concerning this interval of time vary greatly. Yet whichever opinion may be nearer the truth, it is more certain than certain that Moses was far more ancient than the period of Troy (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 1345, 1347 ff.).

There is still greater disagreement concerning the age of Homer, whom our Author declares to have flourished five hundred years or more after that same fall of Troy, and consequently 1,500 or more years after Moses. These divergent opinions of writers are reviewed by Tatian [Col. 0797A] (Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, pp. 166 ff.), by Eusebius, who reproduced his words (Euseb., Book X, *Preparation for the Gospel*, ch. 11, pp. 492 ff.), and also by Clement of Alexandria (Clem. Alex., Book I, *Stromata*, pp. 326 ff.), as well as by other ancient and more recent authors who, whether expressly or while treating another subject, undertook discussions concerning the ancient writers, or concerning chronologists and poets, and specifically concerning the life or age of Homer himself. We too have discussed these disagreements among authors elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 1278, 1347 ff.).

But at whatever time Homer flourished, it is plainly established, as Josephus correctly argues, that among the Greeks there is no written composition older than the works of this poet (Joseph., Book I *Against Apion*, p. [Col. 0797B] 1034). Indeed, if you believe the same Josephus, Homer’s poems were not committed to writing but recited in song; and since they were afterward collected by others from memory alone, many inconsistencies are found in them. In addition, shortly before this Josephus had said that other nations boasted that they had received the earliest use of letters from the Phoenicians and Cadmus, as we have also noted elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 385 ff.). Yet no one, Josephus continues, will be able to exhibit any writing preserved from that ancient period, whether in temples or in public monuments. The books of Moses, therefore, preceded all the rest by a long interval of time—indeed, of centuries.

Nothing, says our Author, was easier to prove (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 19). But because this [Col. 0797C] would have been a rather lengthy undertaking and could not be done without a very great digression from his purpose, he therefore judged that it should be deferred. Whether by these words he promises another work on the subject, and whether he fulfilled that promise, who would dare confidently to assert? Yet lest anyone say that he had advanced the claim rashly, he openly shows everyone the sources and the writers by whom it can be clearly proved. First, indeed, he cites the most ancient archives or repositories of the Egyptians, Chaldeans, and Phoenicians, in which their writings or books were deposited. He calls these nations by name because, according to Josephus (Joseph., ibid.), the Greeks acknowledged that the most ancient tradition of events was preserved among them. But since this investigation might still have seemed most difficult, “their fellow countrymen,” he says, “must be summoned, through whom [Col. 0797D] knowledge was supplied” (Tertull., ibid.). Properly speaking, *municipes* were those who, having been admitted into Roman citizenship, enjoyed the privileges of Romans. But he uses the name loosely to mean inhabitants of the same region. For he names both the nations and their writers individually: from the Egyptians, Manetho; from the Chaldeans, Berossus—both cited by Josephus, as we shall shortly note; and from the Phoenicians, Iromus, king of Tyre, who must be discussed more fully later. If these were insufficient, he adds their followers: Ptolemy of Mendes, who, according to Clement of Alexandria (Clement of Alexandria, Book I, *Stromata*) and, after him, Eusebius (Euseb., Book X, *Preparation for the Gospel*, ch. 12, p. 497), was a priest and [Col. 0798A] set forth the deeds of the kings of Egypt in three books; likewise Menander of Ephesus, by whom, Josephus (Joseph., Book I *Against Apion*, p. 1042) and Theophilus of Antioch attest, the acts and history of the kings of Tyre were written (Theoph., Book III *To Autolycus*, p. 132). To these he joins Demetrius of Phalerum, likewise cited by Josephus, and three others—King Juba, Apion, and Thallus—whom we have discussed in the first volume of our *Apparatus* and in the preceding dissertations.

Finally: “And the one who,” says Tertullian (Tertull., loc. cit.), “confirms or refutes these writers is the Jew Josephus, the defender of Jewish antiquities,” namely, in the books of the *Jewish Antiquities*, which are still in everyone’s hands. Yet Josephus does not confirm or refute these writers, as our Author expresses it, only in those books [Col. 0798B], but also in the other books, *Against Apion*, which we have already cited (Joseph., Book I *Against Apion*, pp. 1039 ff., 1043 ff.). For in these he reproduced a number of passages from the books of the Egyptians—namely, from Manetho the Egyptian—and of Berossus the Chaldean, from whom came treatises on astronomy and Chaldean philosophy.

There is therefore greater difficulty concerning the third writer, whom Tertullian styles “Iromus the Phoenician, king of Tyre.” For in our manuscript codices and the editions earlier than Rigalt’s, the reading is: “Hieronymus the Phoenician, king of Tyre.” But because it was thought that there had been no king of Tyre named Hieronymus, editors preferred to write *Iromus* in the text of Tertullian. They gave no reason for this correction, however, apart from certain words [Col. 0798C] drawn from the fragments of Menander published by Scaliger. Yet with far better right they should have cited Theophilus of Antioch, by whom not only those same Greek words are quoted (Theoph., Book III *To Autolycus*, pp. 131 ff.), but who also calls him [non-Latin text omitted], “Hieromus, king of the Tyrians.” He attests that this was the man who had written many letters to Solomon, while Solomon had in turn written to him—letters said to have been preserved among the Tyrians. Yet it cannot be inferred from this that the king committed anything concerning the antiquity of the Hebrews to writing.

May it not also be inferred that the name Hieronymus should be read in Tertullian’s text from the fact that Josephus, in the first book of the *Jewish Antiquities*, where he discusses the long lives of the earliest patriarchs, wrote the following after mentioning, just as our Author does, Manetho and Berossus: [Col. 0798D] [non-Latin text omitted]. “Mochus, Hestiaeus, and Hieronymus the Egyptian, who treated the affairs of the Phoenicians, agree with us” (Joseph., Book I, *Antiquities*, ch. 4, p. 11). He placed this Hieronymus among those who had left some written account concerning the longer life of Noah and the earlier patriarchs. Yet in the first book *Against Apion*, he observes that Hieronymus was one of those writers who, in composing histories, made no mention of the Jewish nation (Idem, Book I *Against Apion*, p. 1050): [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0799A] [non-Latin text omitted]. “For Hieronymus makes absolutely no mention of us in his history, although he was brought up almost in those very places.” He therefore admits that Hieronymus passed over the affairs of the Jews in that work, although they were not unknown to him. But there Josephus contrasts him with Hecataeus, by whom, he attests, a history concerning the Jews was published. Hieronymus could therefore, in a history or work on another subject, have introduced some discussion of their lives, just as he did concerning those who lived to a great age.

But although Hieronymus may have related either a few things or many concerning Moses and the Jews in some book, by what reasoning can it be proved that he was king of Tyre? Did Tertullian therefore suffer some human lapse here, or were his words corrupted by copyists, or must he be understood, like [Col. 0799B] Theophilus of Antioch, as referring to the letters of the Tyrian king to Solomon? Consider, therefore, which possibility seems more probable to you, or whether you can discover a better one.

## ARTICLE VI. On the age of the other sacred seers after Moses, finger gestures used for reckoning, and census records to be compared

Concerning the age of our other sacred seers after the death of Moses, Tertullian (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 19) thus pronounces against the pagans: “Although the other prophets are subsequent to Moses, the very last of them are nevertheless found not to be later than the earliest of your wise men, lawgivers, and historians.” Yet there he certainly employs several improper modes of expression (Book *On the Resurrection* [Col. 0799C] of Christ, ch. 45; Book *Against Valentinus*, ch. 35). First, he said *posthumant*, that is, “they lived after the death of Moses”; in this sense he wrote *posthumat* and *posthumatum* elsewhere. Second, those whom he here called *extremissimos*, he elsewhere called *postremissimos omnium*, “the last of all.” Third, he called them *retrosiores*, that is, “later and more recent.” But although he employs these obsolete and barbarous expressions, his meaning here is nevertheless clear: our other prophets indeed lived after the death of Moses, but the last of them all are not later than the Greek wise men, lawgivers, and historians.

Furthermore, he proves his opinion by no argument other than setting forth the names of several authors: either because the gentiles could not dispute or resist their public authority, or because it would have taken too long to reproduce every one of their testimonies in this book, in which he wished brevity to be observed. If anyone nevertheless asks that the point be confirmed by at least some testimony, let him hear Theophilus [Col. 0799D] of Antioch arguing as follows (Theophilus of Antioch, Book III *To Autolycus*, pp. 132 ff.): “Zechariah,” he says, “is reckoned the last of the prophets, and he reached the reign of King Darius. But Solon the Athenian, the most ancient lawgiver, is said to have lived in the reigns of Cyrus and Darius. The other prophets, therefore, are older than the other lawgivers, and Moses much more so, since he flourished before the Cretan Jupiter and, as we have said, before the Trojan War itself.” Nor was Theophilus content merely to have said this; rather, by presenting there a very brief chronology, he endeavors to demonstrate it.

[Col. 0800A] Josephus, however, speaks as follows about the age of the historians (Joseph., Book I *Against Apion*, p. 1034): [non-Latin text omitted]. “Those who attempted to write histories among them”—that is, among the Greeks—“namely Cadmus of Miletus and Acusilaus of Argos, and whoever else is said to have existed after him, preceded the Persian expedition”—that of Cyrus and Darius—“by only a short time” (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 1165 ff., 1355 ff.). We have discussed the wise men of Greece and their age in the first volume of our *Apparatus*. We have treated the period of Moses and the other prophets not only in the same cited passage of our *Apparatus*, [Col. 0800B] but also in the preceding dissertation on the works of Lactantius (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. II, arts. 1 and 2).

Finally, Tertullian informs us that, in order correctly to determine the periods of all those prophets and pagan writers (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 19), one must sit down with many documents—that is, books and chronologies—together with the finger gestures used for reckoning. But what, someone will ask, are those reckoning gestures of the fingers, and to what purpose do they serve? We answer, therefore, that they are nothing other than gestures and movements of the fingers by which the ancients were accustomed to count years or other things. For Juvenal sang concerning the years of the long-lived Nestor (Juvenal, Satire 10, v. 240):

> Fortunate indeed was he who through so many ages  
> Deferred his death, and now counts his years upon his right hand.

[Col. 0800C] Pliny, moreover, relates that a statue of Janus had been made with its fingers arranged in such a manner as to indicate the number of 365 days (Pliny, Book XXXIV, *Natural History*, ch. 7, p. 182). We likewise read in Jerome (Jerome, Book I *Against Jovinian*, p. 146): “The number one hundred is transferred from the left hand to the right, and with the same fingers, indeed, but not with the same hand by which brides and widows are indicated on the left, it forms a circle and displays the crown of virginity.” But long before him, Apuleius, when discussing the age of Pudentilla (Apul., *Apology*, II, after the middle, p. 375), wrote: “If you had said thirty years instead of ten, you could have seemed to err through the gesture used in calculation, by opening the fingers that you ought to have curved into a circle. But when you increase by half those forty years which, more easily than the other numbers, are indicated with an outstretched palm [Col. 0800D], you cannot have erred through the gesture of your fingers—unless perhaps, supposing Pudentilla to be thirty years old, you counted two consuls for each year.” Tertullian therefore signifies that greater leisure and a longer digression from his undertaking would be required in order to calculate by those finger gestures the age of so many writers and prophets.

But besides this and the other difficulties of counting years (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 19), “the census records of the Greeks,” he says, “must also be compared.” No one fails to see that the word “books” or “codices” is to be understood there. He calls those books “census records” in which official acts and annals of events had been recorded by scribes and public notaries, or by others. We, however, call these books [Col. 0801A] diaries, and, by the more common designation, public registers of scribes.

But although Tertullian briefly set forth so many arguments to prove the antiquity of sacred Scripture, whoever nevertheless declares that he does not understand their weight and strength seems to have wished to blind himself. Accordingly, we cannot conclude this entire discussion better than with these words of Tertullian himself to the gentiles in another book (Tertull., *On the Testimony of the Soul*, ch. 5): “But since the divine Scriptures that are in our possession, or in that of the Jews—into whose olive tree we, the wild olive, have been grafted—precede secular literature by no merely slight span of time, as we demonstrated in the appropriate place in establishing their credibility, then, even if the soul borrowed these utterances from writings, it must certainly be believed [Col. 0801B] to have borrowed them from ours, not from yours; because earlier writings are more powerful in instructing the soul than later ones, which themselves had to be instructed by the earlier. Even if we grant that it was instructed from yours, the tradition nevertheless belongs to the original source; and whatever you happened to take and transmit from our writings is entirely ours.” Finally, in the *Apologeticus* he further addresses the same pagans as follows (Idem, *Apologeticus*, ch. 45, p. 499): “You should know that even your own laws, which appear to advance toward innocence, borrowed their form from the divine law, as the more ancient.” And certainly in other dissertations of ours we have shown abundantly that pagan writers drew many things from our sacred books, which they thoroughly corrupted with their perverse errors. But there is no one who cannot readily understand from what has been said how correctly our Tertullian proved [Col. 0801C] their antiquity. Let us now proceed to explain their authority.

## ARTICLE VII. How Tertullian demonstrates that the authority of sacred Scripture is divine, and that the pagans cannot plead ignorance of it

Tertullian derives the supreme authority of sacred Scripture not from its antiquity alone, but especially from the fact that it is divine—that is, committed to writing by men who were inspired by the divine Spirit and who certainly foretold the future (Ibid., ch. 20): “For,” he says, “the truth of prediction is a sufficient testimony of divinity.” No one could deny this, not even the gentiles themselves, who attempted to establish from it that their gods were true gods, [Col. 0801D] because they supposed that future events had been foretold by them. Christians, however, weakened the force of this argument as employed by the gentiles for no other reason than that predictions of this sort, made by fictitious gods, were either false or contrived through the arts and deceptions of demons. For the one true God alone, or prophets filled with his Spirit, can certainly foretell the future. Yet, says our Author, we see that very many things have already come to pass in precisely the manner in which they had been foretold by our sacred seers.

He proves this by a brief enumeration of these predictions (Ibid.). For now, as he says, “lands swallow cities,” just as was predicted by the sacred prophets; that is, cities have been absorbed and swallowed by sudden earthquakes [Col. 0802A] and fissures in the earth (Ibid.). Then: “Seas defraud islands”—islands, namely, which we see engulfed by the waves and storms of the sea (Ibid.). Furthermore: “Foreign and civil wars tear apart; kingdoms dash against kingdoms”; that is, wars split apart not only kingdoms, provinces, and cities, but also citizens, relatives by marriage, and those joined by blood (Ibid.). Moreover: “Famines and pestilences, every local disaster, and the populations of the mountains [an uncertain reading] devastate most things.” The reading in our copies and the old editions is better: “and frequent deaths generally lay waste” (Ibid.). For some strive in vain to defend the word “mountains” by contending that it signifies lions, bears, and other beasts of that kind, by which the mountains are frequented and the fields devastated. For our Author is speaking [Col. 0802B] of human deaths, which occur with the greatest frequency through pestilence, famine, and other disasters of this kind.

He continues: “The functions of the seasons and the duties of the elements stray beyond their proper course” (Ibid.); that is, the customary order of the four seasons of the year and the established order of the elements are thrown into confusion, which he expressed by the improper word “stray beyond their proper course.” Indeed, “The form of nature is disturbed by monsters and portents.” Some wish here to distinguish monsters from portents in such a way that monsters occur in human beings and animals, whereas portents occur in inanimate things. But they have doubtless failed to attend sufficiently to Cicero’s statement that notions of the gods were fashioned contrary to nature from “portents of human beings and cattle” (Cicero, Book II *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 215, line 45). And elsewhere: “Or do those things truly terrify us, if at some time something portentous is said to have been born either from a beast or from a human being [Col. 0802C]?” (Book II *On Divination*, p. 247, line 16). A portent, therefore, is no more said of inanimate things than of human beings and beasts, in which some member is manifestly malformed contrary to nature, or is superfluous, or is lacking. Yet if it should ever be proved to us from Tertullian’s words that a portent really is distinguished from a monster, we shall immediately yield.

Why say more? “The lowly,” he says, “are changed by elevation, and the exalted by humiliation” (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 20, p. 390); that is, the status and condition of human beings are so altered that they either fall from a lofty and supreme station to a lowly and most abject one, or, conversely, rise from the latter to the former. Finally: “Justice grows scarce and iniquity increases; concern for every good discipline grows sluggish”—among the pagans, namely, who hated [Col. 0802D] or despised the Christian religion. For later, as we shall see in the appropriate place, Tertullian himself made it manifest to everyone that justice, piety, holiness, and concern for every good discipline were cultivated and disseminated by Christians. Moreover, no one can deny that the disasters, calamities, and disturbances of affairs which our Author enumerates were truly foretold by our sacred seers, unless he has never read, even cursorily, the books of these men and of other historians or chronologists. As Augustine argues against Faustus the Manichaean: “We demonstrate the credibility of the prophets from those events which they sang would come and which are seen to have come” (August., Book XIII *Against Faustus the Manichaean*, ch. 7, p. 255, vol. VIII). But you may consult Augustine himself, who discusses these matters there [Col. 0803A] at greater length. The disasters of the world which Tertullian said had been predicted by our sacred seers will be treated with him below.

We cannot conceal, however, that future events are foretold in other, far clearer words of sacred Scripture. Such, indeed, are the many predictions concerning the coming Christ, his life, miracles, death, and return to life, which are carefully reported by our Author and by Lactantius, as we have shown in the preceding dissertation (Below, ch. 9, art. 2; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 8, arts. 1 and 2). But our Tertullian himself judged—and not without good reason—that the matters which he now mentions were sufficient to prove the divinity of sacred Scripture, so that there was no need of other examples or proofs.

[Col. 0803B] Nor could it be objected that these Scriptures were concealed by Christians and that pagans did not possess the same opportunity to read and examine them. For although it was a matter of the greatest religious importance to Christians to conceal their sacred mysteries from all others, and even from the catechumens themselves, they nevertheless neither hid nor could hide the books of divine Scripture. Accordingly, Tertullian thus addresses a pagan: “Examine the words of God, our writings, which we neither suppress ourselves, and which many accidents transfer to outsiders” (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 31, p. 446). Does he not by these words, “our writings,” designate in general all the books not only of the Old but also of the New Testament, which he elsewhere called the supplement of the old Instrument (Tertull., Book *Against Hermogenes*, ch. 20)? Christians therefore did not suppress these sacred writings; [Col. 0803C] and although they might have wished to conceal them, they were nevertheless transferred by various accidents to outsiders, that is, pagans.

Moreover, from the time of Philadelphus the books of the Old Testament had been translated, as we have said, from Hebrew into Greek and had come into the hands of those same pagans, who could read them and determine whether they saw the fulfillment of all the things foretold in them. Yet even if the gentiles, through carelessness or obstinacy, had not read these sacred books, Christians nevertheless demonstrated most clearly that the prophecies which we see predicted in them long beforehand were true. Finally, the prophets whose words are recorded in these books most invincibly established and confirmed both their words and [Col. 0803D] their teachings through marvelous powers and miracles, as was observed above. From the matters stated in this and the preceding articles, therefore, Tertullian correctly concludes that the authority of our sacred Scriptures is divine; for this reason he calls them “divine writings” and “holy words” (*Apologeticus*, ch. 39, p. 469), and asserts that the truth of our religion and its teachings is demonstrated with absolute certainty by the credibility and antiquity of these divine writings (Ibid., ch. 46, p. 501). Let us now endeavor carefully to investigate and examine what he transmitted and asserted in the books presently before us.

# CHAPTER VII. On God and His Divine Perfections and Attributes, and on the Number and Nature of the Disagreements and Errors of the Pagan Philosophers Concerning Him

[Col. 0804A]

## ARTICLE I. An examination of the sixfold argument by which Tertullian demonstrates that God exists: namely, from the authority of sacred Scripture, the consensus of pagan philosophers, the works of God himself, the inward sense and conscience of the human mind, the infinite perfections of God, and the public and frequently repeated confession of demons adjured by Christians

Such is the perfection of the Christian religion that, in order to demonstrate its [Col. 0804B] truth and the falsity of every other religion, a mere, simple, and unadorned exposition of its doctrine, as Tertullian correctly says, is sufficient. For nothing occurs in it that does not plainly accord with the natural light common to all human beings and with right reason. Our Author sets forth its principal tenets, and he begins with that which is the foundation of all the rest: God himself, who is worshiped and adored by Christians.

He therefore shows plainly by various arguments that God exists and is one. First, he says, this is the very God whom nearly every individual page of our sacred Scriptures most openly asserts and proclaims to be the one God, with absolutely no other (Tertull., *Apologeticus*, chs. 17 ff., p. 375). But [Col. 0804C] we have already proved that the authority of that same sacred Scripture is divine. What it therefore proclaims so frequently and clearly concerning the one God who actually exists is certainly most true. For writers inspired by the divine Spirit, as we have seen, can neither lie nor ever lead us into any error.

If any pagan should refuse the labor of even briefly reading through some of the books of sacred Scripture, let him at least read their beginning and the first words of Genesis; there he will find most explicitly revealed what Tertullian said, namely, that there is only one God: “Who brought forth from nothing this entire mass, together with all its apparatus of elements, bodies, and spirits, by the word with which he commanded, by the reason with which he ordered it, and by the power by which he was able”—that is, by which he truly possessed the ability to create the world (Ibid.). [Col. 0804D] Minucius Felix, as we observed in another dissertation, borrowed some of these words, but omitted those which appear more obscure: “together with the apparatus of elements, bodies, and spirits” (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 21, art. 5). Yet these words signify nothing other than that the entire world, and all the things of which it is composed—namely, elements, bodies, and spirits—were created by almighty God, whose existence is therefore absolutely necessary.

Moreover, by these words Tertullian alludes not only to what was written at the beginning of Genesis, as we were saying, but also to other passages of sacred Scripture in which the divine seers proclaim that the heavens were established by the word of God and that the entire world was made from nothing [Col. 0805A] by his wisdom and power. But because Hermogenes and certain other heretics and pagans imagined that the world was made not from nothing but from preexistent matter coeternal with God himself, Tertullian therefore radically extirpates this false opinion with the invincible force of arguments in a special book composed against the same Hermogenes. But we have already explained elsewhere several arguments by which Lactantius destroys this error. Our Tertullian therefore correctly argues from this as follows: “There will be doubt, I suppose, about the powers of God, who imposed this vast body of the world, brought forth from what had not existed, no less than from the death of emptiness and nothingness, and animated by the Spirit who animates all souls” (Tertull., Book *Against Hermogenes*, chs. 8 ff.; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 21, art. 1; Tertull., *Apologeticus*, ch. 48, [Col. 0805B] p. 520).

You clearly hear that his argument is this: God created the world not from matter preexistent or coeternal with himself, but from nothing. Yet to create something from nothing—and, what is far greater, the entire world—is an act of supreme and infinite power. Therefore God, the almighty creator of the world, exists. This is Tertullian’s first argument, by which, from the authority of sacred Scripture—which no one, as we have said, not even a pagan, can gainsay—he correctly demonstrates that God really exists.

Before we proceed to the other arguments, however, it is worth observing that in the cited text of Tertullian Rigaltius arbitrarily removed the word *animarum* (“of souls”) [Col. 0805C] as superfluous, and wrote *composuit* (“composed”) in place of *imposuit* (“imposed”). For all the manuscript codices and earlier printed editions plainly oppose this correction, for which he gave no reason. Furthermore, could not our Author have used the verb *imposuit* loosely in order to express more forcefully what he had said before: that the world was created from nothing by God and, as it were, imposed upon the death of emptiness and nothingness? He could also have added the word *animarum* (“of souls”) for a certain emphasis, to signify more clearly that God is truly the animator and creator not only of bodies but also of souls.
