But just as Tertullian’s marriage is established, so it likewise seems beyond doubt that he was ordained to the sacred order of the presbyterate. For Jerome [Col. 0731A] and the author of the book *On the Heresy of the Predestinarians* unhesitatingly call him a presbyter of the Church (Jerome, *Catalog. of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115; *Predestination*, ch. 26, p. 28). Alix therefore subscribed all the more readily to Rigault’s aforementioned opinion concerning the books written by Tertullian to his wife when he was already advanced in age, because he thought it could be inferred from them that Tertullian himself, after becoming a presbyter, continued to live with his wife in the customary marital fashion. But this opinion rests both on an utterly ruinous foundation, as we have noted, and on the false prejudices of the Calvinist heresy. And certainly, the greater the zeal for continence with which Tertullian burned, as we have often said, the less probable it is that, after he had been enrolled in the order of presbyters, he failed to follow in the footsteps of the other priests of the Latin Church [Col. 0731B], who abstained from their customary relations with their wives. For one would find very few—at most two or three—who acted otherwise. No one, however, will ever demonstrate by any reasoning that Tertullian set before himself the examples of these latter men for imitation rather than those of the former.

But perhaps some opponent of sacred orders or other will ask us why Tertullian, if he was a presbyter, at one point counted himself among the laity. For in the book *On Prayer* he says: “We, above all, are men of no particular station” (Tertullian, *On Prayer*, ch. 14). Yet even if this were, as they suppose, the meaning of these words, nothing can be concluded from them against Tertullian’s priesthood. For it is not certain whether, when he wrote this, [Col. 0731C] he was not still among the body of the laity.

Some will doubtless object to us that he abolished every distinction between laity and clergy when he spoke of both in the following manner: “Whence come bishops and the clergy? Are they not taken from among all? . . . But when we exalt ourselves against the clergy, then we are all one; then ‘He has made us all priests to God and the Father.’ When we are challenged to match the discipline of priests, we lay aside our mitres and become unequal.” So indeed he writes in the book *On Monogamy* (idem, *On Monogamy*, ch. 12). Similarly, in another work, *On the Exhortation to Chastity*, he says: “The authority of the Church has established the distinction between the order and the people, and the honor sanctified through the assembly of the order; so much so that, where there is no assembly of the ecclesiastical order, you both offer and baptize, and you are a priest for yourself alone” (idem, *On the Exhortation to Chastity*, ch. 7).

[Col. 0731D] But no one is unaware that, when he wrote these things, he had already imbibed the errors of the Montanists, following which he added in the same place: “But where there are three, there is the Church, even though they are laymen” (*ibid.*). Accordingly, in the manner of those in error, either he was inconsistent with himself, or he understood the word “priest” not in its proper and narrower sense but in an improper and broader one, just as it is used more than once by John in the words to which he appears to allude: “He made us a kingdom and priests to God and His Father” (*Apocalypse*, ch. I, 6). And again later: “You have made us a kingdom and priests to our God” (*ibid.*, ch. V, 10). For Tertullian himself, although he was then a follower of Montanus, speaks thus in another book: “Today there is among us [Col. 0732A] a sister who has received gifts of revelations, which she experiences in the Church during the Lord’s solemn rites through ecstasy in the Spirit. . . . After the rites have been completed and the congregation dismissed, as is her custom, she reports to us what she has seen” (Tertullian, *On the Soul*, ch. 9). Yet there he plainly distinguishes himself from the people, or from the entire body of the laity.

He lived a private and solitary life; and he himself testifies that, having cast aside the common garment of all and assumed the philosophers’ cloak, he withdrew from the turmoil of public affairs. For these are his words, spoken under the fictitious persona of his cloak itself: “I owe nothing to the forum, nothing to the Campus, nothing to the senate-house; I keep watch over no office, I preoccupy no rostra, I attend no praetoria; I do not sniff at the drains; I do not worship the railings, I do not wear down the benches, I do not disturb the laws, I do not declaim lawsuits, I do not judge, I do not [Col. 0732B] serve as a soldier. I have withdrawn from the people. My sole business is with myself; I care for nothing else except not to care” (idem, *On the Pallium*, ch. 5).

His morals, moreover, were as chaste and holy as they were unquestionably harsh and severe. He was not merely a follower of stricter discipline, but also its most ardent defender and advocate, even beyond the established bounds of equity. It seems by no means improbable that, deceived by the false appearance of severity that the Montanists presented, he plunged headlong into their heresy. But while the innate harshness of his character inclined him in that direction, the false visions and predictions of certain women imbued with the poison of that heresy drew him into it. Finally, Jerome says that “through the envy and insults of the clergy of the Roman Church, he fell away to the doctrine of Montanus, and [Col. 0732C] in many places he mentions the New Prophecy, but especially in works against the Church” (Jerome, *Catalog. of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115).

Thus he spent the first part of his life—which was undoubtedly the longer—in the Catholic Church, but the second, by a lamentable misfortune that can never be sufficiently deplored, among the Montanist heretics. For, as Jerome continues: “He remained a presbyter of the Church until middle age” (*ibid.*). In the same place that most learned writer adds that he flourished chiefly under the emperor Severus and Antoninus Caracalla and, as tradition reports, lived to extreme old age. If, however, we place our confidence in the more expert writers of our own time, he was born around the year of Christ 160 and died around the year 245. No one, moreover, will deny that the course of his life extended beyond its midpoint; yet, by spending its [Col. 0732D] final part in defending the absurd errors of heretics, he harmed both himself and a great many others. Hence Jerome, whom we have already commended, rightly says: “In Tertullian we praise his genius; we condemn his heresy” (idem, book III, *Apology Against Rufinus*, p. 463). But we must discuss his genius somewhat more fully.

## ARTICLE V. On Tertullian’s genius and manifold learning; whether he was a jurisconsult and the same person as Tertyllianus; on his style and manner of writing, and the celebrity of his name

In the severity of his morals and manner of life, Tertullian followed [Col. 0733A] the innate impulses of a genius that was indeed exceedingly acute and ardent, but no less harsh and vehement. Jerome therefore calls him “a man of acute and vehement genius” (Jerome, *Catalog. of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115). Yet even if Jerome had passed over this in silence, the harsh and impatient character of his genius shines forth everywhere throughout his writings. What wonder, then, if he was often unable to keep himself within the proper bounds of patience, gentleness, and moderation? Indeed, with a spirit as sincere as it was penitent, he openly confessed that he was not fully endowed with the virtue of patience. For he begins his book on that very subject with these words:

> I confess before the Lord God that it was quite rash of me, if not even shameless, to dare to compose a work on patience, since I am wholly incapable of practicing it, being a man [Col. 0733B] possessed of no good quality. For when men undertake to demonstrate and commend some matter, they ought first to be found administering that matter themselves, and to direct the force of their admonition by the authority of their own conduct, lest their words blush for the failure of their deeds.

(Tertullian, *On Patience*, ch. 1.)

But although he was not always able to temper the more violent impulses of his mind through the virtue of patience, he certainly possessed a genius most aptly suited to the easy attainment of any branch of knowledge. How greatly he abounded in every kind of learning may be inferred from these words of Vincent of Lérins:

> As Origen among the Greeks, so among the Latins this man must readily be judged the foremost of all our writers. For what man was more learned than he? Who was more practiced in divine and human affairs? With a certain marvelous capacity of mind, he embraced all philosophy, every sect of the [Col. 0733C] philosophers, the founders and defenders of those sects, all their disciplines, and the entire variety of histories and branches of study. Did he not excel in a genius so weighty and vehement that he proposed scarcely anything to himself for conquest that he did not either break through by acuteness or crush by force? Moreover, who can adequately recount the praises of his discourse? It is knit together by some such compelling necessity of reasoning that it drives to agreement with itself those whom it has been unable to persuade. Almost as many words as it contains, so many judgments; as many thoughts, so many victories. The Marcions know this, the Apelleses, the Praxeases, the Hermogeneses, the Jews, the Gentiles, the Gnostics, and all the others whose blasphemies he overthrew, like thunderbolts, with the many great masses of his volumes.

(Vincent of Lérins, *Commonitory*, I.)

[Col. 0733D] Yet with such great praises he undoubtedly exalts that universal learning of Tertullian with which, above all, he everywhere sprinkled the books he wrote in defense of the Christian religion and orthodox doctrines. Hence, besides Eusebius and Rufinus, whom we have already cited, and many others, Jerome calls him “a most learned man” (Jerome, *Against Vigilantius*, p. 285, and Letter 83, *To Magnus*, p. 656), than whom there was nothing more learned or acute. Jerome unhesitatingly declares that this brilliance shines forth in the very *Apologeticum* now under discussion, as well as in his other treatises against the Gentiles. It would therefore be an entirely useless labor to prove by the testimonies of distinguished writers of every age—as could readily be done—a fact of which no one is ignorant and which the author’s own writings make most manifest to all. [Col. 0734A]

Yet we cannot pass over a particular kind of learning that Eusebius praised in Tertullian with these words: “Τερτουλλιανὸς τοὺς Ῥωμαίων νόμους ἡκριβωκὼς, ἀνὴρ τά τε ἄλλα ἔνδοξος καὶ τῶν μάλιστα ἐπὶ Ρώμης λαμπρῶν” (Eusebius, book II, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 2, p. 41); that is, in Valesius’s translation: “Tertullian was highly skilled in Roman law and the most celebrated among Latin writers.” Long before Valesius, Rufinus had anticipated him in almost the same manner: “Tertullian,” he says, “was a man highly skilled both in Roman laws and institutions, and very distinguished among our writers” (Rufinus, book II, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 2). But those who think it can be concluded from this that our Tertullian [Col. 0734B] was a jurisconsult by profession, and that he was the very same Tertyllianus who actually taught and interpreted the laws publicly, are far from the truth. For the two are sufficiently distinguished by their own respective names. Moreover, who does not know that someone may be thoroughly versed in Roman jurisprudence and law even though he never professed their study in public schools? Eusebius therefore praises there not Tertullian’s profession, but his complete knowledge and expertise in Roman law.

His expression did not equal his great learning. But if you desire fair and competent judges on this subject, here you have the judgment of Lactantius, who is called the Christian Cicero: “Septimius Tertullianus was skilled in every kind of literature, but in expression he was not very fluent, was less polished, and was exceedingly obscure” (Lactantius, book [Col. 0734C] V, *Divine Institutes*, ch. 1, p. 459). Nor should you say that Lactantius is speaking there of Tertullian’s *Apologeticum* and his other books written against the pagans rather than of his remaining works. For Jerome expresses the same judgment concerning all his compositions in these few words: “Tertullian is rich in maxims, but difficult in expression” (Jerome, Letter 49, *To Paulinus*, p. 567). There is no one who does not immediately perceive the faults noted by both men: his difficult, unadorned, and rather obscure discourse.

Others criticize the uneven variety of the arguments he employs. For, they say, among arguments of the greatest weight and utterly invincible force, he sometimes mingles several others devised by the acuteness of his rapid genius, which are of the slightest [Col. 0734D] importance or plainly of none at all.

But almost everyone admits that this faulty manner either of speaking or of arguing, wherever it occurs, is softened and repaired, as though by some marvelous compensation, by the brilliant gravity of his maxims and the supreme force and weight of his other arguments. Indeed, if you wish to hear again Vincent of Lérins, whom we have already cited and who was by no means an incompetent judge, he says: “His discourse is knit together by some such compelling necessity of reasoning that it drives to agreement with itself those whom it has been unable to persuade. Almost as many words as it contains, so many judgments; as many thoughts, so many victories.” For precisely this reason, as the author of the Eusebian *Chronicle* used to say: “He is celebrated in the discourse of all the Churches” (Eusebius, *Chronicle*, at the year of Christ 208). [Col. 0735A] By “Churches” he means not only those of the West but also those of the East. For some of his books, on account of the great value placed upon them, were translated into Greek, as we noted shortly before, and were read by Greeks no less eagerly than by Latins.

Cyprian, moreover, valued them so highly that he called Tertullian his master and was unwilling to let a single day pass without reading one of his books. As a witness to this exceedingly constant reading, we have Jerome, whose trustworthiness is beyond all question and who relates as follows from whom, and with what certainty, he learned this:

> I myself saw a certain Paulus, an old man of Concordia, a town in Italy, who said that, when he himself was quite young, he had seen at Rome the secretary of the blessed Cyprian, who was already advanced in age. He said that this secretary was accustomed to relate to him that Cyprian never allowed a single day to pass without reading Tertullian [Col. 0735B], and frequently said to him: “Give me the Master,” meaning Tertullian.

(Jerome, *Catalog. of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115.)

Indeed, he plainly asserts that sufficient evidence for this is supplied by Cyprian’s own books. For he wrote to Pammachius and Oceanus in these words: “The blessed Cyprian uses Tertullian as his master, as his writings prove; and although he delights in the genius of that learned and ardent man, he does not follow Montanus and Maximilla with him” (idem, Letter 42, p. 342). Nor is there any doubt that very many others, like Cyprian, repeatedly read through Tertullian’s books. For concerning him we again read the following recorded by Jerome: “He wrote many volumes which, because they are known to many, we omit” (Jerome, *ibid.*). [Col. 0735C] But how could they have been so well known to them unless they had read them again and again?

## CHAPTER II. On the date of the *Apologeticum*; to whom it was addressed and dedicated; whether it was originally inscribed with the author’s name; what its title and subject are; by whom it was divided into chapters and chapter summaries prefixed; with what acclaim it was received; by whom it was translated into Greek; and in what style it was composed

## ARTICLE I. At what time Tertullian published the *Apologeticum*

It is beyond all doubt that Tertullian, as we have already shown, is the true and genuine [Col. 0735D] author of this *Apologeticum*. But there is no general agreement about when he composed it. For George of Amiens thinks that it was published by Tertullian in the first years of the emperor Severus, that is, shortly after the year of Christ 193 (George, *Vindiciae Tertulliani*, part III, § 1, p. 20), when Severus began to govern the Roman Empire. He attempts to confirm his opinion from the fact that he thinks that, where Tertullian speaks of the surviving gleaning of parricides who had brought death upon emperors, he was referring to the killers of Pertinax (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35). But because he saw that the name of Albinus, which is read there in Tertullian’s text, was opposed to this opinion, [Col. 0736A] he declares that it was interpolated and transferred to this passage from the book *To Scapula* (idem, *To Scapula*, ch. 2). Thus he wished to cut through the knot of the difficulty by excising a single word. But by what reasoning, pray? By none at all, and contrary to the authority of all the manuscript codices. Once the foundation of that empty opinion has therefore been overthrown, the opinion itself must necessarily collapse entirely. Indeed, its falsity will become still clearer from what will presently be said.

Scaliger and Alix, however, thought that this book appeared in public only after the death of the same emperor Severus, which occurred in February of the year of Christ 211. Yet they provide no reason for their opinion except that they suppose these words of Tertullian—“Only yesterday Severus, the most steadfast of emperors [Col. 0736B], excluded the Papian laws” (idem, *Apologeticum*, ch. 4)—cannot be understood except of an emperor already dead. Does the adverb “yesterday,” then, indicate that he was dead rather than still living? Moreover, Severus is there adorned with the glorious title “most steadfast emperor.” But how could Tertullian have decorated him with this encomium except before he had aroused, by public edicts, a dreadful persecution against the Christians? Yet he is said to have initiated it near the end of the year of Christ 202. Tertullian must therefore be said to have completed this book before that year, and consequently before the emperor’s death. The same point could be proved no less certainly by these words of his: “No Severus enacted laws against the Christians,” which are read in certain manuscript and printed codices of this *Apologeticum* [Col. 0736C] (*ibid.*, ch. 5). Since, however, other editions and our seven manuscript codices read “No Verus enacted them,” we have judged that these words, which will be discussed again later, should not be pressed beyond what is proper.

Alix nevertheless insists that Tertullian appears to allude to his own book *On the Prescriptions Against Heretics* [Col. 0736D] where, in the *Apologeticum*, he argues against them as follows: “We readily prescribe against our adulterators that the rule of truth is that which comes from Christ and was transmitted through His companions, to whom these various later commentators will be proved to be subsequent” (*ibid.*, ch. 47). But can this argument against innovators and corrupters of Christian doctrine be employed by no one except the author of a book *On the Prescriptions Against Heretics*? Moreover, if he intended there to aim at that book, why does he not indicate it in clearer words, rather than using terms which, as others think, can be understood either of a book still to be composed or of one already written? Finally, the learned do not agree about when Tertullian published this book *On the Prescriptions*. The more expert critics judge that he published it before he had defected from the Catholic Church.

Nor, we think, will anyone be moved by Alix’s further objection that Tertullian appears to indicate an unusual eclipse that occurred in the year of Christ 210 (*ibid.*, ch. 20). For the chapter of the *Apologeticum* that he cites contains no indication or suggestion whatever of any particular and unusual eclipse. [Col. 0737A]

Others therefore think that this book was composed by Tertullian while Severus was ruling the Roman world. For he would not have adorned him with the encomium “most steadfast emperor,” as we said shortly before, unless he were still reigning and had not yet openly raged against the Christians. Moreover, when he later discusses traitors, persons guilty of *lèse-majesté*, and public enemies, he says: “Whence came the Cassii, the Nigri, and the Albini?” (*ibid.*, ch. 35)—men, namely, who had contended with the legitimate emperors for supreme imperial power. But after the emperor Pertinax was killed in May of the year 193, Gaius Pescennius Niger assumed the purple and imperial power in Pannonia, and Cassius Clemens joined his party. [Col. 0737B] Niger, however, was defeated by Severus; and after his forces had been routed in a third battle, he attempted in vain to secure his safety by flight. For he was killed by those who pursued him as he fled, toward the end of 194 or the beginning of 195. After this Severus made war upon Albinus, whom he had deceived with false blandishments so that the number of his enemies would not increase. At last, in a battle fought near Lugdunum, Albinus was broken and routed at the beginning of the year 197 and is said to have taken his own life.

Yet even then the civil war was not entirely ended. Supporters of that faction survived, concerning whom our Tertullian says in the same place: “But even those who are now daily revealed as the associates and applauders of criminal factions are a surviving gleaning after the vintage” (*ibid.*, ch. 38). Aelius Spartianus [Col. 0737C] explains this more clearly in the following manner: “Indeed, many who remained loyal to Albinus after his death were defeated in war by Severus” (Spartianus, *Life of Severus*, p. 323, col. 1). Severus therefore took harsh vengeance for Albinus’s revolt: many were killed, his family was also extinguished, and Severus, angered against the people and the senators, came to Rome. Then, after recounting Severus’s various journeys, Spartianus adds: “Meanwhile, at the instigation of Plautianus, he pursued the remnants of Pescennius’s party, so far as even to prosecute some of his own friends as though they had plotted against his life” (*ibid.*, same page, col. 2). From these latter words some think it may be inferred that Tertullian’s *Apologeticum* appeared not only after the remnants of the traitors whom he discusses had been destroyed, but even after the killing of Plautianus, who was put to death at Caracalla’s command in the year of Christ 204 or 205.

[Col. 0737D] In confirmation of this opinion, the following words of the same Spartianus concerning this emperor could also be adduced: “He also killed many on the charge that they had consulted Chaldaeans or prophets concerning his safety, especially anyone whom he suspected of being suitable for imperial power, since his own sons were still young, and because he either believed or heard that this was being said by those who predicted imperial power for themselves” (*ibid.*). For Tertullian said in the same chapter cited by us: “Those who consult astrologers, haruspices, augurs, and magicians concerning Caesar’s life perform the same services”—namely, at the solemn festivals of the emperors (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35).

It cannot indeed be denied that this argument [Col. 0738A] would carry no little weight if the pagans who desired the imminent death of the emperors had only once consulted magicians and others skilled in the art of divination concerning the safety of the emperor Severus alone. Yet Tertullian clearly indicates that such consultation had been proposed and repeated more than once. Furthermore, if we consider more attentively his words about those same seditious factions—“But even now . . . they are revealed daily”—can it not rightly be inferred that he speaks of them as not yet entirely extinguished, but at most lulled into inactivity and no longer daring to raise their heads? Severus therefore did not yet appear to have entered into open enmity with Plautianus. Yet in the year 204 Plautianus gave his daughter Plautilla in marriage to Caracalla, the son of that same Severus. [Col. 0738B] In the following year, however, according to Herodian, fearing the manifest boldness and madness of his son-in-law Caracalla, who then hated his wife, he attempted by plots to seize Caracalla’s imperial power (Herodian, book III, *Life of Severus*, p. 244 ff.). But when these were uncovered, Caracalla ordered him to be killed in the presence of his father Severus. Tertullian’s *Apologeticum* therefore appears to have been written before this year, though not long before it. For why would he have spared the name of Plautianus rather than those of Albinus and the other traitors? Tertullian therefore composed this book after the year 197, when Albinus laid violent hands upon himself, and before 204 or 205, when Plautianus was killed.

It nevertheless seems probable to some that the book came from Tertullian in the later year, when the tenth anniversary of Antoninus Caracalla’s [Col. 0738C] Caesarian rule was celebrated and the fire of the persecution aroused against the Christians blazed more fiercely. For Tertullian discussed these matters several times in this *Apologeticum* (ch. 2). Moreover, although he mentions several laws enacted against the Christians by other emperors, he mentions none enacted by Severus. Did Tertullian not therefore publish this *Apologeticum* before Severus promulgated his edicts against them?

But although in this book he inveighs most bitterly against the pagans, who celebrated the solemn festivals of the emperors with rites no less impious than superstitious, he is not discussing the solemnities of Caracalla rather than those of Severus and the other emperors. As for the persecutions of Christians and the laws written against them, we readily concede that Severus truly favored them at the beginning [Col. 0738D] of his reign. Tertullian himself is a witness to this favor and supplied the following reason for it: “Even Severus himself,” he says, “the father of Antoninus, was mindful of the Christians” (Tertullian, *To Scapula*, ch. 4). “For he sought out the Christian Proculus, surnamed Torpacion, the steward of Euhodia, who had once cured him by means of oil, and kept him in his palace until his death. Antoninus too knew him very well, having been reared on Christian milk.” We also acknowledge that the *Apologeticum* makes no mention of a law enacted against the Christians by that same Severus.

Yet we do not think it can therefore be concluded with certainty that it was published after Caracalla began to rule alone in the year 211, when, during his reign [Col. 0739A], the pagans were already raging and rioting most cruelly against all Christians. For the pagans’ fury against them was so great that the bare and empty pretext of the ancient laws sufficed for them to torture Christians of every sort at all times. Nor is this rashly invented by us. For our Tertullian addresses those same Gentiles in these words:

> How often do you rage against the Christians, partly obeying your own inclinations and partly the laws! How often, even without waiting for you, does the hostile mob attack us in its own right with stones and fires! In the very frenzy of the Bacchanals they do not spare even dead Christians, but tear them from the repose of burial, from what is in a sense the asylum of death—some already altered, some no longer whole—and cut them apart and drag their pieces about.

(Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 36, p. 459.)

A little earlier he had said: “Nor are there any greater ravagers of Christians than the mob” (idem, [Col. 0739B] ch. 35, p. 451)—that blind and frenzied mob, namely, which was driven to harass and kill Christians by its own frenzy and, as it were, innate madness, not by any prescription of the laws. To this is added the fact that the pagans laid upon them the cause of every public disaster. Whenever some disaster occurred, therefore, they immediately cried out, as Tertullian again testifies: “The Christians to the lion!” (*ibid.*, ch. 40).

Yet we seem to hear someone who will still insist that these and many other things in this *Apologeticum* could not have been described by our author so long as the emperor Severus favored the Christians and had enacted no law against them. But if our author mentions no law enacted by Severus, others think that this was done deliberately, so that he would not further provoke the spirit of the emperor, who was then [Col. 0739C] still living, but might more readily recall him to a sounder frame of mind.

These, then, are the considerations upon which the differing opinions concerning the date of this book rest. But after carefully weighing them, if this latter opinion seems to you preferable to the others, it must be admitted that the book was published after the persecution against the Christians had been stirred up by Severus himself—not, indeed, in 197 or 198, as some vainly suppose, but in 202, when Severus actually enacted laws against them and was, as Eusebius says, in the tenth year of his reign (Eusebius, book VI, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 2, p. 101 ff.).

But if anyone judges that the supports of this opinion are not sufficiently strong, he certainly will not deny that, from the points we noted shortly before [Col. 0739D], it may rightly be inferred that this book of Tertullian was published by him sometime after the death of Albinus, that is, after the year of Christ 197, as we have observed. Since Tertullian indicates in the same place that the greater assaults of that faction had been repressed and that only certain remnants of them survived, it must therefore be concluded that the date of this book appears to be assignable to the year 199, or rather 200.

Finally, someone will object that Tertullian at that time, as has often been said, had not reached his forty-fifth year. Yet the supreme learning that shines forth marvelously and everywhere throughout this *Apologeticum* appears to require a more advanced age. Rather, others reply, it required the aid from above of divine light and grace. Moreover, Tertullian was endowed with so excellent and penetrating a genius that by the fortieth year of his life he could without difficulty have become imbued with this very learning—and even greater learning—and filled this entire book with it.

## ARTICLE II. To which governors and presiding officials Tertullian dedicated this *Apologeticum*

The more openly and clearly Tertullian declared at the very beginning of the *Apologeticum* that he addressed it “to the governors of the Roman Empire presiding in a public and elevated place, almost at the very summit of the city” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 1, p. 257), the more vigorously the learned dispute over who these presiding officials were and [Col. 0740B] to what city they belonged. Among very many, indeed, the opinion had long prevailed that the Roman Senate was signified by these words. Others, however, later thought that the presiding officials of Carthage were designated, while still others thought that all the governors throughout the whole Roman Empire were meant.

The supporters of the first opinion rely on the very words of Tertullian that we have cited. For they think that no city other than Rome is indicated. And certainly, they say, what else can that public and elevated summit of the city be except the Roman Capitol, which Tertullian judged should be called by a circumlocution rather than by its own name? Could not the following passage, which we read written by him concerning the eclipse of the sun that occurred when Christ died, also be advanced in confirmation of this opinion: “You have the account of that cosmic catastrophe [Col. 0740C] preserved in your secret archives” (*ibid.*, ch. 21)? For what are those archives if not the records or archives and repositories of the Romans in which public documents were stored? For there he relates that Pilate reported to the emperor Tiberius not only this eclipse but also all the extraordinary events that had occurred before and after Christ’s death. Who would not readily concede that Tiberius ordered these things to be deposited and preserved in those same archives, which Tertullian challenges the Romans to examine?

What Tertullian stated more obscurely, Eusebius reported more clearly in these words, which are enveloped in no obscurity: “Tertullian composed his Apology τῇ ῥωμαϊκῇ συγκλήτῳ προσφωνήσας to the Roman Senate” (Eusebius, book V, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 5, p. 169). They also think that no little [Col. 0740D] strength is added to this opinion by the fact that Tertullian spent a long time at Rome. For Eusebius again left the following account of him in writing for posterity: “καὶ τῶν μάλιστα ἐπὶ Ῥώμης λαμπρῶν. And he was very celebrated at Rome” (idem, *ibid.*, book II, ch. 2, p. 41). To this may also be added Jerome (Jerome, *Catalog. of Ecclesiastical Writers*, ch. 53, p. 115), who testifies that Tertullian was harassed by the envy and insults of the Roman clergy. Where could that have occurred except while he was living at Rome?

But nothing, others say, prevents Tertullian from having been stung by the barbs of envy and slander even while he was living at Carthage. For intercourse between Romans and Carthaginians was so frequent that the reputation for supreme learning and [Col. 0741A] doctrine that Tertullian had earned could have been assailed by the envious clergy of the Roman Church. Indeed, Eusebius appears to them to have referred to nothing other than this reputation in the words cited immediately above. Nor does he thereby indicate any residence of Tertullian in the city of Rome, but rather the very great celebrity of his name and reputation among the Romans on account of his exceptional learning and writings.

Others might nevertheless insist that absolutely no one can deny that he came to Rome at least once. For who would dare deny what Tertullian himself asserted most explicitly? “We have seen at Rome the nobility of gemstones blushing before matrons because of the disdain shown for them by the Parthians, Medes, and the rest of their own Gentile women, among whom they are held almost solely for display” (Tertullian, book I, *On the Apparel of Women*, ch. 6). Yet just as he could go to Rome once [Col. 0741B], so he could go there repeatedly and remain there. Others will reply quite rightly: But how can Tertullian’s other journeys to this city be proved, or that he was actually staying there when he composed the *Apologeticum*?

For this reason many think that it was certainly published at Carthage and presented to the presiding officials of that city. For from the opening of the work, as we have already seen, he calls them those “presiding in a public and elevated place at the summit of the city” (idem, *Apologeticum*, ch. 1, p. 259). Again, elsewhere he says: “In that city”—that is, the city in which he was composing his *Apologeticum*. Yet Rome was not called a *civitas* but *Urbs*, “the City,” and was customarily designated by that name alone by antonomasia. Are these not his very words: “The consuls, by authority of the Senate [Col. 0741C], expelled Liber . . . not only from the City but from all Italy” (*ibid.*, ch. 6, p. 305).
