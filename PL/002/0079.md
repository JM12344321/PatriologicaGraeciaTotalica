“Are all souls, then, in the underworld?” you ask. Whether you wish it or not, both punishments and consolations are already there. You have the poor man and the rich man. And because I deferred something or other to this part, I shall now appropriately discharge it at the conclusion.

[Note: [Col. 0750D] “For why should you not suppose that the soul, etc.?” Count among Tertullian’s errors and paradoxes his teaching that souls do not enter heaven until the day of judgment but remain in the underworld. Hence in chapter LV he said: “Disdaining, perhaps, to receive in Abraham’s bosom the consolation of awaiting the resurrection.” Yet Tertullian shares that view with many others in whom there can be no heresy, since the Church had defined nothing. For I do not think they denied the glory of the beatific vision, or that the saints would enjoy complete blessedness only after the resurrection. Those persons, and Tertullian with them, should be treated more leniently, since perhaps they did not deny that souls see the glory of God. LE PR.]

For why should you not suppose that the soul is both punished and cherished in the underworld meanwhile, as it awaits either judgment, in a kind of preliminary enjoyment and anticipation of it?

“Because,” you say, “the proceedings of the divine judgment must be preserved intact, [Note: [Col. 0750C] All insert *in* except Wouw.] without any foretaste of the sentence; and because the restoration of the flesh must also be awaited, since it shared in the deeds and their rewards.”

What, then, will happen during this interval? Shall we sleep? But souls do not sleep even in living persons. Sleep belongs to bodies, as does death itself together with its mirror, sleep. Or do you want nothing to take place there, whither the whole of humanity is drawn, where every hope is held in reserve? Do you suppose that judgment is tasted in advance, or that it is begun? That it is hastily precipitated, or administered beforehand?

Indeed, how grossly unjust it would be even in the underworld if the guilty are still well off there and the innocent are not yet! [Note: [Col. 0750D] “What? Pam.] after death, trifling amid confused hope and uncertain expectation, rather than already shuddering at the review of one’s life and the ordering of judgment?

Must the soul always await the body in order to suffer or rejoice? Is it not sufficient of itself for either kind of experience? How often, while the body is unharmed, is the soul alone tormented by bitterness, anger, or weariness—often from a cause unknown even to itself! Look [Note: [Col. 0751B] “To that of Zeno.” This is Zeno of Elea, who, when asserting before Dionysius that contempt for death could be attained through philosophy, was tortured by him with savage torments and proved the truth of his answer by his steadfastness. Thus the soul knows how both to suffer and to rejoice without the flesh, even in the underworld; for while the flesh is unharmed, it suffers if it wishes, and while the flesh is injured, it rejoices if it wishes. If it does this by its own choice in life, how much more by God’s judgment after death!

Nor does the soul share every deed with the flesh’s assistance. For divine judgment examines even thoughts alone and bare intentions (Matt. V): “Whoever has looked with lust has already committed adultery in his heart.” Therefore [Note: [Col. 0751B] Some add “even,” Par. Gelen. What now if, even in carnal acts, it is the soul that comes first—the soul that conceives, arranges, commands, and impels? And if it is sometimes unwilling, it nevertheless first considers what it is going to do through the body. Conscience, after all, will never come after the deed. Thus it also accords with this order that the soul should first pay the recompenses that are first owed to it.

In sum, since we understand that prison which the Gospel presents to mean the underworld, and interpret payment of the last farthing as the discharge there, during the delay of the resurrection, of every minor offense, [Note: [Col. 0752B] “We interpret it as something to be discharged there during the delay of the resurrection.” What else is it than the punishment of purgatory if souls are purified during the delay of the resurrection and wash away every minor offense? Having contended, as I think, from the doctrine of faith against every human opinion concerning the soul, we have satisfied at least a just and necessary curiosity. [Note: [Col. 0752B] “We shall have satisfied,” Wouw. codex.] An excessive and idle curiosity, however, will always remain as far from learning as it chooses to extend its inquiries.

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN  
## ON THE FLESH OF CHRIST (C)

### Summary

Intending to discuss the reality of Christ’s flesh, Tertullian first indicates the occasion for the work: those who were disturbing faith in the resurrection were also tearing Christ’s flesh apart with their questions. Chapter I.

Then, coming to his subject, he argues against Marcion, who removed the account of Christ’s birth from the Gospel, and proves Christ’s nativity by an appeal to apostolic tradition. Chapter II.

He then shows that it was neither impossible nor unsuitable for God. Chapter III.

Nor unworthy of God, nor foolish. Chapter IV.

Therefore Christ was not crucified and did not die in an apparition, but truly—however much Marcion also denied this—and He was also truly born. Chapter V.

Next, against Apelles, he argues that Christ did not borrow His flesh from the stars and the substances of the higher world. Chapter VI.

He interposes that Christ’s saying, “Who is my mother, and who are my brothers?” does nothing to support them. Chapter VII.

[Col. 0752C] He adds that, according to the Apelleians, their fiery angel also established the heavenly things out of repentance for his offense; and consequently, if Christ’s flesh was constructed from the stars, it was sinful flesh. Chapter VIII.

Indeed, all the signs of true flesh were present in Christ. Chapter IX.

Again, against certain others, he argues that Christ’s flesh was not “psychic.” Chapter X.

Nor was it merely “carnal.” Chapter XI.

Christ also came, not so that the soul might know itself, but to save it. Chapter XII.

There were likewise different substances in Christ: flesh and soul. Chapter XIII.

Christ alone, moreover, was the bringer of salvation, not an angel. Chapter XIV.

Against Valentinus, he proceeds to show that Christ’s flesh was not spiritual. Chapter XV.

Likewise, against a certain Alexander, he shows that Christ’s flesh was not sinful and, even if it did not come from seed, was nevertheless real. Chapter XVI.

He incidentally proves from the Scriptures that Christ was to be born of a virgin. Chapter XVII.

It was also not fitting for the Son of God to be born of human seed, lest, if He were wholly the son of man according to Ebion’s opinion, He should not be believed to be the Son of God. Chapter XVIII.

Nor does the passage adulterated by Valentinus favor him: “He was born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of a man, but of God.” Chapter XIX.

Moreover, against the same persons, he demonstrates very extensively from the Scriptures that Christ was born not merely through the Virgin but from the Virgin. Chapters XX and XXI.

Accordingly, in all these matters the prophetic saying of Simeon concerning Christ is fulfilled: “Behold, this one is appointed as a sign [Col. 0753B] that will be contradicted.” Chapters XXII and XXIII.

And all those heretics were branded and foretold in the Scriptures. Chapter XXIV.

Finally, repeating by way of epilogue what he has proved in this book, he makes the transition to the following book, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*. Chapter XXV.

Moreover, besides the fact that Tertullian cites the book just mentioned, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, below in book V *Against Marcion*, mention is also made there of the subject and title of this work. “For this reason,” he says in chapter II, “we too prefaced it with a volume *On the Flesh of Christ*, in which we prove it to be solid against the supposed nativity of an apparition and vindicate it as human against the peculiar property alleged of its nature.” And again: “Therefore, as far as the heretics are concerned, we have demonstrated with what wedge we must oppose them; and opposition has already been offered under its own title as well, *On the Flesh of the Lord Against Heresies*, especially in preparation for this question.”

Besides the fact that Abbot Trithemius and Politianus once saw a manuscript of this book, as is shown by passages frequently cited, Rhenanus first published it from the Hirsau and Payerne manuscripts with scholia; later, after collating the Gorze codex, he corrected it and furnished it with annotations. Meanwhile, because it was lacking in the British codex of Laelandi and had not been examined by Gelonius, it was worthwhile [Col. 0754A] to compare it with three Vatican manuscripts. From these we have restored a good many passages and have also added a new summary and our own annotations. PAM.

He demonstrates that Christ assumed human flesh, against four heretics—Marcion, Apelles, Valentinus, and Basilides—or rather, he writes against four heresies: first, that of the Marcionites; second, that of Apelles; third and fourth, those of the Valentinians. VENET.

They would have deceived the same eyes and the same senses that the appearance of flesh deluded.

Certainly the nativity is announced by Gabriel—but what has Marcion to do with that angel of the Creator? Christ is introduced as conceived in the Virgin’s womb [Note: [Col. 0755C] “And in the Virgin’s womb,” Pam. Par. Fran.]—but what has Marcion to do with Isaiah, the Creator’s prophet? [Note: [Col. 0755C] “Prophet” omitted by Seml.; “with the Creator’s essence,” Paris. Fran. Pam.] One who suddenly brought Christ down from heaven hates delays.

“Remove from here,” he says, [Note: [Col. 0755C] “You remove,” Seml.] “Caesar’s ever-troublesome censuses, the cramped lodgings, the filthy swaddling-cloths, and the hard manger. Let the multitude of angels see to itself, honoring its Lord by night. Let the shepherds keep watch over their livestock instead. And let the Magi not be wearied by a long journey; I grant them their gold. Let Herod too be a better man, so that Jeremiah may not boast. But neither let the infant be circumcised, lest He suffer pain; nor be carried to the Temple, lest He burden His parents [Note: [Col. 0755C] “His parent,” Fran.] with the expense of an offering; [Note: [Col. 0755C] “Obligation,” Rhen., Agobardine codex.] nor be handed into Simeon’s arms, [Note: [Col. 0755C] “Of Simeon,” Pam.] lest He thereby grieve the old man who is about to die. And let that aged woman too be silent, lest she bewitch the child.”

[Note: [Col. 0755C] “Remove from here, he says.” Rhenanus correctly noted that these words are presented in the person of Marcion and continue as far as “by these counsels, I think”; for Marcion cut out from the Gospel of Luke—the only Gospel he acknowledged—those opening chapters in which these events are contained, as is soon discussed more fully in book IV *Against Marcion*. All these words, moreover, are spoken ironically. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0755D] “Let the multitude of angels see to itself.” This is wittily said in irony because nighttime is suitable for deception, as though even the angels could have been deceived. The event mentioned comes from the beginning of Luke’s Gospel narrative. RHEN.]

[Note: [Col. 0755D] “So that Jeremiah may not boast.” It is as though he were saying: let Herod not kill the children in Bethlehem, lest Jeremiah—or Micah—boast of his prophecy, also cited in Matthew II, as though it had now been fulfilled. The following words, “lest she bewitch the child,” are likewise ironic; for bewitching belongs to the class of incantation. PAM., with corrections by the editors.]

By these counsels, I think, Marcion dared to erase all the original documentary records of Christ, so that His flesh might not be proved. [Note: [Col. 0755C] Rhen. Pam. add “and.”]

By what authority, I ask you? [Note: [Col. 0755C] The Agobardine codex adds “Produce your authority.”] If you are a prophet, foretell something. If an apostle, preach publicly. If apostolic, [Note: [Col. 0756C] “If apostolic”: if you have imbibed the Apostles’ discipline and teaching. LE PR.] agree with the Apostles. If you are merely [Note: [Col. 0755C] “Merely,” Fran. Pam.] a Christian, believe what has been handed down. [Note: [Col. 0756D] “Believe what has been handed down.” A splendid passage against those who, though they are neither Apostles nor apostolic, nevertheless perpetually dispute about the faith from the Scriptures, when tradition ought to be sufficient for them. This is treated at great length in *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 3. PAM.]

If you are none of these, I should rightly tell you: die. For you are already dead, since you are not a Christian, because you do not believe what has been handed down—the very thing that makes Christians. [Note: [Col. 0756C] “Believed,” Rig. Pam. Par. Fran.] And you are all the more dead, the more you are not [Note: [Col. 0756C] “You are, who do not,” Rhen.] a Christian: you who once were one, but fell away, [Note: [Col. 0756C] “Because,” Wouw., Ursinus’s book.] by rescinding what you formerly believed. [Note: [Col. 0756D] “You who once were one, but fell away.” This passage should also be noted against those who are not ashamed to call heretics Christians, although the author does not wish those who have rescinded what they formerly believed and—just as he says below of Marcion himself—withdrawn from the Church and the faith to be called by that name. Pertinent too is the statement below in chapter 15: “Heretics, by believing, do not believe.” On Marcion’s withdrawal from the faith and the Church, see *On Prescriptions*, chapter 30. PAM.] [Note: [Col. 0756D] “By rescinding what you formerly believed”: by abrogating it, that is. The word is taken from legal usage, for it is employed in various formulas, as in wills: “concerning the rescission of wills,” that is, dissolving and invalidating that compact. LE PR.]

You yourself admit this in a certain letter; your followers do not deny it, and ours prove it. Therefore, in rescinding what you believed, it was as one who no longer believed that you rescinded it; yet it does not follow that, because you ceased to believe, you were right to rescind it. And by rescinding what you believed, you prove that, before you rescinded it, what you believed had been otherwise. Since it was otherwise, it had been handed down in that form. Moreover, what had been handed down was true, since it had been handed down by those whose office it was to hand it down. Therefore, by rescinding what had been handed down, you rescinded what was true. You did so without any right.

PAM.]
