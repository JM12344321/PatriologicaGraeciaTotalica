Nor does he confirm it only there, but also in his book *Against Praxeas*, in these words: “We have given the reason why they are not called two Gods or two Lords, but why the Father and the Son are two; and this not from a separation of substance, but from the dispensation, since we declare the Son indivisible and inseparable from the Father, another not in condition but in degree; and although he is called God when named individually, he does not thereby make two Gods, but one, by the very fact that he is entitled to be called God from the unity of the Father” (Tertull., *Against Praxeas*, chap. 19). You see indeed how explicitly he asserts there, as elsewhere, that the Son of God is another than his Father, not in condition but in [Col. 0827B] degree—that is, not in nature but in person, a person distinguished as second in order—and that by this distinction they are indeed two, or two persons distinct from one another, but cannot be called two Gods, because the substance of both is the same, and therefore both are one God (*ibid.*, chap. 2).

Nor should you say that the term “person” is passed over there in silence. For it is sufficiently implied by this distinction between Father and Son. Since he said that the nature of both is one, it is solely in person that they can be distinguished and be two. Moreover, the term “person” is employed in the same *Against Praxeas* in the very sense in which we now use it, as will be most evident from the passages cited below.

But he maintains and defends this doctrine of a distinct [Col. 0827C] person of the Son and the Father, yet one substance of both, not only against Praxeas but also against another heretic, Valentinus. There were indeed two errors of Valentinus, which he described in these words: “Valentinus distinguishes and separates his *probolae*, that is, his divine emanations, and places them so far apart that an Aeon does not know the Father” (*ibid.*, chap. 8). He refutes the second of these errors, concerning an Aeon that does not know the Father, by the authority of John the Evangelist: “Among us,” he says, “the Son alone knows the Father, and he himself has disclosed the bosom of the Father.” Then he overthrows Valentinus’s first error concerning separated divine emanations in these words, which bear more directly upon our purpose: “The Word is always in the Father, as he said: ‘I am in the Father’; and always with God, as it is written: ‘And the Word was with God’; and never separated from the Father, or other [Col. 0827D] than the Father, because ‘I and the Father are one.’” From this he concludes that this Word and Son of God is distinct from his Father, because he proceeded and was brought forth from him, but is not separated from him, since the nature of both is one. Indeed, shortly afterward he added: “The Son is another than the Father, not by diversity but by distribution; another not by division but by distinction, because the Father and the Son are not the same, being different from one another even in measure” (*ibid.*). But I would now have you observe that the Son of God is said by him to be both another than the Father and not another: another than the Father in person, namely, but not another in substance, because this substance is plainly the same in both.

Since, therefore, the Son is distinct from his Father, because there are two persons in one and the same substance, he rightly [Col. 0828A] wrote of the same Son of God in the *Apologeticum*: “In measure,” just as he said against Valentinus, “he made an alternate number; in degree, not in condition.” But Rigaltius, contrary to the testimony of our manuscripts and of all previously edited codices, wished to correct the former words there as follows: “Another in measure, not in number” (Idem, *Apologet.*, chap. 21). Nevertheless, he gave no reason for this emendation. Did he therefore think, as it appears, that by altering the author’s words in this way he would preserve his antitheses more accurately? But it is unquestionably wrong to alter the text of an ancient author on the slightest conjecture, and much more so, by a perverse alteration, to twist his meaning into another that is plainly opposed to it, and into the very error that he attacks. For Tertullian, as has been abundantly proved, teaches most openly [Col. 0828B] that the Son of God is another than his Father in measure and degree, or in person, but one and the same in substance, and therefore that there are two distinct persons, yet one God. The two persons, therefore, distinct from one another, make an alternate number.

Furthermore, as we shall see in the following article, he openly declares with the whole Catholic Church that there are three persons in the most holy Trinity. He therefore rightly and deservedly said that the Son of God makes an alternate number with his Father. For we have already heard him say: “The Father and the Son are not the same in measure,” that is, in that mode of emanation by which the Son proceeded from the Father (Idem, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 8). The Son therefore makes an alternate number with the Father; that is, each of them, Father and Son, alternately and in turn [Col. 0828C] makes up the number of two persons. That this is Tertullian’s meaning may quite reasonably be gathered from these words of his in the same book: “The Father makes the Son, and the Son the Father. And those who are made from one another can in no way be made by themselves for themselves, so that the Father should make himself a Son to himself, and the Son himself a Father to himself. . . . The Father must have the Son in order to be Father, and the Son the Father in order to be Son” (*ibid.*, chap. 10). Do you hear how clearly he teaches that the Father and the Son are so related to one another that the Father with the Son, and in turn the Son with the Father, make an alternate number?

But because the difference between human beings and God is infinite, he therefore asserts without the least hesitation that the Son of God is another than the Father in degree, but not in condition; that is, by his generation he is another in degree, [Col. 0828D] or second in order (*ibid.*, chap. 2), inasmuch as he is begotten by the Father, but not another in condition—that is, not in substance and nature, which is one and the same in him and in his Father. But new light will certainly be shed upon these statements of his by what he taught concerning the divinity of the same Son and of the Holy Spirit, and concerning the sacred mystery of the Trinity, which must now be unfolded and explained by us.

## ARTICLE III. How explicitly Tertullian explains and defends the orthodox doctrine concerning the divinity of the Son of God and of the Holy Spirit, and concerning the sacred mystery of the Trinity

Tertullian maintains and defends the Catholic doctrine concerning the supreme [Col. 0829A] divinity of the Son of God and of Christ the Lord, not only in the passages we have cited but also in many others, with equal firmness and clarity. But because in these he also asserts and proclaims no less explicitly the Catholic dogma concerning the divinity of the Holy Spirit and the mystery of the most holy Trinity, we have judged that it should be set forth separately in this article. First, therefore, against the Patripassian Praxeas, who babbled that God the Father and the Son were the same, he declares that the following must firmly be believed concerning the mystery of the Trinity, namely, the three divine persons and their one and the same nature: “Let the mystery of the economy be preserved,” he says, “which arranges the unity into a Trinity, setting forth three—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit: three, however, not in condition but in degree; not in substance but in form; not in power but in aspect; yet of one substance, one condition, [Col. 0829B] and one power, because there is one God, from whom these degrees, forms, and aspects are reckoned under the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit” (Tertull., *Against Praxeas*, chap. 2). No one fails to understand that by the term “condition,” as we noted in the preceding article, the same thing is signified as the substance and nature of God; while by the terms “degree,” “form,” and “aspect” are signified the divine persons, their order, and their mode of existence. He therefore manifestly teaches that there are three persons of the most holy Trinity, who admit of number without division, but that their substance is one and the same.

Afterward he sets forth and confirms this doctrine—and, as he himself says, this rule—of the Catholic Church at greater length, in the following manner: “The Trinity, flowing down from the Father through interwoven and connected [Col. 0829C] degrees, does not disturb the monarchy and preserves the condition of the economy. Hold everywhere to this rule which I have professed, by which I testify that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are inseparable from one another, and thus you will recognize both what is said and how it is said. For behold, I say that the Father is one, the Son another, and the Holy Spirit another. Every simple or perverse person wrongly takes this statement as though it expressed diversity and, from diversity, implied the separation of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. I say this, however, of necessity, since they contend that the Father, the Son, and the Spirit are the same, flattering the monarchy against the economy: the Son is another than the Father, not by diversity, nor another by division, but by distinction, because the Father and the Son are not the same, being different from one another even in measure” (*ibid.*, chap. 9). Could the true and Catholic [Col. 0829D] doctrine concerning the sacred mystery of the Trinity be explained more clearly or better? For our Author openly, and against the errors of heretics, professes that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are three persons distinct from one another, yet without diversity, division, or separation, because their substance is one. And he asserts that this is the rule of orthodox faith, handed down in the sacred Scriptures, received from the forefathers, and to be maintained by all.

Therefore, not content to have said this once or twice, he afterward repeats and reinforces it: “We,” he says, “who by the grace of God examine both the times and the causes of the Scriptures, being disciples especially of the Paraclete, not of men, define the Father and the Son as indeed two, and now as three [Col. 0830A] with the Holy Spirit, according to the principle of the economy, which produces number, lest, as your perversity infers, the Father himself be believed to have been born and to have suffered—which it is not permissible to believe, since it has not been handed down in that way” (*ibid.*, chap. 13). When he wrote these words, he had already defected from the Catholic Church to the errors of the Montanists and had imbibed the absurd fiction of a false Paraclete. Nevertheless, he explicitly asserts and maintains a sound and orthodox faith concerning the mystery of the Trinity. Who, therefore, will deny that this principal doctrine of the Christian religion is true, when he defended it so consistently, in whatever state he was and throughout his whole life, and testified that it was attacked only by those who were plainly in error?

Indeed, we previously read this written by him: “How can it be that God should appear to suffer division and dispersion [Col. 0830B] in the Son and the Holy Spirit, who have obtained the second and third places and are such sharers in the Father’s substance, when he does not suffer these among so great a number of angels, who are moreover so alien from his substance?” (*ibid.*, chap. 3). You surely understand with what clarity he again declares that the Father is so distinguished in person from the Son and the Holy Spirit that the Son and the Holy Spirit hold the second and third places. Yet he plainly and absolutely contends and affirms that there is no division or dispersion among them, but that they share the same substance—a substance that is one in them, whereas in the angels, however perfect, it is divided and separate.

But what he not only declares most openly but also proves by the authority of sacred Scripture, [Col. 0830C] and especially by these words of John the Evangelist, “I and the Father are one,” undoubtedly requires no explanation. For after citing them, he continues as follows (*ibid.*, chap. 22): “Here, therefore, the foolish—indeed, the blind—wish to take their stand, failing to see, first, that ‘I and the Father’ signifies two; next, that the final word ‘are’ cannot refer to one person, because it is spoken in the plural; and then because it says, ‘we are one’ in the neuter, not ‘we are one’ in the masculine. For if he had said, ‘we are one’ in the masculine, it might have assisted their opinion. For the masculine ‘one’ appears to signify the singular number. Moreover, although the two are masculine in gender, he says ‘one’ with a neuter word, which pertains not to singularity but to unity, to likeness, to conjunction, and to the love of the Father. . . . By saying, ‘I and the Father are one,’ he shows that there are two, whom he makes equal and joins together.” Afterward he explains this more briefly, but [Col. 0830D] no less clearly and distinctly: “The connection of the Father in the Son, and of the Son in the Paraclete, produces three who cohere, one from another; and these three are one in the neuter, not one in the masculine, just as it was said, ‘I and the Father are one,’ with reference to unity of substance, not singularity of number” (*ibid.*, chap. 25).

Although he does not use the term “person” there, you could nevertheless have observed that he employed it in the preceding passage. But you will see it expressed still more distinctly where he again demonstrates, by various testimonies of sacred Scripture, that there are three persons in the most holy Trinity and only one nature. For he proves this from these words of Genesis: “Let us make man in our image and likeness” (Gen. [Col. 0831A] I, 26); and from these others: “Behold, Adam has become as one of us” (*ibid.*, III, 22). From these he argues as follows: “Because the Son, the second person, his own Word, and the third, the Spirit in the Word, already adhered to him, he therefore spoke in the plural: ‘Let us make,’ and ‘our,’ and ‘us.’” Then shortly afterward: “Finally, the following Scripture distinguishes among the persons: ‘And God made man; in the image of God he made him.’ Why not ‘his own,’ if there was only one who was making and there was no one in whose image he was making him? But there was one in whose image he was making him, namely the Son, who, because he was to become a more certain and truer man, had caused man, who was then to be formed from clay, to be called his image—the image and likeness of the true.” And not long afterward: “I have already declared,” he says, “how you ought to understand the Son as another than the Father: in the designation of person, not of substance; for [Col. 0831B] distinction, not for division. Moreover, although I everywhere maintain one substance in three who cohere, nevertheless the necessity of the sense requires me to call him who commands another than him who acts” (Tertull., *Against Praxeas*, chap. 22).

Earlier, when in disputing against the same Praxeas he manifestly shows that the Son of God, who is his Wisdom and Word, is another than his Father, he introduces Praxeas arguing as follows: “‘Therefore,’ you say, ‘you grant that the Word is some substance, constructed by the imparting of spirit and wisdom’” (*ibid.*, chap. 7)? To this he answers in one word: “Certainly,” and thus freely confesses that it is true. He immediately gives this reason for it: “For you are unwilling that he should possess substantial existence in reality through the property of substance, so that he may be regarded as a certain thing and person, [Col. 0831C] and may thus, being constituted second from God, make two: the Father and the Son, God and the Word.”

We certainly should never finish speaking if we wished to transcribe and elucidate every single statement in which he asserts the sacred mystery of the Trinity against Praxeas as correctly as he does explicitly. Therefore, lest we become too lengthy, we shall add only those passages in which he further shows that there are three persons of one divinity in the most holy Trinity: “Christ,” he says, “finally commanded that they should baptize into the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit—not into one, nor once, but three times, at each name into each individual person” (*ibid.*, chap. 27). That the substance and divinity of those three persons is one, he declared most explicitly not only in the passages [Col. 0831D] already cited, but also in another book, which no one doubts was composed by him while he was likewise following the Montanist heresy. For there he wrote: “The Church, properly speaking, is the Spirit himself, in whom is the Trinity, or three persons of one divinity: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit” (Idem, *On Modesty*, chap. 21).

Furthermore, from all that has thus far been discussed in this and the preceding article, it is sufficiently and, as we think, evidently concluded that Tertullian’s doctrine—whether as a Catholic or as a Montanist, that is, throughout the whole course of his life—concerning the supreme divinity of the Son of God and of the Holy Spirit, their possession of the same substance as the eternal God the Father, and their three distinct [Col. 0832A] persons, was wholly sound and plainly orthodox. It is no less evident that he was thoroughly persuaded that this principal dogma of the whole Catholic Church was founded upon the authority of sacred Scripture, and that it had been transmitted from the Apostles and their successors down to his own age by a sure and perpetual tradition. Finally, it is clear from what has been said that this doctrine was boldly defended by him not only against the heretics who denied it, but also against the pagans, the bitterest enemies of Christians, who most cruelly persecuted and killed them on account of this doctrine. Yet he could certainly have enticed the heretics more easily to his side if he had been able to admit that in the most holy Trinity there is only one person, and no plurality except in name. He could also have reconciled the pagans to himself and appeased their fury against the Christians if [Col. 0832B] he had said that there were three Gods, or at least one greater God and other lesser ones. Nor should you think that this is a gratuitous invention of ours, since Tertullian himself openly and explicitly professes it: “Moreover, if on the ground of our conscience,” he says, “by which we know that the name of God and Lord applies both to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, we were to call them Gods and Lords, we should have extinguished our torches and would have been more timid even in facing martyrdoms, since an opportunity of escape would have lain open to us by immediately swearing by gods and lords, as do certain heretics whose gods are many” (*ibid.*, chap. 13). Tertullian, therefore, like other true Christians, preferred to experience every kind of torment and to be killed rather than in any way weaken or conceal this doctrine concerning the divinity of Christ himself and the mystery of the most holy Trinity, [Col. 0832C] which had been handed down to him by Christ and his disciples. There is therefore no room for doubt that this doctrine of Tertullian and the Catholic Church is true, since it was asserted in this manner by the sacred writers and was constantly and perpetually preserved and defended.

## ARTICLE IV. By what arguments Tertullian attempted to explain the divine and eternal generation of the Son of God, and why he, but not the Holy Spirit, is called the Son of God

It was not enough for Tertullian to maintain and defend his own doctrine, and that of the whole Catholic Church, concerning the supreme divinity of the Son of God and of the Holy Spirit in the manner we have seen (preceding article); he also attempted to explain [Col. 0832D] why the Word and Reason of God is his true Son and ought to be so called. He therefore openly declares to all that God, by the very fact that he is God, has always and from all eternity been rational (*ibid.*). Since, therefore, he cannot be without reason, from the same eternity he brought forth the Word—which Word, as we have noted, ought more properly in Latin to have been called Reason. That divine Word, however, is not an articulated voice like that of a human being, nor some sound of the mouth, struck air, and thus some empty and insubstantial thing; rather, “it possesses,” he says, “spirit as its own substance” (Tertull., *Apologeticum*, chap. 21). Because this was denied by the heretic Praxeas, Tertullian argues against him as follows and proves that this Word of God is a true [Col. 0833A] substance: “‘For what,’ you will say, ‘is a word except the voice and sound of the mouth and, as the grammarians teach, air struck, intelligible to hearing, but otherwise some empty, insubstantial, and incorporeal thing?’ But I say that nothing insubstantial and empty could have proceeded from God, since it was not brought forth from what is insubstantial and empty; nor could that which proceeded from so great a substance and made such great substances be devoid of substance. For he himself made the things that were made through him. How could that be nothing without which nothing was made? How could an insubstantial thing have produced solid things, an empty thing things that are full, and an incorporeal thing corporeal things? For although something can sometimes be made different from that through which it is made, nevertheless nothing can be made through what is empty and insubstantial. Is the Word of God, who is called the Son, who himself is also named God, an empty and insubstantial thing? ‘AND THE WORD WAS WITH GOD, AND THE WORD WAS GOD’” (Idem, *Against Praxeas*, [Col. 0833B] chap. 7)? By this argument, therefore, he shows that the Word—that is, the Speech and Reason of God—is not something insubstantial and empty, but a true substance.

Now this divine Word is a true substance precisely because he was brought forth by God. For “we say,” Tertullian declares, “that he was begotten by being brought forth, and therefore call him the Son of God.” Brought forth by his divine Father, he proceeded from the Father’s own intellect as though from a womb, and therefore he is begotten and is the true Son of God. For by this very generation he is not only like the Father, but the substance of both is also the same. For the Son of God, he says, “did not depart from the Father’s womb, but came forth from it.” Since he did not depart, he has the same substance as the Father; and because he proceeded from him, he is his Son (Idem, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 21).

[Col. 0833C] But let us hear Tertullian again, who elsewhere (Idem, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 8) asserts and confirms this by several testimonies of sacred Scripture: “By proceeding from him,” he says, “the Son was made the firstborn, as begotten before all things, and the only-begotten, as alone begotten from God, properly from the womb of his own heart—that is, his intellect—according as his Father also testifies: ‘MY HEART HAS UTTERED A MOST EXCELLENT WORD’ (Ps. XLIV, 2); and then, addressing him with joy in his person: ‘YOU ARE MY SON; TODAY I HAVE BEGOTTEN YOU’ (Ps. II, 7); and, ‘BEFORE THE MORNING STAR I BEGOT YOU’ (*ibid.*, CIX, 5). So too the Son in his own person professes the Father under the name of Wisdom: ‘THE LORD ESTABLISHED ME AS THE BEGINNING OF HIS WAYS FOR HIS WORKS’ (Heb., chap. I). ‘BEFORE ALL THE HILLS HE BEGOT ME’ (Prov., chap. VIII, 22).” And this [Col. 0833D] is the very reason why the Word, or Speech, of God—but not the Holy Spirit—is, and is called, the Son of God; and why, as Tertullian himself says, “he also properly received the name of Son”: both because that name is given to him in the sacred Scriptures, and because he was first begotten by his Father alone into the likeness of his nature, while the Holy Spirit proceeded from both, as will become evident to everyone from the words of our Tertullian soon to be cited (Tertull., *Against Praxeas*, chap. 8).

But although the Word of God proceeds eternally from his Father, or, as Tertullian himself says, “is derived from no other source than from the substance of the Father” (*ibid.*, chap. 4)—that is, arises from his thought and intelligence—he is nevertheless not thereby separated from him. For [Col. 0834A] the utterly simple substance of God cannot be divided, just as there cannot be several Gods. Therefore he says again: “Everything that proceeds from something must be second to that from which it proceeds; it is not, however, thereby separated. But where there is a second, there are two; and where there is a third, there are three. For the Spirit is third from God”—that is, from the Father and the Son—“just as the fruit from the shoot is third from the root” (*ibid.*, chap. 8). He teaches two things there which must certainly be observed with attentive care. First, he says that the Son of God is another than his Father and second in order, because he is the Father’s eternal Word, who proceeded from his intellect and thought, and is therefore his Son. Second, he says that the Holy Spirit is third because he proceeds from both Father and Son, just as the fruit arises third from root and shoot. But since there [Col. 0834B] he explains and defends against the heretic Praxeas not merely his own opinion but that of the whole Catholic Church, it certainly follows that this was asserted and defended by all orthodox Christians, and even by others not infected with the same or a similar error as Praxeas.

Some, however, object that Tertullian is not there treating the origin of the Holy Spirit, but the order of the persons of the most holy Trinity, in which the Spirit is placed third after the Father. But why, I ask, have those who argue thus omitted these subsequent words of our Author: “As the fruit from the shoot is third from the root, and the stream from the river third from the spring, and the point from the ray third from the sun; nevertheless, nothing is estranged from the womb from which it derives its properties”? Do these words not sufficiently show that he spoke not [Col. 0834C] only of the order of the Holy Spirit but also of his origin? For there, by these comparisons against Praxeas, he demonstrates that although the Son of God is not separated from his Father, he is nevertheless distinguished from him because he derives his origin from him. Then, passing from this to the origin of the Holy Spirit, he argues in precisely the same manner, as you see. He therefore discusses the origin of the Holy Spirit no less than that of the Son.

They nevertheless insist that Tertullian had previously declared most openly: “I think the Spirit originated from no other source than from the Father through the Son” (Idem, chap. 4). But attend, I ask, to how inconsiderately this is objected. For Hilary, as they admit, explicitly taught that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son when he wrote: “But if it were believed that there is a difference between receiving from [Col. 0834D] the Son and proceeding from the Father, it will certainly be judged one and the same thing to receive from the Son as it is to receive from the Father” (Hilary, *On the Trinity*, bk. 8, §20). Yet we read this written by the same Hilary: “From you”—namely, the Father—“through him”—the Son—“is your Holy Spirit.” A little later he prays to the Father as follows: “Grant me that I may worship you, our Father, and your Son together with you; and that I may be found worthy of your Holy Spirit, who is from you through your only-begotten Son” (*ibid.*, bk. 12, §§56–57).

But if Hilary said in the same Catholic sense both that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son and that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son, why should Tertullian not have done so as well? Can the same manner of speaking be approved in one and condemned in the other? Both, therefore, rightly said both that the Holy Spirit [Col. 0835A] proceeds from the Father and the Son and that he proceeds from the Father through the Son. For the Son, as intermediate between the Father and the Holy Spirit, received his principle from the Father alone; and the Holy Spirit derives his origin from him and from the Father. Thus the Holy Spirit is said to proceed from the Father through the Son, who, standing by his origin as an intermediary between them both, received from the Father that the Holy Spirit should proceed from him. Therefore this very procession is derived and traced in one and the same manner from both the Father and the Son.

Since, therefore, the Son is his Word and Speech, who proceeded from the Father’s intellect or thought as from a kind of womb, he is rightly his Son and only-begotten, and, as Tertullian said elsewhere, “properly received the name of Son” (Tertull., *Against Praxeas*, chap. 8). But the Holy [Col. 0835B] Spirit, because he proceeds from both, has never been called, nor ought he to have been called, the Son of either one of them or of both.

## ARTICLE V. Certain examples by which Tertullian attempted to explain the divine generation of the Son of God are set forth and examined

Since the divine generation of the Son of God far surpasses human comprehension and cannot readily be understood by it, our Tertullian therefore endeavored to explain and illustrate it by certain comparisons. But because these are not free from difficulties, they must be examined by us. First, then, he proposes them in the *Apologeticum* as follows: “When a ray is extended from the sun, it is a portion from the whole; but the sun will be in the ray, because it is a ray of the sun. The substance is not separated, [Col. 0835C] but extended: thus Spirit from Spirit and God from God, as light kindled from light. The source of the substance remains whole and undiminished, even if you borrow from it several offshoots of qualities. Thus also that which has proceeded from God is God, and the Son of God, and both are one”—supply: God (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 399). Elsewhere, however, he attempts to elucidate this still more fully by the same and other examples. Let us hear him, if you please: “God brought forth the Word, as even the Paraclete teaches, just as the root brings forth the shoot, the spring the river, and the sun the ray. For these forms too are *probolae*—that is, as he had said a little earlier, emanations—of the substances from which they proceed. Nor would I hesitate to call the shoot the son of the root, the river the son of the spring, and the ray the son of the sun; because every origin is a parent, and everything brought forth from an origin [Col. 0835D] is offspring; much more so the Word of God, who also properly received the name of Son. Yet neither is the shoot separated from the root, nor the river from the spring, nor the ray from the sun, just as the Word is not separated from God. Therefore, according to their pattern, I profess that I speak of two: God and his Word, the Father and his Son. For the root and the shoot are two things, but joined; the spring and the river are two forms, but undivided; and the sun and the ray are two shapes, but coherent” (Idem, *Against Praxeas*, chap. 8, p. 639).

But it must be carefully observed that these comparisons are offered by our Author not as wholly perfect, or as though they were entirely and in every respect similar to the divine generation of the Son. For he himself said, “Much more so the Word of God,” by which words he signifies that all these [Col. 0836A] comparisons are imperfect. And indeed, who does not know that they differ from it as greatly as the creature differs from the Creator? He therefore did not think that the generation of the Son of God could be fully explained by them, but used them so that we might understand it in some manner, according to the feeble powers of our intellect.

Furthermore, these examples are employed by him not in all respects, but only with regard to certain matters pertaining to the divine generation of the Son. For all of them tend only to show that although the Son of God proceeds from his Father, he is nevertheless not separated or divided from him. For since, as he says, the shoot proceeds from the root, the river from the spring, and the ray from the sun, and yet they are not separated or divided from one another but are joined together and cohere without any division, [Col. 0836B] the human intellect may thereby be led, according to its manifestly feeble powers, to know that although the Son of God proceeds from the Father, he is not separated from him, but is joined to him and remains wholly undivided from him in an infinitely more perfect manner than those corporeal things.

Thus, afterward, in order to confirm that the Son of God is not separated from the Father, although he came forth from him by divine generation, he introduces the Son himself speaking as follows: “I came forth from the Father, as the ray from the sun, the stream from the spring, and the shoot from the seed” (*ibid.*, chap. 22). Elsewhere he uses the same comparison of the sun and its ray solely in order to demonstrate that the name of God is not to be attributed to creatures, but to the eternal Father and his Son, whose substance is entirely one. It is better, however, to hear him state this himself: “I shall not,” he says, “call them Gods at all, nor Lords, [Col. 0836C] but shall follow the Apostle, so that if the Father and the Son are to be named together, I shall call the Father God and name Jesus Christ Lord. But when Christ alone is mentioned, I shall be able to call him God, as does the same Apostle: ‘From whom is Christ, who is,’ he says, ‘God over all, blessed forever.’ For I shall also call a ray of the sun, considered separately, ‘sun’; yet, when naming the sun to which the ray belongs, I shall not at once call the ray ‘sun’ as well. For although I shall not make two suns, I shall nevertheless number both the sun and its ray as two things and two forms of one undivided substance, just as I number God and his Word, the Father and the Son” (*ibid.*, chap. 13).

Nor did he stop there, but in again explaining that eternal generation of the divine Word he used another comparison. Although it is much [Col. 0836D] more perfect than the preceding ones, he nevertheless openly acknowledges that it too remains imperfect. It is drawn from a human being’s inward speech and word, and he argues thus: “Observe when you silently converse with yourself: this very act is carried on within you by reason, which meets you together with speech at every movement of your thought and every impulse of your perception. Whatever you think is speech; whatever you perceive is reason. You must speak it in your mind, and while you speak you experience speech as an interlocutor, in which resides that very reason by which, when you speak in thought, you are thinking, and through which, by speaking, you think. Thus speech is in some manner a second within you, through which you speak while thinking and through which you think while speaking; speech itself is another. How much more fully, therefore, does this take place in God, whose [Col. 0837A] image and likeness you also are reckoned to be, since even in his silence he has Reason within himself, and Speech within Reason?” (*ibid.*, chap. 5, p. 637). There he shows that God, who is rational by his nature, had Reason and Speech within himself from eternity, which he brings forth when he thinks and which is therefore another than himself, although the substance and nature of both are the same. Who would not readily concede that this could not have been proved more aptly and clearly than by this comparison with the inward word and speech of human beings? For in this, as he says, the human being is the image and likeness of God. Nevertheless, he admits that this very comparison is plainly imperfect, and that the Word of God is brought forth from God the Father in a far fuller and more perfect manner than the word of a human being is brought forth from the human being.

Furthermore, from what has thus far been said, it ought to be manifest to everyone [Col. 0837B] that Tertullian, whether a Catholic or a supporter of the Montanists, most consistently asserted the supreme divinity of the Son and Word of God. For, as we have seen, he openly teaches that the Son was begotten by the divine thought of his Father and that by this generation he is indeed another than the Father—that is, distinct in person—but by no means divided or separated from him, since the substance and nature of both are entirely one and the same. For God the Father, being rational from eternity, could never have been without thought; and therefore his Word, which is the same as his thought, must be consubstantial and coeternal with him. Is this not plainly the very same doctrine as that of the Council of Nicaea? For in its creed it is established that one must believe in one Lord Jesus Christ, born of the Father, only-begotten—that is, from the substance of the Father—God from God, light [Col. 0837C] from light (or, as Tertullian says, *Apologet.* chap. 21, p. 399, “as light kindled from light”), true God from true God, begotten, not made, consubstantial with the Father.
