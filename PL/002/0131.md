But behold, once again, another little smoke-hole, and from it a conflagration bursting forth which not even the Ocean itself had been able to extinguish. The highest credibility for this incredible spectacle is nevertheless supplied by Fr. Ricciardus, S.J., who, as Kircher testifies in Book IV of the *Subterranean World*, “being present, saw everything with his own eyes, and afterward at Rome related to me orally the outcome of the entire affair, and wished it to be made known to posterity by written testimony.” Fr. Ricciardus’s account, as the highly renowned Kircher committed it with his pen to the memory of posterity, runs as follows:

> “If ever those fires enclosed within the bowels of the earth exerted their force, they did so most of all when, in the year [Col. 1240D] 1650, from September 24 to October 9, they shook that island”—it was Santorini, so called from St. Irene, Virgin and Martyr, to whom it is dedicated—“with so many enormous and frequent earthquakes that the people of Santorini, fearing imminent destruction, lay prostrate as suppliants at the altars day and night. Yet it is impossible either to state or explain how great a fear seized everyone when, after the barriers had been broken, those victorious flames struggled to force a path for themselves through the midst of the sea’s waves, about four Italian miles east of the island of Santorini. For the sea suddenly swelled upward to thirty cubits and, spreading widely over the neighboring lands, overturned everything in its path, so much so that in the very harbor of Candia, although it lies eighty miles away, it shattered both galleys and ships by its sudden assault. The air, meanwhile, infected by those foul-smelling, sulphurous vapors, began both to grow dark and to assume innumerable forms. Here fiery spears and flame-breathing swords [Col. 1241A] seemed to be brandished; there flashing arrows were discharged; here terrifying serpents and dragons seemed to fly about; there thunderbolts and lightning were stirred up, etc., etc.”

Furthermore, he continues, that fiery abyss vomited forth such a mass of pumice that the stones covered the entire surface of the sea. It is absolutely certain that they were carried as far as Smyrna and Constantinople and filled all the shores. The force of the conflagration was greatest during the first two months, since the neighboring sea appeared to boil like a seething pot, and enormous globes of flame and the densest smoke emerged day and night, etc., etc.

But what then? Should I think that another catastrophe ought to be passed over in silence—one similar to the former, indeed far more savage than all the rest—which the Ocean itself, where it flows among the Azores, exhibited in the preceding century? Here, then, are again the very words of Kircher, Book II of the *Subterranean World*, chapter XII, § 4:

> “Six miles from Pico—commonly called *Pico delle Camerine*—lies a place called *la Ferreria*. In that region of the Ocean, on a Saturday in the month of July 1638, fire erupted with such inexplicable violence that not even the Ocean sufficed to extinguish so great a conflagration, despite the fact that fishermen had previously and repeatedly ascertained the depth of the Ocean at that place to be 120 geometrical feet. [Col. 1241B] The area occupied by the boiling fire was as large as would suffice for sowing two *modii* of wheat. It erupted with such violence that, despite the aforementioned depth of the Ocean, it rose to the clouds and reached the upper region of the air, carrying up with it the water itself, sand, earth, rocks, and other enormous masses, which from a distance appeared—an appalling spectacle to those who beheld it—like tufts of cotton. When the material returned to the sea, it presented the appearance of molten pulp. From time to time, the force of raging nature hurled rocks of such immense size to a height of three spear-lengths that one would have said not rocks but entire mountains had been cast forth. Adding to the horror was the fact that, when those rocky mountains hurled aloft fell back and struck others wrested from the bowels of the sea, they burst with a terrible crash [Col. 1241C] into a thousand pieces; these, when afterward taken in hand, crumbled into black sand. Moreover, from the varied and immense quantity of ejected matter and the accumulation of innumerable rocks, a new island arose in the midst of the Ocean, and in its very deepest part. Small at first, measuring five *jugera*, it increased daily until, fourteen days later, it occupied a length of five miles. So great a multitude of fish perished in this conflagration that scarcely eight merchantmen of the Indies could have held them. Because they had been scattered far and wide over the island, the natives gathered them from a circuit of eighteen miles and buried them in extremely deep pits, lest their putrefaction cause some contagion. The smell of sulphur was perceptible over a distance of twenty-four miles.”

Thus Kircher, from the faithful eyewitness (*κατ’ αὐτοψίαν*, “according to direct observation”) report of the Fathers of the Society of Jesus.

### Now none remain

Among these was certainly Pompeii, said to have been founded by Hercules and formerly a Roman colony. Concerning it, our author likewise says in Book II of *On the Pallium*, chapter 2:

> By a cloud of this kind Etruria too was burned up in ancient Volsinii; and, that Campania might hope for all the more from its own mountains, Pompeii was torn away.

Likewise, in the *Apology*, chapter 40:

> Nor did Etruria and Campania complain about the Christians when Volsinii was drenched from heaven and Pompeii from its own mountain.

Tacitus likewise testifies concerning these matters in Book XV of the *Annals*, chapter 22, in these words:

> By an earthquake Pompeii, a celebrated town of Campania, collapsed in great part.

But anyone who wishes to know these events more precisely will find them in Dio Cassius’s *Roman History*, Book XVI, under Titus. And since I have once begun to be expansive and have already burst the narrow bounds of notes, what prevents me from excerpting from that author himself the following passages, which also lend credibility to what was said above, since readers will see that these things are not new, but occurred almost seventeen centuries ago? Xiphilinus, then, gives the following from his Dio:

> Then the sea began to roar at the same time, the whole sky resounded together, and an enormous and sudden crash was heard, as though mountains were collapsing together. Then immense stones first leapt forth and reached the highest summits, followed by a great quantity of fire [Col. 1242A] and smoke, so that it darkened the whole air and concealed the sun, just as though it had suffered an eclipse, etc. Some thought that the world was either being reduced to chaos or consumed by fire, etc., etc. So great was the quantity of ash that it filled the earth, the sea, and even the air itself. This caused great harm not only to human beings, estates, and livestock, but also destroyed all the fish and birds, and completely buried two entire cities, HERCULANEUM and POMPEII, while the people were seated in the theater—not, certainly, for the sake of some spectacle, but because they hoped for safety there and trusted that the place was better fortified against falling ruins.

And this is that very city, whether called Herculanum or Herculaneum, which Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Book I of the *Roman Antiquities*, chapter 44, reports to have been founded by Hercules after his Spanish expedition. After it had been violently shaken under Nero in A.D. 63 and had collapsed in great part, and after it had been completely swallowed up around A.D. 80, it was discovered in the year 1713, and thus after sixteen full centuries. It has cast an immense light upon ancient history and enriched the treasury of Neapolitan antiquities with the richest additions of a new [Col. 1242B] and inestimable treasure. The entire literary world awaits with intense longing the fullest publication of it, which is now laboring under the press.

### *De die*

He meant *in dies*, “day by day” or “daily,” or, as we are otherwise accustomed to say, *de die in diem*, “from day to day.” In this sense our author also says in the *Apology*, chapter 16:

> You live in a novel fashion *de die*—from day to day.

So too Terence, in the *Adelphi*, spoke of “preparing a banquet *de die*,” that is, on each successive day.

### *Sperent*: “let them hope,” or “let them fear”

For *sperare*, “to hope”—just as hope is otherwise by its very nature joined with fear—was also customarily used among the ancients in this unfavorable sense. Thus Virgil, *Aeneid* I:

> If you despise the human race and mortal arms,  
> Yet fear the gods, who remember right and wrong.

And likewise Book IV:

> If I could have feared so great a grief as this—

### [Col. 1242C] *Ignis intrinsecus foeta*

Are the mountains themselves “pregnant”? But who would dare charge Tertullian with so great a grammatical lapse? Should one read *ignis intrinsecus foeti*, “pregnant inwardly with fire”? Yet in that case it would again be ambiguous whether *foeti* should be referred to the mountains, which are pregnant with fire—or rather made pregnant by fire—or to the fire, as though the fire itself were pregnant with sulphur, etc. Or is it preferable, with Latinius, to read *foetu*, “with offspring” or “with fecundity”? I myself certainly think so.

### *Nobis judicii*

That is, “for us of judgment”—of punishment, or of infernal penalty. See above, chapter III, the note on *In judicium*.

### Yet they are never ended

There is no age whose history does not recount such rages of mountains. As regards eruptions of Vesuvius, Kircher indeed supplies a chronicle of this kind, in which he reviews eighteen instances of Etna raging since the first arrival of the Janigenae in Sicily. Others enumerate more than twenty such grim instances of Vesuvius from the time of Christ down to our own. That these enormous furnaces are kindled with perpetual fire is made clearer than daylight by the continuous, dense volumes of smoke which they belch forth even while at rest.

[Col. 1242D] Furthermore, a difficult question has long exercised philosophers: from where come those perpetual conflagrations of such mountains, and the inexhaustible fuel required to sustain such fires through so many centuries? For it might seem that all the subterranean storehouses of fire, and hell itself, however immense, ought long ago to have been emptied through those fire-breathing vents.

Since a longer treatment of this matter lies outside our present purpose, we shall disclose our opinion only briefly.

First, we note that the material cast out by those dreadful convulsions of Etna accumulates chiefly upon the rim of its crater, and sometimes to such a height that it may appear to be a new mountain, or at least a considerable part of a mountain.

Second, this heap of ashes and other ejected matter, already impregnated by itself with various salts, is also commonly enriched by new subterranean additions through hidden channels, especially from the sea, as well as from melted snow—with which the summits of these [Col. 1243A] mountains are almost continually white—from the surrounding salt-laden and nitrous air, etc. This continues until the material, baked by the heat of the underlying fire and melted as though into a pulp, is driven into the utmost fury by subterranean air passages or even by water flowing in from subterranean reservoirs, etc. How great a disturbance even a single drop of water sprinkled upon molten metal in smelting furnaces can raise, and what an incredible devastation of everything it can produce, quite a few persons have at times learned at no small cost to themselves.

### Meanwhile, the punishments of the mountains

He calls “punishments of the mountains” the injuries inflicted by these very mountains, especially for the chastisement of sinners, etc.

### Not of the Judgment

He rather clearly intimates that final JUDGMENT, in which the whole world will be consumed by fire, as we said in *On Baptism*, chapter VIII, in the note “Destined for fire,” and in which the damned will hear that terrible sentence:

> Depart from me, accursed, into ETERNAL FIRE, etc.

Even now the SUPREME JUDGE threatens this through the [Col. 1243B] conflagrations of fire-breathing mountains.

### *Deputabit*

Simply for *putabit* or *aestimabit*, “will think” or “will reckon.” Thus our author also says in *Exhortation to Chastity*, chapter 6:

> It was the old dispensation, which in the Gospel is reckoned as new, etc.

So too Plautus, long before, had said in the *Amphitryon*:

> And let there be nothing for which all do not reckon me worthy.

Terence followed him in the *Hecyra*, where he writes:

> By Pollux, my master truly reckons my labor of little value.

### Sparks

By the same right by which he had called the immense craters of those fire-breathing mountains “little smoke-holes,” he now calls their conflagrations “sparks.” And sparks they certainly are if compared with that immense conflagration of HELL. When our sparks happen to burn our hands, the latter are withdrawn in pain at the first touch. Yet sinners have not yet immersed their hands—I will not say in molten lead, for how slight would that be!—but not even in those fiery torrents of Etna or Vesuvius, that is, in those “sparks,” although perhaps they are destined to burn eternally and never be consumed in that infernal abyss, the proper seat of the element of fire. [Col. 1243C]

### Of the inestimable hearth

That is, a hearth whose immense force of burning far surpasses not merely every estimate we can make, but all the powers of imagination.

### Missiles

In antiquity, *missilia* were gifts of coined gold and silver that were sometimes scattered among the people by emperors, or by the Senate and magistrates, in order profitably to win—or, to use a barbarous expression, to “collect”—their applause and favor. Indeed, objects of other kinds were also sometimes distributed as *missilia*, as Suetonius says in his *Caligula*:

> He also scattered largesses of various kinds.

Later, however, the word also passed into military usage, so that weapons discharged against enemies were likewise called *missilia*, “missiles.” Our author did not hesitate even to call thunderbolts *missilia* in Book II of *On the Pallium*, chapter 2.

### Practice-weapons

An *exercitor* is properly one who teaches another through exercise, as may be gathered from Plautus in the *Trinummus*: [Col. 1243D]

> This man, whoever he is, Curculio, is a trainer; he is teaching this man to run.

Hence those weapons discharged in military exercises and mock practice were called *exercitoria*, “practice-weapons,” and by Seneca, in Book II of *Natural Questions*, *lusoria*, “sporting weapons.”

### Of the Lord’s immersion

That is, of Baptism instituted by Christ the Lord.

### Memorials

*Monimenta*, “memorials,” are so called because they have been set up to admonish (*monere*) our mind to preserve the memory of past events. Junius here reads *munimenta*, “fortifications,” because they fortify us in advance against Gehenna.

### Second reserves

An elegant expression. For among the ancients the *subsidia*, “reserves,” were properly the third line in a battle formation, composed of the *triarii*, veterans, and the bravest soldiers. Placed at the rear, they would therefore settle down upon their knees beneath their standards, ready to come to the aid of an army that might be struggling and to restore its wavering line with fresh reinforcement. See Varro, *On the Latin Language*, Book IV.

### [Col. 1244A] Mute indeed

He calls “mute” those creatures that lack articulate speech.

### Souls

Here he uses *animae*, “souls,” for *animalia*, “animals,” as though employing a part for the whole.

### They recognize

For by the instinct of nature itself, divinely implanted in them, they avoid harmful things, seek wholesome ones, and, when they begin to fall ill, quickly look about for remedies. There can be no doubt that this instinct possesses something analogous to human reason.

### Delays

Among surgeons, a *mora* is a certain channel artificially hollowed out, into which a broken leg, already wrapped with bandages, is placed so that it may remain motionless there and “make a stay” (*moram trahere*). In the same way, arrows deeply fixed in the skin “make their stays” there, and sometimes irrevocably, namely when they have penetrated too deeply to be extracted or shaken out by the wounded animals themselves and thus recalled from the wound.

### With dittany

Aristotle indeed affirms this concerning wild goats in Book IX of the *History of Animals*, chapter 6, in these words:

> They say that in the island of Crete wild goats pierced by an arrow seek the herb dittany, for by this the barbed points are expelled from the body. [Col. 1244B]

He confirms the same thing in *On Marvelous Things Heard*, no. 4. Hence Virgil, *Aeneid* XII:

> Then Venus, the mother, shaken by her son’s undeserved pain,  
> Plucks dittany from Cretan Ida,  
> Its stem clothed in downy leaves and crowned  
> With purple flower. Those grasses are not unknown  
> To wild goats when swift arrows lodge in their backs.

But Pliny, Book VIII of the *Natural History*, chapter 27, transfers this to deer. Yet how many things are sometimes invented or reported with excessive credulity and are not verified! Certainly modern investigators of nature maintain profound silence on this matter, evidently possessing nothing from either their own or others’ sufficient experience by which they could properly establish the credibility of this belief of the ancients.

### To give them eyes

That is, to give them sight. For in this way our author also writes in the *Apology*, chapter 21:

> That he might give eyes, for the recognition of truth, to those already polished and deceived by refinement itself.

This verb, otherwise scarcely used by the ancients, was borrowed from his Tertullian by St. Cyprian in the treatise *On the Vanity of Idols*, near the end, where he speaks of the apostles, who gave eyes to the blind and ignorant for the recognition of truth. [Col. 1244C]

### From its own celandine

Aristotle, Book IV of *On the Generation of Animals*, chapter 6, asserts that swallows produce blind chicks, but says nothing about celandine. Pliny, however, affirms it in the passage cited, generally placing too much trust in the reports of others. This belief passed so thoroughly into popular opinion and adhered so tenaciously that even the very name of the swallows—for this bird is called *χελιδών* (*chelidōn*) in Greek—passed to that plant, which our Germans likewise call *Schwalben-Kraut* or *Schwalben-Wurtz*, “swallow-herb.” But the blindness of newly hatched birds—not only swallows but almost all birds, excepting aquatic species—is not properly blindness at all, but merely a covering of the eyelids, which after several days commonly separates gradually and frees the eyes from their coverings.

### Instituted for himself

That is, instituted for him, so that through repentance he might once again be restored to the grace of God.

### The Babylonian king

This is not that Nebuchadnezzar [Col. 1244D] who was otherwise called Saosduchin and who, in the year of the world 3347, defeated Arphaxad, king of the Medes, on the plain of Ragau; nor the other Nebuchadnezzar, otherwise called Nabopolassar, who is believed to have founded the kingdom of the Chaldeans in the year of the world 3378. Rather, it is the latter’s son Nebuchadnezzar, surnamed the Great, who succeeded to the kingdom in the year of the world 3399. He reduced Jehoiakim, king of Judah, to servitude (IV Kings 24:1); after besieging Jerusalem, he carried Jehoiachin into captivity when the latter voluntarily surrendered himself and all his people (ibid., v. 12 ff.); after Jerusalem had been captured, he killed the sons of Zedekiah, the last king of Judah, before his eyes, put out Zedekiah’s eyes, bound him in chains, and brought him to Babylon (IV Kings, final chapter, v. 7); finally, through Nebuzaradan, commander of his army, he burned the House of the Lord, the king’s house, the houses of Jerusalem, and every house with fire (ibid., v. 9), and thus completely extinguished and utterly overthrew the whole kingdom of Judah.

[Col. 1245A] When, as a result of this and the many other victories he had won over the Syrians, Phoenicians, Egypt, etc., he had become far more exalted and excessively unable to govern himself, God at last showed him in a dream the future penalties of his pride—a pride which could no longer be subdued by any other means. He saw a very tall and beautiful tree, cut down by the hand of an angel, stripped of all its ornament, with its root bound by a band of iron and bronze, etc. Daniel interpreted this dream and earnestly exhorted the king to repentance:

> Redeem your sins with alms, and your iniquities with mercies toward the poor; perhaps he will forgive your offenses.

But when the king too stubbornly refused to obey such salutary warnings, even after being granted an interval of twelve months, he was at last walking in the palace of Babylon and said:

> Is this not BABYLON THE GREAT, which I have built as the HOUSE OF THE KING, by the might of MY power and for the glory of MY splendor?

What then? While the words were still in the king’s mouth, a voice fell from heaven:

> TO YOU IT IS SAID, King Nebuchadnezzar: Your kingdom will pass away [Col. 1245B] from you; your dwelling will be with beasts and wild animals; you will eat hay like an ox; and seven times will pass over you, until you know that the MOST HIGH rules in the kingdom of men and gives it to whomever he wills.

What happened next?

> In that same hour the sentence was fulfilled upon Nebuchadnezzar. He was driven from among men, ate hay like an ox, and his body was drenched with the dew of heaven, until his hair grew like that of eagles and his nails like those of birds.

After he had passed seven entire years among beasts in this most wretched condition and had been sufficiently humbled, he himself relates in what follows, at verse 33, what happened next:

> At that time my understanding returned to me, and I attained the honor and splendor of my kingdom, etc.; my magistrates sought me out, I was restored to my kingdom, and still greater magnificence was added to me.

### Like eagles’ talons

Our Vulgate mentions “the nails of birds,” namely birds of prey, among which eagles certainly stand out.

### [Col. 1245C] A leonine horror

Where our Vulgate has “like EAGLES,” the Septuagint has *λεόντων*, “of LIONS”; hence some in former times fell into the opinion that this king had been transformed into a lion.

### Displaying

There are many points in the history of this terrible metamorphosis which are debated rather contentiously among interpreters. They would certainly require more exact discussion if it were permissible once again—for this has already been done sufficiently elsewhere—to transgress the much narrower limits of our undertaking. Yet because our Tertullian set forth this most grievous example above all others, in order by its terror to compel sinners who presume far too proudly upon themselves to serious repentance, we cannot entirely refrain from dwelling upon it briefly.

I therefore assume, first, against Origen—who was otherwise far more devoted than was justifiable to allegorical senses, and was censured for this by St. Jerome in his *Commentary on Daniel* and thereafter by other interpreters—that this is not a mere allegory but an entirely true history. I am not unaware, however, that most of what is said about Nebuchadnezzar, as some maintain, in Isaiah 14 from verse 8 onward is also to be understood allegorically, according to the mind of the holy Fathers and of the Church herself, as referring to the most wretched expulsion from heaven of LUCIFER—the light-bearer, but one soon extinguished and now the king of darkness. [Col. 1245D]

Second, I assume that nothing can safely be asserted in this matter except what the historical wording in Daniel itself requires. Daniel, under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, transmitted this event to us together with even its minute circumstances; consequently, God himself did not will that anything more should be known to us with certainty.

Third, I say that the Babylonian king was not actually changed into an ox or any other beast. For the human soul itself could not have been converted into the soul of an ox; that would certainly have been to change the essences of things. Nor, after the man’s soul had been expelled, did the soul [Col. 1246A] of an ox enter his body as though through some *μετεμψύχωσις* (*metempsychōsis*, transmigration of souls). That could not have occurred naturally, and the wording of the sacred text in no way compels recourse to a miracle. It signifies only that he ate hay LIKE AN OX, not that he changed into an actual ox. Do oxen have hair like eagles’ feathers, or nails like birds’ talons?

Furthermore, if he had assumed the true soul of an ox, Nebuchadnezzar would certainly have felt no punishment from it, nor could he have been brought thereby to knowledge of the true God, since he would have survived in body alone with no power of understanding remaining. Or will someone perhaps say that the soul of a brute was admitted, by a new miracle, into a partnership with the king’s soul in the same body? Or shall we say, finally, that the man’s soul meanwhile fled elsewhere and lived in exile somewhere outside every body—or was reduced entirely to nothing—and later returned by a right of restoration, or was reproduced, that is, newly created, entered its former body, and restored the king to himself? Who would dare devise such monstrosities of opinion?

Fourth, therefore, I say that the king’s transformation did not occur solely in his imagination, whereby he believed himself to be an ox and ate hay. It occurred plainly in the external bearing and appearance of his body, which assumed hair like that of eagles, nails like those of birds, etc. [Col. 1246B] I frame the hypothesis as follows.

First, Nebuchadnezzar was cast out from among men, that is, from their society; yet he did not therefore cease to be a human being. Rather, seized by sudden frenzy and madness, he lost the use of human reason, escaped or broke through the presence of his attendants and every guard, rushed into fields and forests, and, after his clothes had soon become tattered or—as madmen are accustomed to do—had been torn apart, wandered about aimlessly. Then, when human food was no longer available, since he had by then become wholly possessed by the imagination that he was an ox, he turned also to the ox’s fodder and ate hay LIKE AN OX.

Second, from this continual diet, so alien to a human being, there necessarily followed the most profound alteration of the blood, the other bodily fluids, the animal spirits, and that entire temperament, and at last even the outward condition of the whole body was disfigured. Through such a wild and long-continued diet it became savage and horrible in appearance: his hair resembled eagles’ feathers and his nails the talons of birds, so that Nebuchadnezzar, like an *ἀνθρωπόταυρος* (*anthrōpotauros*, “man-bull”), might at last have terrified not only human beings but even herds of oxen. [Col. 1246C] In this way, finally, it seems possible to satisfy Daniel’s account, as will be evident to anyone who weighs the fourth chapter carefully.

But what of the king during this most disastrous seven-year period? Did he retain the use of reason meanwhile and perceive the magnitude of his calamity, almost as Apuleius testifies about himself in his fabulous *Ass*? Our Tertullian indeed seems to have thought that he did, when he says that the king offered repentance for a LONG TIME—namely for as long as that unhappy condition lasted—and performed *exomologesis*, public penitential confession, through seven years of squalor.

Not a few others believe that intervals of reason at least shone upon the king with some frequency, so that he would from time to time recognize the misery of his condition. For, they say, that condition would not have been punitive if he had felt no distress whatever from it [Col. 1246D] and could not have compared it with the happiness of his former dignity, but instead had wandered with pleasure through fields and forests among wild animals in this depraved state of an alienated mind.

Yet, if I may frankly state my opinion, I incline to the view that the king was wholly deprived of the use of reason throughout that entire period of seven years. The sacred text does not compel us to think otherwise:

> Therefore, he says, AFTER THE END OF THE DAYS, I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted my eyes toward heaven—

He would have raised them more often if, during certain intervals of reason, he had recognized the unhappiness of his condition—

> —and MY UNDERSTANDING—

By this word the use of reason, or the mind’s consciousness and perception, is plainly denoted—

> —was restored to me, and I blessed the Most High, etc.

He confirms the same by that impassioned discourse in which, celebrating GOD’s praises, glory, power, and kingdom, he continues:

> AT THAT TIME my understanding returned to me, and my appearance returned to me, etc.

Thus only then [Col. 1247A] did he at last recognize God, when he recovered both his intellect and his former human appearance.

It is helpful to explain the matter further in this way. When those seven years had elapsed, the king awoke as though from a deep sleep. Those dark spirits which until then had besieged his mind from every side were dispersed—and this could not have happened so suddenly without a miracle. The memory of his monstrous pride and of the deeds he had committed with utmost wickedness because of it returned; the entire misery of his prolonged wretchedness and all its circumstances were vividly presented to his intellect. His pride was at last subdued, and he was driven to acknowledge the true God and to profess both his JUSTICE and his MERCY; thus, finally, he was converted, etc.

### Alas, what ill-treatment!

This is a Hellenism, for the Greeks frequently join the genitive to expressions of exclamation.

### God was receiving him

Did this king practice true repentance, profitable for eternal salvation, by which his sins were erased? And did he thereafter persevere in it and finally obtain salvation itself among the truly penitent? The matter is highly uncertain.

St. Jerome, in Letter VII to Laeta, and St. Augustine, in Letter CXXII—now CXI—to Victorianus, and especially the author of the book *On Predestination and Grace*, chapter 15, formerly attributed to St. Augustine, do seem to favor him. To others, however, that repentance is highly suspect, for they measure it by this king’s inconstant disposition. [Col. 1247B]

Certainly, after he had received from Daniel the interpretation of his dream concerning that STATUE composed of four metals, he was wholly astounded and burst out in these words:

> Truly your God is the GOD OF GODS and the LORD OF KINGS and a revealer of mysteries, etc. (Dan. 3:47)

But how unstable he was in his recognition of the true God, and with how great a pride he afterward swelled up again, is demonstrated by that second dream which followed—of the most beautiful tree soon to be cut down, etc. (Dan. 4)—and then by the terrible sentence pronounced against the king, condemning him to seven years of exile among beasts. For although God still granted him [Col. 1247C] an entire year in which to redeem his sins through almsgiving and other good works, we read that he did none of these things, but rather, swollen with fresh pride, boasted insolently of his own achievements.

And what happened besides? After he had spent seven full years among wild beasts in that most wretched bodily condition, and after he had recovered not only his former form but the kingdom itself, indeed increased with greater magnificence, what then? We read that he finally exclaimed:

> Now, therefore, I, Nebuchadnezzar, praise, magnify, and glorify the King of heaven, because all his works are true and his ways are judgments, and he is able to humble those who walk in pride.

Yet concerning repentance, sorrow of mind, detestation of his former life, a resolve upon a new and better one, etc., we read nothing. And these are precisely the things in which the nature of true and sincere repentance consists.

But how brief was even that acknowledgment of God and that humility? Behold a far greater madness of a mind swelling up anew—indeed a madness wholly incredible, were it not attested by the Prophet! Scarcely a year had elapsed [Col. 1247D], as the interpreters calculate, when:

> King Nebuchadnezzar made a golden statue, sixty cubits high, and set it up in the plain of Dura (Dan. 3:1, etc.).

This statue displayed the likeness either of Nabopolassar, his father, or of Bel, then the greatest of the Babylonian gods, or of Nebuchadnezzar himself; and by the publication of a most severe edict he ordered it to be worshiped. When those three young men, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, refused to commit this sacrilegious crime, he ordered them cast into a fiery furnace. When they were brought forth unharmed, the king, astounded by this new and enormous miracle, exclaimed:

> Blessed be the GOD OF GODS, who sent his angel, etc. (ibid., v. 95).

Indeed, by a public and extremely severe decree he restrained blasphemers against the God of Israel, publicly declaring:

> The MOST HIGH GOD has worked signs and wonders in my presence. It has therefore pleased me to proclaim his signs, because they are GREAT, and his wonders, because they are MIGHTY; his kingdom is an EVERLASTING KINGDOM, and his POWER endures from generation to generation [Col. 1248A] (ibid., v. 10).

This, however, is the very edict in which Nebuchadnezzar proceeds to recount the events that we cited above from Daniel 4 concerning his grim seven-year period in the forests. It is therefore necessary to conclude that the king’s metamorphosis—which should be carefully noted—occurred before this history of the three young men.

Who, I ask, could now promise himself that a king so inconstant possessed sincere and stable repentance, when Daniel thereafter maintains so profound a silence concerning his works, worthy fruits of repentance, and especially a blessed death? How different was the repentance of Manasseh, king of Judah! He was a most impious king, and perhaps even far more criminal than Nebuchadnezzar himself; nevertheless:

> After he was brought into distress, he prayed to the Lord his God and repented greatly before the God of his fathers; he prayed to him and earnestly besought him . . . and removed the foreign gods and the idol from the House of the Lord. . . . Moreover, he restored the altar of the Lord and sacrificed upon it victims, peace offerings, and praise; and he commanded Judah to serve the Lord God of Israel.

What similar things, I ask, do we read as recorded concerning Nebuchadnezzar? He indeed praised and magnified God, etc.; but did he pray, supplicate, and earnestly beseech him, etc.? [Col. 1248B]

And what, finally, if certain distinguished interpreters, including St. Jerome himself, wish the words of Isaiah 14—

> Hell beneath you was disturbed to meet you at your coming. . . . Your pride has been dragged down to hell, etc., etc.—

to be understood of Nebuchadnezzar? But it is better to leave either this king’s repentance and salvation or his damnation among those matters which God, like many other things of this kind, willed to remain unknown to us for the sake of salutary fear and thereby of greater constancy in repentance.

### The Egyptian emperor

That is, Pharaoh, king of Egypt, who is here called “emperor” in a broader sense, since, like other kings, he exercised supreme command over his subjects.

### Long denied to his Lord

For Moses had been commanded by God [Col. 1248C] to proclaim to Pharaoh in these words:

> The Lord God of the Hebrews has sent me to you, saying: Let my people go, that they may sacrifice to me in the desert; and until the present time you have refused to listen.

### By the cleaving of the sea

That is, by that division which the Greeks call *διχοτομία* (*dichotomia*, “bisection”). So too in Book II of *On the Pallium*, chapter 2, he speaks of “the wound of division,” and in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 19, of “the division of flesh and soul.” Solinus likewise used this word in chapter 36, saying:

> The Atlantic tide introduces into our sea the division—or severing—of the world.

### It was available as passable

*Liceo*, an ancient verb, has a passive meaning. Properly, a thing is said *licere* when it is valued at a definite price and offered for sale, while *liceri* means to value it and place a price upon it, as buyers do. Hence the poet’s line:

> The man who does not bid an *as* for me is not worth an *as* to me.

The sea therefore *licebat*, “was offered,” when it was presented by God to the Israelites [Col. 1248D] as passable for their crossing. Elsewhere Tertullian also uses *licere* for *liquere* or *patere*, “to be clear” or “to lie open,” as in *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 36:

> But concerning motion it will be clear elsewhere—

that is, *liquebit*. Similarly, above in chapter VI, for *emendatos liquebit*, “it will be clear that they have been corrected,” some manuscripts substitute *licebit*.
