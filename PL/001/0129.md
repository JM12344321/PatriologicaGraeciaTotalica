# Book on Repentance

[Col. 1223]

# The Book of Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullian, *On Repentance* (C)

## Preface

[Col. 1223C] The subject matter of the book itself teaches that Tertullian wrote the book *On Repentance* at a time when he was not yet following Montanus’s doctrines. For in it those who have fallen after Baptism are urged to repentance, which the Montanists denied ought to be granted:

“Therefore, for all sins, whether committed by flesh or spirit, by deed or by will, the same one who appointed punishment through judgment also promised pardon through repentance, saying: ‘As I live, says the Lord, I prefer repentance to death’” (below, chapter 4).

And in chapter 7 he expressly attacks one of the principal errors of the Montanists by demonstrating that those who [Col. 1223D] committed sins after Baptism can obtain absolution from the Church, provided that they have repented of them.

No one either formerly doubted or now doubts whether this book is Tertullian’s. Erasmus of Rotterdam alone attempted to take it away from Tertullian, and only Beatus Rhenanus followed him; both relied on this single and very weak argument: a difference of style. I do not deny that the style in this book is somewhat more florid, but it does not differ greatly from that of the other works, even those he wrote near the end of his life; the difference lies only in certain somewhat more brilliant allegories and metaphors. [Col. 1224C] Yet if there were leisure to pluck similar blossoms from the other garden plots of the same author, I could easily weave entire garlands.

Moreover, critics of the highest standing long ago perceived that from any mere discrepancy in style, unless reinforced by other and weightier arguments, an author can scarcely be rendered suspect—to say nothing of deciding anything against his authorship. Of these critics I cite merely William Cave, who, in section 4, number 64, of the Prolegomena to his *Literary History*, says that an argument for spuriousness is proved only if the style and the entire texture of the discourse are widely different from the mode of expression [Col. 1224D] employed by the author in his genuine works.

Yet any reader will discover that the style is so far from being widely different that, apart from a few rather elevated phrases scarcely encountered in Tertullian’s other writings, that African and concise mode of expression almost peculiar to Tertullian alone; that distinctive majesty of style; that abundance of thought in nearly every individual word; and that lofty eloquence which, however harsh it may be, nevertheless somehow carries even an unwilling reader away—all triumph no less in this book. I say nothing of other phrases so carefully reproduced from other books by the same author. [Col. 1225A]

Beatus Rhenanus himself then admits that the author of this book uses the same words and figures, and that, whoever he was, he unquestionably belonged to Tertullian’s century and was a most devoted student of Tertullian. Why, then, should he not be Tertullian himself? Who else could have reproduced his certainly singular and scarcely imitable style so skillfully that he deceived St. Pacian, a writer of the fourth century, and everyone else for more than eleven centuries down to Erasmus, so that no one ever detected from a difference of style that this book might have another author?

We therefore judge that nothing more can be inferred from this discrepancy in style, whatever its extent may ultimately be, than that Tertullian undertook this composition while still of more vigorous age and livelier talent. For minds of this ardent and nimble kind customarily fly about various flowers like [Col. 1225B] bees, suck allegories and metaphors even from unrelated subjects, and carry them back into their own hive. Style likewise tends gradually to settle with advancing age and to become more restrained and robust.

Finally, one will not easily find any writer who remains perpetually consistent with himself, especially if he has not carried his pen through one continuous work but through diverse and disconnected subjects, interrupted by varying occasions and by the very passage of years. One subject is by its nature rather gloomy, humble, and barren, sufficiently blunting and depressing even the vigor of youthful talent, and upon it the otherwise rich eloquence of men seems to wither. Another subject, by contrast, is more pleasant and attractive and better suited to the author’s own talent, and can sufficiently arouse and stimulate an otherwise listless mind. Finally, another is so lively and radiates such brilliance that it can utterly kindle and inflame the writer and sometimes even revive sparks long dormant in one already gray with age. [Col. 1225C]

There are also other things that come to a writer from elsewhere and, as it were, indirectly, which can nevertheless sometimes do much to alter his disposition, so to speak. What wonder is it, then, if Tertullian too is not everywhere consistent with himself, but when he first applied himself to writing—although already of mature age—employed a somewhat livelier style, which afterward became rather more settled in his other works, as though compensating for this by adorning them [Col. 1225D] with a greater apparatus of learning?

If, however, we must now indicate somewhat more closely the actual time when he composed this treatise—although this cannot be done precisely—I think that it was certainly committed to writing before the year of Christ 200, probably in 197 or perhaps earlier. Certainly the times when this work and the other, *On Baptism*, were written were not far apart. Yet it can hardly be determined whether, in the order of composition, he placed the book *On Baptism* after this one *On Repentance*, or this one after that.

For my part, however, it seems more probable that he placed this one afterward. For if he had written *On Repentance* first, I believe that in the last chapter of *On Baptism*, where he discusses repentance to be placed before Baptism as a kind of preliminary prayer, he would have made some mention of this subject as already treated more fully elsewhere. [Col. 1226A] Certainly, in the book *On Baptism* itself, at the end of chapter 15, he carefully refers his readers to another treatise on the same subject, previously composed in Greek.

Who, then, would seem to judge badly if he said that Tertullian afterward adopted a new plan and undertook a fuller treatment in a separate book of this subject of repentance, which he had touched upon somewhat briefly at the end of the book *On Baptism*—and that he did so partly for the benefit of catechumens, so that they might conceive a higher estimation of Baptism’s value when they understood that it had to be obtained through such laborious works of repentance? Why, then, should they not thereafter exercise themselves in these works with all the more ardent zeal, so that they might obtain with greater certainty the reward of Baptism, so generously offered, and its grace, and might afterward preserve it [Col. 1226B] all the more securely?

Finally, we should hear the distinguished judgment concerning this book delivered by Beatus Rhenanus himself, a man of great discernment, in the argument prefixed to it: namely, that this commentary is of such a kind that theologians ought to learn it to perfection. “For,” he continues, “it is an excellent monument of antiquity; so sacredly does it teach, so devoutly persuade, and so insistently press that matter supremely necessary to ecclesiastical discipline—*exomologesis*, that is, the act and ministry of repentance.”

Certainly, just as we everywhere learn from Tertullian the traditions and discipline of the ancient Church, so here too, with signal usefulness against our modern innovators, we are taught beautifully and solidly the manner of performing repentance as it was customarily practiced from the earliest centuries, and assuredly by apostolic institution. [Col. 1226C] LUMP.

## Argument

At the beginning he treats repentance in general (chapter 1) and establishes that it is necessary for all sins and transgressions, whether committed by flesh or spirit, by deed or by will (chapter 4). He then distinguishes two kinds of repentance. The first is that which catechumens must place before Baptism, or which is performed before Baptism (chapter 6). The second is that of those who, after unhappily falling into grave sins after Baptism, rise from them through laborious repentance.

In the first part he insists upon the necessity of proving oneself and preparing oneself for the grace of Baptism through true repentance. He does not hesitate to assert that Baptism is fruitless when a person has not repented of his sins or amended his life, [Col. 1226D] and that it is vain confidence for someone who has lived wickedly up to the very day of Baptism to imagine that he will suddenly become a holy man and cease from sin as soon as he has received that sacrament (chapter 6).

The latter part of this treatise concerns repentance after Baptism, which is called *Exomologesis*. He immediately declares (chapter 7) that he speaks reluctantly about this latter repentance, which is the final hope remaining for those who have bound themselves by crimes—that is, atrocious sins—after Baptism, lest, he says, by discussing this new means of rising from sin offered to us by God, he should appear to open a way to sin anew.

He afterward depicts the laborious exercises of that public repentance called *Exomologesis* (chapter 9), demonstrates its necessity (chapter 11), and finally rebukes those who were ashamed to embrace it after committing crimes subsequent to Baptism (chapter 10).

# Chapter I

[Col. 1227A]

Repentance—

[Note: [Col. 1227B] Much has been said, both formerly and more recently, with learning and wisdom about the subject of this book; but much concerning the various forms of repentance among the ancient Christians—especially that called *Exomologesis*, with public satisfaction for crimes—has also been confused with it, both formerly and recently, in a perverse and unsound manner. Nor have some of Tertullian’s best commentators escaped rather severe criticism. No one, therefore, should reproach us if, at the beginning of this book, we briefly touch upon a few principal points [Col. 1227C] and establish the chief conclusions concerning the institution, development, and cessation of solemn repentance.

Among the ancients after Tertullian, the following undertook to describe the same subject at greater or lesser length: Blessed Cyprian, in his books and letters concerning the lapsed and against Novatian; St. Pacian likewise, in his book and second letter to Sympronianus and in his *Exhortation to Repentance*; St. Ambrose, in book II of *On Repentance*; the homilies and discourses on repentance of John Chrysostom and of the blessed (*τοῦ Μακαρίου*) Ephrem; and Fulgentius, in book II of *On the Remission of Sins*, addressed to Euthymius.

Among more recent writers, the most learned Morinus, *On the Sacraments*, book IV, chapters 9 and 10, stands for all and is second to none on this subject. It will therefore suffice here to present his entire teaching reduced to a few brief headings. With that most distinguished man, then, it must be maintained:

1. From the beginning of the Church until the heresy of Montanus—that is, until about the year 175—repentance was shorter and imposed more leniently.

2. From the age of Montanus until Novatus [Col. 1227D]—that is, until about the year 250—repentance was light and brief when compared with the severity of the subsequent age; nevertheless, it then increased somewhat in manner and duration, although the latter had not yet been specifically determined by the Church.

3. After the Novatian schism, repentance was more severe and prolonged than before, and fixed periods of time were prescribed for it, to be completed in various classes; this practice prevailed in the Eastern Church earlier than in the Western.

4. In the Eastern Church after the fourth century, and in the Western after the seventh, the solemn discipline of repentance greatly diminished, and the aforesaid stations of repentance almost fell into disuse.

5. Not all mortal sins, even public ones, nor all clerics publicly guilty of them—at least those in the higher orders—were subject to solemn repentance.

6. Public repentance, except perhaps in the case of the crime of heresy, was never repeated.

The disputed question whether, once the remedy of public [Col. 1228B] repentance had been withdrawn from a relapsed sinner, he no longer had any remedy in the power of the keys, is argued on both sides. Albaspineus affirms this in book II, observation 5; Morinus, book V, chapter 27; and Petavius, in his notes on Epiphanius at heresy 59. Natalis Alexander opposes it in dissertation 10 on the history of the third century; this is also Tournely’s opinion, and that latter view seems altogether to be maintained. Compare *Complete Course of Theology*, vol. 22, p. 691.

Above all, consult the distinguished Father Corbinianus Thomas’s lucid commentaries on this very little book of Tertullian. It would have been highly desirable to insert them continuously at the relevant passages; but because they were finally obtained and received only with excessive difficulty and after considerable delay, they had to be placed at the end of this edition of Tertullian, to which we refer the reader. # Chapter III

The subject itself requires us to indicate what things repentance may rightly and duly concern—that is, what things are to be reckoned as sin—but this may appear unnecessary.

[Note: [Col. 1231D] **This may appear unnecessary.** He judges that discussion of the piety and religion of catechumens can be omitted, since, because they had examined God’s law and voluntarily embraced repentance, they required neither praises of it nor exhortations to it. LE PR.]

For once the Lord is known, the spirit, having been regarded by its own Author, rises of its own accord to the knowledge of the truth; and, having been admitted to the Lord’s commandments, it is immediately instructed by those commandments themselves that whatever God forbids must be reckoned as sin. For since it is established that God is the supreme good, surely nothing but evil could displease the good, because there is no friendship between contraries. [Col. 1231B]

Nevertheless, it will not be burdensome to note briefly that some sins are carnal—that is, bodily—while others are spiritual. For since man is composed from the union of this twofold substance, he sins from no other source than that of which he consists. Yet these sins do not differ from one another because body and spirit are two things. On the contrary, they are for that very reason all the more equal, because the two make one. No one, therefore, should distinguish their sins according to the diversity of their substances, supposing one to be lighter or more serious than the other.

For both flesh and spirit are things of God: the one fashioned by his hand, the other completed by his breath (Gen. 2). Since, therefore, they belong equally to the Lord, whichever of them sins equally offends the Lord. Would you distinguish the acts of flesh and spirit, although they possess such great communion and fellowship in life, in death, and in resurrection [Col. 1231C] (John 5), that they will then be raised together either to life or to judgment—because, of course, they have together either sinned or acted innocently?

[Note: [Col. 1232C] “As innocents,” Rig.]

We have set this premise down so that we may understand that, if either part sins, the necessity of repentance [Col. 1232A] rests no less upon that one part than upon both. Their guilt is shared, and their judge is also shared—namely, God; therefore the remedy of repentance is also shared.

Sins are accordingly called spiritual and bodily because every sin is either enacted or conceived in thought. What consists in deed is bodily, because a deed, like a body, can be seen and touched. What exists in the mind, however, is spiritual, because spirit can neither be seen nor grasped. This demonstrates that not only sins of deed but also sins of will must be avoided and cleansed by repentance.

Moreover, is not the will the origin of the deed? Let those cases be considered separately that are imputed to accident, necessity, or ignorance; once these are excepted, sin is committed only by the will. Since, therefore, the will is the origin of the deed, is it not so much the more liable to punishment as it is principal in guilt?
