It is the Greek word ὅραμα (*horama*), meaning a sight or vision. It is used in Acts 12:9. Latin writers used it more rarely; nevertheless Aldhelm, *On Virginity*, chapter 3, where he speaks of St. John the Evangelist, says that he was “caught up on Patmos in an *oroma*.” There Meursius correctly substituted *oramate* in his *Mantissa Critica*—and I find it printed in this form in the *Library of the Holy Fathers*—despite the pointless objection of Swartius, *Analecta*, book III, chapter 9, who tries in vain to persuade us that *chroma* should be restored there. **I see the Egyptian opposite me rolling in the *apha*.** *Apha* means dust in Greek; it is the Greek noun ἁφή written in Latin letters. Boxers preparing to fight customarily sprinkled their oiled bodies with dust, or rolled in it as it lay on the ground. The sand from which the boxers’ bodies thus became yellow is called *Haphe* by Martial, book VI, Epigram 66, in this verse:

He is speaking of the woman Philaenis, who imitated men in boxing. **And he called for silence.** This was customarily done through a herald or trumpeter. From Lucian and others we learn that the conventional formula for this in Greece was ἄκουε πᾶς, “Hear, everyone.” This practice was especially employed when prizes were to be publicly distributed. For that reason Apuleius, in the *Florida*, places a herald beside the proconsul on such an occasion. Seneca, Epistle 74, provides testimony concerning the trumpeter: “Let us too overcome all things, whose prizes are neither a crown nor a cloak, nor a trumpeter imposing silence for the proclamation of our name, but virtue and steadfastness of mind.” HOLST.

**If she defeats him, she will receive this branch.** For here he assumed not only the role of superintendent of the gymnasium, as was said above, but also that of the βραβευτής, the awarder of the prize. “The *brabeutae*, or awarders of prizes,” says the *Etymologicum*, “are properly called as though they were *rhabdeustae*—that is, givers of staffs or shoots; for they gave branches of palm or of some similar tree as tokens of victory”: βραβευταὶ κυρίως λέγονται, οἱ τὴν ῥάβδον ἀπὸ φοίνικος ἢ τινος ἄλλου διδόντες σύμβολα τῆς νίκης, ῥαβδευταί τινες ὄντες. **And I approached the gladiatorial trainer.** She calls the person whom we described above rather as superintendent of the gymnasium and awarder of the prize a “gladiatorial trainer,” perhaps not without a certain misuse of the term. HOLST.

**To the Gate of Life.** The passage requires the restoration: “To the *Sanavivarian* Gate,” as below, § XX, verse 24: “They were called back to the *Sanavivarian* Gate.” There perhaps *Sanavivaria* should be restored from the earlier passage. From these places it is clear that there was a gate in the amphitheater which, at least in Africa, was popularly called the *Sanavivaria*. What gate this was must be investigated. Lipsius, *On the Amphitheater*, chapter 21, teaches that, besides various small gates or posterns surrounding the lowest part of the amphitheater and serving various purposes, especially the release of wild beasts, there were two larger gates directly opposite one another. He conjectures that one was called the Libitinarian Gate, and a little later he himself calls it the Gate of the Dead; through it, evidently, the dead bodies of gladiators or the remains of beast-fighters were dragged out of the arena. He gives no name for the other gate, which, as he says, led to the forum. I do not doubt that, if he had seen this passage, he would have affirmed that it was called the *Sanavivarian* Gate. Nor is the etymology obscure: since those who had survived the dangers of the arena safe and alive were through it restored to the forum and the city, it was rightly named *Sanavivaria*, the Gate of the Safe and Living. The two passages here favor this conjecture. In the first, after Perpetua had conquered in the boxing match and overcome her Egyptian adversary in the arena, she began, as she herself says, “to go in glory to the *Sanavivarian* Gate”—evidently in the manner of those victorious in such contests, who after victory went out amid applause through the gate that led to the forum. The other passage, § XX, relates that the crowd, moved by compassion for Saints Perpetua and Felicitas after they had been badly mauled by the beasts, called them back to the *Sanavivarian* Gate. Evidently gladiators or beast-fighters whom the people spared withdrew there, admonished by the crowd’s favorable acclamation, so that they might escape the danger. POSSIN.

**Behold, they are here; behold, they are here.** These are the words of those pointing people out with honor, like “This is he,” and similar expressions used when people known by reputation were pointed out by the fingers of those who met them. POSSIN.

**We crossed a stadium along a broad road.** The reading must certainly be “along a broad road,” for in the vision this was adapted to a practice then commonly known. Walkways in gardens and porticoes were customarily measured by stadia. The Glosses of Galen: Σταδιεύς, ὁ σταδίοις ἀναμετρῶν τὸν περίπατον, “a measurer by stadia: one who measures a walkway in stadia.” Hence Vitruvius, book V, chapter 9, speaks of “stadium-porticoes,” where he advises that square or oblong peristyles in wrestling schools be constructed so as to have a circuit of two stadia for walking. **They were burned alive.** Law 28, *Digest*, *On Punishments*, calls burning alive the supreme punishment. Hence the people of Smyrna, after demanding a lion for St. Polycarp, subsequently demanded ζῶντα κατακαῦσαι, “that he be burned alive.” Rufinus thus elegantly translated the words of Eusebius. During the Alexandrian persecution under Decius, this burning alive was likewise frequent, as may be seen from the letters of Dionysius in Eusebius, book VI, chapter 34. HOLST.

**And they drove them away.** The expression seems to be used in the sense of “they pushed them away”—that is, removed them to a distance, as though they had advanced beyond what was permissible. For it is added: “And it seemed to us as though they wished to close the gates.” POSSIN.

**Correct your people, etc.** A rebuke of a negligent or erring bishop is not foreign to a vision granted for the benefit of a particular church. Thus certain bishops are rebuked in the Apocalypse. POSSIN.

**As though returning from the circus and contending over their weariness.** So the manuscript; but it should undoubtedly be read, “contending over factions.” This passage signifies that the church of Thuburbo had been divided into parties by the factions and disputes of Optatus the bishop and Aspasius the presbyter. Hence the angels had said to them a little above, § XIII, verse 17: “If you have any disagreements among yourselves, forgive one another.” HOLST.

**CHAPTER V.—I. But God summoned Secundulus from the world while he was still in prison, by an earlier departure.** Cyprian, Epistle VII, concerning one who similarly died beforehand: “Withdrawn by divine favor in the very midst of the torments, he attained glory not through the final completion of his punishment, but through the swiftness of his death.” For, as Seneca says, *Consolation to Marcia*, chapter 20: “Death loosens the chains of captives and leads out of prison those whom powerless authority had forbidden to leave.” HOLST.

**Nevertheless, though not his soul, his flesh certainly knew the sword.** It would seem preferable to read: “Nevertheless, though not his flesh, his soul certainly knew the sword,” etc. By “sword,” evidently, he means the torments and hardships of prison, in accordance with Psalm 104 concerning Joseph: “They humbled his feet in fetters; iron passed through his soul.” And concerning the Blessed Mother of God, Luke 2: “A sword will pass through your own soul.” He means that Secundulus was no less a martyr than the others. For, as Cyprian says in *On Mortality*: “Among the servants of God, in whom confession is contemplated and martyrdom is conceived in the mind, a spirit devoted to the good is crowned by God as judge. It is one thing for the will to be lacking for martyrdom, and another for martyrdom to have been lacking to the will. As the Lord finds you when he calls, so likewise does he judge you. For God seeks not our blood, but our faith.” HOLST.

**II.—Lest she be delayed because of her womb.** Thus Pontius the deacon concerning St. Cyprian: “A man so necessary was delayed from the completion of martyrdom.” HOLST.

**It is not permitted for pregnant women to be presented for punishment.** This was a very ancient Egyptian law: τὴν ἔγκυον, ἂν ἁλῷ θανάτου, μέχρι τέκῃ, φυλάττειν, “If a pregnant woman has been condemned to death, she is to be preserved until she gives birth.” Plutarch reports this in *On the Delays of Divine Vengeance*, where he also records that the Greeks adopted the same law from the Egyptians; Aelian confirms this when, in *Various History*, book V, chapter 18, he relates that it was observed by the Areopagite Council. It must therefore also have been given a place in the collection of Attic laws.

Furthermore, together with other good laws, this law also passed from the Greeks to the Romans. Ulpian, Law 3, *Digest*, *On Punishments*: “The punishment of a pregnant woman condemned to death is deferred until she gives birth. Indeed, I know that it is observed that she is not even subjected to judicial torture so long as she is pregnant.” And Law 18, *Digest*, *On the Status of Persons*, records this rescript of the emperor Hadrian: “A free woman who, while pregnant, has been condemned to the ultimate punishment gives birth to a free child, and it is customary for her to be preserved until she has delivered her offspring.”

Philo, in his book περὶ φιλανθρωπίας, *On Humanity*, testifies that Moses was so far from excluding this most equitable law from his commonwealth that he extended it even to brute animals:

> ἀλλ’ ὅ γε νόμος ἔξω περιῤῥαντηρίων ἐλαύνει καὶ ὅσα τῶν ζώων κυοφορεῖ· μὴ ἐπιτρέπων, ἄχρις ἂν ἀποτέκῃ, σφαγιάζεσθαι, τὰ κατὰ γαστρὸς ἐν ἴσῳ τοῖς ἀποκυηθεῖσι λογιζόμενος.

“The law excludes pregnant animals from the sacred precincts and does not permit them to be slaughtered before they have given birth, regarding the offspring enclosed in the womb in the same way as those already born.”

He then adds:

> συνιέναι δὲ μοι δοκοῦσιν ὁρμηθέντες ἔνιοι τῶν νομοθετῶν καὶ τὸν ἐπὶ κατακρίτοις γυναιξὶν εἰσηγήσασθαι λόγον, ὃς κελεύει τὰς ἐγκυούσας, ἂν ἄξια θανάτου δρῶσι, μὴ θανεῖν, μέχρις ἂν ἀποτέκωσιν· ἵνα μὴ ἀναιρουμένων συναπόληται τὸ κατὰ γαστρός.

“Hence, it seems to me that certain legislators, proceeding from a common understanding, also established the law concerning women condemned to death, which commands that pregnant women, even if they have committed a capital crime, are not to be put to death before they have given birth, lest the offspring in the womb perish together with them when they are executed.”

**From the assistants of the *cataractarii*.** *Cataractae* are strong gates suspended by ropes or chains in such a way that they can be lowered and raised as circumstances require. Gates of this kind have remained in widespread use at the entrances of cities and fortresses to repel an enemy incursion, and they are commonly called Saracen gates. Either such gates were once used to guard prisoners—hence καταράκτης is used for “prison” in the Septuagint at Jeremiah 20:2, on which passage see Origen, Homily 18, p. 257—or they were barriers or latticed doors. Because they consisted of wood fitted together in a lattice in the form of a portcullis, they were called *cataractae*, and the doorkeepers of prisons were called *cataractarii*. Their assistants were guards keeping watch at the gate. Libanius, *Oration on the Custody of Prisoners*, calls these men τῆς θύρας κυρίους, “masters of the gate,” and arbiters of access to the prisoners. In Acts 12:6, φύλακες πρὸ τῆς θύρας, “guards stationed before the door,” are mentioned. HOLST.

**Another will be in me who will suffer for me.** Namely, Christ suffering in the martyrs. Thus Christ says to Saul when he was persecuting the Christians: “Why do you persecute me?” Acts 9:4. **Why, then, do you not permit them to refresh themselves?** That is, to be fed somewhat more generously and abundantly. HOLST.

**Criminals of the Most Noble Caesar.** That is, of Geta, the son of the emperor Severus, on whose birthday, as stated above, they were to fight. The title “Most Noble” was proper to an emperor’s younger sons. See Photius, *Bibliotheca*, p. 187; Leunclavius, *Pandects of Turkish History*, p. 99. Panvinius, in the *Fasti*, pp. 244–245, reports two inscriptions which establish this. The same title appears on a coin of this very Geta in Hulsius, with the figure of *Nobilitas* on the obverse; likewise in Biaeus.

Furthermore, he calls them *noxii*, “criminals,” using the term in the sense of the pagans, who inflicted the extreme penalties appropriate to criminals upon the most innocent class of human beings. Yet we find that, in general, those condemned to death for any cause—even apart from a crime—were called *noxii* when assigned to beast-fights or gladiatorial games. Thus Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 16, calls those hired fighters *noxii* who voluntarily bound themselves over to the gladiatorial trainer for combat with the sword, or freely went to fight the beasts and, as St. Cyprian says, “fought the beasts not from crime but from madness.” Tertullian nevertheless notes that Christians were sometimes distinguished from *noxii* in the persecutors’ own records, truth occasionally escaping from liars.

But to return to the more common usage, the Latin word *noxii* did not always correspond to the Greek κακοῦργοι, “criminals,” but sometimes to ἐπιθανάτιοι, “those destined for death,” indicating persons marked out to die either by gladiatorial weapons or by the teeth of wild beasts. They were called beast-fighters, persons assigned to the games, and slaves of punishment. Hence Tertullian, *On Modesty*, learnedly translates St. Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 4:9, ἐπιθανατίους ἀπέδειξεν ὡς, as: “He chose us as though we were beast-fighters.” Quintilian, *Declamation* 9, calls them “slaves owed to punishment, who were fed for the games and for certain death.” HOLST.
