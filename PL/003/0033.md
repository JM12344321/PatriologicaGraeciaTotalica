**The Phrygians, the Mother.** There is no need, with Sigismund Gelenius, Laurentius Barrensis, Fulvius Ursinus, and Christian Cellarius, who followed them, to restore “the Phrygians, the Great Mother”; for the sole manuscript resists this, and Cybele is sometimes called “Mother” *par excellence* (κατ’ ἐξοχήν). Virgil, *Georgics* IV, 64:

> Raise the clashing din, and shake the Mother’s cymbals all around.

And *Aeneid* III, 3:

> Hence the Mother, dweller on Cybele, and the Corybantian bronze.

For thus, with Servius, I think the passage should be read, not “Cybele,” as the more recent editions have it. So too Arnobius, book V, p. 167. **DAVIS.**

**The Romans, all.** Shortly afterward he will say: “They adopted the rites of all nations.” And a little later: “They initiated the rites of all religions.” **RIGALT.**

**Extended.** It must certainly be written *propagarunt*, “they extended.” **HEUM.** There is no need, since the singular refers back to “their power and authority.” **LINDNER.**

**While they exercise.** That is, the Romans; it ought to be *exercet*, “it exercises,” like the preceding *propagavit*, “it extended.” But the best authors are accustomed to speak in this way. Caesar, *Gallic War* VI, 24: “That nation down to the present time confines itself within those territories and possesses the highest reputation for justice and military renown, and now remains in the same poverty, want, and endurance as the Germans.” **LINDNER.**

**While they fortify the city.** Thus the royal manuscript. Others read “the world.” **LINDNER.**

**Gods whom another would already have despised when angry.** Thus the manuscript, as it seems; and from it Sabaeus, Gelenius, Heraldus, Rigaltius, and others. But Wouwerius and Elmenhorstius read, “gods whom another, now angry, would have despised,” a reading that greatly pleases both me and Gronovius. For what Livy says in book I, chapter 31, has often occurred: people angered by their fortune have abandoned the worship of their gods. **DAVIS.**—Some have wrongly printed “angry” in the singular. The first edition correctly has “angry” in the plural. Yet even those who print the plural have erred by failing to put a comma before this word. The gods, of course, were said to be angry when some city fell into the enemy’s power. [Col. 0252B] Hence Florus II, 6, 20 called the gods “turned away from Carthage.” At such a time the pagans used to despise their own gods, and even to inflict “savage blows upon the sacred gods,” to use Ovid’s words from *Amores* I, 7, 6. On this custom see Pitiscus on Suetonius, vol. I, p. 767, and Bayle in his book on comets, chapter 132. **HEUM.**

**While captured, within hostile walls.** While the Romans were on the Capitol, how narrowly did they escape being captured and falling under enemy dominion? Yet even there, within walls that had now become hostile—that is, occupied by the enemy—they worshipped conquered divine powers. This yields an excellent sense. The word “captured” now advances beyond what he had said above, “captured except for the Capitol alone,” making clear that by this distinction he wished to imitate Virgil’s line from *Aeneid* VII: “Could the captured be captured?” **GRON.**—The penultimate word is due either to a sleepy copyist, or the text should read: “while at last they erect altars even to unknown divine powers.” **DAVIS.**—Davis successfully changed *in* to *vir*; but *hostilibus* should also have been changed to the singular *hostili*. Thus chapter VII, “through fear of the enemy.” **HEUM.**—Lindner followed Davis’s reading. The sense, he says, is this: after violently capturing the cities of their enemies, the Romans, even amid the savagery of victory, where all other piety is ordinarily banished, nevertheless revered the conquered divine powers. **ERIC.**

**While they erect altars, while even.** Perhaps it should be read: “sometimes even to unknown divine powers.” For the rest, I confess that I do not adequately understand why he adds “and to the shades.” The subject of unknown gods is well worn. Even children know that the shades were regarded as gods; but why are they mentioned here? I therefore wondered whether the text should read: “to unknown and empty divine powers.” **HERALD.**—Gronovius defends the reading and holds that *dum* means *etiamdum*, “still”; or else that one should read: “long ago, even to unknown divine powers.” **LINDNER.**

**To divine powers and to the shades.** Caecilius appropriately joins the divine Shades with unknown divine powers. Appropriately, I say, from the standpoint of Epicurus, who denied that there were any Shades or subterranean kingdoms. For “Shades” is the name given to those who have already ceased to exist and therefore are nothing. Caecilius despises unknown gods because they are unknown, and the Shades, or the dead, because they are dead and now nothing. **RIGALT.**

**Earned.** Supply “to receive.” This zeugma was not unknown even to Cicero, book XI, Letter 18: “But if he should by chance collect any strength, he will perceive that neither the senate lacks judgment, nor the Roman people courage, nor, while you live, the republic a commander”—supply “lacks.” Or rather it is syllepsis, as in the same author, book XIII, Letter 10: “See to it, then, if you value me as highly as Varro thinks”—supply “you value me.” Likewise book IX, Letter 8: “But now what is there for whose sake we should wish to live without these things? For my part, even with them I scarcely wish to live; but if they are taken away, not even scarcely”—supply “do I wish to live.” That this is indeed the manuscript reading is inferred from the silence of Heraldus and Rigaltius. Meursius, however, indicates that *merunt* is read instead of *meruerunt*, and from this corrects it to *inierunt*, “entered upon,” or even *regnaverunt*, “reigned.” Gronovius reads *iverunt*, “went,” namely into their power, as though accompanying the gods. Heumannus wants: “they even earned the right to reign over them.” Davis reads *merent*, “they earn.” Heumannus informs us that the reading adopted by us—*meruerunt*, “earned”—appears in the Roman edition. **LINDNER.**

**Has remained.** Lindner reads *mansit*, “remained,” with nearly all the printed editions. The Roman edition has *maneat*, “may remain.”

**Antiquity … added antiquity.** It is somewhat inelegant to say that antiquity “adds age” to ceremonies. Yet expressions of this sort occasionally escape even the most elegant writers. Cicero, *On Divination* II, 33: “For antiquity erred in many matters, and we see it now transformed either by practice, learning, or age.” Velleius Paterculus I, 4 uses a similar form: “In the ensuing period a great mass of Greek youth, seeking settlements because of its abundance of strength, poured into Asia.” Other examples may be found there and in *Readings from Cicero* II, 5. **WOPK.**—Heumannus accordingly rewrites the passage: “It was accustomed to attribute to sanctified shrines as much [sanctity] as age had added to them.” Even earlier Fulvius Ursinus had rewritten it with *adstruxerat*, “had added.” **LINDNER.**

**ARGUMENT.—Every nation, and afterward the Romans, worshipped its own divine powers in such a way that through their worship the Romans attained supreme dominion over the whole world.**

Since, therefore, fortune is either certain or nature uncertain, how much more reverent and better it is, O guardians of truth, to accept the teaching of our ancestors, to cultivate the religious practices handed down to us, and to fear the gods with whom you were first imbued by your parents rather than to know them too familiarly; not to pass judgment upon the divine powers, but to trust our predecessors, who, while the age was still unsophisticated, at the very birth of the world, deserved to have the gods either gracious or as kings! [Col. 0251A] Hence throughout all empires, provinces, and towns we see individual peoples possessing their own rites and worshipping local gods: the Eleusinians Ceres, the Phrygians the Mother, the Epidaurians Aesculapius, the Chaldaeans Bel, the Syrians Astarte, the Taurians Diana, the Gauls Mercury, and the Romans all the gods.

Thus their power and authority occupied the whole circuit of the world; thus it extended their empire beyond the paths of the sun and the bounds of Ocean, while they exercised religious courage in arms, while they fortified the city with the sanctity of rites, with chaste virgins, and with the many honors and titles of priests; while [Col. 0252A] besieged and captured except for the Capitol alone, they worshipped gods whom another would already have despised as angry; and, through the battle lines of the Gauls, who marveled at the audacity of their religious devotion, they advanced defenseless against weapons but armed with religious observance; while captured, within hostile walls and while victory was still raging, they revered conquered divine powers; while they sought foreign gods everywhere and made them their own; while they erected altars, even to unknown divine powers and to the shades.

Thus, while adopting the rites of all nations, they also [earned] kingdoms. Hence the uninterrupted course of their veneration has remained, and is not broken but increased by the passing of a long age; for antiquity has been accustomed to attribute as much sanctity to ceremonies [Col. 0253A] and shrines as age has added to them.

They hesitated, and Marcus Curtius is said to have rebuked them by asking whether there was any Roman good greater than arms and courage. **ARGUMENT.—Roman auspices and auguries, when omitted, brought repentance; when observed, success.**

Yet our ancestors did not act rashly—for meanwhile [9] I myself would dare to concede this, and thus to err in the better direction—when they devoted their efforts to observing auguries, consulting entrails, instituting rites, or dedicating shrines. Look to the record in the books: you will find that they initiated the rites of all religions either so that divine indulgence might be repaid, or impending wrath averted, or wrath already swelling and raging appeased.

Witness is the Idaean Mother, who by her arrival both established the chastity of a matron and freed the city from fear of the enemy. Witnesses are the consecrated statues of the equestrian brothers at the lake, just as they had shown themselves: on panting horses, foaming and steaming, they announced the victory over Perseus on the very day on which they had brought it about. Witness is the renewed performance of the games because Jupiter had taken offense, as revealed through the sleep of a common man. The duly accepted self-devotion of the Decii is also a witness. Curtius too is a witness: by the mass or honor of his rider he leveled the yawning chasm of the deep abyss. [Col. 0255A]

More often than we wished, the presence of the gods has also been attested by auspices that were disregarded. Thus the Allia became a name of ill omen; thus the expedition of Claudius and Junius against the Carthaginians became not a battle but a deadly shipwreck. Flaminius despised the auguries so that Lake Trasimene might become both swollen and discolored with Roman blood; and Crassus both earned and mocked the curses of the Furies, so that we might demand back the standards from the Parthians.

I omit ancient matters, which are numerous; and I disregard the poets’ songs concerning the births, gifts, and offerings of the gods. I also pass over the destinies foretold by oracles, lest antiquity appear excessively fabulous to you. Direct your attention to the temples and shrines of the gods by which the Roman state is both protected [10] and armed: they are more majestic through the indwelling, present, and sojourning divine powers than wealthy in the insignia and offerings of worship. [Col. 0255B]

Hence, filled and mingled with God, seers anticipate the future, provide caution against dangers, remedies for diseases, hope for the afflicted, aid for the wretched, consolation in calamities, and relief in hardships. Even in sleep we see, hear, and acknowledge the gods whom by day we impiously deny, refuse, and perjure. [Col. 0256A]

> **Burned.** This is the entirely genuine reading supplied by the Roman edition and the royal manuscript. Gelenius had printed “mocked,” yielding an absurd meaning. **HERALD.**—The Roman edition has “mocked.” But the true wording was restored by Leopardus, *Emendations* XI, chapter 11, and Ludovicus Carrio, *Emendations* II, chapter 18. Minucius is plainly saying the same thing as Diogenes Laertius IX, 52: Ἐξεβλήθη πρὸς Ἀθηναίων, καὶ τὰ βιβλία αὐτοῦ κατέκαυσαν ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ, “He was expelled by the Athenians, and they burned his books in the marketplace.” **HEUM.**—Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I, chapter 23: “And his books were burned in the assembly.” **RIGALT.**
>
> **Men, I say.** This is epanalepsis, extremely common in Cicero; book I, Letter 9, §67: “I have also written—for I am even withdrawing from speeches to the gentler Muses—I have therefore written three books in the Aristotelian manner.” See Cortius on Cicero XV, Letter 10, §1. **LINDNER.**
>
> **Of an unlawful, deplorable, unlawful, and desperate faction.** They say that the manuscript reads “defiled” (Wouwerius, namely—a suspect witness, openly unreliable now as often elsewhere. **EDD.**). But no one has yet produced any ancient codex other than the royal copy. The royal manuscript very clearly reads “unlawful.” What Ferrarius and Priscianensis printed, “renowned,” is rightly rejected. Yet “unlawful,” though defended by the antiquity of the manuscript, nevertheless falls flat between “deplorable” and “desperate.” Therefore, if conjecture is permissible, I would read *incitae*, “frenzied,” meaning excited, furious, rabid. Catullus in his *Galliambic Poem*: “Drive some into frenzy, drive others rabid.” *Incitus* is formed like *percitus*. In Prudentius’ *Vincent* concerning unclean spirits:
>
> > Who secretly drive you, frenzied, into every crime.
>
> **RIGALT.**—Wopkenius reads “incestuous.” For my part, I have no doubt that the text ought to read “of deplorable ignorance.” Below too, in chapter XII, the Christians are called by the pagan Caecilius “unlearned, uncultivated, coarse, rustic”; and above, in chapter V, “untrained in studies, strangers to literature, inexperienced in the arts.” Livy VII, 2 also uses *inscitia* for ignorance or lack of knowledge. **HEUM.**—Gronovius makes *incultae*, “uncultivated,” as though Δρυχαρνεῖς [uncertain Greek reading], from *incilitae*, which according to Rigaltius stands in the manuscript. Others read “declining.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **Which in nightly.** Because of the anaphora, Heumannus reads “who in nightly.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **[Col. 0258D] With solemn fasts.** Davis, in his second edition of our Minucius, rightly called Jacob Gronovius’ correction—which substitutes *olentibus*, “foul-smelling”—itself foul. **HEUM.**—No one will doubt that, at the time when Minucius wrote, Christians already observed solemn fasts. Tertullian, *To His Wife* II, p. 128, *Works*, vol. II, Paris edition, 1566–68: “If fasts are to be observed, the husband may hold a banquet on that same day.” But to understand this passage of our Minucius correctly, the following points must also be observed. The Romans regarded Christian rites as most criminal mysteries, like the Bacchanalia they had formerly experienced, concerning which see Livy XXXIX, 10. They slanderously alleged that Christians celebrated those mysteries in their nightly gatherings. Thus they claimed that Christians met on an appointed day and, through the eating of human flesh and promiscuous intercourse after the feast, conspired together and bound themselves to every crime. Caecilius calls that human flesh “inhuman food.” He says that the Christians “bind themselves in a covenant,” using a term drawn from the character of mystery rites; but they are bound together not through sacred rites, like initiates in the mysteries of Roman religion, but through abominations—namely, the eating of human flesh [Col. 0259A] and promiscuous intercourse. We discuss at greater length this malicious Roman slander against the Christians in the *Historical Exercise on the Hatred of the Human Race Formerly Charged against Christians by the Romans as a Cause for the Persecution of the Christian Name*, chapter 2, §7. [Col. 0259B] **GRUNER.**
>
> **Inhuman food.** I read “human food.” Florus III, 5, 10, concerning Lucius Sulla: “And first, by siege and famine, he drove Athens—a city, who would believe it, the mother of produce—to human food.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **A light-shunning nation.** Thus the manuscript and, according to Rigaltius, the first Roman edition—not *lucifuga*. Gesner’s *Thesaurus of the Latin Language*, vol. III, p. 154, must therefore be corrected, since it falsely testifies that the manuscript and old books have *lucifuga*, while the common editions have *lucifugax*. **LINDNER.**—By “nation” Minucius means a sect or class. Thus Phaedrus II, Fable V, 1: “There is at Rome a certain nation of busybodies.” Cicero likewise used “nation” for the sect of the Epicureans, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, chapter 29. **DAVIS.**
>
> **Mute in public.** Thus the manuscript. Some correctly restored “mute in a public place,” which already appears in Sabaeus’ Roman edition. The cause of the error is obvious. **HEUM.**—Thus chapter 19, “to speak in public”; chapter 28: “We thought that these men should not be heard at all.” Quintilian, *Declamation* 13: “Life is lived in public, and all resources are gathered into a common fund.” I do not know why Heraldus retained “in a public place.” Gronovius cites Gellius VI, 14, “to leave in the common space”; Plautus, *Bacchides* I, 2, 53:
>
> > It has now come into my mind.
>
> **LINDNER.**
>
> **As they despise tombs.** Tertullian, *On the Spectacles*, chapter 13: “We spit upon temples no less than upon tombs.” Since the bodies of the dead had been placed and buried in very many pagan temples—a fact demonstrated extensively by Clement of Alexandria, Arnobius, Eusebius, and others—the Christians, because of that practice, frequently both called and regarded them as tombs. Hence also Ammianus Marcellinus XXII, concerning Gregory, bishop of Alexandria: “Returning from the emperor’s court, when he was passing a splendid temple of the Genius, surrounded as usual by a crowd, he turned his eyes toward the building itself and said, ‘How long will this tomb stand?’” Pertinent to this argument too are Tiberius’ words in Tacitus, *Annals* IV: “These are my temples in your minds; these are the most beautiful and enduring images. For structures built of stone, if posterity’s judgment turns to hatred, are despised as tombs.” **HERALD.**—Because *despiciunt*, “despise,” occurs again immediately afterward, Rigaltius rightly suspected a copyist’s error. The passage he cites from Tertullian, *On the Spectacles*, chapter 13, opens the way to an emendation. Christians knew that temples had been erected at the places where those worshipped as gods had been buried. Since scholars agree that our Minucius had Tertullian before his eyes, it is readily apparent that he wrote: “They spit upon temples as tombs.” Therefore remove “despise,” and join “the gods” with what follows: “They ridicule the gods and their rites.” Continue now: “Wretched themselves, they pity….” The Roman edition—and the royal codex—has *miserentur*, not *miserantur*. The inserted *fas est*, “if it is right,” seems to have arisen because the copyist wrote *sacer* twice and then did not wish to delete the first occurrence. **HEUM.**—These alterations arose because very learned men could not endure the repetition of *despiciunt*. Yet one must endure the word “part” six times in Caesar, *Gallic War* I, 12; five times in chapter 49 of the same book; and four times in chapter 1 of book IV. **LINDNER.**
>
> **Wretched themselves, they pity.** Thus Heraldus punctuates. The common punctuation is: “Wretched themselves, they pity, if it is right to say so, the priests….” Heraldus’ punctuation alone is correct. It is well known that “honor” is used for a magistracy; see Rittershausen on Pliny, Letter XI, 11, 2. Purple, moreover, is an insignia of magistrates. See Andreas Steyer, *Dissertation on Purple as an Insignia of Sacred Rank*. **GRUNER.**—He aims at wordplay, as in “to die after death,” 9, 8; “rites more hideous than every sacrilege,” 27, 2; “they are deceived and deceive,” 10, 2; and “either it must be punished or one must be ashamed.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **With astonishing folly and incredible audacity they despise.** The royal codex inserts no interjection or punctuation, whereas the printed editions have: “Oh, astonishing folly and incredible audacity! They despise….” **EDD.**
>
> **Present things.** “And therefore certain” must be supplied from the second clause. John 1:18 must be supplemented in the same way. **LINDNER.**
>
> **Thus for them fear … soothes.** The manuscript has “renewed consolations.” Here “to soothe” means to soften, alleviate, or mitigate, as in Columella VII, 16: “Goat’s milk soothes the fiery savagery of erysipelas.” This passage also makes clear the construction of the verb “to soothe” with the accusative. “Fear” is fear of present death and tortures. “Renewed consolation” is the sort hoped for by those who are to live again in another life. He said “to soothe with renewed consolation,” as Ovid, *Metamorphoses* X, 555:
>
> > The poplar soothes with its shade.
>
> The sense is therefore: that false hope that they will one day live again mitigates their fear of present death and tortures. Indeed, Lactantius VII, 22, 8 says “the souls of the righteous brought back to life” for “the righteous brought back to life,” or “the souls of righteous persons brought back to life”; for Latin authors often attach an adjective to a different noun, as Virgil, *Aeneid* II, 713:
>
> > And the ancient temple  
> > of deserted Venus.
>
> That is, an ancient and deserted temple.—Having explained my own opinion concerning this passage, I shall append in order the conjectures and emendations of the most learned men, so that each may choose whichever he wishes. Heraldus printed: “Thus for them fear, and false hope, soothes with renewed consolation.” Gronovius interprets the manuscript reading by assigning a pregnant sense to the verb: “False hope, by soothing or in the act of soothing, gives them renewed consolations against fear.” Davis reads: “Life soothes their fear; false hope, their renewed consolations.” Wopkenius: “Thus for them a perverse and false hope promises ridiculous consolations,” that is, foretells or pledges them, as in chapter XI, “foolish consolations.” Heumannus: “Thus for them, amid fear, the false hope of a restored consolation offers soothing promises.” For *recidivum* denotes what is restored and, as it were, reborn after it has vanished and perished. Grunerus: “Thus false hope deceives his fear and promises renewed consolations.” Ovid, *Tristia* V, 7, 39:
>
> > I occupy my mind with studies and beguile my sorrows.
>
> **LINDNER.**

**ARGUMENT.—By no means tolerable is the impious rashness of Theodorus, Diagoras, and Protagoras, who wished either wholly to abolish the religion of the gods or at least to weaken it; but far less tolerable is the secretive and light-shunning nation of the Christians, who spit upon the gods and, while fearing death after death, do not meanwhile fear to die.**
