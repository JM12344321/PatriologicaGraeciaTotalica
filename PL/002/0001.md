## THE BOOK OF [Col. 0011D] QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN ON PRESCRIPTIONS AGAINST HERETICS

Thus we remove them from every dispute as persons who must not be heard. No other prescription [Col. 0012D] is at issue here. ## PROEM

 Although no one would deny that Tertullian’s book *On Prescriptions* is by far the finest of all his minor works, and that by this single weapon all the heresies of every age are laid low and utterly destroyed, everyone nevertheless knows the difficult controversy that arises at the very threshold of the work: at what time in particular—before or after the author’s defection to the Montanists—this book was either published or composed.

The common Catholic opinion is that this exceptional work could not have grown within the confines of heresy. One may enumerate the most learned [Col. 0009B] and distinguished men supporting this view: Baronius (at the year 197, no. 11), Tillemont (*Mémoires pour servir à l’Histoire ecclésiastique*, III, note 6  on Tertullian, p. 300 ff.), Dom Rémy Ceillier (*Histoire des auteurs ecclésiastiques*, II, ch. 28, art. VI, p. 395), Fleury (*Histoire ecclésiastique*, II, book V, no. 28), and J. A. Möhler (*Patrologie oder christliche Litterargeschichte*). Since Möhler is more recent and therefore expresses the mind of the others more fully, it is worth quoting his words here: “Such is the spirit of this book, and such the manner in which it is woven throughout, that the sincerity of a Catholic author is evident. For once he had gone wholly over to Montanist tenets and openly withdrawn from the Church, he could no longer have asserted with such weight and integrity of fact and language this chief argument of prescription, [Col. 0010A] by which he himself was afterward vigorously assailed and pierced through by Catholics.”

They are further moved to this opinion by three other arguments:  (1) nothing in this book savors of open heresy; (2) indeed, unmistakable signs show that the author was then joined in communion with the Roman Church; (3) nowhere does he make the slightest mention of a certain Artemon, a most wicked heresiarch who denied Christ’s divinity. It may therefore be inferred that the book was written before Artemon’s time, and consequently before Tertullian’s fall.

Such arguments they ordinarily advance. Yet they do not plainly and adequately overcome Tertullian’s own testimony after his fall, when in the books *Against Marcion* he declares that this work *On Prescriptions* [Col. 0010B] had not yet been published, as is discussed more fully in Dom Lumper’s dissertation placed at the beginning of this edition.

 After weighing the considerations on either side, one must therefore admit a middle path between the two positions, on which the disputants may safely meet and agree. The arguments on both sides seem to compel us to conclude that the work was composed at one time and published at another.

First, we believe that Tertullian conceived this book and wrote its first draft while staying at Rome, when his faith was still whole and he was fierce and exultant, as it were, with a convert’s fervor. He saw all the heresies besieging the Apostolic See from every quarter with united force dashed against the Rock by the single stroke of tradition. At the same time, being closely associated [Col. 0011A] by necessity with the foremost men then flourishing in the City—Caius the presbyter and Hippolytus, above all the illustrious disciples of St Irenaeus —he received from them the books of so great a master. From them too he discovered, adopted, and found proven the most ready way to defeat any innovators without battle or combat: the wholly secure course of traditions and prescriptions. Hence he fixed deep in his mind the plan of dragging the new enemies of truth to judgment and, without examination of the subject or litigation of the cause, expelling them from every suit merely by the force of certain exceptions and sending them away condemned. (Cf. Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* II.2, p. 41; Tertullian, *On the Dress of Women* I.6; Jerome, *Illustrious Men* 53.)

When this plan had nearly been executed or was hastening toward completion, [Col. 0011B] there arose—whether from the envy of the Roman clergy, the ungovernable character of Septimius, or the exceedingly fierce spirit of the African—a truly lamentable storm, which  tore him from the ship of the Church and the harbor of Catholic faith and drove him upon the pathless reefs of Montanism. Yet Tertullian remained to some extent an indefatigable defender of truth. When he was now devoting the whole last part of his life to controversies, he did not reject his earlier labors, but published that renowned monument of Catholic prescription.

We have therefore thought it better to place this work midway between the books of Tertullian the Catholic and Tertullian the Montanist, judging [Col. 0011C] that it looks to both faces of his genius and both parts of his life and belongs to each by the same title.

## ARGUMENT

This book may be divided into two parts.  The first weighs five preliminary considerations that pave the way for prescriptions; the second sets forth nine principal methods of prescribing against heresies.

**First part.** First proposition: Heresies must exist, must possess great power, and must achieve much in overthrowing souls. Chapters I–III. Second: Heresies are to be shunned, as worse than dissensions and persecutions. IV–V. Third: Heresy is a choice, and the very thing in which choice is made is the source of condemnation. [Col. 0011D] VI. Fourth: All heresies flow from one of two sources, either secular wisdom or itching curiosity.  VII–XII. Fifth: The rule of faith must be kept whole and never torn apart in controversy. XIII–XIV. [Col. 0012A]

**Second part.** It presents this **first** prescription: heretics are not to be heard in a dispute over Scripture.  XV–XIX. **Second:** the apostles received sound teaching from Christ himself to hand down to posterity, and the churches received it from the apostles; none is to be received from anywhere else. XX–XXVII. **Third:** the Church’s teaching is true because it is one; the heretics’ is false because it is diverse. **Fourth:** that which was handed down first is true and divine. XXIX–XXXI. **Fifth:** the continuous succession of bishops from the apostles is firm testimony to the true Church. XXXII. **Sixth:** our teaching is true, since the apostles certainly do not condemn it [Col. 0012B] but defend it; every other is false, being foreign or unknown to the apostles.  XXXIII–XXXV. **Seventh:** to find the truth the apostolic churches must be consulted, and above all that happy church upon which the apostles poured out their entire teaching together with their blood. XXXVI–XXXVII. **Eighth:** heretics have no right whatever to the Holy Scriptures. XXXVIII–XL. **Ninth:** no truth can coexist with the perverse conduct of heretics; where there is fear of God, there are honorable seriousness, deliberate fellowship, respectful advancement, devout submission, reverent appearance, modest procession, a united Church, and all things belonging to God. XLI–XLV. The final chapters follow, [Col. 0012C] containing a catalogue of heretics; concerning its authenticity see the annotation appended to those chapters. EDITORS.

## ARGUMENT BY JAMES PAMELIUS

Writing against the heretics or heresies then rampant, and seeing that disputations accomplished nothing or little, Tertullian judged that they must be refuted by fixed prescriptions, or without a reconsideration of their doctrines, as he says elsewhere.

## CHAPTER V
