Aristo of Chios. In the Roman edition it reads *Aristo the Stoic*. Some editions have *Aristo of Chios, the Stoic*. But Louis Carrion, *Emendations*, book II, chapter 18, correctly judges that the final word ought to have been omitted. HEUMANN.—What Minucius attributes to Aristo of Chios, Cicero attributes to Aristo, says Elmenhorst, who has slipped through carelessness; for *Aristo* is neither dative nor ablative, but nominative. Compare Bayle’s *Dictionary* under the entry ARISTO. LINDNER.

And in the realities themselves. That is, “as regards the realities themselves.” Meursius, deriving these ablatives from the preposition *de*, did not hesitate to emend the text to “both in the realities themselves and in the persons,” in place of “names.” LINDNER.—“Both in the realities themselves [Col. 0296D] and in the names.” Minucius means that Plato distinguished the supreme Deity not only by a true name, but also by correct attributes, when he declares God to be the maker and parent of all things. See, among others, Clement, *Recognitions*, book VIII, chapter 20. DAVIS.

By his own name. A heavenly discourse is Christian. LYONS EDITOR.—Because Cicero altogether denies that God can be named, and Apuleius, book I of *On Plato’s Doctrine*, says that God has no name, Buhierius therefore believes it clearer than daylight that in our author one must read: “he himself is without a name.” Against this emendation, Le Clerc, defending the received reading in the *Bibliothèque ancienne et moderne*, vol. XVI, p. 223, says that Minucius asserts nothing contrary to the principles of this foremost philosopher. He who professes that God, “by his own name,” is the parent of the world denies that God has a name. For “parent of the world” is not a name, since Plato himself in the *Timaeus* calls God πατέρα τοῦδε τοῦ παντός, “the father of this entire universe,” while preserving the proposition by which he denies that God has a name. LINDNER.

[Col. 0297B] Almost the same as these things. The Roman edition also has it thus. Fulvius Ursinus had no reason to recast it as follows: “Almost the same things as are also ours.” HEUMANN.]

**SUMMARY.—Furthermore, the poets called him the Parent of gods and human beings, the creator of all things, mind and spirit. Indeed, even the more eminent philosophers themselves held almost the same views as Christians concerning the one God.**

What of Maro of Mantua? Does he not speak more openly, more nearly, more truly? “In the beginning,” he says, “the heaven and the lands,” and the other members of the world, “a spirit nourishes from within, and a mind infused throughout sets them in motion. From this comes the race of human beings and beasts,”[^26] and whatever else belongs to [Col. 0292B] living creatures. Elsewhere the same poet calls this mind and spirit God; for these are his actual [printed: “words”] words:

[Col. 0293A]

> For God passes through all  
> The lands, the tracts of sea, and the depth of heaven.  
> From him come human beings and beasts, from him rain and fire.

What else do we proclaim God to be but mind, reason, and spirit? Let us review, if you please, the doctrines of the philosophers: you will find that, although their expressions vary, they nevertheless converge and agree in the realities themselves upon this single opinion.

(XII) I pass over those primitive men of old who earned the title “wise” from their own sayings. Let Thales of Miletus stand first of all, since he was the first of all to discourse about heavenly things. Thales of Miletus said that water was the origin of things, but that God was the mind which formed all things from water. Indeed! A theory of water and spirit too deep and sublime to have been discovered by a human being—it was handed down by God. You see that the opinion of the foremost philosopher [Col. 0294A] agrees profoundly with ours.

Next Anaximenes, and afterward Diogenes of Apollonia, maintain that air—boundless and immeasurable—is God. Their agreement concerning the divinity is likewise similar. Anaxagoras’s description and motion of infinite mind is called God. For Pythagoras, too, God is the mind that passes through and pervades the whole nature of things, from which the life of all living creatures is also derived. Xenophanes is well known for teaching that the entire infinite together with mind is God; Antisthenes, that the gods of individual peoples are many, but that the one natural God is supreme; and Speusippus [printed: Zeuxippus], that God is the natural, animate power by which all things are governed.

What of Democritus? Although the first inventor of atoms, [p. 26] does he not very often call God the nature that emits images, and intelligence? Strato likewise calls nature itself God. Even that Epicurus who imagines the gods either idle or nonexistent nevertheless places nature above all. Aristotle varies, yet still assigns sovereignty to a single power. For at one time he calls mind God, at another the world, and at another places God over the world. Aristotle of Pontus varies, assigning sovereignty at one time to the world and at another to the divine mind. Heraclides of Pontus likewise attributes divine mind to God, though in varying ways.

Theophrastus, Zeno, Chrysippus, and Cleanthes are themselves diverse in their accounts, yet they all return to the unity of providence. For Cleanthes discussed God now as mind, now as soul, now as ether, but most often as reason. Zeno, his teacher, holds that natural and divine law, and at one time ether [Col. 0295B] and at another reason, is the origin of all things. Likewise, by interpreting Juno as air, Jupiter as heaven, Neptune as the sea, Vulcan as fire, and similarly demonstrating that the other gods of the common people are elements, he gravely exposes and refutes the general error.

[Col. 0296A] Chrysippus says almost the same: he believes that God is divine power, rational nature, at one time the world, and also the necessity of fate; and in his physical interpretation of the poems of Hesiod, Homer, and Orpheus, he imitates Zeno.

(XIII) Diogenes of Babylon also makes it his discipline to expound and discuss how Jupiter was born and Minerva came into being, and how terms for the other things of this kind are names of things, not of gods.

(XIV) Xenophon the Socratic denies that the form of the true God can be seen, [p. 27] and therefore says that it ought not to be sought. Aristo of Chios [printed: “the Stoic”] says that it cannot be comprehended at all. Both perceived the majesty of God through their despair of understanding him.

Plato’s discourse concerning God is clearer, both in the realities themselves and in their names; it would be wholly heavenly if it were not sometimes sullied by an admixture of civic persuasion. Thus, in Plato’s *Timaeus*, God is, by his own [Col. 0296B] name, the parent of the world, the craftsman of the soul, and the maker of things heavenly and earthly. Plato first declares that to discover him is difficult because of his exceedingly great and incredible power, and that, once you have discovered him, to speak of him publicly is impossible.

[Col. 0297A] (XV) These things are almost the same as ours: for we both know God and call him the parent of all, and we never proclaim him publicly unless we are questioned.

## CHAPTER XX

[Note: [Col. 0297B]

**CHAPTER XX.—One God . . . names.** The celebrated Heumann regards these words as suspect of being a gloss; but they cohere well if one interprets them to mean that those philosophers designated one God, although by many names. In the same way we explained the words of this chapter: “Chrysippus says almost the same,” namely, divine power, and so forth. Lactantius I.5: “For whether you call him nature, ether, reason, mind, fatal necessity, divine law, or anything else, it is the same as that which we call God. Nor is the diversity of appellations an obstacle, since in their very signification all things return to one.” LINDNER.

**The antiquity of the unlearned.** So far as I know, [Col. 0297C] virtually all the interpreters translate these words as though it made little difference whether the word “unlearned” were absent or present. Thus the French translator: “We must not allow ourselves to be carried away by the fables of antiquity.” Yet that word was by no means to be neglected. For Octavius distinguishes between the antiquity of the unlearned and that of the learned, or philosophers. His purpose is to teach that those who rashly follow antiquity because of some preconceived opinion should listen to the philosophers rather than to the unlearned, since both parties equally possess the authority of age, while the philosophers possess in addition the authority of reason. LINDNER.

**Delighted or captivated.** Cicero similarly joins “rejoice” and “be captivated” in *Letters to Friends* VI, letter 7: “With your prudence, you understand what things Caesar rejoices in and by what he is captivated.” Our author is much given to joining synonyms, such as “more sublime and loftier,” “inventions and lies,” “defend and protect,” and “err and be mistaken,” in chapters 19, 22, 28, 32, and elsewhere. Moreover, synonyms of this kind are rather often joined by the conjunction *vel* (“or”) in place of *et* (“and”). Sulpicius Severus, *Sacred History* II.15: [Col. 0297D] “That he had paid the Lord sufficient penalties for his sins or crimes,” where we accumulate several expressions. Compare what was said above on chapter VIII. There was therefore no reason for Davis to banish the words “delighted or” and read only “captivated by its fables.” For he objects without justification that this is not the place to discuss the delight or charm of fables. We are quite appropriately said here to be “delighted” by fables of any kind if we readily lend them our ears and highly esteem them. Similarly, in Lactantius I.7 the multitude of gods is said to delight people; and below, in chapter 22, the inventions and lies by which people readily permit themselves to be captivated are called sweet. Compare Cicero, *On Divination* I.55. WOPKENS.

Heumann, on Lactantius I.20 and IV.15, shows that copyists often wrote *monstruosa* for *monstrosa*, following popular pronunciation. But Bünemann has already observed that Scheffer, Heinsius, Goes, and Burmann more correctly use *monstruosus* in prose than *monstrosus*, which is more familiar to poets. Gronovius proves from a fragment of Varro cited by Nonius that *mira miracula*, “marvelous marvels,” can be said: “To the same place they had gathered I know not what marvelous marvels.” As regards emendations, Meursius proposes “monstrous marvels”; Heraldus, “monstrous, marvelous marvels”; Davis, “dreadful marvels”; Cellarius and Heumann, “monstrous marvels”; and Gruner, “other monstrous, marvelous marvels.” LINDNER.—“Marvelous marvels.” Nothing should be changed. It is a play on words, like “the wretched take pity” above and “to die after death.” GRUNER, work cited, p. 226.—So too Davis in his second edition, where he also observes that the ancients used similar expressions. Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, book I, p. 15, and book II, p. 185, said *gratas gratias*, “welcome thanks.” Ausonius, *Epigram* 20: “Accustomed to drink wine unmixed and wine that was pure wine,” etc. See the notes above at the end of chapter 8. GALLAND.

[Col. 0298C] **Horses entwined with their human beings.** The learned J. Wower is mistaken in conjecturing that one should restore “horses entwined with half-human beings”; for the common reading must be retained. Arnobius, book I, near the end: “The destroyer of death itself permitted his human being to be slain.” For the force and meaning of this pronoun one should consult J. F. Gronovius, *Observations*, book I, chapter 11. DAVIS.

**It is permitted.** Without doubt *libet*, “it pleases,” must be restored. HEUMANN.

**It was pleasing for them to hear.** So the German: *Es war ihnen lieb zu hören*, that is, “they heard willingly.” Propertius I.20.13:

> May it not be for you to approach harsh mountains and cold rocks,  
> Gallus, nor always lakes devoid of life.

Here “may it not be for you to approach” means “may you not approach,” “may you not wish to approach”; see Passeratius in his notes. There was consequently no reason for Heumann to restore: “It was for them, being willing, to hear.” LINDNER.

**Birds from human beings.** In place of “out of human beings.” This is Tertullian’s style, as I observed on Lactantius V.9. This reading is therefore entirely sound, contrary to the opinion of an anonymous writer in *British and Dutch Critical Observations*, vol. I, p. 29. HEUMANN.—“Birds from human beings, and wild beasts human beings” [a corrupt reading]. Wower’s edition has “birds from human beings, and human beings from wild beasts.” This is indeed a little better, yet I think something is missing, for in this form Minucius does not mention the transformation of human beings into wild beasts. I therefore think one should restore: “Birds and wild beasts from human beings, human beings from wild beasts, and trees and flowers from human beings”? Clement, *Recognitions*, book X, chapter 26: “They say that from human beings come stars, trees, animals, birds, springs, and rivers.” DAVIS.

**Birds and wild beasts.** The manuscript has “and wild beasts, human beings.” But who, even without a warning from me, fails to see that the word *homines*, “human beings,” is a most disgraceful gloss? For Minucius establishes two classes of things into which fables report that human beings were transformed: one animate, namely birds and wild beasts; the other inanimate, namely trees and flowers. Wower, with some openly and Gronovius hesitantly agreeing with him, had published “birds from human beings, and human beings from wild beasts.” Scheffer’s reading is bad: “birds and all wild beasts from human beings.” In my judgment, however, it is utterly wrong. LINDNER.

**Because they cannot occur.** The manuscript codex correctly reads thus. Several editors had printed, very badly: “If they had happened, they would happen—things which cannot happen; therefore they did not happen.” I add that “they would happen” is expressed briefly for “they would happen even now,” and “because they cannot happen” for “because they cannot happen in our own time.” For the rest, I stated in *Poecile*, vol. II, p. 282, what ought to be thought of this argument of Minucius. HEUMANN.

**Similarly indeed, also regarding the gods.** German: *Gerade so, wie sie auch in Ansehung der Götter*, etc., “Just as they also did in regard to the gods.” In the manuscript the words are transposed: *similiter ac vero*. Since this is foreign to Latin usage, Rigaltius punctuated it: *Similiter ac vero.* [Col. 0299C] Wower and Elmenhorst published “similarly indeed regarding the gods,” omitting the particle *ac*; Gronovius, absurdly, *simili veratro*; Scheffer, “similarly and truly”; Heumann, “by a similar error.” None of these emendations seems to present us with the true text of Minucius. *Similiter vero* means “similarly, certainly.” Cicero, *To Quintus his Brother* I.1: “For you this both always was very easy and certainly ought to have been so.” *Ac* follows *similiter* in Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* III.3: “But you, who ask this, act just as if you were to ask me.” LINDNER.

**Improvident, credulous.** We have several times noted such asyndetic expressions above. Heumann reads *improvide creduli*, “improvidently credulous,” as above in chapter 14: “incautiously credulous.” LINDNER.

**They believed out of their primitive simplicity.** Heumann prefers “they believed primitive simplicity,” that is, they believed the ancients, who were conspicuous for great simplicity and ignorance. But who are those simple ancients in relation to the forefathers, unless they are the forefathers themselves? And those forefathers did not believe primitive simplicity because the ancients were to be preferred to their own predecessors, but rather believed *out of* primitive simplicity. LINDNER.

**Memories in statues.** Louis Carrion, *Emendations*, book II, chapter 18, was the first to restore this in place of the printed “more insistently.” Heraldus teaches that “in statues” is also read in the manuscript. For plural “memories” Heumann reads the singular “memory.” LINDNER.

**In short, also.** I have printed it thus, following Heraldus and Rigaltius; from this I suppose it stands thus in the manuscript, although the other editions delete the second word without cause. GRONOVIUS.

**Stronger than her sex.** For women can easily be deceived, as Ovid says, *Heroides* II.63:

> To deceive a trusting girl is no laborious glory.

Likewise:

> Both loving and a woman, I was deceived by your words.

She who therefore did not allow herself to be deceived was stronger than her sex. Heumann restores “or than her sex.” LINDNER.]

**SUMMARY.—But if the world is ruled by Providence and governed at the command of one God, the ignorant past must not drag us into the error of mutual agreement, since it was delighted by its own fables and introduced ridiculous traditions. It is no less clearly demonstrated that the worship of the gods was always foolish and impious, since the earliest mortals venerated their kings, illustrious leaders, and inventors of arts, on account of their notable deeds, just as though they were gods.**

I have set forth the opinions of almost all the philosophers [Col. 0297B] whose more distinguished glory lies in having designated one God, although by many [Col. 0298A] names. Thus anyone might suppose either that Christians are now philosophers, or that philosophers were already Christians then.

But if the world is ruled by Providence and governed at the command of one God, the antiquity of the unlearned, delighted or captivated by its own fables, ought not to carry us away into the error of mutual agreement, since it is refuted by the opinions of its own philosophers, whose authority rests upon both reason and antiquity.

For our forefathers possessed such ready faith in falsehoods that they rashly believed even other monstrous, marvelous marvels: Scylla with her many forms, the multiform Chimaera, the Hydra reborn through its fruitful wounds, the Centaurs—horses entwined with their human beings—and whatever rumor is permitted to invent, [p. 28] they were pleased to hear. What of those old women’s tales about [Col. 0298B] birds and wild beasts arising from human beings, human beings, and trees and [Col. 0299A] flowers arising from human beings? If these things had happened, they would still happen; because they cannot happen, they therefore did not happen.

Similarly, indeed, our forefathers were improvident and credulous also regarding the gods, and believed out of their primitive simplicity. While they reverently worshiped their kings, while after their deaths they longed to see them in images, while they eagerly sought to preserve their memories in statues, things assumed as consolations became sacred rites.

In short, both before the world lay open to commerce and before nations mingled their rites and customs, each nation venerated its own founder, or an illustrious leader, or a chaste queen stronger than her sex, or the discoverer of some function or art, as a citizen of good memory. Thus both a reward was given to the dead and an example to those yet to come.

## CHAPTER XXI

[Note: [Col. 0299D]

**Read the writings of the Stoics.** *Lege*, “read,” is imperative. If anyone should happen to be surprised that I give this warning, let him know that in Cellarius’s index these words are entered under the heading *Lex Stoicorum scripta*, “Law of the Stoics written.” LINDNER.

[Col. 0300B] **Or the writings of the wise.** Cellarius judged that these words should be deleted. Others restore: “Read the writings of the historians, or the writings of the wise.” But nothing should be changed, for among the best authors a genus is placed beside a species. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* V:

> Daulian fields and Phocian fields, with a Thracian soldier,

although Daulis is a town of Phocis. DAVIS.—It pertains to the boastfulness of the Stoics that they claimed for themselves above all others the title of “wise men,” a fact that escapes no one even slightly acquainted with Seneca’s writings. Thus “the wise” κατ’ ἐξοχήν, “preeminently,” are the Stoics. But why was mention of the Stoics made before? Was it so that a genus might be placed beside a species? I do not think so. What, then? One must read “historians,” not “Stoics.” This is proved:

1. Because Heumann observed that even in a certain passage of Jerome some copyists wrote “Stoics” and others “historians”: *Poecile*, vol. II, p. 344.
2. Because Lactantius speaks similarly, V.4: “Human testimonies first had to be adduced, that is, those of philosophers and historians.”
3. Because Minucius actually did adduce the testimonies of Stoics and historians. Euhemerus, Prodicus, and Persaeus are included under “the wise”; Alexander the Great, Nepos, Cassius, Thallus, and Diodorus under “the historians.”
4. Because the gloss “Stoics” could easily have displaced the word “historians,” since copyists did not notice that *vel*, “or,” was used adversatively, as in chapter I.

Gronovius thinks that Minucius wrote “or the maxims [constitute] wisdom” (*vel scita sapientiam*) [an uncertain conjectural reading]. LINDNER.

**Considered gods because of the merit of their virtue or service. Euhemerus brings out**, etc. The passage suffers from its punctuation and must be divided thus: “You will recognize. Euhemerus traces how they were considered gods because of their merits, and,” etc. For this is what that ancient writer accomplished. Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, chapter [Col. 0300D] 42: “Those who teach that brave, famous, or powerful men attained to the gods after death—are they not devoid of every form of religion? This line of reasoning was especially developed by Euhemerus.” Consult Lactantius, *On the Wrath of God*, chapter 2. See also *Divine Institutes*, book I, chapter 11. DAVIS.

**By prodigies.** From this Desiderius Heraldus restores “Posidippus”; but Nicolas Rigaltius very well restored the text from Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, chapter 42: “What,” he says, “of Prodicus of Chios, who said that those things which benefited human life were counted among the gods?” Here one must read: “What of Prodicus of Ceos?” The *Suda*: Πρόδικος Κεῖος ἀπὸ Κείου τοῦ νήσου, “Prodicus of Ceos, from the island of Ceos.” In Plato, too, he is everywhere called Κεῖος, “Cean.” Erasmus, however, in the *Adages*, and J. A. Fabricius, *Bibliotheca Graeca*, book II, chapter 18, falsely call this sophist “Cian,” although Fabricius correctly identifies him as Cean in book XXIII, chapter 41 of the same work. DAVIS.—Accordingly Sevin should not be heard either; in the *Memoirs of the Royal Academy of Inscriptions*, vol. VIII, p. 2, he proposes changing “by prodigies” to “he betrays.” The source then breaks off with *Is*, “he.” HEUMANN.

**With new [crops].** Delete “crops,” which, if Wower is to be believed, does not appear in the Roman edition either; he very aptly adduces this statement of Pliny: “Individual discoveries added certain persons to the number of the gods.” Fulvius Ursinus likewise deleted “crops.” HEUMANN.

**That Alexander the Great, the Macedonian.** To Wower the last of these words seemed spurious; to me the penultimate one does. For our author did not mean to praise “that Alexander,” but to distinguish him from other Alexanders. HEUMANN.—“That Alexander the Great, the Macedonian.” Nicolas Rigaltius, followed by Christopher Cellarius, maintains that Minucius made a mistake and that Alexander surnamed Πολυΐστωρ, “the Polymath,” rather than Alexander the Great, must be meant. Yet Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, chapter 24, says: Ἡρόδοτος μὲν οὖν . . . καὶ Ἀλέξανδρος ὁ τοῦ Φιλίππου, ἐν τῇ πρὸς τὴν μητέρα ἐπιστολῇ . . . φασὶ παρ’ ἐκείνων (ἱερέων) ἀνθρώπους αὐτοὺς γενέσθαι μαθεῖν, “Herodotus, then . . . and Alexander the son of Philip, in his letter to his mother . . . say that they learned from those priests that these gods had been human beings.” So too Augustine, *City of God*, book VIII, chapters 3 and 27. Indeed, the excellent and most learned bishop John Fell, in his notes on Cyprian’s *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 12 of the Oxford edition, demonstrates by many arguments that this suspicion of those learned men is wholly groundless. DAVIS.—The very learned Sablonsky, however, strives with all his might to prove that the letter of Alexander the Great was spurious: *Egyptian Pantheon*, Prolegomena 15–16. See our annotations on Tertullian’s *Apology*. EDITORS.

**There Vulcan.** The Royal manuscript has “to him.” HERALDUS and RIGALTIUS.—What then? Do Heraldus and Rigaltius so despise the manuscript codex that they reject what they themselves saw in it? They mentioned it, however, because of Ursinus, who printed “he,” while they themselves lapsed into something worse. Or did they not know that this particle, in precisely the sense “while I sit there,” has been so carefully noted in Plautus and Terence? I therefore did not hesitate to restore to Minucius what is his own, for I know that it is read thus in the sole manuscript. GRONOVIUS.—Rigaltius correctly restored *illic*, “there,” in place of the manuscript’s *illi*, “to him.” Below, chapter 22: “There he died; there he had sons.” HEUMANN.—**Uranus.** I did not hesitate to restore this in place of what appears in the common editions—and also in the sole Royal manuscript, EDITORS—namely, “Vulcan.” See Lactantius discussing the same subject, I.13: “Ennius, to be sure, in his *Euhemerus*, says that Saturn did not reign first, but Uranus, his father. Although Uranus had several sons, Ennius mentions none of them except Cronus, that is, Saturn; and, passing over Saturn’s sons, he records only the family of Jupiter.” Thus everything becomes very easy. How convoluted, by contrast, is Heumann’s emendation: “He makes Vulcan prince of the Lemnians, born of Vesta and Jupiter.” LINDNER.

**You look down upon.** I hold it certain that the order of the argument has been interrupted by an exchange of several pages in the codex or by some other accident. I do not think it can be restored except by rearranging the dislocated parts. The words of Minucius appear to me to cohere as follows:

Chapter XXI.4: “There he makes Vulcan the prince of all, and afterward the race of Jupiter. For Saturn is the prince of this lineage and swarm,” etc.

Chapter 22 as far as chapter 23: “From this it is evident that those were human beings whom we both read were born and know to have died. Examine the sistrum of Isis beside the swallow, of course, and you will discover that those whom we read were born also died.”

Chapter 21 as far as chapter 23: “And in those same opinions the wretched grow old, though the truth is readily accessible—but only to those who seek it. Who therefore doubts,” etc., chapter 23.

Compare also the *Arrangement* prefixed by me to this work. LINDNER.—Rigaltius testifies that the manuscript very clearly reads not *dispice*, “examine,” but *dispicis*, “you perceive,” which he also attempts to interpret; Jacob Gronovius does the same out of his superstitious veneration for the manuscript codex. Davis, however, rightly calls this interpretation exceedingly harsh and convoluted; following Salmasius, he judges that the text should be restored as: *Respice sis Isidis ad hirundinem sistrum*, “Pray look at the sistrum of Isis beside the swallow.” But even this phrasing is not fluid and elegant. Worse still is the correction he offered in his second edition: “It laughs at the ears of grain of Isis and the swallow.”

Nor can I approve Wower’s conjecture, in his note on Sidonius Apollinaris, book II, letter 9, p. 418, restoring *dispicis*, supposedly the genitive of *dispex*. I confess that the divinations of a certain British critic in *Miscellaneous Critical Observations*, vol. II, pp. 54 ff.; vol. IV, p. 442, do not please me; indeed, I doubt whether they will please anyone. He thought that Minucius’s words “and concerning the ears of grain of Isis” could cohere with what precedes if one assumed that the preceding verb *facit*, “he makes,” was understood here in the sense “he represents.” LINDNER.—Elsner too, I hope—a man flourishing in singular talent and learning—will pardon me for confessing that not one of his conjectures appears successful to me. In his *Critical Essay*, published in 1744, chapter 11, pp. 111 ff., he judges that one should read either, with certain scholars, *Despice Isidis ad hirundinem sistrum*, “Look down upon the sistrum of Isis beside the swallow,” or *Despice Isidis hirundinum sistrum*, “Look down upon the swallow-sistrum of Isis.” On p. 114 he also interprets *despice* as “look at in passing” or “look upon with contempt.” But since he thinks that *ad hirundinem* can be said for “in the likeness of a swallow,” a certain critic in the *Literary News*, published at Hamburg in 1744, p. 8, rightly denies that the character of the Latin language permits such an expression.

The best μάντις, “diviner,” appears to me to be J. Huber, a doctor of law from Basel and a man highly skilled in textual criticism. While residing for several years at the University of Göttingen, he recast these words in private conversation as follows: *Aspice Isidis ac Anubidis sistrum*, “Look at the sistrum of Isis and Anubis.” It is agreed that Anubis was the son of Isis. HEUMANN.—I, however, retain Cellarius’s reading and explain it thus: *Dispice*, that is, “consider the sistrum of Isis beside (*ad*, that is, next to) the swallow.” For Isis was depicted with a small swallow and a sistrum. Just as the sistrum was a querulous instrument, so too the swallow was a querulous creature suited to lamentation. Hence it is easy to infer funerals and deaths. Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 15: “Look closely at the beauties of the Lentuli and Hostilii,” etc. LINDNER.—Others, freely tearing apart and rearranging this mutilated passage this way and that, have gone off into different opinions. It will therefore suffice merely to indicate the most recent attempts, namely those of the Lyons editor, who might perhaps favor these:

“Separate Isis’s sistrum from the swallow . . .”

or:

“Look at the ears of grain of shaggy Isis, and the sistrum,”

or, if preferred:

“The barley ear of Isis and the sistrum,”

or finally—spare us, reader!—

“From the asp of Isis to the resounding sistrum.”

[Col. 0302D] “Now close the channels, boys!” EDITORS.

**And sprinkled.** I read “and scattered.” I shall append the words of the scholiast on Lycophron, which shed no little light on this passage:

> Ὄσιρις ὁ καὶ Διόνυσος, βασιλεὺς ὑπάρχων Αἰγύπτου ὑπὸ Τυφῶνος τοῦ ἀδελφοῦ καὶ τῶν συνωμοτῶν μεληδὸν κατετμήθη, καὶ ἐξετέθη πολλαχοῦ. Ἶσις δὲ ἡ καὶ Δήμητρα, γυνὴ Διονύσου, μετὰ Ὥρου τοῦ υἱοῦ τὸν Τυφῶνα καὶ πάντας τοὺς σὺν ἐκείνῳ φονεῖς ἀναιρεῖ· τὰ δὲ τοῦ Διονύσου μέρη καὶ μόρια συλλέξασα, ἑκάστῳ ναὸν καὶ ἱερὰ ᾠκοδόμησεν.
>
> Osiris, who is also Dionysus, while king of Egypt, was cut limb from limb by his brother Typhon and the conspirators, and his pieces were exposed in many places. Isis, who is also Demeter, the wife of Dionysus, together with her son Horus, killed Typhon and all the murderers who were with him. After gathering the parts and limbs of Dionysus, she built a temple and sacred precincts for each one.

I refrain from transcribing passages from other authors, of which there are abundant numbers. MEURSIUS.

**Your Serapis.** Here Octavius again taunts Caecilius derisively because, when he encountered and noticed an image of Serapis, he had brought his hand to his mouth and pressed a kiss upon his hand with his lips—that is, had worshiped it. RIGALTIUS.

**Deaths and funerals.** So it stands in the manuscript. Julius Firmicus, *On the Error of Profane Religions*, p. 408: “But in these funerals and lamentations, which truly are funerals that happened”; where I read “that happened,” while Heumann reads “and shameful funerals.” LINDNER.

[Col. 0303B] **Bald priests.** An elegant reading presented by the Royal manuscript. Previously all editions had “and other priests.” It is well known that the priests of Isis shaved their heads, and in our youthful notes on Martial, *Epigrams* XII.29, we made several observations on these words:

> The bald linen-wearers flee, and the sistrum-bearing throng.

Artemidorus I.23:

> Ξυρᾶσθαι δὲ δοκεῖν τὴν κεφαλὴν ὅλην, πλὴν Αἰγυπτίων ἱερεῦσι καὶ γελωτοποιοῖς, καὶ τοῖς ἐξ ἔθους ἔχουσι ξυρᾶσθαι, ἀγαθόν, πᾶσι δὲ τοῖς ἄλλοις πονηρόν.
>
> To dream that one’s entire head is being shaved is good for Egyptian priests, jesters, and those who customarily shave, but bad for everyone else.

Prudentius, in the first book *Against Symmachus*:

> The rites of Isis, always mourning the lost Osiris,  
> Farcical and laughable, with their bald men.

HERALDUS.

**Cynocephalus.** He is also Anubis; Mercury, according to Plutarch’s *On Isis*. He was worshiped by the Egyptians with a dog’s face. Apuleius: “Anubis, raising aloft a dog’s high-stretched neck, bearing a caduceus in his left hand.” WOWER.

**Yet these Egyptian rites were once.** Jacob Ouzel supposes that nothing prevents us from saying that Minucius refers to the fact that, just as the Egyptians worshiped Isis, so the Romans worshiped Cybele with the same solemnities and ceremonies. In this the learned man is greatly mistaken, for it was the custom of the Romans to worship no gods unless they had been publicly adopted. See the learned J. Le Clerc on Pedo Albinovanus, *Elegy* I.24. Hence, when Piso and Gabinius were consuls, the rites of Isis were expelled from Rome, although the superstitious populace afterward received them again.

Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 6: “Serapis, Isis, and Harpocrates, together with their Cynocephalus, were prohibited from the Capitol—that is, expelled from the senate-house of the gods—by the consuls Piso and Gabinius, who certainly were not Christians. After demolishing even their altars, they renounced them. You restored them and bestowed upon them supreme majesty.” See also the same author, *To the Nations*, book I, chapter 10. Arnobius, book II, p. 95: “Did you not, after the consulships of Piso and Gabinius, enroll among the number of your gods the Egyptian deities named Serapis and Isis?” It is clearer than the midday sun that Minucius had this in view. DAVIS.

**And surrounded by a serpent.** According to Heraldus, the Royal codex has “surrounded,” but “carried about” is preferable. Ovid, *Fasti* IV.497, on Ceres:

> She yoked bridled serpents to her chariot,  
> And thus passed dry-shod across the waters of the sea.

Hieronymus Alexander also defends this reading in his *Explanation of an Ancient Tablet Engraved with the Image and Symbols of the Sun*, p. 754 of Graevius’s edition. The riddle Gronovius adduces—namely, torches surrounded by a serpent—does not prove the manuscript reading. For because Ceres is not represented on that coin as seated in a chariot, the serpents cannot be shown yoked to her chariot; instead, the torches are depicted simply as surrounded by a serpent. LINDNER.

**Libera.** Proserpina was so called. Cicero, *Against Verres*, [Col. 0304B] book IV, p. 1281 in Lambinus’s edition: “Libera, whom they also call Proserpina, was carried off from the grove of Enna.” And *On the Nature of the Gods*, book II, chapter 24: “Because we call those born from us *liberi*, ‘children,’ those born of Ceres were therefore named Liber and Libera; this is preserved in the case of Libera, but not likewise in that of Liber.” See also Arnobius, book V, pp. 171, 182. DAVIS.

**And which.** So the manuscript and the Roman edition.

**The ringing is drowned out.** I infer from Heraldus’s silence that this stands in the manuscript, since, had he read something different, he certainly would not have left the passage untouched. Meanwhile, Rigaltius testifies that the text there is *initus eliditur*, “the entrance is drowned out.” How these accounts can be reconciled I cannot see. I do see, however, that *tinnitus*, “ringing,” could easily have arisen from *initus*, and I approve it. Just as our author says “to drown out a ringing,” so Celsus IV.4 said “to drown out a whistling,” [Col. 0304C] and Pliny XI.51, “to drown out a sound similar to sneezing.” That ringing, however, was drowned out by the cymbals of the Corybantes so that the father would not hear the crying—that is, the frequent and unceasing crying. Rigaltius prefers *intus eliditur*, “it is drowned out within”; Wower, *tinnitus editur*, “a ringing is produced”; Heumann, *vagitus tinnitu eliditur*, “the crying is drowned out by the ringing.” LINDNER.

**Cybele Dindymena.** This emendation is owed to the celebrated Heumann and is far preferable to all others that have yet seen the light. Rigaltius wished to read “the Dindyma of Cybele”; Davis, “Cybele Dindymena’s”; Cellarius, “Cybele Dindymena” in the accusative. According to Meursius, the manuscript has: “Cybele Dindyma—it is shameful to say what,” etc. LINDNER.—**Cybele Dindyma.** Τὰ Δίνδυμα, “the Dindyma.” Nothing needed to be changed here. Virgil: “Go through the lofty Dindyma.” Servius: “The mountains of the Mother of the Gods.” Catullus called her the goddess of Didymus, or Dindymus. RIGALTIUS.—The manuscript presents “Cybele Dindymena,” whence the old editions exhibit “concerning Cybele Dindymena.” John Meursius, however, judged that the accusative “Cybele Dindymena” should be restored, and Christopher Cellarius printed it thus. [Col. 0304D] To me it appears scarcely doubtful that one should read *Cybelae Dindymena*, “the Dindymena of Cybele”; for the rites of Cybele were named thus from the frenzy by which their participants were driven. DAVIS.

**And she herself.** It is clearly apparent that “she herself also” must be restored. HEUMANN.

**And half-men.** Who, pray, besides the Galli, worshiped Cybele by inflicting punishment upon their own bodies? No one. One must certainly read: “The Galli, half-men, worship her”; the copyists’ stupidity introduced the conjunction after it had been deleted. DAVIS.—What if Minucius added that conjunction emphatically? Namely, with this sense: “The Galli worship Cybele to such an extent that, even as half-men, they worship her through punishment of their own bodies.”

**And disgraces.** Correctly restored thus in place of the manuscript’s “and honors.” Heumann. Heraldus thinks “mockeries and disgraces” was written. LINDNER.

**As though turned backward.** So it stands in the manuscript, and so Heraldus, Rigaltius, Davis, and Gronovius printed these words, although the last suspects that they contain some corruption and that one should read “as though he proceeds in either direction.” But I cannot approve that correction, however the most learned man may defend it in *Le Clerc’s Malicious Mockery*, p. 47. Nor am I any more pleased by the conjecture of Ursinus, Wower, and Elmenhorst, “as though also turned backward.”

A passage of Plautus, *The Merchant* I.1.90, defends the manuscript reading as uniquely correct: “She sends a slave along with me, who formerly, when I was a very small boy, had been my tutor, as though so that he might serve as my guardian.” In these words *ut*, “so that,” is redundant, as *quod*, “that,” not infrequently is. *Aversus*, moreover, means the same as “from behind.” Livy XXXIII.8: “because they were cut down from behind.” Aurelius Victor, *Origin of the Roman People* VI.2, on Cacus, the slave of Evander: “He stole the cattle of his guest Recaranus and, to prevent any evidence, dragged them backward into the cave.” It is well known that the ancients gave Janus two faces. Ovid, *Fasti* I.89 ff.:

> And let there be a face that sees what is behind,  
> And one that sees what is before him.

The reasons why the ancients imagined him two-faced are given by Vossius, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry* I.19, and Everard Otto, *On the Guardianship of Public Roads*, part I, chapter 7, p. 126. GRUNER.—I add Lactantius III.24: “I do not know what to say [Col. 0305C] about those who, once they have gone astray, persist steadfastly in folly, except that I think prudent and knowledgeable men undertake to defend falsehoods as though so that they may exercise their talents upon evil subjects.” Compare Bünemann there. LINDNER.
