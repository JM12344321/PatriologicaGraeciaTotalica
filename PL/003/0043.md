[Col. 0341B] Nor grasp. Davisius, failing to see how *grasp* was relevant, read *discern*; another learned man, in the *Leipzig Acts* for October 1733, read *behold*. Tacitus indeed uses such a construction in *Agricola* 40: “When Agricola had been seen and beheld”; and chapter 45: “The chief part of our miseries under Domitian was to be seen and observed.” Colerus there attacked this construction without justification. **SUMMARY.—** Nor can it be said that Christians conceal what they worship because they have no shrines or altars [Col. 0338B]. For they are persuaded that God cannot be confined within any temple, nor can an image of him be fashioned. [Col. 0339A] Present everywhere, however, he sees all things, even the more secret thoughts of our minds; and we live almost with him and in his bosom.

**(XXVI)** Do you suppose that we conceal what we worship because we have no shrines and altars? For what image shall I fashion for God, when, if you judge rightly, man himself is God’s image? What temple shall I erect for him, when this entire world, constructed by his work, cannot contain him? And when I, a human being, dwell more spaciously, shall I enclose the power of such majesty within one little building? Is he not better dedicated in our mind—indeed, consecrated in the innermost part of our breast? Shall I offer to the Lord sacrificial animals and victims which he has brought forth for my use, so that I may cast his own gift back to him? That would be ingratitude, since an acceptable victim is a good disposition, a pure mind, and a sincere judgment. [Col. 0339B] Therefore, [p. 48] he who cultivates innocence makes supplication to the Lord; he who cultivates justice pours a libation to God; he who abstains from fraud [Col. 0340A] propitiates God; he who rescues a person from danger slaughters the choicest victim. These are our sacrifices; these are God’s sacred rites. Thus among us the more religious man is the more just.

But, you say, we neither display nor see the God whom we worship. On the contrary, we believe him to be God precisely because we can perceive him but cannot see him. For in his works and in all the movements of the world we behold his power continually present: when it thunders, flashes, and hurls thunderbolts; when it becomes serene. Do not be surprised that you do not see God: everything is driven, shaken, and agitated by wind and gusts, yet neither wind nor gust comes before the eyes. Even in the sun, which is the cause of sight for everyone, we cannot see: its rays drive back our vision, the gaze of one looking at it is dulled, and if you look longer, all sight is extinguished. [Col. 0340B] What, then? Could you endure the very maker of the sun, that fountain of light, when you turn away from his flashes and hide from his thunderbolts? Do you wish to see God with [Col. 0341A] fleshly eyes when you cannot behold or grasp even your own soul, by which you are given life and speech?

**(XVII)** But, you say, God is ignorant of human conduct and, being stationed in heaven, can neither visit everyone nor know individuals. You err, human being, and are deceived. For how is God far away, when all things in heaven and on earth, and those beyond this province of the world, are known to God and filled with God? Everywhere he is not merely very near us but infused within all things. Consider the sun once again: fixed in heaven, it is nevertheless diffused over every land; equally present everywhere, [p. 49] it enters into and mingles with everything, yet its brightness is nowhere impaired. How much more does God, the author and observer of all things, from whom no secret can be kept, enter into darkness and enter into our thoughts, which are like a second darkness. We do not merely act [Col. 0341B] beneath him; we live also with him, so to speak, almost.

**CHAPTER XXXIII.—Flatter ourselves.** Here, if nowhere else, one may see how full of hazard is the task of emending ancient authors. Cellarius had noted at these words: “He answers the objections of chapter 9.” Since the distinguished Heumann believed this and emended the text to “we flatter ourselves: we seem numerous to you,” he suffered a human lapse. For the author is not referring to the passage cited by Cellarius, but to chapter 10, where Caecilius had attacked divine providence with an argument drawn from the frequency and multitude of human beings. It is exactly like the man in Juvenal, Satire 13:

> If they are therefore concerned to punish every guilty man,  
> When will they reach me?

Octavius now replies that human beings have no reason to flatter themselves about their great numbers, and so forth. For he already answered chapter 9 [Col. 0342D] in chapter 31. Compare, if desired, the *Arrangement of This Dialogue* prefixed to this edition.  
—LINDNER.

Kings only. Johannes Meursius emended this to: “Kings know the condition of their kingdom through the various duties of their ministers.” But the learned man departed from the manuscript reading because he did not understand its construction (*syntaxis*, σύνταξις), which will be sufficiently clear if the sentence is punctuated thus: “Kings alone know all that pertains to their kingdom through the duties of their ministers.” He said “all things of their kingdom” for “all the affairs of their kingdom,” or “their entire kingdom,” as in “the shaded parts of the roads,” “the expanses of the fields,” and similar expressions. Tacitus, *Histories* III, 4: “They looked to the foremost measures for peace”; and V, 10: “He held all the plains … with his victorious army.” So frequently elsewhere.  
—DAVIS.

I agree with the learned man. For only kings—because, distracted among all things, they cannot attend to individuals, and, occupied with individuals, cannot meet the needs of all—know everything in their kingdom through the duties of ministers. God, however, has no need of informants.  
—LINDNER.

—HEUMANN.

There is no need. For *indicium* is used substantivally for *index*, “an informer.” Lactantius, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors* VIII, 4: “There was no lack of very wealthy [Col. 0343B] senators who, after informers had been secretly suborned, were accused of having aspired to imperial power.”  
—LINDNER.

So long as they worshiped him chastely…. According to Meursius, the manuscript presents “for so long then chastely.” I think it should be read: “so long as they worshiped him chastely….” For authors customarily insert pronouns of this kind after a parenthesis (*parenthesis*, παρένθεσις). Cicero, *Academica* IV, chapter 7: “But if the conceptions were false” (for you seemed to call concepts [ἐννοίας] “conceptions”), “if, therefore, these were false.” The same author, *On Fate*, chapter 16. So also Caesar, *Gallic War* III, chapter 22. J. F. Gronovius collected many passages of the ancients concerning this usage in his notes on Livy XXV, 27, and Seneca, *On Anger* I, 3.  
—DAVIS.

And with the elements. A comma has been placed most wrongly after *command* in every edition I have seen, and thus it has not been possible to recognize that *and* means *also*. Read: “at God’s command, with even the elements striving in their support.” Among other things, our author has in view the history of Joshua, who obtained the sun and moon, and even rainstorms and hailstones, as though they were [Col. 0343C] auxiliary forces. In place of *overwhelmed*, which hardly suits this passage, I would write *defeated*. Nor does *striving in their support* quite satisfy me. Did Minucius perhaps write *assisting* or *fighting alongside them*?  
—HEUMANN.

*Overwhelmed* is well said of the few, since Virgil, *Aeneid* II, 424, said of the many, “We are overwhelmed by their number”; and Ovid, *Fasti* I, 488:

> That storm overwhelmed mighty men.

Aulus Gellius does several times mention a rhetorician of that name in *Attic Nights* I, 4, and IX, 1 and 15. The point of somewhat greater importance to antiquarians (*philarchaioi*, φιλαρχαίοις) arises when we ask how Flavius Josephus came to be mentioned here. For our author sets Jews in opposition to Romans “from the contrary side” (*ex enantias*, ἐξ ἐναντίας) [Col. 0343D]. This happened because Josephus was a very celebrated author and treated the same subject as Julianus.

**SUMMARY.—** But if it is nevertheless said that God was of no benefit to the Jews, the writers of Jewish annals are certainly most trustworthy witnesses that the Jews first deserted God before they were themselves deserted by him.

Nor should we flatter ourselves because of our numbers: we seem numerous to ourselves, but to God we are very few. We distinguish peoples and nations; to God this entire world is one household. Kings alone know all that pertains to their kingdom through the duties of their ministers; God has no need of informants: we live not merely under his eyes but also in his bosom.

But, you say, it did the Jews no good that they too worshiped one God with the greatest superstition, at altars and temples. You slip into ignorance if, forgetful or unaware of earlier [Col. 0342B] events, you remember only the later. For they too worshiped our God [Col. 0343A]—for the same God belongs to everyone. So long as they worshiped him chastely, innocently, and religiously, so long as they obeyed his salutary commands, they grew from a few into a countless people, from needy men into the rich, from servants into kings. Though few, they overwhelmed many; though unarmed, they overwhelmed the armed men pursuing them as they fled, at God’s command and with even the elements striving in their support. Read their writings again; or, if you take greater pleasure in Roman authors, let us pass over the ancients and consult Flavius Josephus or Antoninus Julianus concerning the Jews. Then you will know that by their own wickedness they deserved this fate, and that nothing happened to them which had not [p. 50] previously been foretold should they persist in their obstinacy. Thus you will understand that they deserted God before they were deserted by him, and that they were not, as you impiously say, captured with their God, but were surrendered by God as deserters from his discipline.

**CHAPTER XXXIV.—Or difficult not to believe.** That is, “to believe with difficulty or not at all.” The distinguished Gruner explains it in almost the same way, except that he reads “either difficult or not to believe.” I, however, would merely transpose the words: “difficult or not.” Thus chapter 12: “And God endures it, disregards it, (or) either does not wish or is unable to assist his own.” In this way, I think, the least violence is done to the manuscript, which reads: “or difficult not to believe.” We could also read “that it is difficult,” but the former reading is preferable. Some editions have: “Either that an unforeseen fire should fall, or to believe it difficult.” Rigaltius thinks the true reading can be restored thus: “either to believe that an unforeseen fire should fall, or that it is difficult for it not to fall.” Boxhorn [Col. 0344C] finds it far more pleasing if it is rewritten: “Either that an unforeseen fire should fall, or not to believe that it is sufficient.” Gronovius restores: “Either that an unforeseen fire should fall or burst apart.” Wopken thinks the author wrote with less constraint: “But for you to find it difficult or impossible to believe that conflagrations of the world or an unforeseen fire will fall is a common error.” In Heumann’s judgment the words of Minucius are: “But not to believe in the world’s conflagration is a common error.”  
—LINDNER.

Who does not know? Under Heumann’s hypothesis, by which he expels “or difficult,” these words also had to be deleted. He who doubts believes with difficulty; he who does not know does not believe.  
—LINDNER.

The fresh water of springs to nourish the seas, destined to pass into the force of fire. An old copy has suggested to us a splendid emendation of this formerly very corrupt passage. For it reads exactly thus: “The fresh water of springs to nourish the seas, destined to pass into the force of fire.” This reading must be understood as follows: “that the act of nourishing is destined to pass into the force of fire” is an established Stoic opinion. But the words that follow—“that, once the moisture has been consumed, this entire world catches fire”—are superfluous and savor of a note carried over from the margin. They say nothing other than what was already stated more elegantly: “the fresh waters of springs nourish the seas, destined to pass into the force of fire”; that is, the seas will no longer nourish the springs with fresh waters, but that nourishment will pass [Col. 0344D] into the force of fire: that sustenance will be transformed into flames, and torrents of fire will boil up from the source of the springs.  
—RIGALT.

Gelenius printed “to be nourished by the fresh water of springs, or by the sea”; Cellarius did not differ. But, as it seems to me, Nicolaus Rigaltius made an elegant emendation from the Royal manuscript. It is certainly not rare for an infinitive to be used as a noun. Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* I, chapter 8: “I do not purchase the act of repenting at so high a price.” Arnobius II, p. 27: “Not knowing what the very act of fearing is.” So too Sulpicius Severus, *Sacred History* II, chapter 3.

I think it should further be noted that this passage still suffers from defective punctuation. Since [Col. 0345A] Nicolaus Rigaltius did not notice this, he wrongly [Col. 0345B] expelled seven words as a gloss. Punctuate: “The fresh waters of springs—the seas’ act of nourishing destined to pass into the force of fire?” For these words depend upon what precedes. The author then continues: “It is an established Stoic opinion that, once the moisture has been consumed, this entire world catches fire.” These are nearly the words of Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II, 46: “From this our philosophers think that what Panaetius, they used to say, doubted will occur: that in the end the entire world will catch fire, since, once the moisture has been consumed, the earth could no longer be nourished, nor could the air return, whose origin could not exist once all water had been exhausted.” The same author, III, 14: “What then? Do you not also hold that every fire needs sustenance and cannot possibly continue unless it is nourished? And that the sun, moon, and remaining stars are nourished, some by fresh waters and others by seawater?” Lucretius I, 231:

> Whence do the sea, its native springs, and rivers flowing  
> Far away maintain their supply? Whence does the ether feed the stars?

There are therefore three things about which no wise man doubts: first, that all things—and heaven foremost among them—began; [Col. 0345C] second, that they will sometime cease; and third, that meanwhile they are nourished. The hinge of the entire argument also turns upon these three divisions. Heaven began; therefore God exists. Heaven will cease; therefore there is an end of the world and punishment after death. Heaven is nourished; therefore providence exists.  
—DAVIS, RIGALTIUS.

To distort. That is, to obscure or divert. Pythagoras handed down the doctrine of resurrection, but of the soul alone and not also of the body, and thus with a halved faith; but so that the truth might be distorted still further, he adds that human souls return into animals, when he should have said that they return into their own bodies. Heumann thinks Minucius wrote: “They also add, in order to turn savagery back, that human souls return into beasts, birds, and wild animals.” “To turn savagery back” means to transfer savagery into gentleness, as Cicero speaks in *For Sestius*, chapter 42.  
—LINDNER.

That opinion is worthy of a mime’s vice. Thus the exemplar. “A mime’s vice” means “in the manner of a mime,” namely, seeking laughter from every possible source. Minucius, from Christian austerity, calls this manner a vice and contrasts the mime’s vice with a philosopher’s study. What is found in some [Col. 0346C] editions, “the abuse of a mime,” might appear to answer “a philosopher’s study” more aptly. But Minucius rightly says that the opinion that human souls will return into the bodies of cattle and beasts is worthy of a mime’s vice. For among the other vices of the mimetic art, surely this is the most disgraceful: mimes often assume the forms of cattle and beasts. Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 48: “There will also be need of many idle jests if we wish to be wanton on this subject: who might appear to be transformed into which beast.”  
—RIGALT.

Rigaltius labors in vain when he strives to vindicate this reading for Minucius. The entirely true reading is “the abuse of a mime,” which is presented by the Roman edition, Elmenhorstius, Wowerus, and Cellarius, and approved by Jacob Gronovius. Minucius means that the opinion concerning the transmigration of souls is unworthy of a philosopher’s serious reflection, but so ridiculous that it could provide even a theatrical mime with material for entertaining people. Elmenhorstius appropriately cited here Lactantius VII, 12: “The opinion of a madman was ridiculous, more worthy of a mime than of a school.” [Col. 0346D] The word *abuse* will seem extremely appropriate here if you read Manucius’ observation on Cicero, *Letters to Friends* IX, 16: “Now I come to your jests, since, following Accius’ *Oenomaus*, you introduced not, as was formerly customary, an Atellan farce, but, as is now done, a mime.”  
—GRUNER.

Critics long ago saw that Minucius wrote “the jeering of a mime.” They are aided by the Roman edition, which, according to Heraldus, has “hostile vice,” but according to Wowerus “a mime’s jeering.” On this word see the observations of philologists on Phaedrus I, 6, 5. Add Ovid, *Metamorphoses* V, 675, and Cicero, *Letters to Quintus* II, Letter 1, where jeering is contrasted with silence, as it also is in *For Archias*, chapter 6. The same author, *Letters to Friends* I, Letter 5: “When Pompey spoke for Milo, he was assailed by shouting and jeering.” And *Against Verres* V: “The banquets were conducted not with that silence … nor with that modesty … but with the greatest shouting and jeering.” You see that in Minucius “a mime’s jeering” does not mean serious insults but mockery and ridicule, customarily delivered with loud shouting. Hear [Col. 0347B] Lactantius speaking similarly in VII, 12, 31: “Since this opinion of a madman was ridiculous and more worthy of a mime than of a school, it did not even deserve a serious refutation.”  
—HEUMANN.

As at first to have been capable of being fashioned. Wowerus [Col. 0347C] substituted “as at first he could be fashioned” entirely in vain, as we observed earlier in the *Tullian Readings*. A similar infinitive appears just above: “Who does not know that heaven, just as to have begun, will cease?” in place of “as it began”; chapter 33: “Thus you will understand that they deserted him before themselves to have been deserted,” that is, “before they were deserted.” Orosius III, 2: “Whoever does not know that these peoples now grow old in theaters just as they then to have wasted away in military camps.” See V, 1, and frequently elsewhere.  
—WOPKEN.

To be nothing after death … to have been nothing. Heumann expels these words, evidently to free the author from the error of which Dupin accuses him in his *Ecclesiastical Library*: maintaining that souls die together with the body. If this is the true method of vindicating the Fathers from the stain of error, there is hope that the Fathers will shortly appear immune from every error. It is manifest that Minucius speaks of the body alone. God first fashioned it and will form it anew. What is being born from nothing other than having been nothing before one’s origin? And what is restoration from nothing after [Col. 0347D] death other than being nothing after death? I detect no error here. He speaks in the same manner as our Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 48: “Consider again what you were before you existed: certainly nothing, for you would have remembered if you had been anything. **SUMMARY.—** There is nothing astonishing if this world is finally to be consumed by fire, since all things that have a beginning also have an end. Nor do the ancient philosophers dissent from the doctrine of a conflagration of the world. It is nevertheless certain that God can raise from death to life the human being whom he fashioned from nothing. All nature, moreover, rehearses the resurrection to come.

But regarding the conflagration of the world, to believe either that an unforeseen fire will fall, or to find it difficult not to believe this, is a common error. For which of the wise doubts, who does not know, that all things which have arisen perish, and that things which were made pass away? That heaven too, together with everything contained in heaven, will cease just as it began? That the fresh water [Col. 0345A] of springs nourishes the seas, which nourishment is destined to pass into the force of fire? It is an established Stoic opinion that, once the moisture has been consumed, this entire world catches fire; and the Epicureans hold precisely the same opinion concerning the conflagration of the elements and the destruction of the world. Plato speaks of portions of the world now being flooded and says that, at alternating intervals, they now catch fire; and although he said that the world itself had been fashioned eternal and indissoluble, he nevertheless adds that to its own maker, God alone, it is both dissoluble and mortal. Thus there is nothing astonishing if that mass is destroyed by him by whom it was constructed.

You observe that the philosophers argue [Col. 0346A] the same things that we say—not because we have followed in their footsteps, but because, from the divine proclamations of the prophets, they imitated the shadow of a corrupted truth. Thus also the more renowned of the wise—Pythagoras first, and Plato chiefly—handed down, with a corrupted and halved faith, the condition of being born again. For, once bodies have been dissolved, they hold that souls alone both remain perpetually and repeatedly pass into other, [p. 51] new bodies. To these doctrines they add the following in order to distort the truth: that human souls return into cattle, birds, and wild beasts. This opinion is worthy not of a philosopher’s study but of a mime’s vice.

Yet [Col. 0347A] for the present purpose it is enough that your wise men agree with us to some degree even on this point. Besides, who is so foolish or brutish as to dare deny that, just as a human being could at first be fashioned by God, so he can be formed anew? That he is nothing after death and was nothing before birth? That just as it was possible to be born from nothing, so it is possible to be born anew and restored from nothing? Furthermore, it is more difficult for that which does not exist to begin than for that which once existed to be repeated.

Do you believe that anything perishes even for God if it is withdrawn from our dull eyes? Every body—whether it dries into dust, is dissolved into moisture, compressed into ashes, or attenuated into vapor—is withdrawn from us, but is reserved for God in the keeping of the elements. Nor, as you believe, do we fear any injury from burial; rather, we follow the ancient and better custom of interment.

Consider [Col. 0347B], then, how all nature, for our consolation, rehearses the resurrection to come. The sun sinks and rises; the stars fall and return; flowers die and revive; after old age the shrubs put forth leaves; seeds grow green again only after they have decayed. Thus the body conceals its greenness in the age, as trees do in the wintry season, with a feigned dryness. Why are you impatient for it to revive and return while winter is still unripe? We too must await [p. 52] the springtime of the body.

Nor [Col. 0348A] am I unaware that most people, conscious of their deserts, desire rather than believe that they will be nothing after death. For they prefer to be utterly extinguished rather than restored for punishment. Their error is increased both by the unrestricted freedom allowed in this age and by God’s very great patience: the more delayed his judgment, the more just it is.
