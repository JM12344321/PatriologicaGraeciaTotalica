[Note: [Col. 0206C] All read “pronounced.” Junius says *praenuntiavit*, “foretold,” would be clearer.] By tolerating evil as good instead of rooting it out, he has been found its champion: [Note: [Col. 0206C] Semler places a comma here.] wickedly, if by his will; shamefully, if by necessity. God will be either the servant [Col. 0206C] or the friend of evil, since he associates with the evil of matter, to say nothing of his even working from its evil.

## CHAPTER XI

And yet on what grounds [Note: [Col. 0206C] “On what grounds for us?” All read more correctly than Semler, who has: [Col. 0206D] “not from us.”] does Hermogenes persuade us [Note: [Col. 0206D] “That matter is evil?” He had just replied to Hermogenes’ objections as though granting their truth; here, however, he rises against him and demands that he demonstrate that matter is evil and show from whom he derived this claim. LE PR.] that matter is evil? For he cannot avoid calling that [Col. 0207A] evil to which he ascribes evil. For we have established that what is eternal cannot admit diminution [Note: [Col. 0207C] Junius reads *definiimus deminutionem*.] or subjection, [Note: [Col. 0207C] All read “cannot, what is eternal,” except Semler and Oberthür, who read: “but if it is so.”] so that it might be reckoned inferior to another coeternal being. Thus even now we say that evil cannot belong to it, because it cannot thereby be subjected—since what is eternal can in no way be subjected.

But since, on other grounds, it is agreed that what is eternal, like God, is the supreme good—and that it is precisely through being eternal that he alone is God, and thus through being God that he is good—how will evil inhere in matter, which, because it is eternal, must necessarily be believed to be the supreme good? Or if what is eternal can also be susceptible to evil, the same may be believed of God; and Hermogenes has eagerly transferred evil away from God without cause, if evil is compatible with eternity by being compatible with matter.

Moreover, if what is eternal can be believed evil, evil will be unconquerable and insuperable [Col. 0207B] because it is eternal. And thus [Note: [Col. 0207C] “And thus,” Rigault; Junius: “and yet almost in vain.” But Wouwer’s codex reads: “and then we labor in vain … from ourselves; then God too … about to punish. ## CHAPTER XII

Come now, let us believe matter to be evil and supremely evil, namely by nature, just as we believe God to be good and supremely good, likewise by nature. Furthermore, nature must be held certain and fixed: persevering in evil in the case of matter, just as in the case of God it is unconvertible and unchangeable in good. [Note: [Col. 0208C] Namely, because if nature in matter is changed, it will be capable of being changed from evil into good, and in God from good—not into evil. Semler, Oberthür.] Namely, because if nature in matter is changed from evil into good, it will also be capable of being changed in God from good into evil.

At this point someone will say: “Then children of Abraham will not be raised up from stones, the brood of vipers will not produce fruit of repentance (Luke III.7–8), and children of wrath will not become children of peace (Eph. II.3), if nature is [Col. 0208B] not mutable.” You will appeal to these examples rashly, O man, for things that have come into being—stones, vipers, and human beings—do not apply to the case of matter, which is unbegotten. Since their nature has an institution, it can also have a cessation.

But hold matter, once and for all, to have been determined as eternal, as unmade, as unbegotten; and therefore it must be believed to possess an unchangeable and incorruptible nature. This follows even from Hermogenes’ own proposition, which he advances [Note: [Col. 0208C] Junius reads “sets forth.”] when he denies that God could have made anything from himself, because what is eternal is not changed. For, in losing what it had been while through change it becomes what it was not, it would not be eternal; whereas the Lord, being eternal, can be nothing other than what he always is.

By this same definition I shall deservedly strike him back. I find equal fault with matter when from it—evil and supremely evil—even good and supremely good things are made by God: [Col. 0208C] “And God saw that they were good,” and “God blessed them” (Gen. I.[Col. 0209A] 3, 31)—surely because they were supremely good, certainly not because they were evil and supremely evil. Matter therefore admitted change, and if so, it lost the condition of eternity; indeed, it died to its own form. But eternity cannot be lost, because unless it cannot be lost, it is not eternity. Therefore matter could not have admitted change either, because, if it is eternal, it can in no way be changed.

## CHAPTER XIII

And the question will be asked: How were good things made from it, when they were in no way made through change? Whence was there in what is evil and supremely evil a seed of good and supreme goodness? Certainly a good tree does not produce evil fruits, because God too is nothing but good; nor does an evil tree produce good fruits, because matter too is nothing but supremely evil.

Or if we grant matter some seed of good as well, it will no longer be of a uniform nature, that is, entirely evil, [Col. 0209B] but rather twofold, that is, [Note: [Col. 0209D] In the Venetian edition the words “entirely evil, but rather twofold, that is” are absent, doubtless through a typographical error.] of a good and an evil nature. And it will again be asked whether light and darkness, sweetness and bitterness, can agree in good and evil. Or if the diversity of both good and evil could have come together, and matter could have possessed a twofold nature fruitful in both kinds of fruit, then good things themselves will no longer be credited to God, just as evil things will not be charged to matter; rather, each kind, derived from matter’s own property, will belong to matter. In that case we shall owe God neither gratitude for good things nor resentment for evil ones, because he produced nothing from his own ingenuity.

## CHAPTER XIV

By this it will be manifestly proved that he was enslaved to matter. For even if it is said that, although occasioned by matter, he nevertheless brought forth good things by his own choice, as though he had found the good [Col. 0209C] in matter—even though this too is shameful—yet certainly, when he also brings forth evil things from that same matter, by bringing forth at least these not according to his own choice, he serves matter, having nothing else he can do but bring forth evil from evil: unwillingly, surely, insofar as he is good; from necessity, insofar as he is unwilling; and from servitude, insofar as he acts from necessity.

Which, then, is more worthy: that he created evil things from necessity, or from his will? Certainly he created them from necessity if from matter; from his will, if from nothing. For now you labor without cause to prevent God from being established as the author of evils, because even if he made them from matter, they will be attributed to him who made them, precisely insofar as he made them.

Plainly, it makes as much difference whence he made them as if he had made them from nothing; and it makes no difference whence he made them, provided he made them from the source from which it better befitted him to make them. But it better befitted him to make them by his will than from necessity—[Col. 0209D] that is, from nothing rather than from matter. It is more worthy to believe God to be the free author even of evils [Col. 0210A] than their slave. Power of any kind befits him more than impotence.

Even if we grant on this basis that matter possessed no good at all, but that the Lord, if he produced anything good, produced it by his own power, other questions will equally arise. First, [Note: [Col. 0209D] “If good did not exist at all in matter.” Since a good tree does not produce evil fruit, and conversely, by what reasoning, heretic, will you prove that anything good was made from matter, which you make evil? LE PR.] if good did not exist at all in matter, good was not made from matter, since matter did not possess it. Next, if it was not from matter, then it was made from God. If it was not from God either, then it was made from nothing. For this alone remains under Hermogenes’ arrangement.

## CHAPTER XV

Furthermore, if good was made neither from matter, because it did not exist in matter as evil matter, nor from God, because nothing could be made from God, as Hermogenes maintains, good is now found to have been made from nothing, as having been [Col. 0210B] made from no source—neither from matter nor from God. And if good came from nothing, why not evil as well? Indeed, why not everything from nothing, if anything came from nothing? Unless divine power was insufficient to produce everything, though it did produce something from nothing.

Or if good proceeded from evil matter, because it came neither from nothing nor from God, it will follow that it proceeded from a transformation [Note: [Col. 0209D] The Venetian edition wrongly reads “confession.”] of matter, contrary to the denied [Note: [Col. 0209D] All read “contrary to the denied,” except Semler and Oberthür.] possibility of transforming what is eternal. Thus Hermogenes will now deny that good could have arisen from the very source from which it did arise. Yet it must necessarily have proceeded from one of those sources from which he denied that it could proceed.

Moreover, if evil did not come from nothing in order that it might not belong to God, from whose will it would appear to derive, but instead came from matter so that it might belong to that from whose substance it was made, then here too, as I have said, God will be held the author of evil. For with the same power and [Col. 0210C] will he ought either to have brought forth all things good [Note: [Col. 0209D] Rigault and the Venetian edition insert “good” from Wouwer’s codex.] from matter, or to have brought forth only good things—though not all good things—yet he also brought forth evil things: [Note: [Col. 0209D] Junius reads: “or he would have brought forth only good things (though not all good things); or also evil things”; but this is unnecessary.] surely either willing evil things to exist, if he was able to ensure that they did not exist, or unable to make all things good, if he willed this but did not accomplish it. It makes no difference whether the Lord became the author of evil through weakness or through will.

Or what reason was there that, after making good things as one who was good, he should also bring forth evil things as one who was not good, when he did not produce only things congruous with himself? [Note: [Col. 0210D] Latinus reads “as good, as not good.”] What necessity was there, after producing his own work, for him also to attend to matter’s business by bringing forth evil as well, so that he alone might be known as good from the good, [Note: [Col. 0210D] In the other editions the question mark is placed only after “evil.”] while matter might not [Note: [Col. 0210D] Junius reads “so that it might be known.”] be known as evil from the evil? Good would have flourished more fully without the breath of evil. For Hermogenes himself attacks the arguments of certain people [Col. 0210D] who say that evil things were necessary for illuminating good things, which are understood from their contraries.

[Col. 0211A] Therefore either there was no place for bringing forth evil even for that reason; or, if some other rationale requires evil to be introduced, why could it not also have been introduced from nothing? That very rationale would excuse the Lord from being regarded as the author of evil, just as it now excuses him when [Note: [Col. 0211C] *Cum* is inserted in the other editions; Semler says this is better, though he also [Col. 0211D] omits it.] he produces evil things from matter. [Note: [Col. 0211D] *Cum* is absent here from all editions.] If it excuses him, the question is thereby everywhere and from every direction driven to the point that they reject. For by failing to examine the rationale of evil itself and to discern how they may either attribute evil to God or separate it from God, they expose God to numerous and still more unworthy demolitions.

## CHAPTER XVI

Therefore, by way of laying the groundwork for this issue, which may perhaps be reconsidered elsewhere, I for my part define the alternatives thus: either both the good and the evil that God made from matter must be attributed to God, or both must be attributed to matter itself, from which he made them; or both must be attributed to both, [Col. 0211B] because the two parties—the one who made and that from which he made—are mutually bound; or one must be attributed to the one and the other to the other, for there is no third party besides matter and God.

Now, if both belong to God, God will appear to be the author of evil also; but God, being good, will not be the author of evil. If both belong to matter, [Note: [Col. 0211D] Semler and Oberthür read “matter.”] matter will appear to be the womb of good also; but matter that is wholly evil will not be the womb of good. If both belong to both, matter will in this respect too be compared with God, and the two will be partners, equally allied to evil and good; but matter must not be equated with God, lest it produce two gods. If one belongs to one and the other to the other—surely good to God [Note: [Col. 0211D] “Surely to God,” thus Wouwer, correctly; Semler, “surely God”; Rigault, “surely to God.”], and evil to matter—neither evil is attributed to God nor good to matter. Yet God makes both good and evil when he makes them from matter.

If these things are so, [Col. 0211C] I do not know how Hermogenes’ position [Note: [Col. 0211D] Semler reads “Hermogenes.”] can escape, since he thinks that God, [Note: [Col. 0211D] Rhenanus reads “who, God.”] who in whatever manner created evil from matter—whether by will, necessity, or reason—is not the author of evil.

Furthermore, if the author of evil is the one who made it, while matter is plainly his partner through supplying the substance, [Note: [Col. 0211D] “Matter is plainly his partner through the furnishing of substance.” He not infrequently uses this word *suggestus*, sometimes for ornament or the addition of decoration, as here and in *On the Dress of Women*, chapter II: “You should know that you must reject not only the equipment of fabricated and elaborated lust, but also,” etc. Sometimes it means a framework or texture, as in *On the Pallium*, chapter 1, where there is “sacred furnishing.” The glosses do not depart from this meaning, for *suggestus* is rendered by σῆμα, “sign,” and ὕφος, “texture”; and elsewhere ὕφος means woven fabric. Also in the *Apology*, chapter 16: “the array of images.” LE PR.] you now exclude [Note: [Col. 0212C] Rhenanus reads “you excuse.”] the very rationale for introducing matter. For God is nevertheless shown to be the author of evil even through matter, if matter was posited precisely to prevent God from appearing the author of evil.

Matter therefore being excluded, since the rationale for it is excluded, it remains established [Col. 0212A] that God made all things from nothing. We shall see whether this includes evil things too, when it becomes apparent which things are evil and whether the things that you currently suppose evil really are evil. [Note: [Col. 0212D] Rhenanus reads “and meanwhile even the things you suppose,” with punctuation before “meanwhile.”] For he produced these things too more worthily from his own will, by producing them from nothing, than under another’s prejudgment, had he produced them from matter. Freedom, not necessity, befits God. He would better have willed to create evil things from himself than have been unable not to create them. [Note: [Col. 0212D] Latinus inserts “because.”]

## CHAPTER XVII

The status of the one God vindicates this rule: he is not otherwise one unless he is alone; [Note: [Col. 0211D] “Not otherwise one.” Take this rule from Tertullian as one common to others: God is either one or none. For it is impious and damnable to conceive anything equal to God. He alone is Creator and preserver, he alone Lord and arbiter of all things; and, if it is permissible to apply that profane saying to the most solemn of all subjects, οὐκ ἀγαθὸν πολυκοιρανίη, “the rule of many is not good.” LE PR.] not otherwise alone unless there is nothing with him. Thus he will also be first, because all things are after him; thus all things are after him, because all things are from him; thus they are from him, because they are from nothing. In this way the rationale of that Scripture too may stand: “Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who was his counselor? Or whom did he consult? Or who demonstrated to him the way of understanding and knowledge? Who has given to him, so that it will be repaid to him?” (Rom. XI.34–35). No one, surely, because no force, no matter, no nature belonging to another substance was present with him. [Note: [Col. 0212D] Semler reads “will be present.”]

Moreover, if he worked from something, he must necessarily have received from that very thing both counsel and the method of his arrangement, [Col. 0212B] as well as [Note: [Col. 0212D] Junius reads “and the way.”] the way of understanding and knowledge. For he had to work in accordance with the quality of the thing and according to the character of matter, not according to his own will—so much so that he would have made evil things according to a nature not his own, but that of the substance.

## CHAPTER XVIII

If matter was necessary to God for the works of the world, as Hermogenes supposed, God possessed matter far worthier and more suitable—not to be sought among philosophers, but to be understood among the prophets: [Note: [Col. 0212D] “His Wisdom, namely.” He means the Wisdom of God, which so surpasses human minds and thought that all the wisdom of the world is utter foolishness before God. Hence he teaches that this Wisdom must be sought not among the philosophers but among the Prophets. For one who does not know God cannot speak rightly about God, as is the case with the philosophers, whom Septimius calls creatures of vainglory; rather, the prophets and saints were inspired by the Spirit of the same supreme God. LE PR.] namely, his own Wisdom (*Sophia*). [Col. 0212C] For she alone knew the mind of the Lord. “Who knows the things of God and what is in him, except the Spirit who is in him?” (1 Cor. II.11). But Wisdom is Spirit. She was his counselor; she herself is the way of understanding and knowledge (Prov. VIII). From her he made, by making through her and making with her.

“When he prepared heaven,” she says, “I was present with him; and when he made strong the things above the winds—the clouds—and when he established the firm springs [Note: [Col. 0212D] Rhenanus, Semler, and Junius read “mountains.”] of that which is beneath heaven, I was joining things together with him. I was the one in whom he rejoiced, and daily I [Col. 0213A] delighted in his presence” (ibid.): when he delighted after he had perfected the world, and took delight in the sons of men.

Who would not commend her instead as the source and origin of all things, and as the matter of all matters—[Note: [Col. 0213C] “Of matters” is absent from Pithoeus’ book. Rigault. Junius reads “but matter of matters—not,” and says that this was appended from the Hirsau codex on Rhenanus’ authority.] not subordinate to him, not different in condition, not restless in motion, not formless in aspect, but innate to him and his own, orderly and beautiful—as the sort that God [Note: [Col. 0213C] “Of which God could have had need? Needing his own more than another’s,” Semler and Oberthür. The Hirsau codex has “of alien stock”; from this Rhenanus proposed: “his own rather than things of alien stock.”] could have needed, needing what was his own rather than what belonged to another?

Indeed, as soon as he perceived her as necessary for the works of the world, he immediately created and generated her within himself: “The Lord,” she says, “created me as the beginning of his ways for his works; before the ages he established me, before he made the earth, before the mountains were set in place; but before all the hills he generated me; before the abyss [Note: [Col. 0213C] Junius reads “likewise before the abyss.”] I was generated” (ibid.). [Note: [Col. 0213D] “I was generated. Let him recognize.” Rhenanus and Oberthür; Semler reluctantly and with disapproval.]

Let Hermogenes therefore recognize [Col. 0213B] that the Wisdom of God is proclaimed to have been born and created for this very reason: so that we should believe nothing unbegotten and uncreated besides God alone. For if even that which was within the Lord, from him and in him, was not without a beginning—namely, his own Wisdom, generated and created from the moment when she began to be stirred in the mind of God for arranging the works of the world—much more [Note: [Col. 0213D] “It is inadmissible that anything existed without a beginning.” You will learn everywhere and often from Tertullian’s preceding books his use of the impersonal verb *capit*, which among the Greeks is ἐνδέχεται, “it is possible.” Here “it is inadmissible that anything existed” means simply “It cannot be.” RHEN.] is it impossible that anything outside the Lord existed without a beginning.

But if this same Wisdom of God is the Word in respect of meaning—Wisdom—[Note: [Col. 0213D] Junius reads “the Word is meaning, Wisdom”; Pithoeus’ book, “meaning and Wisdom.”] and if “without him nothing was made that was made” (John I.3), just as nothing was arranged without Wisdom, how is it possible that anything besides the Father was older than the Son of God, the Word, the Only-begotten and Firstborn? And in this way it would certainly be more nobly born, to say nothing of the fact that the unbegotten would be stronger than the begotten [Note: [Col. 0214C] Franek inserts “begotten.”], and the unmade more powerful than the made. [Note: [Col. 0214C] Semler and Oberthür read “made thing.”] For that which [Col. 0213C] needed no author in order to exist will be far more exalted than that which had some author in order to exist.

Accordingly, if evil is unbegotten, while the Word of God was born—[Note: [Col. 0214C] Semler and Oberthür read: “unbegotten, that is, the Word of God.”] “For he uttered,” he says, “the best word” (Ps. XLIV.1)—I do not know whether evil can be made subordinate to good, the stronger to the weaker, the unbegotten to the begotten. Thus by this designation too Hermogenes places matter before God, by placing it before the Son. For the Son is the Word, and “the Word was God” (John I.1); and “I and the Father are one” (John X.10)—[Col. 0214A] unless, perhaps, the Son will calmly endure having placed before himself what is equated with the Father.

## CHAPTER XIX

But I shall also appeal to Moses’ original document, from which the opposing party too tries, willy-nilly, [Note: [Col. 0214C] Junius reads *ingratiis*, “unwillingly.”] to support its suspicions, lest it should appear not to be instructed from the source from which it ought. Accordingly, it seized opportunities [Note: [Col. 0214C] Junius reads “opportunities there.”] from certain words, as is generally the custom of heretics: to twist every simple expression.

[Note: [Col. 0213D] “For even the very beginning in which God made heaven and earth.” Whoever interprets Moses’ words, “In the beginning God created heaven and earth,” as follows seems readily able to resolve all these quibbles: “At the beginning at which heaven and earth came into existence, God created them.” For this is what Moses meant: by the names “heaven” and “earth” he signified the whole universe as created by God. Then he explains in what order and manner each of its principal parts was created. RIG.—Whatever Tertullian and his annotators may have said, many doctors of the Church are nevertheless found who understand Genesis I.1, “In the beginning,” as referring to the Word of God; thus Basil, Ambrose, and others. Others attribute another sense to these words, as may be seen in Cornelius à Lapide or in the *Complete Course of Sacred Scripture*. EDD.]

For they wish even the very “beginning” in which God made heaven and earth to have been something substantival and corporeal, which could be interpreted as matter. We, however, vindicate for each word its own proper meaning: [Note: [Col. 0214C] Semler reads “when we vindicate its proper meaning.”] that *principium*, “beginning,” means an initiation and was appropriately placed in reference to things beginning to be made. For nothing that [Col. 0214B] is to be made can exist without a beginning; indeed, the very moment in which it begins to be made is its beginning.

[Note: [Col. 0214D] “Thus *principium*, or beginning,” etc. It is likely that, in order to indicate this meaning of *principium*, the author therefore elsewhere customarily translates the cited Scripture as “At the commencement God made heaven and earth,” as above in *On Baptism*, chapters 3 and 5. PAM.] Thus *principium*, or beginning, is a word for inception, not the name of some substance.

Now, if God’s principal works are heaven and earth, which he made before all things, the things made first are properly the beginning of his works. [Note: [Col. 0214C] Latinus reads “of the universe.” In his printed *Corrections*, C. Macrus has *universorum*, “of all things.” Junius.] Scripture therefore rightly prefaces its account in this way: [Note: [Col. 0214D] Others read: “Scripture therefore rightly prefaces its account.” Junius: “The things made first are properly the beginning, since they were made first; and Scripture therefore rightly prefaces its account: ‘In the beginning.’”] “In the beginning God made heaven and earth” (Gen. I.1), just as it would have said, “At the end God made heaven and earth,” if he had made them after everything else.

Or if “beginning” is some substance, then “end” too will be some kind of matter. It is certainly possible for something substantial to be the beginning of another thing that is to arise from it, as clay is the beginning of an earthen vessel and seed the beginning of a plant. But when we thus use the word “beginning” as a term of origin rather than of order, [Col. 0214C] we specifically add mention of the very thing which we wish to be the beginning of another thing.

Otherwise, if we say, for example, “In the beginning the potter made a basin or jar,” “beginning” will no longer signify the material; [Note: [Col. 0214D] All the editors and Junius read “will signify.”] for I have not called clay the beginning, but have indicated the order of the work, because before all else the potter first made the basin and jar and was thereafter going to make the rest. The word “beginning” will pertain to the ordering of the works, not to the origin of the substances.

[Col. 0215A] I can also interpret “beginning” in another way, though still not irrelevantly. [Note: [Col. 0215C] Semler and Oberthür read “They can also.”] For in Greek the word for “beginning,” which is ἀρχή (*archē*), signifies not only order but also sovereign authority. [Note: [Col. 0215D] “For in Greek the word for beginning, which is ἀρχή.” Xenophon, book I *On Government*, Aristotle, *Politics* I, and Plato, *Republic*, also agree with Tertullian concerning the meaning of ἀρχή as “sovereignty.” PAM.]

Hence they call rulers and magistrates ἄρχοντας (*archontas*). [Note: [Col. 0215D] “Hence they call rulers and magistrates ἄρχοντας.” It is noteworthy that, wherever the Greek has ἄρχοντες, the author customarily retains that word in his translation of Scripture, as above in *Against the Jews*, chapter 9, and below in book III *Against Marcion*. For, citing Isaiah chapter I, he says: “He also calls your rulers ἄρχοντας, the *archontes* of Sodom.” Likewise, in *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chapter 20, he translates another passage from Isaiah III thus: “He will come into judgment with the elders and the *archontes* of the people.” PAM.] [Note: [Col. 0215D] “They call rulers and magistrates *archontas*.” A man learned in antiquity could not omit this word when speaking of the government of the Athenians. Among them, when Codrus died, the succession of kings came to an end, and the commonwealth was entrusted first to perpetual praetors or magistrates [Col. 0216D] called *archons*, thirteen in number. Afterwards seven were appointed for ten-year terms, and finally annual archons were established. The first archon was Medon, as Pausanias relates in his *Achaica*. Since he was disabled in one foot, his brother Neleus maintained that he could not be appointed ruler at Athens. When the matter was referred to Apollo, he pronounced in favor of Medon. LE PR.]

Accordingly, even under this meaning, “beginning” will be understood as sovereignty and power. For in sovereignty and in power God made heaven and earth.
