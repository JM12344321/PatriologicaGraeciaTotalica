[Col. 1353C] **PROPUDIOSUS**, unchaste, shameless. II, 1042 B, “Lest they too mutter something about your Caesars, who were equally shameless.”

**PROSAPIA** means lineage or family. Ancient gloss: *prosapies*, γενους καταγωγή, “descent of a family.” *Prosapia*, ἐκ προγόνων εὐγένεια καὶ συγγένεια, “nobility and kinship derived from one’s ancestors.”

**PROSCRIBERE.** II, 668 A, “Yet each writer has prefixed a notice of the form of his work and the main heading of his subject concerning the soul.” II, 101 A, “Proclaimed King of the Jews upon the cross,” i.e. declared so by the placard affixed by the provincial governor. II, 1008 A, “But it is uncertain to whom, because neither the person nor the case is specified.”

**PROSECARE**, to offer to God in sacrifice. I, 503 A, “Among the Gauls, those of more advanced age are sacrificed to Mercury.” I, 316 A, “Yet at the very end he ordered a cock to be sacrificed to Aesculapius.”

**PROSPECTE**, with the greatest foresight. I, 304 A, “Even concerning your gods themselves, what your fathers had decreed with foresight.”

**PROSPECTUS.** II, 98 A, “According to the Apostle’s foresight,” [Col. 1353D] i.e. as the Apostle providently determined.

**PROSPER.** II, 1030 C, “So prosperous in respect of their times,” a Tertullianic phrase, as is the expression below, “distinguished in their lusts.”

**PROSPEX.** I, 616 A, “An augur in omens, foresighted in events,” i.e. provident, prudent.

**PROSPICERE.** I, 425 A, “If she foresaw that she should pass over to her avengers.” The sense is: Cybele rightly passed over to the Romans together with her worship because she knew that perhaps the avengers of Ilium would arise from them. But how could she foresee so distant a turn of fortune when she could not discern the few days remaining in Aurelius’s destiny? II, 150 A, “Through which it foresees her rather than through the passions,” *tois pathēmasi prooratai* (τοῖς παθήμασι προορᾶται), in a Greek construction.

**PROSPICIAE.** II, 580 A, “Their *prospiciae*.” Ciaconius reads *prosiciae*. Festus: “*Prosicium*: that which, after being cut off, is cast away.” The Glossary: *Prosiciae*, αἱ τῶν θυμάτων ἀπάρχαι, “the first offerings from sacrificial victims”; and elsewhere, *prosiciae*, ἀκροθίνια, “first fruits” or “choice spoils.”

**PROSTARE.** II, 709 B, “Helen, made cheaper by having stood [Col. 1354A] for sale under a placard in the final extremity of degradation,” i.e. she had been a prostitute in an archway or public brothel, whose public placard advertised her infamy by notice.

**PROSTITUERE.** I, 502 A, “To expose useless lamps at midday.” Haverkamp, with the other interpreters, says that the expression is emphatic: the scant light of lamps and torches is “prostituted” when set against the most brilliant rays of the noonday sun. I should prefer, however, to interpret it as “to display openly.” II, 891 C, “Either to be covered or exposed,” i.e. to be set openly on view with the covering removed and to proceed without a veil before everyone’s eyes. II, 912 A, “To expose the whole face.”

**PROSTITUTIO**, public display or establishment. I, 433 A, “Under the public display of images.”

**PROTELARE.** II, 409 C, “But he prolonged the lives of six hundred thousand persons,” i.e. he continued, extended, and protracted the tenure of life and strength for six hundred thousand human beings. I, 1233 B, “And will prolong it to the harbor of divine mercy,” i.e. will conduct it as far as a harbor, however distant. [Col. 1354B] I, 630 A, “So as to prolong even ignorance into an opportunity,” i.e. such is the power of pleasures that it does not wish its man to be taught that spectacles must be avoided by a Christian for as long as an opportunity to view them may still be hoped for.

**PROVOCARE.** II, 88 A, “Summoning it”—namely, the common possession of instruments—“to the distinction between rational and irrational things,” i.e. requiring that it be suitably and prudently divided into two classes: the rational, whose construction and use are joined with reason, and the irrational, whose whole refinement lies outside and contrary to reason itself.

**PROXIME**, very recently, at the nearest preceding time. I, 372 A.

**PSYCHICUS.** II, 156 A, “And the bond remains among the Psychics.” The Psychics are contrasted with the Pneumatics, i.e. the “spiritual,” just as he contrasts “psychic faith” with spiritual discipline in *On Fasting*, chapter 1. The Montanists, or Cataphrygians, were called “spiritual” by their own party because they arrogated to themselves spiritual revelations and “inspired” (*enthousiōdeis*, ἐνθουσιώδεις) prophecies. Those, however, who denied that these extraordinary prophecies and revelations were ordinary occurrences [Col. 1354C] in the Church were called “Psychics,” from 1 Corinthians 2. Tertullian, however, in some measure followed a certain middle path. For, like a Montanist, he acknowledged the Paraclete, i.e. the regular spirit of extraordinary prophecies and revelations; he rigorously urged fasts, compelled martyrdoms by forbidding flight during persecutions, and condemned second marriages. But he abstained from the Montanists’ rites and from those impious and more atrocious proclamations concerning Montanus and Prisca. Hence Augustine, in *On Heresies, to Quodvultdeus*, distinguished the Tertullians or Tertullianists from the Montanists, who were also called *Kataphryges* (κατάφρυγες). II, 953 B; 954 B, “The outer and inner sausages of the Psychics.” I, 532 A, “Concerning which there is a dispute between us and the Psychics.”

**PTISSANA**, πτισάνη, peeled barley. The Greeks drenched barley with water and, while it was swelling, exposed it to the sun; then they pounded it in a mortar with a wooden pestle until it shed its husk and chaff. I, 532 A, [Col. 1354D] “After the pattern of barley-groats,” i.e. like *ptissana*.

**PUBLICARE**, to bring into public view. II, 112 B, “For he who is not made public among men is made public in the Lord.”

**PUBLICATIO.** II, 892 B, “Every public exposure of a good virgin is the suffering of violation,” i.e. the uncovering of her head.

**PULSARE.** I, 391 A, “The same Spirit strikes,” i.e. filled the minds of the prophets with truth and impelled them to foretell the future. II, 796 A, “Having knocked at salvation, though without entering truth,” i.e. the Platonists, champions of the immortality of souls, knocked upon salvation’s door, but, since they did not enter truth, remained ignorant of the character and manner of immortality. I, 609 B, “Then the philosophers are obtuse, when they knock at the doors of truth.”

**PULVIS.** I, 1306 A, “And that very black powder.” “Black powder” is what the Greeks and Latins called *calliblepharum*: it was made from burned leaves of roses and palms, from *ampelitis*, and from other substances, and was used to extend the beginnings of the eyes. [Col. 1355A] Junius reads *exodia*, i.e. the ends and outlines are extended with pigment to beautify the face.

**PUMEX.** II, 1042 A, “With the pumice of a mirror,” i.e. with a mirror polished with pumice, exceedingly smooth and highly polished.

**PUNIRE.** I, 1270 B, “Against every apparatus for overthrowing or punishing the faith.” “For punishing the faith,” i.e. for exercising and testing it by punishments and torments.

**PURGARE.** II, 427 C, “Elsewhere I prefer to clear away,” i.e. to refute and remove: a metaphor from agriculture. I, 306 A, “That I may clear for myself a road to plainer matters.”

**PURGATRIX.** I, 1205 A, “She purified herself with cleansing water.”

**PURUS.** I, 700 B, “We sacrifice as God commanded, with pure prayer, without incense and without a victim.” So too I, 1244 A, “To know only pure food and drink,” i.e. to use bread and water alone, without any other foods or seasonings. So Juvenal: “Where kings observe the festal Sabbaths with a bare foot,” i.e. with an uncovered foot. Thus “with pure prayer” means by prayer alone and unadorned, proceeding [Col. 1355B] from a holy and innocent mind. The Gentiles offered lifeless things or brute beasts, incense or oxen, to false gods; Christians offer the true God a rational soul that speaks praises and thanks to God. Athenagoras expressly explains this in his *Embassy for the Christians* to Antoninus and Commodus, also citing Paul’s words, in which he describes the victim as θυσίαν ζῶσαν, ἁγίαν, εὐάρεστον τῷ θεῷ, τὴν λογικὴν λατρείαν ἡμῶν, “a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to God—our rational worship.” Tertullian himself says in the *Apologeticum*: “I offer to God the best and greater victim, which he himself commanded: prayer proceeding from chaste flesh, an innocent soul, and the Holy Spirit.” And in book IV, chapter 1, *Against Marcion*, on the words of Malachi, “A pure offering is offered to my name”: “namely, simple prayer from a pure conscience.” And in chapter 9 he says that a person ought to offer a gift to God at the temple—namely, prayer and thanksgiving in the Church through Christ Jesus, the universal priest of the Father. Minucius Felix, after saying, “We have no temples or altars,” adds: “A good disposition, a pure mind, and a sincere judgment are an acceptable victim. These are our sacrifices; these are God’s sacred rites.” [Col. 1355C] Finally, Justin, *Against Trypho the Jew*, testifies that our Eucharist is performed with pure prayer, or prayer alone. (These passages are found in Rigaltius.) I, 1269 A, “Content with simple nourishment and a pure drink of water.”

**PUSILLITAS**, smallness. II, 210 A, “Whatever power belongs to it rather than smallness,” i.e. “rather,” or “more,” than smallness. A very frequent Greek ellipsis.

**PUTATIVUS**, that which does not truly exist. II, 335 A, “The supposed corporeality in Christ.” II, 335 C, “To have assigned him a supposed nativity,” as though he had not truly and actually been born. II, 754 C, “Who introduced a merely apparent flesh of Christ.”

**PYCTES**, πύκτης, boxer. II, 134 A, “The boxer himself does not complain.” II, 978 B, “Boxers grow strong through dry diets.”

**PYRIPHLEGETHON**, the fiery river of the underworld, where the punishment is οἰμωγὴ τῶν ἐπὶ τοῦ πυρὸς ὀπτωμένων, i.e. “the wailing of those roasting in the fire,” as Lucian says in the *Necyomantia*. I, 520 A.

## Q

[Col. 1355D]

**QUA**, because, insofar as. I, 441 A, “They know, inasmuch as they are human, who also gave them a soul,” i.e. because, or insofar as, they are human beings, they also know from whom they received their soul. Or thus: they know who gave them the soul by which, of course, they are human and differ from brute beasts. I, 483 A, “If in any way the fruits of trees attempt anything there,” i.e. “if in any manner,” just as *aliqua* in Virgil means “in some manner.”

**QUAESTIO.** II, 581 A, “In questions concerning prepositions,” i.e. in the subtle examination of prepositions; hence in what follows he expounds and demonstrates *ex*, *per*, and similar terms. But the author treated the matter more fully in *On Prescription*, chapter 49. II, 172 C, “If I do not resolve this point through questions drawn from the Old Scripture.” II, 187 C, “After Philip and the whole substance of that question.” II, 307 A, “To love questions,” i.e. to apply oneself with complete zeal to those holy inquiries of which Isaiah later says, Καὶ δεῦτε, διελεχθῶμεν, “Come now, and let us reason together.”

[Col. 1356A] **QUANTI**, for *quot* (“how many”), just as *tanti* stands for *tot* (“so many”). I, 266 A; 281 A, “For how many are predisposed toward evil.” II, 179 C, “How many things I shall prescribe for you,” i.e. how very many. II, 253 C, “How many worthless slaves insult royal names.”

**QUANTO.** II, 219 B, “How much more he does not supply one to whom they may be ascribed,” i.e. *quanto magis non comparat*. II, 626 A, “And how much more incredible,” with “more” understood. II, 503 A, “The nearer this little work is to being dispatched briefly,” i.e. the nearer this book is to being completed and brought to an end.

**QUANTUM**, as concerns, if one considers. I, 281 A, “So far as interpretation is concerned.” II, 232 B, “Insofar as he is properly a painter.”

**QUATENUS**, equivalent to “because.” II, 226 B, “Because this pertains only to bodies,” etc. II, 176 B, “Because the Scriptures so teach,” i.e. since, seeing that.

**QUI.** II, 236 B, “As some beauty does,” i.e. some particular beauty.

**QUICQUID**, for anything whatever, anything at all. II, 43 B, “Whatever he corrects under this name.”

[Col. 1356B] **QUINIO.** II, 656 A, “Even in the civil law there is found a certain Greek woman who gave birth to a set of five sons.” “In the civil law,” i.e. in the books of the jurists, which the ancient grammarians also commonly cite in this manner. Pliny, *Natural History*, book VII, chapter 3: “There is also found in the Peloponnese a woman who four times gave birth to five children, and the greater part of each entire delivery survived.” Aristotle: Ἀνὰ πέντε γὰρ ἔτεκε, καὶ τὰ πολλὰ αὐτῶν ἐξετράφη, “For she bore five at a time, and most of them were reared.” He does not, however, mention the Peloponnese. Strabo too, drawing on the same Aristotle, tells the story differently in book XV, and Gellius differently in book X. Finally, Aristotle himself recalls that he spoke about the same matter elsewhere, in a passage no longer extant. Gaius, book I, *On Fideicommissaries*, as cited in Law 3, *On Doubtful Matters*, and Julianus, I, 36, *On Payments*, relate precisely the same story about an Alexandrian woman that our Septimius here relates about a Greek woman.

**QUINQUATRIA.** I, 674 A, “What schoolmaster is without a tablet of the seven idols? Yet he will attend the Quinquatria.” The *Quinquatria* are the festival days sacred to Minerva, [Col. 1356C] on the first of which sacrifices were made to Minerva, on the following three public shows were held, and on the fifth, finally, a purification. Ovid, *Fasti* III:

> One day lies between, and Minerva’s rites are held,  
> Which take their name from five days joined together.

The first of these days fell in March, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of April; the last was commonly called the *Tubilustria*. All were celebrated with gifts called *Minervalia*, especially those that beginners and novices in every discipline presented to their teachers. Ovid, in the same place:

> You too, who by Phoebus’s art drive illnesses away,  
> Bring a few gifts from your stores to the goddess.  
> Nor you, the crowd deprived of a fierce teacher’s fee,  
> Despise her: she attracts new pupils.

These, then, were the Greater Quinquatria. On the Lesser, see Varro, book V, *On the Latin Language*, and Festus. The meaning is that there is no schoolmaster who does not have in his school or classroom a tablet of the seven idols, that is, [Col. 1356D] of the seven planets—the Moon, Mars, Mercury, and the others—from which the days of every week take their names. Or, if anyone should be found without such a tablet, he would nevertheless attend the Quinquatria. It is not worth the effort to investigate more carefully what use schoolmasters made of this tablet or register of the seven days. Perhaps it stated or indicated what had to be assigned to or recited by pupils on each day. Juvenal, *Satire* VII:

> Nothing succeeds for the Arcadian youth, whose wretched head  
> Dread Hannibal fills for me every sixth day.

On that day, evidently, the total for the entire week was collected. On the seventh day there was rest and leisure. Tertullian himself, *To the Nations* I, chapter 13: “It is certainly you who have admitted the Sun into the register of seven days, and from those days have chosen that one in preference—the day on which you either omit the bath or defer it until evening, or take care to have leisure and a meal.”

[Col. 1357A] **QUISQUE**, for *quisquis*, “whoever.” II, 611 B, “Or whoever succeeded him in inheriting the kingdom.”

**QUO.** II, 216 A, “In that there too he through whom all things were made is revealed,” i.e. *quod*, “that.” II, 522 B, “Wherefore neither does the Apostle here,” i.e. “for which reason.” II, 518 B, “To what end, then, do you give me two gods?” i.e. to what purpose, for what reason?

**QUOMODO**, in whatever manner, or by whatever means. II, 471 B, “But in whatever manner the Marcionites wish to be believed.”

## R

**RANUNCULUS.** I, 659 A, “As though drippings of honey came from a poisonous little frog,” i.e. as if someone were to press honeycomb together with the lungs of a poisonous toad; for honey mixed with poison would then drip forth—honey indeed, but poisoned. Juvenal, *Satire* I:

> …who, about to offer her thirsty husband  
> Mild Calenian wine, mixes a toad into it.

**RAPERE.** I, 266 A, “Whom it seizes,” with “evil” understood, i.e. those who pursue evil and perverse things. I, 1203 A, “Since every underlying material must necessarily seize the quality of that which hangs over it from above.”

[Col. 1357B] **RATIO.** I, 278 A, “A certain principle of rival activity,” i.e. a certain being actively engaged in rivalry. This is understood of the devil, who envies Christ’s Church. I, 526 A, “What principle governs the universe,” etc.; by “principle” is to be understood the wisdom of the supreme God. II, 122 A, “After the manner of torture,” i.e. in the form of torture. II, 143 C, “A certain rational principle has commanded us to treat these things too as sport,” i.e. reason itself compels and commands us to mock those dreams and trifles.

**RATO.** II, 710 A, “For no one holds this life duly accounted for except when all the things that charge it have been settled,” i.e. no life is held valid and entered into the accounts unless it has discharged and completed everything pertaining to the conduct of life. These are forensic expressions.

**REBELLARE.** II, 480 D, “What peace comes from one against whom there has been no rebellion?” i.e. against whom no rebellion has been committed.

**RECAPITULARE**, that is, to bring back to the beginning, or to review from the beginning. II, 512 C.

[Col. 1357C] **RECEPTUM.** II, 141 B, “And throughout the several stories of the heavens they begin to be examined concerning their reception.” Latinus proposes *de receptu*; Rigaltius, however, reads *de praecepto dispici*, “to be examined concerning the command,” i.e. inquiry and examination are made into whether we obeyed Christ’s command and whether we were steadfast in confessing Christ’s name before the authorities.

**RECIDIVATUS.** II, 796 A, “They certainly declared that the soul’s return is corporeal,” i.e. its restoration after a fall. II, 818 A, “Which promise the restoration of the flesh.” II, 837 C, “The restoration of the Jewish condition.” II, 697 A, “The Samian sage is Plato’s authority for the souls’ capacity to fly back through restoration.”

**RECIDIVUS**, revived, restored to life. II, 261 A, “They argue for the reliability of the returning year.” II, 424 B, “Awakened to the hope of eternal life by examples of restoration,” i.e. of things revived and renewed. Servius explains *recidiva* as things restored after a fall. Thus Virgil, “restored Pergama,” *Aeneid* IV, 7 and 10. Pliny, with the physicians, called recurring fevers *recidivae*, παλινδρόμους, ἀναπτομένας, “returning, flaring up again.” II, 810 B, “The entire creation is restored,” i.e. all created things are restored and renewed from the beginning. [Col. 1357D] II, 690 A, “Recollection itself after forgetfulness is memory restored.” II, 1022 A, “Thus adultery renewed from its former state.”

**RECIPROCARE.** II, 335 B, “To return all things in Christ to their head,” i.e. to their beginning. II, 78 C, “How long shall we draw the saw back and forth along this line?” See **LINEA**.

**RECIPROCICORNIS**, having horns curved backward and twisted, ἑλιξοκέρως, “spiral-horned.” II, 1031 B.

**RECOGITATUS.** II, 466 A, “In the recollection of those women.”

**RECOGNOSCERE**, to read carefully. I, 391 A, “While we suffer them, let them be read; while we read them carefully, they are verified,” i.e. all those things take place meanwhile as we read these words cursorily; but when we read carefully, we see that those earlier predictions are already verified by the outcome.

**RECORPORARE**, to transform into another body or another form. II, 804 A, “To re-embody it in stronger material.” II, 837 B, “And the flesh’s capacity to be re-embodied.” [Col. 1358A] II, 706 C, “For those who must be punished with labors and servile tasks will of course be re-embodied as asses and mules.”

**RECORPORATIO**, re-embodiment. II, 837 C.

**RECTOR.** II, 218 A, “I find the Word to be the arbiter of the ruler.” The Dijon manuscript reads: “I find the Word to be the arbiter of the creator.”

**RECUPERATIO.** II, 1022 C, “After recovery and reformation.” Here he uses *recuperatio* for treatment and healing, just as above he called impurity “irrecoverable.”

**RECUSARE**, to abandon. II, 1037 A, “When both scales and years are abandoned”; others read *recursantur*, “recur.” II, 336 A, “Clearly you have rejected the falsehood of the nativity,” i.e. you have refuted it by adducing grounds: a forensic term.

**REDANIMARE.** II, 811 B, “They should not be thought to be reanimated, but reformed.”

**REDANIMATIO**, reanimation. II, 837 C.

**REDINVENIRE.** II, 729 A, “Sophocles the tragedian rediscovered it through a dream.”

**REDIRE**, to be dissolved. II, 247 A, “Liquids return through fire,” [Col. 1358B] i.e. not even the liquids there are liquid unless, when fires are applied, they are dissolved into liquid; they are frozen, bound fast by frost.

**REFECTIO.** II, 798 B, “You abolish the restoration of the flesh precisely when we are asserting it,” i.e. its resurrection.

**REFICERE**, to be restored or replaced. II, 1047 A, “It easily directs itself, easily restores itself.” II, 809 C, “And surely he who made it is capable of restoring it.”

**REFRIGERARE.** I, 704 A, “We bring relief to the needy,” τοῖς πένησιν ἀναψύχομεν. A Hellenism. Varinus says: ἀναψύχω means τὸ τὸν λειποθυμοῦντα ἐπαναφέρω, ἢ τὸ ἀνάπτω καὶ ἀναλαμβάνω, “to revive one who is fainting, or to kindle and restore”; and *refrigerium* has the same sense in very many passages.

**REFRIGERIUM.** II, 708 A, “Through an eternal sentence both of punishment and of refreshment.”

**REFRIGESCENTIA.** II, 722 A, “But neither shall I admit any cooling.”

**REFORMATUS.** II, 563 A, “Re-formed as the guardian of the Aeons,” i.e. reformation.

**REFUGA.** II, 1309 B, “A fugitive from the mines is transformed”: one who removes himself [Col. 1358C] from the common lot of those condemned to the mines; or, as Rigaltius holds, one who, after being condemned to the mines, fled from them.

**REGIO.** II, 235 A, “From either region,” i.e. from the place or seat of each, namely good and evil.

**REGULA.** II, 26 B, “The rule of faith.” Here and elsewhere he calls *regula* what we commonly call the Apostles’ Creed: κανὼν τῆς ἀληθείας, σωμάτιον τῆς ἀληθείας, “rule of truth, body of truth,” in Irenaeus. II, 197 B, “The rule of truth.” II, 157 A, “That this rule had run its course from the beginning of the Gospel.” II, 254 B, “Because the rule of the supreme and great God, once established, forbids it,” i.e. once that rule is laid down, or that foundation cast, there cannot be two gods. II, 271 A, “I shall bring them under rules,” i.e. I shall bind in proper order and place the points relevant to explaining this question. II, 272 B, “I hold out another rule against him,” i.e. I employ another argument against him and unfold the rule of truth to him.

**RELIGIO.** I, 346 A, “Religion goes around the taverns begging.” Pamelius understands this of the priests of Cybele, who demanded alms door to door; Haverkamp, however, understands it of a public invitation to a lease and commends the words in *To the Nations* I, chapter 10: [Col. 1358D] “Trade is advertised by religion; sanctity begs for a lease.”

**RELIQUATIO.** II, 746 B, “There will be no remainder of times not yet established.”

**RELIQUATRIX.** II, 710 A, “Leaving a balance of sins,” i.e. owing an outstanding balance in the accounts. For *reliquatores* are persons who do not pay in full and completely what they owe someone, but remain debtors for some part of it.

**REMEABILIS.** II, 705 C; 706 A, “Indeed, just above they also affirm that it is capable of returning into bodies.”

**REMEDIARE**, to assist or remedy. II, 52 A, “We should remedy it by subtraction, addition, or transformation.” II, 447 A, “That they were therefore remedied,” restored to health. I, 703 B, “They have been healed either from demons or from illnesses.”

**REMISSA**, remission. II, 403 B, “We have spoken about the remission of sins.”

[Col. 1359A] **REMUNERARI**, used passively. I, 502 A, “They are rewarded with statues and salaries.”

**RENIDENTIA.** II, 733 B, “And their smiles during sleep,” i.e. laughter. For in the ancient Glossary *renidere* also means τὸ μειδιᾶν, “to smile.”

**RENUNTIARE**, to withdraw from a place that one had occupied without right. I, 404 A, “It follows that the false thing should withdraw.” II, 395 B, “And indeed this must immediately be declared,” i.e. stated. II, 103 A, “Being now about to declare it more fully in writing,” i.e. to define and declare it in my written response. II, 81 B, “These examples, therefore, will have made a counter-declaration,” i.e. an announcement will have been made in opposition and from the contrary side. II, 658 A, “For they say that for this reason too the soul must be declared incorporeal.”

**RENUNTIATIO.** II, 754 B, “Its declaration will furnish the rule for our resurrection,” i.e. a demonstration or declaration made by a pronouncement. I, 638 B, “Our single renunciation directed against idolatry.”

**REPASTINARE**, properly, to dig up again with a *pastinum*, the vine-dressers’ two-pronged implement. II, 303 C, “So that the commission of an injury might be rooted out by fear of an immediately impending retaliation.” [Col. 1359B] I, 1326 B, “Not digging up anew the use of your riches or refinements.” I, 1246 B, “And some two-pronged implement of iron or bronze for digging around the nails.” II, 735 A, “Or, after the manner of a vine, renew life by layering”—i.e. just as a farmer does with a vine, renewing it by a shoot—“with its age made new.”

**REPASTINATIO**, uprooting or cutting back, which is opposed to propagation. II, 921 B, “Not without a principle of propagation at the beginning and of uprooting at the end.”

**REPERCUTERE**, to turn an opponent’s own argument against him, like a missile which, rebounding from a shield, wounds the one who threw it and recoils upon him. I, 488 A, “This, you say, can also be turned back against your God.”

**REPERTRIX.** II, 394 B, “Nor does it impart joy to the Church that found it.”

**REPUERASCERE.** II, 544 A, B, “The Apostle too commands us to become children again.”

**REPUTESCERE.** II, 702 B, “Lest some condition of burial putrefy again.” See **CONDITIO**.

[Col. 1359C] **RES.** II, 1032 A, “Let the matter now come from elsewhere; let arguments now be sought elsewhere”: let us pass to other arguments, drawn from elsewhere.

**RESCULPERE.** II, 959 C, “For the first people had carved anew the first man’s crime.” Augustine, on Psalm 6: “For when we are converted, that is, when by changing our former life we carve the spirit anew.” Rufinus, on the same Psalm: “When we are converted, that is, when by changing our former life we are carved anew.”

**RESERARE**, to break and open. I, 308 A, “Who opened the bloodstained mouth for the judge?” i.e. that beastly gape of a man resembling a wild animal, closed by the row of teeth as though by a bar, was broken open by force, so that pieces of human flesh not yet sent down into the stomach might be discovered in the mouth.

**RESIGNARE**, to open, break open. I, 303 A, “Because they broke open the compartments of the wine cellar.” For they secured the cellars in which they stored wine and food not only with a bar but also with a seal. II, 782 A, “From the earth, that is, from flesh not yet opened to generation.” II, 378 A, “Jonathan, [Col. 1359D] Saul’s son, removed through supplication the guilt of breaking the fast.” He calls a fast that was to be infringed *resignatum*; for Jonathan had provided the occasion for violating the fast when he put honey to his mouth. II, 849 B, “For opening the Old Testament and confirming the New,” i.e. for explaining the Old and confirming the New. For wills were opened by unsealing and made binding by sealing.

**RESONARE.** II, 906 C, “Which sound publicly,” when a signal is given by trumpet or bell, concerning the three more notable public hours.

**RESPERGERE.** II, 1001 C, “The stains of flesh defiled again after baptism,” i.e. flesh sprinkled and stained again, namely with new filth after the washing.

**RESPONDERE.** II, 233 B, “That formless and uncultivated matter is reflected.” An image or object is said to *respondere* in a mirror in the sense that it is presented, represented, reflected, or rather displays itself opposite to be viewed. He therefore denies that matter is reflected in this ornamented [Col. 1360A] world, i.e. that it displays a corresponding answer and image of itself. II, 91 B, “And after Christ to answer to another master,” i.e. by entering into a new pledge to bind oneself to perform duties for a master other than Christ, who thinks and commands otherwise than he does.

**RESPONSIO.** II, 865 A, “For the soul is sanctified not by washing but by its answer.” The author calls *responsio* the pledge by which we in turn bind ourselves to the Lord who promises grace. The Apostle calls it ἐπερώτημα συνειδήσεως ἀγαθῆς, “the pledge of a good conscience,” from the reciprocal nature of the parties, in 1 Peter 3.

**RESUSCITARI**, to rise, said of stars. II, 1033 A, “The distinct confusion of the stars sometimes casts something down and sometimes raises it again.”

**RETRACTARE**, to refute. I, 280 A, “No one objects: is he not therefore good?” etc., i.e. no one refutes the words of those making the charge by saying, “See whether,” etc. II, 549 A, “They will be none other than those which are being refuted.” II, 136 C, “Nor does anyone object.” II, 403 B, “But we have not at once refuted the other matters by this proposition,” [Col. 1360B] i.e. we do not wish this proposition to be understood here as applying to the other arguments. *Retractare* often means to treat carefully and reconsider. II, 231 B, “We reconsidered something in what preceded.” I, 1255 B, “Who could ever discuss his goodness at length?” i.e. who could ever say enough about his goodness? But all the ancient copies read, *quis de bono ejus latere retractet*, i.e. who would consider concealing or privately suppressing his goodness? At I, 392 A, it means to censure: “Perhaps whether his standing is criticized on this ground.” For Tertullian says that the sect is carefully investigated for the sake of disparagement.

**RETRACTATUS.** II, 157 B, “A place must also be allowed for reconsiderations,” i.e. for more careful examination and refutation. II, 248 A, “That heretics must be convicted without an examination of their doctrines.” II, 474 A, “As was also proved in the examination of your Gospel.” II, 931 B, “In the general examination.”

**RETRO**, formerly. II, 246 B, “It was done by us formerly,” πεποίηται ἡμῖν, “it has been done by us.” A Hellenism. I, 432 A, “Those who formerly hated,” i.e. those who hated before now. So very frequently.

**RETROSIOR**, one who is farther back, i.e. older, more ancient. [Col. 1360C] I, 387 A, “Yet the very latest of them are found to be no more ancient than the foremost of your sages.”

**RHETORICARI.** II, 301 A, “Thus the heretics provoke us to engage in rhetoric as well as in philosophy.”

**RISILOQUIUM.** II, 1245 A, “When, among those about to mock him, it consists in derisive speech.”

**RIVALITAS.** II, 988 C, “How many rivalries shall I defend?” For very many lovers defend their beloved women from rivals by homicide, destroying those rivals by various kinds of death.

**ROBORATUS.** II, 691 A, “But how much stronger is the outcome of being refuted by women than of being approved?” Latinus, Rigaltius, and others read *ruboratior exitus*, “a more shameful outcome,” rather than *roboratior*. He calls an outcome *ruboratior* when it inflicts greater blushes and shame.

**ROMANITAS.** II, 1040 A, “If Romanness is the salvation of all,” etc., i.e. if embracing Roman customs is useful and glorious for all peoples, why do you now depart toward the Greeks through pursuits that are altogether dishonorable and useless?

[Col. 1360D] **ROMPHAEA**, ῥομφαία, sword. II, 745 A, “Unless it is because the sword that guards Paradise yields to no one.”

**RUCTARE.** I, 413 A, “Who are healed by belching.” Haverkamp reads “who are bent over.” This concerns seers who, after conceiving the divine power through sacrifices and the burning of whole offerings, are in labor pains and ὀδύναις, “agonies”; they therefore bend over while the spirit they have conceived strives to burst forth through their throats. II, 815 A, “Into whom some death has belched,” i.e. into whom someone, seized by the swift action of poison, breathed out his stricken soul with a belch while drinking.

**RUDIS.** II, 222 B, “That is crude which is imperfect.”

**RUMA**, also called *rumen*, is the breast in women; in certain animals, however, it is the projecting part of the throat through which swallowed food is brought back up, as Servius says—or, according to Nonius, the place in the belly into which food is received and from which it is returned. Festus: “The rumen is the part of the neck through which food is swallowed.” II, 800 A, “And from the crops of birds.”

[Col. 1361A] **RUMPERE.** I, 301 A, “Or of those who are still breaking whips,” on whose backs remain fresh traces of the whips broken during the beating; Persius wittily calls such persons “yesterday’s Quirites” in *Satire* III, 106.

**RUNCARE**, to root out completely, remove. II, 1035 B, “He undertook to root out and clear away.”

**RUNCINA**, an instrument with which a work was polished. I, 340 A.

**RUPEX**, uncultivated, rustic. II, 1041 A, “That man in the presence of a rustic.” I, 404 A, “Not those who were boors and still savage human beings.” II, 655 B, “But what will so many and such great souls of boors and barbarians do?”

**RUSSATUS.** II, 77 A, “And then reddened by hope of his own blood.” Military tunics were red, φοινικοὶ χιτῶνες, “scarlet tunics.” Here the soldier who became a Christian is said to be reddened, in place of his red tunic, by hope of his own blood, that is, by the hoped-for purple of martyrdom. Red military garments occur in Trebellius; and in Juvenal, “Lacerta in red.”

**RUSTARE.** *Ruscum* or *ruscus* denotes brambles and thorns; hence *ruscare* or *rustare*, and hence also *rustariae falces*, [Col. 1361B] sickles designed for uprooting thorns. II, 1035 B, “He undertook to root out and clear away.” Others read *ruspare*, i.e. “to search.”

## S

**SACERDOS.** II, 1045 A, “A priestly platform,” i.e. a platform belonging to a priest. Similarly I, 907 B, “a left-hand conscience,” i.e. a conscience aware of what is sinister. II, 926 C, “Through a priest ordained on the ground of monogamy, or even confirmed on the ground of virginity,” i.e. called to the priesthood from the number of the once-married, or from the number of virgins, ἀγάμων, “unmarried persons.”

**SACERDOTIUM.** II, 1031 A, priests.

**SACRAMENTUM**, religion. I, 363 B, “And from this learn the whole order of our religion,” i.e. advance to a knowledge of our religion. II, 623 B, “So too Jesus, because of the sacred significance of his name.” I, 520 A, “Only concerning our sacred rites.” II, 32 B, “One tradition of the same religion,” i.e. of the same Gospel. II, 38 A, “A silent mystery,” i.e. a more remote and secret sacred doctrine. II, 45 B, [Col. 1361C] “Because of a difference in doctrine.” II, 588 A, B, “I who have betrayed so great a mystery even by ridiculing it,” i.e. so great a secret, so arcane and hidden a doctrine. II, 594 A, “For the great mystery of the name.” Irenaeus: καὶ τοῦτ’ εἶναι τὸ μέγα καὶ ἀπόκρυφον μυστήριον, “and that this is the great and hidden mystery.” II, 157 B, the οἰκονομίας sacramentum, “mystery of the divine economy,” means the doctrine of the Trinity. II, 622 B, “Who was prepared among the representations of this mystery,” i.e. both so that he himself might be an image and so that he might be numbered among the types and images of Christ, of which there were very many under the Law in the Old Testament. II, 835 B, “For at that time this wood had a symbolic significance.” II, 138 B, “Lest antiquity perhaps had its own sacred ordinance,” i.e. lest someone suppose that martyrdoms were ordained for the Old Testament, not the New; for the Law, not the Gospel. Thus he speaks of the “sacrament of martyrdom.” II, 963 A, “But fasts will also obtain from God recognition of mysteries,” i.e. skill in interpreting sacred figures and images, namely those divinely presented in dreams. The *sacramenta*, therefore, are dreams sent by God. II, 964 C, “By what means recognition of mysteries might be obtained from God.” [Col. 1361D] II, 470 A, “The mysteries of these figures.” II, 660 A, “And it both sees and hears mysteries,” i.e. parables, enigmas, figures, and mysteries of the sort shown or suggested through dreams, by which God’s will is signified or disclosed. Irenaeus, book V, chapter 7, rendered ἄρρητα ῥήματα in Latin as *sermones inenarrabiles*, “words that cannot be uttered.” II, 973 A, “It is the law of the sacrament,” i.e. the law of Christian communion is that whatever belongs to any of our people belongs to us. II, 261 A, “Sacred signs of a dry and burning nature,” i.e. signs or mysteries. II, 280 A, “Not even the sacrament of his faith itself,” i.e. baptism. I, 1197 A, “Blessed is the sacrament of our water.” II, 317 C, Christ is called “the sacrament of human salvation.” II, 487 D, “Of what does he interpret it as a figure belonging to an alien sacrament?” i.e. a sacred sign given by an alien god. II, 943 C, “By presbyters and deacons of the same sacrament,” i.e. monogamists. II, 823 A, “So that it may be the form of the mystery,” concerning the resurrection. I, 1268 B, “Love is the supreme sacrament of faith.” I, 405 [Col. 1362A] A, “From its own consciousness the same soul makes a pronouncement from the word of the sacrament.” Haverkamp reads *animae eadem exsacramenti*: *exsacramentum* formed from *exsecrare*, so that the words mean: even the uneducated common people now frequently use, as a curse, the name of Satan, the author of this class—namely, of demons. A full stop must be placed after *generis*.

**SACRIFICIUM.** I, 1329 B, “Or a sacrifice is offered,” i.e. sacred prayers by which the pious present themselves to God, as Clement of Alexandria explains at length in *Stromata* VII, and Tertullian in *Apologeticum*, chapter 30, and *To Scapula*, chapter 2. I, 1303 A, “Sacrifices without scruple.” II, 927 A, “Your sacrifice will ascend with an unveiled brow.” See **PURUS**. II, 806 B, “For there are also sacrifices pleasing to God—I mean the struggles of the soul, fasts, late and dry foods, and the filth that accompanies this duty.” I, 1182 A, “Most people do not think that one should take part in the prayers of sacrifices.” He is speaking of fasts and the Eucharist, sacrifices which were celebrated especially with prayers.

**SAECULARIS.** II, 52 C, Secular writings are contrasted with the sacred Scriptures.
