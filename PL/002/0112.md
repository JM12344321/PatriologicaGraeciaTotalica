## CHAPTER IV

To these he proclaims that there are two fathers and divided kingdoms [Note: [Col. 1057D] “To these he proclaims that there are two fathers and divided kingdoms,” etc. We have entitled this chapter “On the Heresies of Marcion,” since he proclaims to them that there are two fathers—that is, Gods; that the Lord is the cause of evil; he denies that there is judgment for the guilty, against which there is below the book *On the Judgment of God*; he denies that the flesh can rise; and finally [Col. 1058C] he teaches that Christ’s body was fictitious, made under a semblance. All these matters are treated generally and more fully in the aforementioned *On the Prescription against Heretics*, chapter 51, heresy 17, and individually throughout the five books *Against Marcion*. Further, a little below we corrected *falsum sine nomine numen* (“a false divinity without a name”) in place of *nomen*.];

[Col. 1057B] That the Lord who founded the world is the cause of evil—

He who fashioned flesh and made it live by his breath,

Who gave the Law and spoke through the voice of the prophets.

He denies that this one is good, although he admits that he is just:

Cruel, harsh, one whose savage delight is war,

Dreadful in judgment, softened by no prayers.

He urges that there is another, never known to anyone,

Who exists nowhere, a false divinity without a name,

Who established nothing and uttered no commandments.

This one, he says, is good, for he judges no one,

But distributes life to all alike and begrudges it to none.

He denies that there is judgment for the guilty, and carries about

For the wretched a bloody poison, sweet and mixed with honey.

He denies that the flesh can rise, though he himself

Caused its ruin and wickedly stripped it after despising it.

[Col. 1057C] Therefore he is accursed and suffers grief without end,

Always hostile to it, because with manifold wounds he strives

To drive out its life and the salvation from which he himself fell.

And in this matter also he would deceive mankind: that Christ

Came suddenly to earth, but was joined by no union of flesh,

Being instead spirit in form and a body fashioned beneath a semblance,

So that what does not exist might appear to exist.

## CHAPTER V

[Col. 1058A]

Does he therefore teach that it befits God to sport in darkness with those he has persuaded [Note: [Col. 1058D] “Does it therefore befit God to sport in darkness with those he has persuaded,” etc. We give this chapter the title “Refutation of the Heresy Concerning Christ’s Fictitious Body.” The author treated this argument more fully above in *On the Flesh of Christ*. Here we corrected *quae sancto in corpore versans* (“all the things which, while living in a holy body”) in place of *versis*. We also read “sent by the true God, the Father,” etc., in place of the transmitted “but by God,” especially since both Tertullian and other Christian poets customarily shorten final *o*, even in adjectives.],

To lie—a wickedness—or falsely to call himself a man?

He is carried, walks, is clothed, duly rests,

Is handled, suffers, is suspended, and is buried.

While dwelling in a holy body, he accomplished in the proper sense

Everything belonging to humanity—sent by the Father, the true God

Who created all things—seeking his own, not another’s.

He was known by his very work to peoples who had long hoped for him,

And was thoroughly known to the world through the prophets’ voice.

Yet now, at the manifest threshold of death,

They seek a wandering, unknown Lord and abandon the known one.

Their faith is false, their God false, their reward void [Note: [Col. 1058D] “Their reward void,” etc. This pertains to the reward of works, discussed more than once above; in the lines that follow he alludes to chapter 15 of the First Epistle to the Corinthians.],

Their resurrection false, their conquest of death false,

Their martyrdoms empty, and even the name of Christ vain.

[Col. 1058B] By teaching that he came like a magical mist,

In their madness they confess that the doer of the deed was a liar:

That he suffered nothing, and that the people were fashioned without guilt.

Is God truthful in this way? Are these the honors rendered to the Lord?

Alas, wretched men, destroyed to no purpose by a thankless death,

Who, with a blind guide, fell headlong into a hidden pit [Note: [Col. 1058D] Fabricius correctly interprets *in fossa ruistis* as “you fell into a dug-out place,” amid hidden thorns.].

And just as one rich through the gift of treasure in a dream

Exults and holds it in his hands, deluded by an empty hope,

So, deceived, you hope for the empty shadow of a gift.

## CHAPTER VI

[Note: [Col. 1058D] “Ah! the wasting maw,” etc. We have entitled this chapter “Against the Heresy Concerning God as the Author of Evils,” against which see above, book II *Against Marcion*. We necessarily corrected *tabidi* (“wasting”) in place of *tacidi*, especially because the former is an epithet of the dragon.]

Ah! the wasting maw and death-bearing prey of the dragon!

In place of peace you savagely hope for flattering murders.

You dare to blame the God who created such great things;

Upon whose earth, with excessive loving-kindness poured forth,

You rush about unmindful, abundantly praising his gifts,

[Col. 1058C] Rejecting the maker himself while approving what he made—

The craftsman of the world, whose work you yourselves are [Note: [Col. 1057C] *Cujusne*. Obert.]?

He who granted you, though small, great honors,

Sowed the crops, and subjected all animals to you;

Who makes the seasons of the year fruitful in their appointed months;

Who bestowed sweetnesses, varied drinks, rich foods and fragrances,

Pleasant flowers and the welcome shade of groves;

[Col. 1059A] Who granted wondrous juices to flourishing herbs,

Pours out springs and rivers in sweet waters,

Adorns heaven with stars, and illuminates the whole world—

The sole immeasurable Lord, both just and good,

Known from his work, though seen and known by no one.

Although foolish nations, covered by error [Note: [Col. 1059C] *Copertae* in place of *coopertae* (“covered”), as also below in book II, chapter 1: “With the spear-point covered”; and book V, chapter 9: “Covered by wave and strait.” He also uses this figure in the perfect tense in book III, chapter 4:

> He covered the desire for sin by a violent death.],

And flourishing in wealth, praise him under another’s name,

They nevertheless recognize the Maker and fear to blame him.

Nor does anyone but you alone—the new gateway of Gehenna—

Ungratefully choose to curse your own Lord.

Through Marcion, with Cerdo as his teacher [Note: [Col. 1059C] “With Cerdo as his teacher,” etc. On the heresies of Cerdo, Marcion’s teacher, see the aforementioned *On the Prescription against Heretics*, chapter 51, heresy 16.],

A terrible apostate assigned you these savage gifts of death;

Nor did it occur to you that, after seducing you away from Christ’s name,

He bore you down to the depths in Marcion’s name.

Tell me: among the many things, what single thing displeases you?

[Col. 1059B] Or what has God made that should not be extolled with praise?

Is it perhaps that, through his overly abundant patience,

He permits you, though unworthy, to behold the pleasant light?

You read what is true, but teach it mutilated as false;

You approve that what comes afterward and is still to be was done before.

Or what difference is there in believing your incredible doctrine?

## CHAPTER VII

Nor is it strange that the cunning one captured you while unarmed [Note: [Col. 1059C] “Nor is it strange that the cunning one captured you while unarmed,” etc. We corrected *Nec mirum* (“Nor is it strange”) in place of *nimirum*. It is worth noting that he calls heresy [Col. 1059D] an “incorrigible crime,” since daily experience teaches us that this is entirely true of those who have once become heretics.],

Persuading you that there are two fathers who are Gods—though condemned by one—

He who in the beginning had seduced everyone.

Afterward he distributed among many the plague that flowed

From manifold wounds, and the incorrigible crime.

He persuaded unspeakable men, filled with every madness of magic [Note: [Col. 1059D] “Unspeakable men, filled with every madness of magic,” etc. He alludes to Simon Magus. On his claim to be the supreme Power, see the aforementioned *On the Prescription against Heretics*, chapter 46, heresy 1; and concerning Helena, his prostitute, see *On the Soul*, chapter 34. On his flight, see Hegesippus, *On the Destruction of Jerusalem*, book II, chapter 2, and blessed Maximus’s sermon on Saints Peter and Paul.],

To call himself the supreme Power [Note: [Col. 1059C] *Pervasit*. Pamelius, Obert.];

[Col. 1059C] To practice deception with a prostitute, traverse wickedness, and fly.

[Col. 1060A] For Valentinus has a mad God [Note: [Col. 1059D] “For Valentinus,” etc. Concerning him, see the aforementioned book, chapter 49, heresy 12, and the complete book *Against the Valentinians.], and assigns to eternity

Thirty heavens and a father called the Depth.

He taught that one should be baptized twice, the body being conveyed through flame [Note: [Col. 1059D] “He taught that one should be baptized twice, the body being conveyed through flame,” etc. He means certain heretics who baptized twice, once in water and once in fire; yet I have found no mention of them elsewhere, except that Philastrius, though still rather obscurely, appears in his book *On Heresies* to attribute to the Herminians and Seleucians the teaching that baptism should be performed not in water but in fire, under the pretext of John the Baptist’s words, Matthew 3: “He will baptize you in spirit and fire.” [Col. 1060C] We have substituted the Tertullianic form *tingui* for *tingi*.].

Basilides commanded belief in as many gods [Note: [Col. 1060C] “Basilides commanded belief in as many gods,” etc. Epiphanius treats this heresy of Basilides at length in Heresy 31, where he enumerates by name 365 gods, as many as there are days in the year. We also said something about it above in the aforementioned *On the Prescription against Heretics*, chapter 46, heresy 4. What the author adds—“and, finally, as many worlds”—is peculiar to him.]

As the year has days—and, finally, as many worlds.

Marcus, arguing subtly by means of numbers [Note: [Col. 1060C] “Marcus, arguing subtly by means of numbers,” etc. *Marcus* must certainly be corrected here in place of *Marcion*, for neither statement is written of Marcion, but of Marcus’s heresy [Col. 1060D], constructed from the Greek alphabet, in which he argued by numbers up to 888. . . . On the magical tricks he employed in offering the chalice, see *Against the Valentinians*, chapter 4. Moreover, the author’s statement that the chalice is offered also serves to confirm the Christian sacrifice.],

Taught that through magic the chaste form should be violated,

That the chalice should be offered and changed into blood by prayers.

He persuaded Ebion that Christ was born from human seed [Note: [Col. 1060D] “He persuaded Ebion that Christ was born from human seed,” etc. On this heresy of Ebion, see above, *On the Flesh of Christ*, the whole of chapter 18; and on his having proposed the Law together with the Gospel, see the aforementioned *On the Prescription against Heretics*, chapter 48, heresy 11.],

And taught him to be circumcised and devote himself to the Law,

Forsaking the fountains and taking up again the elements of the Law.

I do not wish to prolong the final outrage in words,

Or to relate every cause or every name.

It is enough, through a few examples, to mark the many cruelties,

The unspeakable men and savage instruments of the dragon,

[Col. 1060B] Through whom those who have now uttered so great a crime without witness

Continually labor to blame the Maker of the world.

## CHAPTER VIII

But draw back your foot [Note: [Col. 1060D] “But draw back your foot,” etc. We necessarily corrected *Revocate* (“draw back”) in place of *revocare*, and *dum spatium datur* (“while an opportunity is given”) in place of *detur*. Then the conjunction *vero* is used with a short final syllable in “Believe in the true Father, truly”; and in “because he believed beforehand,” *credere* is used in the sense of “to entrust.” The reading “finally, the enclosed” appears corrupt. We emended *poenamque merentes* (“deserving punishment”) in place of *merentem*, because *populos* (“peoples”) precedes.] from the savage robber’s cave,

While an opportunity is given, and God—ever patient in loving-kindness—

Forgives the wretched all deeds committed through error.

Believe indeed in the true Father, who founded the world,

Who sent the true Lord to restore the fallen

Who were unequal to the Law and submerged in the gulf of sin,

And to bring the salvation promised long ago through the prophets.

He who gave the commandments also forgives sins.

He rightly requires something here, because he entrusted it beforehand;

Or the Lord grants lavishly, as though paying debts to servants.

Finally, as for the peoples shut in and deserving punishment,

[Col. 1060C] Once the indictment is erased [Note: [Col. 1060C] *Delecto*. Obert.] he himself commands that all be washed [Note: [Col. 1060D] “Once the indictment is erased, he himself commands that all be washed,” etc. Fabricius observed, not unreasonably, that the author here uses *elogium* for the definite establishment of some matter, if the old reading *Deleto elogio* is retained. But since in the passages cited above—*Apology*, chapter 2, and elsewhere, so far as I recall—the author always uses this word unfavorably for the heading of an accusation, we thought that *Deleto elogio* (“once the indictment is erased”) should instead be read, just as in *To Scapula*, chapter 4, he says *scisso elogio* (“the indictment torn up”), alluding to the Apostle’s words in Colossians 2: “Erasing the handwriting of the offense that stood against us.”].

## CHAPTER IX

[Col. 1061A]

Therefore the whole human being believes, and the whole is washed.

He abstains, or truly suffers wounds for the Name;

The true human being rises, and death, truly conquered, will be silent.

But the soul, only one part of the human being, will not receive the palm

While leaving behind its own part—the flesh that labored beside it [Note: [Col. 1061C] “Will receive the palm,” etc. This serves [Col. 1062C] to confirm reward according to the merits of works, as do “rewards are repaid,” “the equitable payer of reward,” “through merit alone,” and finally “after merited,” etc., “honors.” Moreover, the following verses appear corrupt:

> Nor unlawfully to wish to blame the Lord by teaching  
> That he should raise—or whom?—the soul, as though it had suffered ruin, etc.

Likewise this verse:

> To see what it had once been, when turned to dust.

Here the passage seems mutilated.].

Having contended in the arena, the two together deserve it in union.

It is a great wickedness for two to bear the weight in chains,

One of whom is rich and the other destitute,

And for the wretched one to be despised while rewards are repaid to the fortunate.

The just God does not do this—the equitable payer of reward,

Good and rich, whom we believe capable of all things,

Gracious toward the ungrateful, his will full of compassion.

Rather, to the one in whom mortal need is greater,

So that it may become equal, the rich one, preferred above its companion,

[Col. 1061B] Will freely bestow whatever it lacks: thus we are commanded to believe.

Nor unlawfully to wish to blame the Lord by teaching

That he should raise—or whom?—the soul, as though it had suffered ruin,

And free it from death, while the living soul retained

An abundance gained solely through merit, because it acted bravely;

While the flesh, which always shared the common labor,

Should be abandoned to the earth and become death’s perpetual prey.

Did the human soul, then, please God through courageous deeds?

By itself it could in no way have pleased him without the flesh.

It bore chains and punishments; the flesh carried bonds upon its limbs;

It despised death, yet by death abandoned the flesh.

[Col. 1061C] The soul suffered in giving birth; the flesh was cut off from life by a wound.

[Col. 1062A] The former seeks rest; the latter, poured into the dust by the sword,

Was abandoned to fish, birds, putrefaction, and ashes.

Unhappy, it is broken and mangled; scattered, it dissolves.

Did it not deserve to rise? For what could it alone have committed

When lifeless? What envious cause of life

Hinders or forbids the flesh from receiving God’s gifts

And living forever united with its companion?

To see what it had once been, when turned to dust?

After its deserts, let it be restored and bear to God the honors of praise,

Not ignorant of itself—once frail, mortal, and sick—

So that, after its struggles, living power may show what it can accomplish:

For he who is good and mighty begrudges life to no one.

Was this flesh death’s captive, and will this bound thing perish,

Which the Lord fashioned with wondrous wisdom and skill?

By his wondrous power he himself raises it;

The leader himself recalls it, and clothes it with his glory.

[Col. 1062B] Therefore God’s craftsmanship and wisdom fitted together the body

Which he desires to be made for these purposes; does his power lack the means to restore it?

No cause can resist holy loving-kindness,

Lest the cause of evil be greater than supreme power;

So that humanity, now saved by God’s gift, may know—

Once mortal, but now clothed in inviolable and immense light,

Alive and whole in body—

God, immeasurable in power and everlasting in loving-kindness,

Through Christ the King, through whom the way of light has been opened;

And now, in new light and filled with both gifts,

Joyful amid the delightful fruits of the living paradise,

May praise him forever, rich in the heavenly court.

# BOOK II: ON THE HARMONY OF THE OLD AND NEW LAW

[Col. 1061]

## SUMMARY OF BOOK II, ALSO BY THE AUTHOR HIMSELF

Then the following book, joined to the first, teaches the mysteries of the Law

And what the one God handed down in the new covenant.

### NOTE

By these rather obscure words the author indicates that this book was written on the concord of the Old and New Law; we have therefore recently added that description to the title. We have also corrected *Deus* (“God”) in place of *divis* (“divinities”): first, because the author more than once lengthens the first syllable of the word *Deus* in these books; second, because the ancients were not accustomed to call any beings *divi*; and finally, because “one God” serves the present argument against Marcion’s many Gods. PAMELIUS.

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 1061D]

After faith was shattered by apostates breathing out the enemy [Note: [Col. 1061D] “After faith was shattered by apostates breathing out [Col. 1062D] the enemy,” etc. We emended *emersit pestis operta* (“the hidden plague emerged”) [Col. 1063C] in place of *pectis*, which seemed to have crept in through a typographical error. Likewise, a little below:

> Unexpectedly killing the unwary by the final death,

in place of “The unexpected thing denying.” Moreover, the phrase “that he might charge his own wicked deeds to God” is Tertullianic [Col. 1063D], as are *suasos* for *persuasos* (“those persuaded”), “fasten them to punishments,” and shortly afterward “set the Lord’s words against one another.”],

[Col. 1062D] And, massed together with deceits, the hidden plague emerged

Of its own accord: a lying mind that despised the Divine Majesty

[Col. 1063A] Inflicted wounds and, growing pale upon its deceitful path,

Mingled its impious words with the sayings of the saints

And sowed wretched darnel among the good seed.

Then, wishing the whole cause of the fall to be established—

Namely, that it might the more swiftly charge its own wicked deeds to God

And be able to fasten those it had persuaded to punishments—

It covered falsehoods with truths and transformed harsh things into gentle ones;

And, with the spear-point concealed by rosy garlands,

Unexpectedly killed the unwary by the final death.

It is the supreme wickedness that men cut off and sunk

By madness so deep should divide into parts the Divine Majesty

That must be feared without end; that they should follow Christ’s sublime deeds

With false praise, yet blame the deeds that came before—

God’s innumerable miracles, never seen [Note: [Col. 1063D] “God’s innumerable miracles, never seen,” etc. We joined to this verse:

> Nor heard before, nor contained in any heart,

and placed after it:

> So rashly to fashion an unlawful crime in words,

because there he alludes to Isaiah 64 and 1 Corinthians 2: “What eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has entered into the human heart.” Hence we also corrected *contentaque* (“contained”) in place of *contemptaque*; likewise, shortly afterward, *quia corde gravatis* (“because their hearts are weighed down”) in place of *gravati*. Moreover, an error seems to underlie this verse as well:

> Dull, they think things borne by the Spirit worthy of animals;

or the passage is mutilated and cannot be remedied without a manuscript codex.],

Nor heard before, nor contained in any heart.

[Col. 1063B] So rashly to fashion an unlawful crime in words:

That the two Testaments sound against one another;

To set the Lord’s words against the prophets;

To seek to prove that their meaning is utterly different;

To drag down the whole cause of the Law [Note: [Col. 1064C] “And does not read.” Obert.] and make it infamous;

And to reject the former lives of the holy fathers,

Whom God drew into friendship and toward his gifts.

A work without a beginning is accepted in its smaller part:

Though four come from one, and from the four comes one,

Only one pleases them, and they reject the remaining three parts;

And in many matters they lay claim to Paul as their authority.

From Paul himself, moreover, their madness raged against the final words:

Whatever he said concerning the ancient covenant

Appears harsh to them, deservedly, because their hearts are weighed down.

The apostolic weight, the grace of the resplendent word,

[Col. 1063C] Brings light before the mind, yet they cannot see;

Dull, they think things borne by the Spirit worthy only of animals.

## CHAPTER II

But you who have not yet, under the guidance of a false Divinity,

Become wholly reprobate in mind and penetrated death’s inner depths:

[Col. 1064A] Learn that from the fountain flows an everlasting river [Note: [Col. 1063D] “From the fountain flows an everlasting river,” [Col. 1064C] etc. Bede testifies that Christian poets commonly lengthen final *e* when two consonants follow in the next word; for example, both this author here and more than once afterward, Juvencus, book IV, “Behold the scribes,” and Prudentius in the *Cathemerinon*, “by a wondrous slaughter.” He alludes to the twice-six apostles, whose sound went out into all the earth and into the world’s four winds—that is, to the ends of the earth—as Psalm 18 reads. We corrected *Sordes diluere* (“to wash away filth”) in place of *sordide*. Here he unusually uses “dead destinies” for dead human beings, and shortly afterward we corrected *Electaque fide* (“received by chosen faith”) in place of *electoque*.],

Which grace nourishes, with twice-six fruits;

It goes out into the earth and the world’s four winds;

Into so many parts flows the fountain’s one color and flavor.

So too the Church flows forth through the apostolic word,

From Christ’s womb, filled with all the Father’s glory,

To wash away filth and revive the dead.

One Gospel, fourfold in number, was spread among the nations

And received with chosen faith; the bountiful teacher

Paul hands it down pure and without fault.

Apart from this, he forbade the holy Galatians to depart—

Those whom false brothers persuaded to be circumcised

And taught to follow the elements, abandoning their new freedom,

And to serve the old shadow of things to come.

These were the reasons Paul had for writing to the Galatians,

[Col. 1064B] Not so that they should receive a small part of the Gospel

As though it were the entire body, abandoning the greater part.

Indeed, the Gospel is not the words of a book, but Christ himself,

Sent into the world—if you are willing to see—

Who came from the Father, alone bearing good tidings

Attested beforehand; his immense glory fulfills them,

Showing by his work how great the world’s Creator is.

His faithful followers—Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John—

Wrote down his deeds together with his sayings,

Speaking pure words, not foreign ones,

Made holy by God’s Spirit, with so great a teacher present.

## CHAPTER III

Here the Paschal lamb, the sacrificial victim, is suspended upon the wood [Note: [Col. 1064D] “Here the Paschal lamb, the sacrificial victim, is suspended upon the wood,” etc. He alludes to 1 Corinthians 5: “Christ our Passover has been sacrificed”; and “who was to be life and God” refers to what follows, “promised,” after which comes the archaic form *illectaverat*, and soon afterward the penultimate syllable of *poteritis* is lengthened in poetic fashion.].

Writing his decrees to Corinth, Paul declares that this Passover

Was slain, and was to be life and God,

[Col. 1064C] Promised to the fathers, whom he had attracted before.

Perceive what power can do, and what the image of Passover can do;

Thus you will be able to see what the true Passover is.

Lest the faithful father and prophet [Note: [Col. 1064D] “Lest the faithful father and prophet,” etc. He means Abraham, to whom (Genesis 22), in place of his son Isaac as the sacrifice,

> A lamb caught by its head among thorns is shown,

as also in what follows:

> That through its death, once atonement had been made,  
> It might serve as a sign,

[Col. 1065D] referring to Exodus 12, where the doorposts and many thresholds are commanded to be marked with the blood of the Paschal lamb. In place of “many” I would prefer to read “mute,” but I have changed nothing. To these he adds the Passover celebrated in Joshua 5. A little afterward we corrected “or by its blood it erases death” in place of “grieves.”], to whom the pledge and heir

Was dear—the son whom God had perhaps granted him—

Should sacrifice him to God, he was rightly tested in his love:

A great ordeal. In place of holy blood, blood is supplied;

A lamb caught by its head among thorns is shown

As a holy victim to God. So that through its death, once atonement had been made,

It might serve as a sign and pledge of the ravaged nation,

The doorposts and many thresholds are marked with blood—

A mighty aid: its flesh, a witness, is believed to be food.

Jesus crossed the Jordan, took possession of the land,

And, according to the Law, instituted Passover, rejoicing and sacrificing the lamb.

The remaining great kings and holy prophets as well,

Not ignorant of the promises of certain salvation

And, filled with awe, unwilling to transgress the immense Law,

Celebrated Passover in due order—the great reality to come,

Beheld in a mirror through an image of supreme power.

Finally, if with swift mind you retrace the beginnings,

[Col. 1065B] You will find something exceedingly deadly after the impious words:

Alas, credulous man, easily stripped of the covering of life!

So that humanity might be clothed with skins, a lamb is suspended;

Either it slays sins, or by its blood erases death,

Or covers the naked, or warms a wound with its fleece [Note: [Col. 1065D] “Or warms a wound with its fleece,” etc. Here, by poetic license, the final *o* of *suo* is shortened, as it frequently is afterward in these books; concerning the same usage in other Christian poets, see Bede. We also emended *ut pro peccatis* (“so that, offered for sins”) in place of *aut*, so that the preceding “Is not the blood of a beast” becomes a question, with *non* understood in the Tertullianic manner as *nonne* (“is it not?”). Moreover, he also proves from John 1 that Christ is the Lamb [Col. 1066D].].

Is the blood of a beast worth more than human blood,

So that, offered for sins, it may put an end to wrath—

Or for that of a multitude, as though the lamb were more precious?

A mighty aid, the safeguard of so great a salvation:

Could the victim offered be a sufficient price for the redeemed? [Note: [Col. 1065D] “Salvation.” Pamelius, Venetian edition.]

But almighty God, creator of heaven and earth,

Immeasurable, living, perfect, everlasting in light,

Is not appeased by these things, nor does he rejoice in the blood of cattle.

Let every beast be slaughtered, let every flock burn away upon the altars,

So that a single animal might gain pardon or atone for sin:

[Col. 1065C] By no means did the wise but defiled image of the Lord—

Foul flesh—obtain honors at so paltry a price.

Rather, hope and the faith promised long ago to mortals

Accomplished this and bore it: an image of the great design,

Forethought by the Father and prepared in abundant loving-kindness—

That Christ would come to earth and become human.

When John saw him—the first to inaugurate baptism,

Companion of the prophets and also a mighty apostle,

The sure forerunner sent forth and faithful witness,

August in life and distinguished by sublime praise—

He finally showed to those seeking the Passover of God, the true Lamb,

That he had come: the one who by his own blood would discharge

The penalties of the deed and the sins of many;

Good for the reprobate, dear for the worthless—the one;

Human in body and God in life, slaughtered like a lamb,

[Col. 1065D] That he might receive us and pour out himself for us.

[Col. 1066A] Thus it pleased the Lord to despoil proud death;

Thus pitiable humanity could hope for salvation.

Paul proclaims that this Lamb was sacrificed as our Passover.

The sublime Lord does not suffer a likeness in form

To an unfeeling beast; that is not why he is called a lamb.

Rather, it is because his renewed fleece clothes these bodies,

Providing a covering for many while he himself never fails.

## CHAPTER IV

Thus the Lord, by the Father’s power, clothes in his own light

Those whom he redeemed by death—a light always present in him [Note: [Col. 1066D] “Thus the Lord, by the Father’s power,” etc. “To gain possession of the flock” and “strong to rescue” are Tertullianic; thus we read *eripere* with the final *e* lengthened, where one might expect *eriperet*. Likewise “a virgin drawn aside by the dragon”; and not much afterward, “without the debt of death,” which we corrected in place of “the due [feminine]”; likewise “the footprints of death” in place of “death.” Again, “he extends his hand,” etc., and “to touch the wood” belong to the author’s style.].

Therefore the shepherd who lost his sheep seeks it himself.

Prepared to tread upon the oak, the thorns, and the grapes,

Or to overcome the wolf’s fury and gain possession of the flock,

Strong to rescue, freely scorning its teeth,

[Col. 1066B] He offers himself, a lion covered in a sheep’s skin,

Deceiving it by his appearance, about to seize its jaws by his blood.

Thus Christ seeks everywhere for Adam, captured by violence,

Walking himself along the path by which death accomplished the fall,

Surveying all the memorials of the ancient men,

Examining each one, himself the single fulfillment of every pattern;

Beginning from the womb to expel hostile death,

Traversing every age in silent wisdom together with the flesh’s seed,

Conceived in the womb, taking on what was owed,

Prepared to cleanse all and restore to the Maker the many

Whom the one man had scattered.

And because a man, frightfully plunged into an evil pit,

Had fallen, and a virgin drawn aside by the dragon had persuaded him,

It pleased his design to abandon the heavenly covering.

The tree exposed them as naked; black death compelled them.

[Col. 1066C] From the same substance, restored by a similar design,

Now renewed, returns the flower of flesh and the guest of peace:

Flesh from the betrothed virgin, not born from human seed,

Joined to its own Maker, without the debt of death.

An angel bears these commands of the Father through the bright stars,

So that the tidings may be believed on the angelic report:

So that virgin may pay what virgin owes, and flesh what flesh owes.

Entering through such means, the immense little child,

The older boy and youthful man, follows the footprints of death.

Afterward, when the rightful age of manly strength was complete—

The age accustomed gradually to decay along with its companion life,

To change in appearance into ugly wrinkles and enfeebled limbs

As the veins were emptied of blood—

He halted, not allowing the condition of his flesh to grow old,

At the age when Adam recklessly extended his hand to touch the tree.

[Col. 1066D] On the same day and in the same place where glorious Adam fell [Note: [Col. 1066D] “On the day and in the place where glorious Adam fell,” etc. Thus we emended the text in place of “dearest.” What he says here he shortly repeats more clearly in these words:

> [Col. 1067D] The place is Golgotha, once the Calvary of the head, etc.  
> Here our ancestors taught that a great bone was found;  
> Here, we have received, the first human being was buried, etc.

This is also found in blessed Cyprian’s treatise *On the Resurrection of Christ*; see there the similar passages from the ancients in our notes, no. 3. It is peculiar to this author that he writes that Christ suffered on the same day on which Adam fell.],

[Col. 1067A] When that same day returned in the revolving years,

The mighty athlete entered the contest in the arena of the tree.

He extended his hands, pursued punishment instead of praise,

And conquered death, because he voluntarily left death.

He stripped off the spoils of flesh and the debt of death;

And, once the ruler of the world had been conquered, affixed to the tree

The serpent’s spoil—a mighty trophy taken from the apostates.

As a sign of this Moses had suspended a serpent,

So that all who had been struck by the many serpents

Might look upon the modeled and transfixed dragon.

Afterward, when he came to the secret waters of the underworld,

Victorious, he disclosed his captive by his light

And fulfilled the Father’s command with his power standing by;

He himself willingly took up again the body he had abandoned.

This had been the cause of death; it became the same road of salvation.

[Col. 1067B] That one was a messenger of deceit, but this one a messenger of peace;

A bride killed her husband, but a bride gave birth to the lion;

A virgin harmed a man, but a man born of a virgin conquered.

As an image of this, when the body had been lulled to sleep,

A woman, the husband’s rib, was taken from his side.

Upon awakening he called her flesh of his flesh

And bones from his bones, speaking with prophetic understanding.

Wondrous is the faith; rightly Paul is its most certain authority,

For he teaches that Christ from heaven is the second Adam.

Truth itself shines, employing its own patterns;

It does not desire to display vigorous strides with borrowed things:

That is the work of a pauper, one lacking power of his own.

The great Paul taught these learned mysteries,

Clearly seeing that in Christ is your glory, O Church [Note: [Col. 1067D] “Clearly seeing that in Christ is your glory, O Church,” etc. This verse is corrupt, and I could not remedy it because manuscripts were lacking. Meanwhile, the first syllable of *Ecclesia* is shortened, as is peculiar to the author both above in book I, chapter 6, “The Church flows forth through the word,” here, and shortly afterward:

> This is the true mother of the living people, the Church,

and below in book III, chapter 7:

> That Christ is the head, whose members are the Church.

I suspect an error also underlies “the woman was blood.”].

[Col. 1067C] From his side, while his lifeless body hung high upon the tree,

Blood and water flowed.

The woman was blood; the water was the new gift of the washing.

This is the true mother of the living people, the Church:

Flesh from Christ’s new flesh, and bone from his bones [Note: [Col. 1067C] “Bones.” Venetian edition.].

The place is Golgotha, once the Calvary of the head [Note: [Col. 1068D] “The place is Golgotha,” etc. Here the middle syllable of this word is lengthened, whereas in the *Hymn on the Lord’s Passover*, attributed to Cyprian, it is shortened:

> There is a place which we perceive to be at the center of the whole world;  
> The Jews in their ancestral tongue call it Golgotha.

So too in Juvencus, book IV: “Where the countryside bears the name Golgotha.” Shortly afterward we emended “to be raised by the power of the dripping water” in place of “to raise.”];

Thus the ancestral tongue first named it.

Here is the earth’s center; here is the sign of victory.

Here our ancestors taught that a great bone was found [Note: [Col. 1067D] “Of the ancient one.” Obert.];

Here, we have received, the first human being was buried.

[Col. 1068A] Here Christ suffers; the earth is soaked with holy blood,

So that the dust of ancient Adam, mingled with Christ’s blood,

May be raised by the power of the dripping water [Note: [Col. 1068C] “To raise.” Obert.].

This is the one sheep whom the shepherd determined, on the Sabbath,

To draw alive from the pit of the underworld [Note: [Col. 1068D] “Or, on the Sabbath, called the whole lifeless man back alive,” etc. It is peculiar to this author that he intimates that the raising of Lazarus (John 11) took place on the Sabbath; for it is clear from what follows—“As the holy day began,” etc.—that he is speaking of Lazarus.].

For this reason on the Sabbaths he healed the prematurely dead limbs

Of all flesh; or, by giving sight, perfected the eyes

Of the man blind from birth—eyes which he had not previously given;

Or, on the Sabbath, called the whole lifeless man back alive

From the tomb, with the people present.

He is himself the Maker of the new and the good Restorer of the old,

Either supplying what was lacking or restoring what had been lost.

By this he taught that, as the holy day began, he would be able

To accomplish these things fully, according to the covenant, for those who hoped in him.

## CHAPTER V

[Col. 1068B] What? If the flesh dies and no hope of salvation is given [Note: [Col. 1068D] “What? If the flesh dies and no hope of salvation is given,” etc. We emended *Quid?* (“What?”) in place of *quod*, and *salutis* (“of salvation”) in place of *saluti*. “To heal,” etc., is Tertullianic. “Will the Lord’s power not be able,” etc., has the sense “Surely it will be able.”],

What reason did Christ have to fashion himself as a human being,

To heal human beings, or to care for the flesh?

Powerful corruption can dissolve the body into decay:

Will the Lord’s power not be able to restore what has been dissolved?

Those who believe that their bodies—not the Lord’s own—are dissolved in destruction

Do not believe the Lord, who wills to raise his own submerged bodies.

They say either that he is unwilling to bestow the good or that the Mighty One cannot,

Unaware of how great a crime they suckle,

Daring to set feeble arguments above death.
