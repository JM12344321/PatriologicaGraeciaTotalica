# DISSERTATION ON THE *APOLOGY* OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, THE TWO BOOKS *TO THE NATIONS*, AND THE ONE BOOK *TO SCAPULA*

(BY DOM NICOLAS LE NOURRY)

PRIEST AND MONK OF THE ORDER OF SAINT BENEDICT, OF THE CONGREGATION OF SAINT MAUR

## CHAPTER ONE

### Analysis of the *Apology*, and Who the Tertullian Named in Its Title Was

[Col. 0705B]

### ARTICLE ONE

#### Analysis of the *Apology*

When Tertullian saw, with no slight grief of mind, that Christians were being persecuted and killed by the pagans with such injustice that they were permitted neither to defend themselves openly nor to be heard publicly by the judges, he undertook to plead their cause before the governors of the Roman Empire through the hidden power of written works.

First, therefore, he complains most gravely that all Christians were regarded and tortured by pagans who neither knew them nor wished to know them, contrary to every [Col. 0705C] law of equity and nature, and with an utterly implacable hatred. Indeed, the public testimony to this ignorance all the more clearly convicted the pagans of wrongdoing because, as soon as people learned what the Christian religion was and what kind of religion it was, they immediately abandoned the worship of false gods and embraced it throughout the whole world.

Nor should it be said that they were then being led into evil. For it is the disposition of an evil person to seek hiding places, overwhelmed by shame and fear. A Christian, by contrast, even when condemned and tortured by every kind of torment, glories, rejoices, and exults in his profession (chapter 1).

Furthermore, if a Christian is guilty of sacrilege, incest, homicide, or any other crime, as the pagans babbled [Col. 0705D], why is he condemned by them, contrary to divine and human laws, without any accusation of a crime, without a witness, without any prior examination of a single circumstance, of the time, or of accomplices, but solely on the prejudgment attached to the name?

Pliny, therefore, moved by this injustice, asked the emperor Trajan what should be done with Christians who had been convicted of absolutely no crime or offense except an obstinate determination to persevere in the worship of God and Christ and in their own discipline. But he received no other answer from Trajan than that they were not to be sought out, yet, if brought before the judges, ought to be punished. What, I ask you, could be more unjust than a law that forbids Christians to be sought out on the ground that they are innocent, yet commands them to be condemned as guilty (chapter 2)?

[Col. 0706B] In addition, divine and human laws provided that no one guilty of any crime whatsoever should ever be subjected either to punishment or to judicial torture except in order that he might confess his crimes. Christians, by contrast, were tortured so that they would deny both the crimes of which they were usually accused and the fact that they were Christians. The battle, therefore, concerned the Christian name alone. And certainly the sentence pronounced against them by the judges from the tablet, omitting the specification of any crime, declared nothing other than that Christians were condemned.

Yet the pagans sinned all the more gravely against the most sacred laws because they repeatedly acknowledged that the conduct of Christians was chaste and holy, and that those among their own pagan population who adopted the name of the Christian faith [Col. 0706C] always became better people. Nevertheless, they raged with no less cruelty against these converts than against all the others, because they had assumed the Christian name.

“But,” the pagans used to say, “the name of their founder displeases us.” What, then, Tertullian replies, can be hateful in the name of Christ? Is it not derived from pleasantness or kindness? What is there in it that is more deserving of hatred than the very names of philosophers, physicians, or cooks, transmitted by their founders and received by others? Finally, the name of no sect ought to be considered evil or hateful unless it is first certainly ascertained and proved what evil exists within that sect itself (chapter 3).

[Col. 0706D] The pagans insisted that the enactment of laws issued by the emperors bound them to persecute and destroy Christians. “But this exception,” Tertullian says, “is utterly inept.” For no one is bound by any unjust law that either prohibits what is good or commands something iniquitous. Yet such were the laws enacted against the Christians.

Moreover, the pagans could not deny that many laws issued by their own legislators and by others likewise subject to human weakness were afterward corrected, curtailed, or repealed by others because they appeared harmful or useless. Were not the laws of Lycurgus corrected by the Lacedaemonians? Were not the Papian laws concerning the begetting of children either corrected or rendered obsolete by the emperor Severus? Was not the law concerning the cutting of debtors into [Col. 0707A] pieces altered? Nor is this surprising. For laws are commended neither by antiquity nor by the dignity of their author, but by equity alone. Yet no law was ever more unjust than that which commanded Christians to be condemned merely because of their name, without their case having been heard (chapter 4).

Tertullian demonstrates that this law was not only new and unjust but also tyrannical from the following consideration. During the reign of Tiberius, when the Christian name began to be disseminated through various parts of the world, Tiberius, having heard of Christ’s miracles, wished him to be enrolled among the gods. Although he did not accomplish this because of the senate’s opposition, he nevertheless persisted in his opinion and threatened danger to the accusers of Christians.

Among his successors, Nero was the very first—alas, what an impious and criminal man!—to rage against Christians with the imperial sword [Col. 0707B]. Domitian, no less cruel than Nero, attempted the same thing, but quickly restrained his own efforts. Marcus Aurelius stood far apart from both of them: when rain had been obtained from heaven through the prayers of Christian soldiers, he ordered that those who accused Christ’s followers should be punished more severely. Before him, Trajan, without issuing any law, prohibited Christians from being sought out. Nor does any law or decree of the emperors Hadrian, Vespasian, Pius, or Verus against them survive. Thus only the worst, most wicked, and most impious emperors raged against them (chapter 5).

Tertullian then, in order to close off to the pagans every foolish line of argument based upon the institutions and laws of their ancestors, makes it plain to everyone by a brief induction [Col. 0707C] that they themselves had overthrown the laws and more sacred statutes concerning expenditure, canvassing for office, abstinence from wine, divorce, and the worship of the gods.

Roman consuls expelled Liber and his mysteries from Rome and all Italy, just as Piso and Gabinius, likewise consuls, expelled Serapis, Isis, and Harpocrates from the Capitol and the council chamber of the gods; these deities were subsequently restored by the Romans only after returning from exile. Finally, all the pagans had renounced the customs of their forefathers in clothing, food, furnishings, wealth, and even language itself. They therefore reproached Christians in vain for trampling down and overthrowing the authority of their ancient fathers, which they themselves had destroyed as boldly as they had done so with impunity (chapter 6).

From there our Author passes to the false and fabricated abominable [Col. 0707D] crimes of the Christians. He refutes them in such a way as to demonstrate with the utmost clarity that the Christians were as entirely innocent of them as the pagans were actually guilty.

First, Christians had long been defamed as though, in the manner of wild beasts, they devoured the flesh of a child whom they had killed and then, after the lights had been extinguished by the activity of dogs, wallowed most foully in every kind of sexual violation and incest. Yet nothing could have been easier than to convict Christians of such great crimes, if those crimes were really perpetrated by them. For their enemies and foes were always as numerous as the Jews, the soldiers, the household servants, almost all their wives, and even the pagans who continually persecuted them. Yet none of these ever learned of such deeds, much less disclosed them [Col. 0708A] or sold silence about them to the Christians for any price.

And indeed, who could have revealed these crimes? Certainly not the Christians, who concealed their mysteries no less scrupulously than did the pagans. Nor the pagans, who, being completely excluded from the Christians’ assemblies, could undoubtedly not know what was done in them. “But these things,” they said, “were spread by common report.” Yet what is more insubstantial than rumor? Since it always arises from envy, hatred, suspicion, or the pleasure of deceiving others, it persists only as long as it lies (chapter 7).

Tertullian insists that those crimes were so horrible that no one ought ever to have believed them or could ever have committed them. Christians promised their followers eternal happiness. Who could bring himself [Col. 0708B] to believe that they promised it to a person who killed an infant, ate bread dipped in its fresh blood, and afterward polluted himself by unspeakable incest? Indeed, no one could ever be persuaded that these things could be done by a pagan who possessed any sense of shame and humanity.

Nor should you say that the candidates for the Christian religion were deceived by others. Who could have deceived them? The father of the sacred rites, perhaps, or another person by whom they were instructed? But by what fraud, by what deceptions, could he teach them what they had to do in order to perpetrate those accursed crimes? Nevertheless, grant, if you will, that these things were committed by them. Why, after they had finally learned of and performed those acts, did they never complain of the fraud practiced upon them, but all [Col. 0708C] remained so steadfast in the horrible doctrine handed down to them? Did they fear that they would be punished if they revealed these things? On the contrary, they would thereby have won the favor of the pagans. But they preferred to perish rather than live beneath such a consciousness of guilt (chapter 8).

With the same ease with which Tertullian clears the Christians of such great crimes, he shows that the pagans were thoroughly convicted of the same or entirely similar offenses. In Africa, infants were openly sacrificed to Saturn—who did not spare his own sons—down to the first proconsulate of Tiberius, and secretly thereafter. The Gauls were accustomed to sacrifice older persons to Mercury, the Taurians their guests to Diana, and the Romans bestiarii—who were nevertheless human beings no less than the others—to Jupiter in the public games.

Moreover, others, with no less cruelty, deprived infants of life either by abortion or by exposing them after birth to imminent death [Col. 0708D]. To this is added the fact that not only did certain nations, like Catiline, ratify a compact by shedding their own blood, but the Scythians were also said to eat human beings. The priests of Bellona were consecrated with their own blood; and gladiators, like those suffering from epilepsy, drank the blood of other human beings killed in the arena.

Others also, at public spectacles, ate the flesh of a stag that had lain in human blood, or of a boar that had bloodied a human being, or the stomachs of a bear that still belched forth the entrails of people killed in those games. Finally, in a still more horrible crime, which ought never even to be named, they swallowed the substance of a human being. Christians, by contrast, recoiled [Col. 0709A] from every kind of cruelty; and even amid the cruelest tortures it was unlawful for them to taste either animal blood, anything strangled, or carrion offered to false gods (chapter 9).

What reason, moreover, did the pagans have for charging Christians with incest, of which the most criminal Jupiter had furnished an example and of which the Persians were guilty? The Macedonians too were suspected of the same crime, for they mocked the actor who, while performing the tragedy of Oedipus, lamented that his own mother had been violated by him.

What more need be said? Wicked pagans, accustomed to sexual violations, blindly inflicted sexual outrage even upon their own children, who had formerly been exposed and were no longer recognized by them. Christians, by contrast, either persevered in the state of virginity or always abstained from every transgression after marriage.

[Col. 0709B] From the false and fictitious secret crimes of the Christians, Tertullian passes to those that the pagans charged were manifest and public. The chief of these was the charge of sacrilege and violation of the divine majesty, brought against them because they utterly denied all worship to the gods.

Tertullian does not deny that this would undoubtedly be a great crime if those whom the pagans worshipped were truly gods. But the consciousness of the pagans themselves bore witness that they were not gods but mere human beings. If anyone should still dare to resist, he would immediately be refuted by innumerable testimonies from their own writers, who identify the places where those fictitious gods were born, where they lived their mortal lives, and where, after those lives ended, they were buried.

[Col. 0709C] Since it would have taken too long to enumerate every one of these gods by name, our Author demonstrates the point by the example of Saturn, the first of all the gods. For all writers on antiquity assert that Saturn was truly a man who, after many journeys, was received in Italy with Attic hospitality by Janus or Ino, and gave the name Saturnian to the mountain and city he inhabited and to all Italy.

This was declared no less plainly by the tablets first fashioned by him there and by the coin stamped with an image, on account of which he is said to preside over the treasury. He was therefore a human being, not the offspring of heaven and earth, as some have fabricated. Who, then, would dare deny that all the other gods who derive their origin from him were human beings as well (chapter 10)?
