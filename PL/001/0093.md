Nor did the Jews’ untamed and blind obstinacy stop here. Although Christ, by miracles hitherto unheard of, was making it most manifestly clear to everyone that he had come from the bosom of his eternal Father, they dared to attribute these miracles to magical arts. “Indeed,” Tertullian continues, “because they had presumed him to be merely a man on account of his lowliness, it followed that they regarded him as a magician on account of his power, since by a word he expelled demons from men, restored sight to the blind, cleansed lepers . . . . showing himself to be λόγον (“the Word”) of God, that is, that primordial Word, the first-begotten, supported by power and reason” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, [Col. 0858D] p. 400). So it stands in Rigault’s edition. In our manuscripts and the earlier editions, however, it reads: “showing himself to be,” or “showing himself [to be] the Word of God, that is, λόγον (“Word”), that”; and after the word “supported,” they add: “the same who by the Word both made all things and had made them.” But since the earlier words were transposed by Rigault, he ought at least to have given some reason why he deleted the latter ones. Did he reject them because there is a solecism there, or because he thought them useless? But what if the pronoun “the same” refers to λόγον (“the Word”)? Do we not read in John’s Gospel, “The Beginning, who also speak to you” (John, ch. 8, v. 25)? Is there any less of a solecism in these words than in Tertullian’s? Finally, the last words, which we read in all our manuscripts, [Col. 0859A] appear neither superfluous nor of little service to Tertullian’s purpose.

Rigault therefore showed greater diligence when, in the same place, he wished readers to be advised that in certain copies of our Author and of Cyprian the reading is indeed *magnum* (“great”), but that *magum* (“magician”) appears to be the more suitable reading. Quite rightly. For Tertullian later said: “Who, then, is this Christ with his story, if he is a man of ordinary condition, if a magician?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 20, p. 25). We have also observed that in the Harleian manuscript, where *magnum* occurs, two dots have been placed beneath the letter *n*, marking it as superfluous and to be deleted. Tertullian’s passage was transcribed by Cyprian as follows: “When Christ Jesus, in accordance with what had previously been foretold by the prophets, expelled demons from men by the command of his word and voice, [Col. 0859B] restored paralytics, cleansed lepers . . . . . the Jews, who believed him to be only a man on account of the lowliness of his flesh and body, regarded him as a magician because of the unrestrictedness of his power”—and in our Tertullian, “because of his power” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*). In the four more ancient manuscript codices of this work of Cyprian which we have obtained, however, the reading is *magnum*; in others, *magum*. Nevertheless, the latter reading seems preferable to the former, because both authors are discussing Christ’s divine power, by which he performed miracles almost without number and hitherto unheard of, which the most shameless Jewish slanderers had dared to ascribe to his magical arts. We have discussed in the preceding and other dissertations these miracles of Christ, by which he most clearly proved that he was the Son of God and, having assumed a human body, [Col. 0859C] lived a mortal life (Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 9, art. 2 ff.; ch. 18, art. 3).

## ARTICLE III. That Christ, as he himself had foretold, was driven to the cross by Pilate, procurator of Syria, through the violent votes of the Jews, and voluntarily yielded up his spirit with a word; concerning the eclipse of the sun at that time, recorded in the archives, and concerning Christ’s burial

Although so many and such astonishing miracles of Christ most conclusively demonstrate against the Jews, and against all others whatsoever, that he was truly God and man and lived among men, Tertullian nevertheless did not think this sufficient. For he proceeds and demonstrates no less clearly that this truth can be further established and confirmed [Col. 0859D] by other similar miracles performed by Christ before and after his death. Let us therefore follow him and hear him discoursing on Christ’s death:

“Because they were refuted” by “his teaching” (the manuscripts read *qua*, “by which”), he says, “the teachers and chief men of the Jews were so exasperated, especially because an immense multitude was turning toward him, that at last, when he had been brought before Pontius Pilate, who was then governing Syria on behalf of Rome, they extorted from him by the violence of their votes that he be delivered to them for the cross” (the manuscripts add “Jesus”) (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 400).

Cyprian borrowed this from our Author in such a way that he also excerpted some of his words: “Their teachers,” he says, “and chief men”—that is, those Jews whom he refuted by both teaching and wisdom—[Col. 0860A] “inflamed with anger and provoked to indignation, finally handed him over, after he had been detained, to Pontius Pilate, who was then governing Syria on behalf of Rome, demanding his cross and death by violent and persistent votes” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*). That both authors, and later Lactantius, so explicitly call Pilate procurator of Syria is not without difficulty; but we attempted to resolve it in our dissertation on Lactantius’s works. We also explained why the Jews are said to have been exasperated by Christ’s teaching—that is, made harsher—or, as Cyprian says, inflamed with anger and indignation: because he was dissolving and rendering obsolete their law (Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 18, art. 2).

Tertullian adds: “He himself had also foretold that they would act thus. This would be too little” (the pronoun [Col. 0860B] “this” is absent from the manuscripts), “if the prophets had not also foretold it long before” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 401). But Cyprian explains this somewhat more fully and clearly in these words: “He himself had foretold that they would do this, and the testimony of all the prophets had likewise preceded it, declaring beforehand that he must suffer, so that he might not merely experience death but conquer it” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*). Not merely one, but all the Evangelists have testified with the greatest possible unanimity how Christ foretold the death that the Jews would inflict upon him. We have shown elsewhere that it was also announced by the prophets of the Old Testament.

Then our Author continues: “And yet, fastened to the cross, he yielded up his spirit with a word, forestalling the executioner’s office” (Matt., ch. 20, vv. 18, 19; Mark, 10.35; Luke, 10, [Col. 0860C] 33 and 18.31; John, 3, 4 and 8; Acts 12.32; Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 8, art. 2; Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 401). So Rigault indeed prints it. But observe here, as elsewhere, his truly remarkable diligence. For he noted no variant reading other than “and finally” in place of “and yet.” But in all our manuscript copies, as also in those previously printed, the passage reads: “And yet, fastened to the cross, he displayed many distinctive signs peculiar to his death. For with a word he voluntarily yielded up his spirit, forestalling the executioner’s office.” If all these words displeased Rigault, why did he remove them without giving any reason, including the word “voluntarily,” which is found in Cyprian, Tertullian’s intermediary and faithful interpreter, as follows: “Crucified, forestalling the executioner’s office, he voluntarily yielded up his spirit” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, [Col. 0860D]). Could Rigault not have understood from this that these were Tertullian’s own words, which Cyprian had undoubtedly read in his copy of this *Apologeticum*? Had he also forgotten, or could he have ignored, what we observed to have been written similarly by Lactantius: “Suspended and fastened, he cried to the Lord with a loud voice and of his own accord laid down his spirit” (Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 18, art. 3)? Observe in passing that the words “with a loud voice” mean the same thing as those of our Tertullian, “yielded up with a word.” Why, then, did Rigault scrape away other words of the same Tertullian, by which he wished to prove against the Jews and others that Christ, God and man, died of his own accord, as the miracles then performed by him demonstrated?

[Col. 0861A] What Tertullian and, later, Cyprian mean by saying “forestalling the executioner’s office” is explained more plainly and explicitly in these words of Lactantius: “When, fastened to the cross, he had laid down his spirit, the executioners did not think it necessary to break his bones, as their custom required” (Lactantius, bk. 4, *Divine Institutes*, ch. 26). Lipsius discusses this custom; if you please and have the leisure, you may consult him (Lipsius, bk. 2, *On the Cross*, ch. 14, p. 663, vol. 3).

Meanwhile, let us proceed and see what Tertullian adds about the dying Christ: “At the very moment,” he says, “when he was breathing his last, daylight was withdrawn while the sun marked the world. Those who did not know that this too had been foretold concerning Christ naturally thought it an eclipse” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 401). These words, and no more, are read in all the codices, both the earlier printed editions [Col. 0861B] and those written by any hand. But in his edition Rigault added: “failing to discover its cause, they denied it.” Thus he added these words to Tertullian’s text, just as he had removed quite a few words from it in many other places. From what source he took them he does not tell us, nor indeed can we conjecture. He used two manuscripts of this work, the Puteanus and the Pithoeanus, as well as variant readings from the Fulda manuscript. We have before us both the Puteanus, venerable for its antiquity, and the Pithoeanus; and these additional words do not occur in them. Rigault therefore ought to have stated whether they are found among the variant readings of the Fulda copy and why he preferred the authority of that manuscript alone to all the others.

We have discussed elsewhere the eclipse of the sun [Col. 0861C] next mentioned by Tertullian and its prediction (Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 8, art. 2). How greatly it struck not only the Jews but also the Gentiles may be inferred from these subsequent words of our Author: “And yet you have that event which befell the world recorded in your archives” (Tertullian, ibid.). These words are written thus in our manuscripts, as in Rigault’s edition. But who would not praise the singular diligence of that learned man, who there advised us that other editions have *archivis*, “archives,” in place of *arcanis*, “secret records”? He would nevertheless undoubtedly deserve greater praise if, after noting this and certain other similar readings of surely very little weight, he had not omitted many others of far greater importance. For *archiva* means precisely the same as *arcana*, which, on the testimony of Festus and Servius, are so called because they are secret and [Col. 0861D] closed, and everyone is barred from them (Festus, bk. 20, *On the Meaning of Words*; Servius on Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. 1, v. 266).

Our Author moreover asserts that this eclipse of the sun was recorded in the Gentiles’ secret archives because it and other events that occurred before and after Christ’s death were written down by public notaries or others and preserved in those same Gentiles’ archives. What these archives or secret records were—whether those of the Romans or of other Gentiles—may perhaps be gathered from what we shall say shortly concerning Pilate’s letter to the emperor Tiberius about Christ.

Meanwhile, however, we shall note that Rigault once again laid hands upon these subsequent words of Tertullian: [Col. 0862A] “The Jews took him down from the cross, placed him in a tomb, and surrounded it with the great diligence of a military guard” (ibid.). In our manuscript copies and the earlier printed editions, however, it reads: “Then the Jews . . . . . surrounded it with a large military force, with the diligence of a guard.” What harm was done there by the words “with a large military force”? Did not our Author thereby express more forcefully that the number of soldiers who guarded Christ’s tomb with the greatest diligence was not small? But let us proceed with the rest.

## ARTICLE IV. Concerning Christ’s return from death to life, his appearances in Galilee, his commissioning of the disciples to spread the Gospel, his ascension into heaven, and Pilate’s report of all these things to the emperor Tiberius; why the Caesars would have believed in Christ if they had not been necessary to the age

[Col. 0862B]

Tertullian continues and assures us that Christ foretold that on the third day after his death he would be recalled to life (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 401). Quite rightly, as we have explained elsewhere (Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 8, art. 2). Christ kept his promises and returned to life. After he had thus risen, in the words of our same Author, “nothing was found in the tomb except the grave-clothes of the tomb” (ibid.). In our manuscripts and other editions, the reading is “of the buried man,” and indeed more suitably. For *exuviae* is said primarily of human beings, and sometimes of beasts. Hence Lactantius says: “Nothing [Col. 0862C] was found in the tomb except the wrappings in which they had enclosed the enshrouded body” (Lactantius, bk. 4, *Divine Institutes*, ch. 29; Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 19, art. 2).

In that same dissertation of ours on Lactantius’s books, we also showed how he explained what Tertullian had said concerning Christ’s death, burial, and resurrection, to which the following is appended in the same place: “Nor did the risen Christ bring himself before the general public, lest the impious should be freed from error” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 402). There we demonstrated that this manner of speaking, employed not only by Tertullian and Lactantius but also by Cyprian and our sacred writers, is attacked entirely without justification by certain heterodox writers (Dissert. on Lactantius, place cited above, and ch. 17, art. 5).

[Col. 0862D] Tertullian next relates the following concerning Christ after he had been recalled to life: “He spent forty days with certain disciples in Galilee, a region of Judaea, teaching them what they were to teach” (Tertullian, ibid.). What, then? Did Christ spend that entire period in Galilee alone and with only some of his disciples? Did he not appear elsewhere and to all the disciples? But because Tertullian aimed at brevity in this book, he does not deny this; rather, he judged it sufficient to relate what was done in Galilee. Cyprian, however, though following him, wrote less restrictively and more generally: “He appeared to his disciples as he . . . . . had been . . . . . and remained for forty days, so that they might be instructed by him in life-giving precepts and learn [Col. 0863A] what they were to teach” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*). We have discussed these appearances of Christ and the teaching with which he imbued his disciples in other dissertations (Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 19, art. 3; and Dissert. on Caecilius, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*, ch. 4, art. 2).

In those same dissertations we also explained the meaning of these subsequent words of Tertullian: “Thereafter, when he had appointed them to the duty of preaching throughout the world, he was snatched up into heaven, enveloped by a cloud” (Tertullian, place cited). In our copies, however, and in others printed before Rigault, we read: “he was received into heaven.” Could Rigault not endure that form of expression? Why, then, did he not rather use these words of Cyprian: “He was raised into heaven, enveloped by a cloud” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*)? Would he not, if that were permissible, have [Col. 0863B] corrected the teacher better from the disciple? But authors must be left their own words. Otherwise, the genuine works of the ancient writers would be done for.

Tertullian adds that Christ ascended into heaven “with far better grounds than your Proculuses among you Gentiles are accustomed to assert concerning the Romuluses” (Tertullian, place cited). Yet again, in those same manuscripts of ours and printed codices older than Rigault, the reading is: “with far greater truth . . . Proculuses are accustomed to assert concerning Romulus.” Who does not see that this is the preferable and genuine reading of Tertullian? For there he asserts that it is far truer that Christ ascended into heaven than that Romulus did, although “Proculuses”—that is, those who misuse Proculus’s perjury—are accustomed to affirm that they saw Romulus ascending into heaven.

Finally, our Author added: “Pilate reported all these things concerning [Col. 0863C] Christ to Caesar, then Tiberius, and was himself already a Christian according to his conscience. And the Caesars too would have believed concerning Christ if the Caesars had not also been necessary to the age, or if Christians could have been Caesars” (ibid.). Other words written by him earlier mutually shed light upon these. They are as follows: “Tiberius, when it had been reported to him” (our manuscripts and the old editions read “when there had been reported”) “from Syria-Palestine the things which” (Pamelius: “the fact which”) “had revealed there the truth of this divinity” (the same manuscripts and other earlier editions: “had revealed”), “referred it to the senate with the prerogative of his own vote. The senate rejected it because it had not itself approved it. Caesar remained of the same opinion and threatened danger to the accusers of Christians” (Tertullian, ibid., ch. 5, p. 291).

Eusebius transcribed this whole passage of Tertullian, [Col. 0863D] explicitly naming him. But he first relates that it had become customary for provincial governors to report to the emperor whatever new event occurred among them. “For this reason Pilate,” he says, “reported to the emperor Tiberius the resurrection of our Savior Jesus Christ, whose fame was celebrated throughout Palestine; he also indicated that he had heard of many other miracles performed by him, and how, after being restored once more to life, he was already regarded as God by very many. They say that Tiberius referred the matter to the senate, but that the senate spurned such a report—ostensibly, indeed, because it appeared that the senate’s authority had not been awaited in the matter, although there was an ancient law among the Romans that no one should be regarded as a god without a decree of the senate; but in reality because [Col. 0864A] the proclamation of the saving divine doctrine did not require human authority and assistance. Therefore, when the senate had rejected the report concerning the Savior, Tiberius nevertheless persisted in his former opinion and is said to have attempted nothing harsher against the teaching of Christ” (Eusebius, bk. 2, *Ecclesiastical History*, ch. 2, p. 40). Thus Valesius translated Eusebius’s Greek words, which, because they are somewhat lengthy, we do not transcribe here.

Eusebius then translated Tertullian’s words into Greek as follows: Τιβέριος οὖν ἀγγελθέντος αὐτῷ ἐκ Παλαιστίνης τοῦ δόγματος τούτου, ἔνθα πρῶτον ἤρξατο, τῇ συγκλήτῳ ἀνεκοινώσατο (“Tiberius, then, when this doctrine had been reported to him from Palestine, where it first began, communicated it to the Senate”). Δῆλος ὢν ἐκείνοις ὡς τῷ δόγματι ἀρέσκεται· ἡ δὲ σύγκλητος, ἐπεὶ οὐκ αὐτὴ ἐδεδοκιμάκει, ἀπώσατο (“making it clear to them that he approved the doctrine; but the Senate, since it had not itself approved it, rejected it”). Ὁ δὲ ἐν τῇ αὐτοῦ ἀποφάσει ἔμεινεν, ἀπειλήσας θάνατον τοῖς τῶν χρίστιανῶν κατηγόροις (“But he remained firm in his decision, threatening the accusers of the Christians with death”). Valesius rendered these in the same Latin words that, as we have seen, appear [Col. 0864B] in our codices and in editions before Rigault. In his notes, however, approving Rigault’s emendation, he judges the Greek words of Eusebius—or of another translator, as he himself supposes—to be corrupt. But if he had detected the changes made by Rigault in Tertullian’s text, he might perhaps rather have endorsed the Greek writer.

Whatever the case may be, Tertullian’s meaning is sufficiently clear from either reading, and no difficulty can arise concerning it except in the words by which Tiberius is said already to have been a Christian “according to his conscience.” For in the same place Tertullian said that the Caesars did not believe because they were Caesars. But there the verb “to believe” seems to mean nothing other than the public profession of the Christian religion. Tiberius was therefore a Christian according to his conscience, [Col. 0864C] but he did not dare to profess it openly.

But why, you will ask, did our Author add: “The Caesars would have believed if they had not been necessary to the age”? Rigault answers that this was said because, as we shall see below, they were persuaded that the world—which they did not wish to be destroyed so soon—would be overthrown together with the Roman Empire. But what necessity for the life of one Caesar or another can, I ask you, be inferred from this error? Could Tertullian not have meant that the Caesars were necessary to the age—that is, bound by such necessity to the customs of the age and of its peoples, and especially to its established religious institutions, that they could not undermine or change them without seditions and even the imminent danger of the overthrow of both their lives and the empire?

## ARTICLE V. That all the things said concerning Christ as God and man are true; that no one may lawfully lie about his religion; that Christians, without any ambiguity or dissimulation, openly and even amid the most dreadful tortures cried out that they worshiped God through Christ; and that this could not be condemned by the Jews, who worshiped God through Moses, nor by the Gentiles, who had learned through Orpheus, Musaeus, Melampus, and Trophonius to worship false gods

[Col. 0864D]

All the things which we have thus far seen written by Tertullian concerning Christ, whether as God and the Son of God or as true man, he affirms to be most true with such assurance that he leaves no room, [Col. 0865A] not even the slightest, for doubting them. For he addresses the pagans themselves as follows: “This is the order of our teaching; this is the account we have published of both our sect and our name, together with its author” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 403). By *census* he means an enumeration, review, and explanation. In this matter, he says, let no one suppose that there is anything else whatsoever which I have wished to conceal or dissemble. For nothing can be truer than what I have related, because, he says, no one may lawfully lie about his religion.

This principle had been planted so deeply in Christian hearts that they preferred to be tortured and killed by any of the most exquisitely devised torments rather than, when questioned about their religion, deny or dissemble anything—indeed, even merely their Christian name. Accordingly, Justin Martyr, as we have said elsewhere, similarly [Col. 0865B] told the Gentiles: “Consider, ὅτι ἐκ τοῦ εφ` ἡμῖν εἶναι ἀρνεῖσθαι ἐξεταζομένους, οὐ βουλόμεθα ζῇν ψευδολογοῦντες (“that, although it is in our power to deny it when questioned, we do not wish to live by speaking falsely”) that it is in our power to deny it when we are questioned; but we do not wish to live falsely”—that is, we do not wish to live by lying about or disguising our religion (Justin, *First Apology to Antoninus*, p. 57; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 357). They considered it such a sacrilege to answer ambiguously when questioned about their religion that, as Tertullian himself also testifies, even those who most feared martyrdom preferred to be tortured and killed rather than, in confessing the three persons of the most holy Trinity, speak of several gods.

If you ask why every lie or any dissimulation whatsoever concerning religion is utterly condemned, Tertullian will answer that “he who says that he worships something other than [Col. 0865C] what he does worship denies what he worships, and transfers worship and honor to another; and by transferring it, he no longer worships what he has denied” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 403). But Rigault chose to delete the words “and honor.” Who will deny that they are not useless there? For worship and honor are not altogether synonymous; and even if they were, no one would deny that Tertullian could have used both. Yet this is of the least importance, except for noting Rigault’s audacity. Of far greater weight, certainly, is our Author’s reasoning: one who says, for example, that he worships Jupiter, while he worships another God, denies that Jupiter is a god, transfers the worship and honor of that same Jupiter to another, and thus, by transferring them, does not worship Jupiter himself, as he claimed, but denies that he is a god.

[Col. 0865D] Therefore we Christians, he says, casting aside absolutely all dissimulation, fraud, and ambiguity, openly declare—and, while you pagans torture us, cry aloud, lacerated and bloody—“We worship God through Christ” (ibid., p. 403 ff.), through whom, or under whose teaching, we learned who the true God is whom all ought to worship. “But Christ,” the pagans objected, “was a man.” Let it be so, Tertullian replies; Christians should not for that reason have been tortured and killed by the pagans, unless the latter condemned Jews, Greeks, and even Romans in the same manner. For “the Jews,” he says, “learned through the man Moses to worship God” (ibid., p. 23). In his books Moses taught them by what method and rites God ought to be worshiped by them.

[Col. 0866A] “Among the Greeks,” our Author continues, “Orpheus in Pieria, Musaeus at Athens, Melampus at Argos, and Trophonius in Boeotia bound men by initiations into the worship of false gods” (ibid.); that is, they tied and obligated them. We have shown elsewhere that the rites and festivals of Liber were introduced by Orpheus and were therefore called Orphic (Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 30, art. 3). In another place we cited the words of Diodorus Siculus, who relates that Hercules came to Athens to be initiated into the mysteries of the Eleusinian Mother, over which Musaeus, the son of Orpheus, was προεστηκώς (“standing at their head”), the presiding official, and thus taught how men were to be bound by them (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. 2, ch. 35, p. 1280).

Moreover, as we noted in our dissertation on the works of Lactantius, [Col. 0866B] the same Diodorus Siculus committed to writing that Melampus brought from Egypt into Greece the orgies consecrated to Bacchus (Dissert. just cited; Diodorus, bk. I, *Library*, p. 61). He also appended a fabulous narrative concerning Saturn and the Titans and the passions of the gods, which, although it is not approved by everyone, nevertheless contributes not a little to our Tertullian’s purpose.

Trophonius, moreover, was said to be the son of Erginus—or rather of Apollo—whose oracle, as Tertullian says elsewhere, stood in Boeotia (Tertullian, place cited, and *On the Soul*, ch. 46). Pausanias, Lucian, Cicero, Philostratus, Erasmus, and others [Col. 0866C] cited by us in the preceding dissertation on the works of Arnobius have handed down much more concerning him and the temples of the gods, especially that of Apollo at Delphi, built by this same Trophonius (Pausanias, bk. 9, *On Boeotia*, p. 311; Lucian, *Dialogues of Menippus*, etc., p. 116 ff.; Cicero, bk. 1, *Tusculan Disputations*, p. 134; Philostratus, bk. VIII, *Life of Apollonius*, p. 420 ff.; Erasmus, *Adages*, cent. 1, adage 77; Dissert. on Arnobius, ch. 24, art. 2; Dissert. on Lactantius, ch. 30, art. 4). Who does not see that their testimony proves more than sufficiently that men were bound by that man, and by the others mentioned by Tertullian, through initiations into the gods instituted by other men? Finally, he added that Numa Pompilius burdened the Romans with most elaborate institutions. We have demonstrated how true this is.

Tertullian therefore rightly concludes from these things that Christ, even if he had been only a man, was no less entitled than they to devise some divinity [Col. 0866D] which he might teach men to worship. But Christ was not only man; he possesses true divinity together with his eternal Father, a possession, as our Author says, of his own, because it belongs to him just as it belongs to his Father (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 21, p. 404). For he did not, like Numa Pompilius, civilize *rupices*—that is, woodland-dwellers, wild and barbarous men, as Tertullian himself explains elsewhere (the same, *On the Pallium*, ch. 4, and *On the Soul*, ch. 6)—by instilling terror of false gods; rather, he opened the eyes of the polished and urbanized to the knowledge of the true God—that is, gave them the ability to see or know the true God. For in the same sense he said elsewhere of the swallow: “It knows how to open the eyes of its chicks again with its own celandine” (the same, *On Repentance*, ch. 12). Therefore [Col. 0867A] Cyprian, imitating him in his customary manner, teaches, like Tertullian, that Christ’s disciples were dispersed throughout the world so that they might open the eyes of the blind and ignorant to the knowledge of the truth (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*). Indeed, Pontius, Cyprian’s deacon, wrote of him in his life: “The second birth had not yet opened the eyes of the new man with the full splendor of divine light” (Pontius, *Life of Cyprian*, near the beginning). Who, then, from what has thus far been discussed, does not perceive how justly Tertullian concludes that Christians were most unjustly tortured and killed by the pagans and Jews because, after recognizing the true God through Christ and professing his religion, they wished neither to deny nor even to dissemble either?

# CHAPTER X. Concerning Angels and Demons

[Col. 0867B]

## ARTICLE I. That the names of angels and demons were known to Gentile poets and philosophers, particularly Socrates and Plato, and also to magicians and to the common mass of uneducated people, who frequently used the name “demon” as a curse; why that name was so detestable; that all angels were created perfect by God, but the wicked corrupted themselves voluntarily; concerning their separation from God and their marriage with the daughters of men, from which a still more corrupt race of demons is said to have arisen

Tertullian passes to another doctrine of the Christian religion concerning angels and demons (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 22, p. 404). He endeavors to explain it [Col. 0867C] with all the greater care because the pagans were more firmly persuaded that demons, lurking beneath the images and names of the dead (ibid., ch. 23, p. 404)—that is, of false gods—produced signs, miracles, and oracles “by which they contrived belief in their divinity” (ibid.); that is, by which the demons endeavored to persuade men that the gods worshiped by the pagans were true gods and, consequently, that their religion was true. We have already seen this error of the pagans, upon which their entire religion principally rested, rooted out by Minucius Felix, Lactantius, and others (Dissert. on Minucius, ch. 18, art. 1, and on Lactantius, ch. 20, art. 4). But because these writers drew many things from this book of our Author and from it, as from a pure spring, watered their little gardens, his opinion must therefore be examined by us with greater care.

[Col. 0867D] First, therefore, he teaches that angels and demons are certain spiritual substances whose names were not unknown to the Gentiles, whether poets or philosophers (place cited), as we have also shown elsewhere. Among the philosophers he specifically names Plato and Socrates, who had an attendant demon, “plainly,” he says, “dissuading him from the good” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 22, p. 406). In another book he had said: “A demonic spirit found Socrates while he was still a boy” (the same, *On the Soul*, ch. 39, p. 341). We treated this demon of Socrates in the cited dissertation on the *Octavius* of Minucius and in the other works cited there (Dissert. on the *Octavius* of Minucius, ch. 18, art. 1). Moreover, in the same place we made clear that, as Tertullian further notes, magicians are witnesses to both names—that is, Angel and Demon [Col. 0868A] (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 22, p. 405).

He further suggests another reason why demons were recognized not only by these people but by pagans of every kind: “Even the uneducated common people,” he says, “frequently use it as a curse” (ibid.). So Rigault; but two of our more recent manuscripts read “in use,” the more ancient manuscripts “in use for curses,” and the oldest editions “for use in curses.” Our Author’s meaning is that when the pagan common people spoke evil of others and cursed them, they were accustomed to call them demons or invoke the name of the Demon. For he continues: “For they also pronounce Satan, the prince of this evil race, in accordance with the soul’s own consciousness, [Col. 0868B] with the same word of execration.” In the old manuscript and printed codices, the reading is “of sacrament” rather than “of execration,” but this is undoubtedly due solely to copyists’ carelessness. For in another book Tertullian explains his meaning clearly enough:

“When we affirm that demons exist—surely as if we did not also prove it, since we alone drive them from bodies—some adherent of Chrysippus mocks us. Your own execrations answer both that they exist and that they endure the abhorrence expressed in your curse. You call a man a demon because of his uncleanness, wickedness, insolence, or any stain which we attribute to demons, or because he is troublesome enough to compel hatred. Finally, in every aversion, rejection, and detestation, you pronounce the name of Satan, whom we call the angel of wickedness, the author of every error, and the corrupter of the whole age” (the same, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, ch. 3).

It was therefore the pagans’ custom, when speaking evil of others [Col. 0868C] and cursing them, to call them demons, or to devote and execrate them with dreadful curses in the name of the Demon and Satan. If you desire another witness to this custom, you have Augustine, who further advises us that wherever the name Demon or Demons occurs in Sacred Scripture, it signifies evil spirits. Here are his very words:

“But we, speaking as Scripture speaks, according to which we are Christians, read that some angels are good and some bad, but never that good angels are Demons. Wherever this name is found in those Writings, whether they are called Demons or Demon-spirits, none but evil spirits are signified. And peoples everywhere have followed this custom of speech to such an extent that even among those who are called pagans and contend that many gods and demons ought to be worshiped, [Col. 0868D] scarcely anyone is so literate and learned that he would dare say in praise even of his own servant, ‘You have a Demon’; but if he wished to say this to anyone, he could not doubt that he would be understood in no other way than as intending a curse” (Augustine, bk. 9, *City of God*, ch. 19, p. 233).

He later added: “So detestable is the name of demons that we must by every means remove it from the holy Angels” (ibid., ch. 23, p. 236). Augustine, therefore, like our Tertullian, distinguishes a twofold class of angels: the one good, who are called Angels without qualification; the other the demons, whose detestable name always signifies evil spirits.
