The third opinion interprets this of the HOLY SPIRIT; toward it [Col. 1145D] I am impelled by the far greater number of the holy Fathers. “It is more true,” says St. Basil, *Homily II on the Hexaemeron*, “and has been approved by our predecessors, that that spirit was called the HOLY SPIRIT OF GOD,” etc. And St. Ambrose, Book I of the *Hexaemeron*, chapter 8, says: “We, agreeing with the judgment of the saints and the faithful, understand the Holy Spirit,” etc. St. Jerome likewise affirms, in *Hebrew Traditions on Genesis*, that this is said not of the spirit of the world, but of the Holy Spirit, who is also called the Vivifier of all things from the beginning. And he confirms the very same thing in the *Letter to Oceanus*, saying: “The unformed and invisible matter was being oppressed by the vastness of the abysses and by shapeless darkness; the Spirit of GOD alone was borne over the waters after the manner of a charioteer, and was bringing the nascent world to birth under the figure of Baptism.” But what of St. Augustine? Here too, in Book XIII of the *Confessions*, chapter 3, he excellently discloses his thought: “I now grasped,” he says, “the FATHER in the name of God, who made these things”—heaven and earth—“and the SON in the name of the Beginning, in whom he made these things; and, seeking the TRINITY, I sought it in [Col. 1146A] the words of his Scriptures; and behold, your SPIRIT was borne over the waters,” etc. Hence it is not surprising that, on the basis of so great a consensus of the Fathers, the Church too adopted this opinion in the Blessing of the Baptismal Font, singing as follows: “O GOD, whose Spirit was borne over the waters amid the very beginnings of the world, so that even then the nature of water might conceive the power of sanctification,” etc.

But what prevents us from combining these three opinions, as it were, into one? Let us therefore say that by the SPIRIT OF GOD is understood, first of all, wind or elemental air; that this air, set in motion by a singular power of GOD, flowed through the waters and was, as it were, incorporated into them, and thus rendered them fertile for the generation of living things; and that the Holy Spirit, invisibly concealed beneath this air, in some fashion initiated those same waters into the power of one day sanctifying the soul through Baptism.

**Was borne.** The Hebrew expression that the Septuagint renders by ἐπεφέρετο, “was borne over,” is said to possess a somewhat more arcane significance among the Hebrews. Indeed, St. Basil, in the *Hexaemeron*, chapter 1, adducing the testimony of a certain Syrian interpreter [Col. 1146B]—commonly believed to have been St. Ephrem, Basil’s distinguished friend—speaks as follows: “That expression ἐπεφέρετο, ‘WAS BORNE OVER,’ he says”—that is, the Syrian—“is interpreted as συνέθαλπε, ‘WARMED AND CHERISHED,’ by analogy with ἐζωογόνει, ‘ANIMATED,’ after the likeness of ἐπωαζούσης ὄρνιθος, ‘A HEN BROODING OVER HER EGGS,’ and imparting a certain vital power τοῖς ὑποθαλπομένοις, ‘TO THE THINGS BEING WARMED BENEATH HER,’ namely, those eggs.” Diodorus of Tarsus agrees, discussing the aforesaid passage of Genesis as follows: “For the Hebrew expression [Hebrew word absent in the supplied text], corresponding to the word ἐπεφέρετο, is intended to signify that, just as a hen broods over her eggs, gently touching them with her wings as though to animate them, so too the Spirit was borne over the waters, imbuing them with vital warmth.” St. Jerome confirms this very point in the *Letter to Oceanus* and in the book *Hebrew Traditions*, as does St. Ambrose, Book I of the *Hexaemeron*, chapter 8; this may be seen more fully in Noël Alexandre, vol. I, First Age of the World, Dissertation I, Article 2, Proposition 2. Finally, St. Augustine explains it still more subtly in *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*, chapter 4: “Just as the craftsman’s [Col. 1146C] will is borne over the wood,” etc., “or even over the members of his own body, which he moves in order to work.” And St. Thomas, Augustine’s faithful follower, likewise interprets it thus in Part I, question 66, article 1.

**Substance.** Supply the omitted verb “is.”

***Dignationem***, in place of *dignitatem*.

**Seat.** Thus Tertullian anticipated those Fathers mentioned above with the words “The Spirit of God,” and no less clearly suggests the same opinion in Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 26—although, if we wish to speak the truth, he does so with little consistency. For in the book *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 32, discussing Amos 4:13—in the wording of the Septuagint—στερεῶν βροντὴν, “who makes thunder firm,” καὶ κτίζων πνεῦμα, “and creates spirit,” etc., he argues as follows: “He shows that spirit to have been created which was assigned to the created earth, which was borne over the waters, the balancer, breather, and animator of the universe—not, as some suppose, that the SPIRIT OF GOD himself is meant, for the waters would not have been capable of sustaining their Lord—but he speaks of that spirit of which the winds also consist,” etc. [Col. 1146D] Either, therefore, he wrote this forgetful of his former opinion, or, to gratify his Montanus—for by then, alas, he had already surrendered himself entirely to him—who is reported by some to have sacrilegiously vaunted himself as the Holy Spirit, and indeed is greeted by Tertullian himself with the title “Paraclete” in *On Monogamy*, chapter 1, he perhaps struck out his former opinion.

**Liquid.** The liquid element of air.

**Always.** That is, from the very beginning of its creation.

**Joyful.** It is opposed to the gloomy abyss.

**Simple.** Of a substance uniform throughout.

**Placed beneath.** So that upon it the Holy Spirit might be carried over the waters after the manner of a charioteer, as St. Jerome explains in Letter 83 to Oceanus. See above.

**Modulating.** *To modulate* means to arrange a thing harmoniously according to a fixed mode, or according to a more elegant pattern and measure. Hence the waters are here called “modulating,” because [Col. 1147A] from them, as though they were themselves assigning form and furnishing the prescribed patterns for each species of things, this universe was arranged and coordinated.

**Was established by God.** Others read *a DEO* instead of *DEO*. Yet there is no reason entirely to condemn the ordinary reading. For *DEO* may be understood, according to Greek idiom, as “through GOD,” while *constitit* may mean “stood firmly,” or, rather, “was constituted.”

**Separated in the midst**, that is, separated in the middle by this very firmament. For thus the sacred text says in Genesis 1:6–7: “Let there be a firmament IN THE MIDST OF THE WATERS, AND LET THERE BE A DIVISION”; or, as our Vulgate has it, “let it divide the waters from the waters. And GOD made the firmament and divided the waters,” etc. Genesis 1:6–7.

**Might suspend**, namely, in the center of the universe; for there GOD “suspended the mass of the earth with three fingers.” Isaiah 40:12.

**Adaptable**, tractable or suitable for receiving figures of this kind. [Col. 1147B]

**Moist**, in place of *succulent*, as Varro likewise says in Book II, chapter 11: “Freshly shorn wool was called *succida* from the sweat of the sheep.”

**Into mud.** By Greek idiom, *limo* stands for “into mud,” into which the earth had been softened by the water remaining upon it.

**Or grace.** As Cicero says in Book II, Letter 3 to Quintus: “The other things of this kind in our affairs are full of dignity and grace,” that is, of charm.

**Natures.** *Ingenium* is also said of inanimate things, at least improperly, to mean a force innate or internally generated within them, which elsewhere we are also accustomed to call their disposition. I suspect, however, that *ingenita*, “innate qualities,” should be read instead of *ingenia*, “natures.”

**Instrument**, for almost infinitely many uses and effects.

**To appear** (*parere*), in place of *apparere*, as our Author likewise says in *On the Shows*, chapter 8: “Before these, three altars appear for three gods.” See also Book I *Against Marcion*, chapter 15, and elsewhere.

[Col. 1147C] **Eternal.** From the context I strongly suspect that *terrenam*, “earthly,” should be read. Yet I think *aeternam*, “eternal,” can be tolerated, so that the meaning would be: “upon whose command, as it were, eternal life depends,” etc., since water acquired the power of sanctifying, in a certain fashion, even at the very beginning of its existence, as was said above and will be explained more fully in part in the following chapter. Moreover, that water is a symbol of eternal life was foreshown by Christ himself to the Samaritan woman in John 4:10 and following, especially verse 14, when he said: “The water that I shall give him will become in him a fountain of water springing up into eternal life.”

**In the heavenly**, that is, through something heavenly—namely, the Sacrament of Baptism—as a means supremely necessary for our salvation, which we finally attain in heaven.

## Notes on Chapter IV

**By its very condition**, that is, through that initial power of sanctifying which it possessed within itself after receiving it soon after its creation. [Col. 1147D]

**Over the waters.** See the notes “Spirit” and “was borne” on the preceding chapter.

**About to reform.** Ibid., note “To be reformed.”

**Borrowed.** Thus also in Exodus 3:2 Mount Horeb, on which the Lord appeared to Moses in a flame of fire from the midst of the bush, acquired a certain singular sanctity from the presence of GOD. GOD therefore said to him: “Remove the footwear from your feet, for the place on which you stand is HOLY GROUND.” Ibid., verse 5. A similar example occurs in Joshua 3:5, and several others elsewhere.

**May seize.** May acquire, as though drawing it to itself by a certain force.

**Easy to enter and settle in.** This is a Hellenism; for a Greek would say εἰσδῦναι καὶ ἐγκαθέζεσθαι πρόχειρον, whereas a Latin would say, “easily penetrating and settling within.”

**Subtlety.** He seems to employ a certain corporeal analogy adapted to the senses, drawn from particular oils [Col. 1148A] and dyes that penetrate even the most solid substances. But how a spiritual substance or quality is received in a corporeal subject is indeed much more difficult for philosophers to explain.

**Conceived**, perhaps in place of *began*; or the sense is: “it conceived the power of sanctifying,” that is, received it into itself.

**In that respect in which**, that is, insofar as it is indeed the genus, etc.

**In the species** (*in specie*). Perhaps *in speciem*, “into the species,” should be read.

**May be washed away.** Some codices have *deluatur*; for the ancients used *deluere* for “to wash away,” and in Festus they said *deluvium* instead of *diluvium*.

**In the Tiber.** At Rome, namely, which he designates by the Tiber, the river flowing through that city. Tertullian assumes, as a wholly undoubted fact, that Peter was at Rome and suffered there together with Paul—not only in this passage but also elsewhere. Thus in Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 5, he says: “We see also what the Romans close at hand proclaim, to whom Peter and Paul left the Gospel sealed with [Col. 1148B] their blood.” Parallel to this are the words he has in *On Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 36: “The Church of the Romans likewise declares that Clement was ordained by Peter.” Where could this have occurred but at Rome? For Clement was Roman by birth, and did not leave Rome until, already Bishop of the Romans, he was driven into exile by Trajan. Again, in *Scorpiace*, chapter 15, he speaks as follows: “Nero was the first to stain the rising faith with blood at Rome. Then Peter is girded by another when he is bound to the cross; then Paul obtains the birthright of Roman citizenship when he is reborn there through the nobility of martyrdom.” Thus, by the beginning of the third century, this tradition had already become firmly established in the Carthaginian Church and, indeed, throughout Africa at large, although no one—not even any of the heretics whom Tertullian had so sharply and everywhere assailed—dared to attempt to overthrow it.

Nor was Carthage so isolated from the Romans that events of some distinction that had recently occurred could be conveyed back and forth only belatedly and by uncertain rumor. [Col. 1148C] For an entire colony of Roman citizens had been transferred there by Julius Caesar; Africa itself had been committed by his successor Augustus to the authority of the Senate, and was thereafter administered as a Roman province by annual proconsuls; and it was constantly frequented by ships from Italy because grain was exported almost daily—for it is exceedingly well known from Tacitus, Book III of the *Histories*, chapter 48, and from very many writers of Roman history, both Greek and Latin, that Africa was called the granary of Italy in those days—as well as for the sake of other merchandise. I therefore ask and adjure: on whose authority would not only Carthage, but all Africa, have allowed so great and so persistent a falsehood to be smeared upon it? Or how could it have been so negligent in investigating the truth itself more carefully, when innumerable opportunities existed, that not even Tertullian—elsewhere by no means sparing of insults against the Roman Church, especially after he had fled as an impious deserter to his Montanus—would have torn the lying mask from that tradition? Surely he would not have failed to charge the Roman clergy [Col. 1148D] with this imposture, had he ever been led even to suspect it. Or would not the proconsuls themselves—and foremost among them Clodius Macer, who, according to Tacitus, *Histories* I, chapter 7, governed Africa during Nero’s final years, or those who succeeded him, Vipsanius Apronianus, Vigellius Saturninus, and others, and especially Scapula, the persecutor of the African Church, to whom Tertullian had written so spiritedly on behalf of the Christians—have hesitated, once its falsity was detected, to uproot a tradition so glorious to the Christians and so powerful in confirming them in their faith? And then, if Peter died elsewhere, is he alone among the Apostles to be believed to have suffered martyrdom in so obscure a place that not even a rumor of it reached Tertullian, or anyone else before or after him, whether living at Carthage, at Rome, or anywhere whatsoever? Or, finally, is the Roman Church alone of such unknown origin that, although the first founders of almost all the other churches, even far more obscure ones, are identified from [Col. 1149A] at least probable knowledge, all antiquity was deceived in assigning the origin of the Roman Church?

Accordingly, this tradition of the Carthaginian Church did not arise there rashly or through crafty fabrication. Rather, it was transmitted to posterity by all those who had seen Peter at Rome, had heard him preaching, and had been eyewitnesses to his martyrdom; and it must be believed beyond doubt to have been brought to Carthage, and thence to the other African churches, together with the Gospel that those churches had received from the Roman Church, as St. Augustine testifies in Letter 162. It was so firmly established throughout all the churches everywhere in the world that it could never thereafter have wavered against the testimony of so many witnesses and public monuments, stones, churches, and so forth.

You see, therefore, that even this testimony of Tertullian alone—to say nothing now of the wholly unanimous agreement of the other most ancient Fathers, some of whom were also contemporaries of Peter himself—is sufficient to trace this tradition back to its very sources and to break the audacity of the sectarians [Col. 1149B], by which they now drag into controversy a fact so manifest that no other heretic or schismatic before the sixteenth century of the Church ever dared to contradict it in the least. Our modern men alone are evidently so keen-nosed—or rather, so snub-nosed—that they now begin to scent what none of the ancients could smell out. And if it pleased me to argue against such men at greater length, or if doing so were worthwhile, I would bring forward in a long array even those who, though most alien from our sacred rites, otherwise enjoy the greatest authority among them: Pearson, Grotius, Ussher, Chamier, Blondel, Pierre du Moulin, and others, by whose names alone the few others of recent birth could easily be overwhelmed. For now it is enough to have adduced even two of them, Samuel Basnage and William Cave. The former, although in his *Political-Ecclesiastical Annals*, at the year 42, no. 8, he obstinately denies that Peter was Bishop of Rome, nevertheless, at A.D. 64, no. 9, unwaveringly affirms that he was at Rome in these [Col. 1149C] words: “There has never been any tradition surrounded by a greater number of witnesses; thus Peter’s coming to the City cannot be doubted without overturning all the monuments of history.” The latter, in *Ecclesiastical Writers: Literary History*, p. 5, says: “That Peter was at Rome and held a see there for some time, we confidently affirm together with the whole assembly of antiquity.” Then, after producing witnesses beyond all exception and drawn from the remotest antiquity, and after citing St. Ignatius of Antioch, a disciple of St. Peter himself, Papias of Hierapolis, Irenaeus, a disciple of St. Polycarp the Apostolic Man, Dionysius of Corinth, Tertullian, Gaius the Roman presbyter, and Origen, he proceeds as follows: “After so many venerable names and such illustrious monuments of earliest antiquity, who will call into doubt a fact handed down so clearly and consistently? Surely, if so dense a cloud of witnesses and so harmonious a judgment of the ancients may be plucked apart according to the whim of any individual intellect, the memory of the first centuries will be utterly done away with, and no one will be permitted to know anything beyond [Col. 1149D] his own age.” So it is that certain of our heterodox opponents, while everywhere thrusting their own judgments forward as truth, and while scraping together from every quarter so many insulting fables against our religion—some of which they themselves also invent—demand that immediate credence be given to them, although even the whole of antiquity is insufficient to obtain credence from them in return. But since we have thus far been carried almost wholly beyond the limits of our undertaking, anyone who desires still more will find it abundantly in our Calmet’s special dissertation on St. Peter’s Roman journey.

**Philip**, who is more commonly believed to have been not the Apostle, but one of the first seven deacons elected in Acts 6:5.

**Fortuitous**, that is, presented by chance.

**Repaid.** This statement certainly has the flavor of irony. Moreover, Tertullian does not here intend to make the Baptism conferred by Peter entirely equal to John’s Baptism, which had been only a preliminary [Col. 1150A] figure and, as it were, a forerunner of the former—for below, in chapter 10, he establishes a manifest and more than single difference between the two. He wished only to confirm that every kind of water diffused throughout our world, since it is entirely the same as that primordial water, has now likewise been adapted by the Holy Spirit to confer salvation upon human beings, in whatever manner this may finally occur. Yet it certainly must not be denied that our Baptism, being a sacrament instituted by Christ himself, possessed a far more efficacious power than John’s Baptism, which did not of itself confer the remission of sins, but merely aroused people to acts of repentance through which that remission was effected. On this subject our interpreters and theologians may be consulted throughout, and we shall confirm it more fully in its proper place.

**Prerogative.** That is, sharing, as though by transfusion, in that privilege and in the character of that primitive water.

**Sacrament of sanctification**, or that hidden and spiritual [Col. 1150B] power of sanctifying the soul.

**God having been invoked.** This invocation is made in the blessing of the baptismal font. From this passage alone it can be sufficiently gathered that this rite was already employed in Tertullian’s time and probably descended from the Apostles themselves.

**Is over the waters**, that is, is above the waters and, as it were, floats upon them.

**Imbibe.** Whether this power is truly real and physical, impressed upon the waters by the Holy Spirit and physically causing baptismal grace, or merely moral, is vigorously disputed by the Scholastics in their treatises on Baptism.

**For a simple act**—the accusative *ad simplicem actum* in place of the dative *simplici actui*—namely, the act of washing.

**In place of filth**, that is, as a counterpart to filth, or in the stead of filth.

**In the spirit**, or in the soul; for sin is committed by the will alone and leaves in the soul a spiritual stain, which the Scholastics commonly call habitual sin.

**Liability.** The flesh or body certainly contracts no guilt properly so called, nor does that tinder by which the flesh [Col. 1150C] lusts against the spirit—Galatians 5:17—possess the character of sin except in an improper sense. Yet because, as they again say in the schools, actions belong to concrete subjects, and consequently it is not the soul alone but the whole human being—as the concrete subject consisting of body and soul—that sins in the flesh and through the flesh, the liability both to guilt and to punishment is in some fashion extended to the body or flesh itself, namely because of the soul’s intimate union with the body. Thus it is not the body by itself and for its own sake that is punished, but the soul in the body and through the body, and therefore the whole human being who sinned.

**Because of its ministry.** The things said above are therefore chiefly true of carnal sins, or of those accomplished by the flesh, however it may finally cooperate.

**Because by medicinal waters**, that is, by waters imbued with medicinal power, such as natural hot springs and mineral waters. Elsewhere, though more rarely, *medicatus* is also used to mean “poisoned,” as Suetonius relates that the emperor Claudius was removed from the living by a poisoned mushroom, and Silius, Book VI, sings of missiles treated with poison.

**Intervention.** He alludes to the Sheep Pool, concerning [Col. 1150D] which see John 5 and our Author more explicitly in the following chapter.

**Is washed clean**, namely, from that guilt which it incurred through the body and through corporeal and sensible pleasures.

**Is cleansed.** That is, from the stain it contracted in the soul intimately joined to it.

## Notes on Chapter V

**Of spiritual things**, that is, devoid of all understanding, etc., and discerning only carnal things. Perhaps this sentence might be punctuated as follows: “alien to all understanding, they supply the power, or virtue, of spiritual things with the same efficacy,” etc.

**Supply**, or attribute.

**Empty**, that is, vacant and destitute of every power of this kind.

**Are initiated.** *To initiate*, a verb used especially of sacred rites, means to consecrate, to imbue with sacred things, or rather to render suitable for them. “There were,” says Casaubon on Athenaeus, Book VI, chapter 15, “certain stages to be observed in participation in the mysteries. The first [Col. 1151A] of all was ἡ κάθαρσις, ‘purification’”—namely, a purification to be performed by certain washings—“then followed the lesser mysteries, by which they were instructed and prepared for the greater; the final act was that of the Greater Mysteries and what was called ἐποπτεία,” namely, the viewing of certain secret sacred objects, to which the initiated were not admitted until a full year had elapsed, as Plutarch relates in his book *On the Obsolescence of Oracles*.

What has been said thus far is admirably confirmed by Clement of Alexandria, who argues as follows in Book V of the *Stromata*: “Not without reason, therefore, in the mysteries practiced among the Greeks, τὰ καθάρσια, ‘purifications’ or ‘lustrations,’ hold the first place, just as among the barbarians τὸ λοῦτρον, ‘the washing.’ After these come μικρὰ μυστήρια, ‘the Lesser Mysteries,’ which possess a certain foundation of instruction and προπαρασκευῆς, ‘preparation’ or ‘initiation,’ for the things to come,” etc. And in Book VII of the *Stromata* he says that before the transmission of the mysteries or sacraments, καθαρμούς τινας προσάγειν τοῖς μυεῖσθαι μέλλουσιν ἀξιοῦσιν, “they consider it necessary to apply certain expiations or purifications [Col. 1151B] to those who are to be initiated into the mysteries,” etc. Cicero, moreover, in Book II of *On the Laws*, says that those mysteries are called *initia*, “initiations.” Indeed, even among us Christians it is commonly said that someone is “initiated into Holy Orders,” and the first tonsure is called a kind of initiation into them.

**Of some Isis.** Io, daughter of Inachus, after giving birth to Epaphus, her son by Jupiter, was changed into a cow through the agency of the jealous Juno. After recovering her human form, she married Apis, king of the Egyptians, under the new name of Isis, and, because of the very great benefits she bestowed upon that nation, was after her death enrolled by the Egyptians among the gods together with her husband. Some believe that this thoroughly fabulous goddess—because *Isis* in the Cimbrian language means “IS, IS”—represented the infinite WISDOM OF GOD, who says of herself in Proverbs 8:14: “Counsel and equity ARE mine, prudence IS mine, strength IS mine.” Plutarch, in his book *On Osiris and Isis*, certainly records that the following inscription was once found in a temple of Isis:

[Col. 1151C] Ἐγώ εἰμι πᾶν τὸ γεγονὸς καὶ ὂν καὶ ἐσόμενον.

“I am ALL that WAS, that IS, and that SHALL BE.”

Others believe that beneath the figure of Isis lies the Earth, the Moon, the Dog-Star—because the Nile was accustomed to inundate Egypt at the rising of this star—Ceres, Minerva, or some other goddess. Certain writers cited by the most illustrious and learned Huet, Bishop of Avranches, in *Demonstration of the Gospel*, Proposition IV, chapter 10, no. 1, disregarding all distinction of sex, entirely confuse her with Osiris, Astarte, Saturn, Cybele, and others. And what of the fact that the same Huet contends that, among the idolatrous Egyptians, Moses was transformed into Osiris, his wife Zipporah—and indeed also his sister Miriam—into Isis?

Moreover, whoever this Isis may have been, it is nevertheless established that sacrifices were once offered to Isis not only by the Romans and many other peoples, but even by the Suebi, as Tacitus reports in *On the Customs of the Germans*. Her idol was brought from Egypt to the Black Sea and thence carried upstream by the Danube [Col. 1151D] to that region. You will find more in Plutarch’s book *On Isis*, Pitiscus’s *Lexicon*, Natale Conti’s *Mythology*, Book VIII, chapter 18, Dempster’s *Roman Antiquities*, Book II, chapter 22, and others.

As regards the rites of Isis, moreover, they are said to have been exceedingly secret, to have been performed only at night, and to have been customarily conducted by women alone. Herodotus, in *Euterpe*, Book II, testifies that the priests of Isis were accustomed to wash in cold water daily, three times by day and twice by night; and Lucian, in *On the Syrian Goddess*, relates that a jar of water was customarily carried in procession among her mysteries. Juvenal too, in Satire 6, sings of a woman of this kind, a priestess of Isis:

> Breaking the winter ice, she will descend into the river,  
> Three times in the morning Tiber she will immerse herself,  
> And wash her fearful head in the very whirlpools . . .

And shortly afterward:

> . . . If radiant Io commands,  
> From Meroë she will carry waters to sprinkle in the shrine  
> Of Isis, which rises close beside the ancient sheepfold. [Col. 1152A]

**Of Mithras.** “Those who transmit the initiations—or mysteries—of Mithras,” says St. Justin Martyr in the *Dialogue with Trypho*, “record that he was born from a rock, and they call the place in which those who believe in him are said to be initiated a Cave.” Hence, on a certain ancient marble, Rosinus, in his *Additions to Dempster’s Roman Antiquities*, Book II, chapter 8, read:

> TO THE GOD, THE SUN, MITHRAS, FLAVIUS SEPTIMIUS ZOSIMUS, U.P., PRIEST OF THE GOD BRONTON AND OF HECATE, ESTABLISHED THIS CAVE.

These Mithraic rites, which were customarily conducted in hidden caves, were peculiar to the Persians. By the name of Mithras, as Macrobius testifies in Book I of the *Saturnalia*, chapter 17, they worshiped the Sun and were accustomed to sacrifice to him, amid the harmonious singing of hymns, a bull drawn by its horns from a cavern. The idol therefore had a lion’s face and a tiara, wore Persian dress, and with both hands gripped the horns of a struggling bull, as we learn from Statius, Book I [Col. 1152B] of the *Thebaid*, where he writes:

> . . . whether you prefer to be called the rosy Titan,  
> According to the rite of the Achaemenian nation, or fruitful Osiris,  
> Or Mithras, who beneath the rocks of the Persian cave  
> Twists the horns reluctant to follow.

Tertullian testifies concerning the washings customarily employed in initiations into this deity in the final chapter of *On the Soldier’s Crown* and in *On Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 40, where we offer more in note g.

Moreover, the monuments of the ancients everywhere testify that washings of this kind generally preceded the sacrifices of the gentiles. Thus Hesiod:

> Never pour ruddy wine to Jupiter or the gods above  
> Before you have washed your hands in the pure water of a river.

Thucydides too should be heard; in Book II of the *Peloponnesian War* he speaks as follows: “They also used the spring Callirrhoë in matters of the greatest importance, and even now, from ancient times onward, its water is customarily used before weddings and in other sacred rites.” Pausanias likewise, in Book I of the *Eliaca*, chapter 164, testifies as follows concerning [Col. 1152C] the same matter: “Neither the Sixteen Women”—these were placed in charge of the rites and games of Juno in Elis—“nor the magistrates of the Eleans undertake any part of their office before they have purified themselves with a special pig and with water drawn from the spring Piera.” Timarchides, cited by Natale Conti in Book I of the *Mythology*, chapter 4, indeed records that a certain Asterius was struck by lightning and killed because he had touched the altar of Jupiter with impure—that is, unwashed—hands. And what of the requirement that prayers could be offered to the gods only after washings of this kind had first been performed? Among the Romans, when a sacrifice was to be made—as Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Book VIII of *Roman Antiquities*, chapter 71, is an ample witness—“after their hands had been washed and the victims purified with clean water,” etc., “AFTERWARD THEY PRAYED, and only then did they order the attendants to slaughter them.” Lactantius, Book V of the *Divine Institutes*, chapter 20, wittily ridicules such people: “Defiled,” he says, “by every crime, they come to pray and imagine that they have sacrificed piously if they have washed their skin—as though any rivers could wash away the lusts enclosed within the breast, or any seas could purify them.”

But see the origin of this superstitious rite, and [Col. 1152D] read everywhere how those wicked spirits are all too truly apes of the Divine SPIRIT! “When the demons heard this washing proclaimed through the Prophet,” says St. Justin Martyr in *Apology II*, speaking of our Baptism, “they brought it about that those who were about to enter their shrines and approach them,” etc., “should purify themselves by sprinkling with water; indeed, through their prompting and agency they arrange that those who go out should even wash their whole bodies before coming to the temples in which they are stationed.” Moreover, that part of a temple in which those about to sacrifice washed or cleansed themselves—see the preceding chapter, note “Into the species”—was properly called the *delubrum*, a term whose meaning was afterward extended to the temple itself. Others, however, explain the etymology of that word differently.

**Carry forth.** It is established from Ovid, Book IV of the *Fasti*, and Lucan, Book I of the *Civil War*, that this was customarily done with the Mother of the gods. The Greeks—and, following them, our Author in *On the Shows*, chapter 8—called her μεγάλην Μητέρα, “the Great Mother”; the Phrygians called her Rhea and Cybele; the Assyrians, according to Macrobius, *Saturnalia* I, chapter 23, Adergatis; the Egyptians, according to Servius on *Aeneid* VIII, Isis; the Africans, in St. Augustine’s *City of God* II, chapter 4, the Heavenly Goddess; and the Germans, in Tacitus, *Histories* VII, chapter 20, Hertha [a parenthetical form is absent in the supplied text]. [Col. 1153A] She was thus carried to the river Almo. A distinguished parallel passage occurs in Tacitus, *Annals* XV, chapter 44: “Juno was propitiated by the matrons, first on the Capitol and afterward by the nearest sea. From there water was drawn, with which the temple and the image of the goddess were sprinkled.”

Moreover, *to carry forth* here appears to mean carrying idols out of the temple and, after first washing them in water, carrying them around through the streets and squares of the city in public supplications or processions, of which the gentiles had many and which still exist in Asia.

**Country houses.** *Villae*, as though *vehillae*, were originally the name for certain huts in the countryside, built only of turf or some other material dictated by necessity, for conveying and storing produce there, and for receiving farmers at night, together with shepherds and their flocks, and so forth. Later, when they had become the settled dwellings of country people [Col. 1153B], they were built somewhat more substantially; finally, they also became retreats of the nobility for pleasure and summer residence, and thereafter were customarily inaugurated all the more solemnly with lustral sacrifices and sprinklings of that kind.

To this category also belonged the festival of the Parilia, customarily celebrated in April in honor of the god Pales, concerning which Ovid, *Fasti* IV, verse 735, writes:

> At early twilight let the shepherd purify the well-fed sheep;  
> First let him sprinkle them with water, etc.

For this purpose that rustic and fitting formula preserved in Cato, *On Agriculture*, chapter 141, was used to invoke the rustic god: “May you forbid, ward off, and avert visible and invisible diseases, barrenness and devastation, calamities and inclement weather,” etc. Indeed, the shepherds themselves also had to be purified with fire and water; concerning them Ovid writes in the same place, verse 725:

> Surely I leapt three times across the flames arranged in a row,  
> And the laurel branch scattered glistening drops of water.

[Col. 1153C] But what prevents us from also referring to this category those Ambarvalian sprinklings during which the Ambarval victim was led around the fields, as though “going around the plowed lands,” etc.?

**Houses.** I adduce just one passage for this purpose, Theocritus, Idyll 24, where the seer Tiresias orders the house of Amphitryon to be expiated because of the serpents that Hercules, gripping them in both hands in his cradle, had strangled:

> Καθαρῷ δὲ πυρώσατε δῶμα θεείῳ  
> Πρῶτον· ἔπειτα δ᾽ ἅλεσσι μεμιγμένον (ὡς νενόμισται)  
> Θαλλῷ ἐπιῤῥαίνειν ἐστεμμένον ἀβλαβὲς ὕδωρ.

> First fumigate the house with pure sulfur; then, as custom ordains, sprinkle garlanded, harmless water mixed with salt, using a leafy branch.

**Temples.** Another distinguished testimony concerning the purification of temples occurs in Tacitus, Book IV, chapter 53. When the temple of Capitoline Jupiter, destroyed by the soldiers of Vitellius, was to be restored by the emperor Vespasian, the Vestal Virgins, together with boys and girls whose fathers and mothers were still living, washed the entire site with water drawn from streams, springs, and rivers, so that it might be dedicated with solemn rite, etc. This rite, however, was far more ancient, being traced back to King Numa himself. Plutarch says in his [Col. 1153D] *Life* that Numa assigned to the Virgins of Vesta, as sacred water, the spring that irrigated the place—namely, the field in which he was accustomed to meet with the nymph Egeria—so that, drawing from it, they might purify and sprinkle the sacred building.

**Cities.** There were also the sacred Amburbalia, when the city and its sacred boundary were purified, as in Lucan, *Pharsalia* I, verse 593:

> Then he orders the entire city to be encircled by its fearful citizens,  
> And its walls to be solemnized with a festal purification.  
> The sacred pontiffs encircle the long boundary  
> Along its outermost limits, etc.

This purification was performed every year in February; for Numa dedicated this second month, as Macrobius says in Book I of the *Saturnalia*, chapter 13, “to the god Februus, who is believed to possess power over purifications; and it was necessary that the state be purified”—which the ancients called *februari*—“during that month, so that it might render the due rites to the divine Shades.”

[Col. 1154A] **Sprinkling.** Junius thinks *aspargine* should be read. But what need is there for this change? More polished Latin authors say *asperginem*, as Virgil does in *Aeneid* III:

> . . . The rocks foam with salt spray.

I do indeed find *asparginem* used by Callistratus in Law 4 of the Digest, title *On the Rhodian Law of Jettison*, but with another meaning, namely, for damage contracted by an object from water, especially rainwater—although some do not fail to read *aspergine* there also.

**At the Apollinarian games.** When Hannibal was in Italy, these games were first celebrated by decree of the Senate, on the instruction of the verses of the seer Marcius, then perhaps newly discovered, as is established from Livy, Book XXV, chapter 12, and Macrobius, Book I of the *Saturnalia*, chapter 17. Here, however, our Author has in view rather that most celebrated Egyptian rite performed for Horus at Buto, on the island of Chemmis—for Herodotus testifies in *Euterpe* that Apollo was designated among them by this name—or the games consecrated by the Dorians to Triopian Apollo, concerning which the same Herodotus speaks in *Clio*.
