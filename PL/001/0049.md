## CHAPTER XXIX

**ARGUMENT.—**Nor should this cause surprise, since those very gods acknowledge Caesar as their lord: it is he who keeps them safe by his guards, not they who preserve him unharmed by their divine power, for they have experienced him both angry and benevolent. ## CHAPTER XXX

**ARGUMENT.—**For Christians alone invoke on behalf of the emperor the true God, the living God—that God whom the emperor himself knows by nature to be greater than himself, to whom he owes everything, and from whom he expects everything. In a most weighty discourse he shows that they piously, truly, and rightly request and obtain from this God those things which ensure the safety of the emperor, the world, and themselves, [Col. 0441A] unlike the prayers of the Gentiles. “Slaughter them,” he says, “in the very posture of supplication; wrench out the soul as it entreats God on the emperor’s behalf; charge truth and devotion to God with irreligion, if you can.”

For the safety of the emperors [Note: [Col. 0441B] “For the safety of the emperors.” *To Scapula*, ch. II: “Thus we pray all the more for the emperor’s safety, asking it from the one who can provide it.” Indeed, a notable confirmation of this is the engraving on a gem, a cast of which Dom Du Cange, in his *Dissertation on the Coinage of the Later Age*, p. 20, testifies to be preserved in Louis Chaluc’s manuscript *Dactyliotheca*, which belongs to Sainte-Geneviève in Paris. The gem bears the monogram of Christ, with A and Ω (“Omega”) at the sides, and around them these characters: **SAL. DO. N. ALEX. FIL. LUCE**; that is, as they explain it: “The salvation of our lord Alexander, son of Mamaea, shines.” Such a monogram inscription is also found on coins of Constantius and Decentius. Yet the most illustrious Du Cange doubts whether it was engraved in the time of that Alexander, because the phrase “our lord” raises a suspicion against it; this phrase, however, can be taken in its ordinary sense, not as a designation of God, as our author says in ch. XXXIV. But I think that gem should instead be referred to one of the Byzantine emperors named Alexius, so that it reads: “The salvation of our lord Alexius, son of Manuel, shines.” Otherwise the gem’s owner was already a Christian under Alexander Severus.

Indeed, the Gentiles invoked no other than Jupiter himself, their supreme god, for Caesar’s safety. Martial VII.59, with considerable flattery:

> Ruler of the venerable Tarpeian hall,  
> Whom we believe to thunder while our leader is safe,  
> Though each man weary you with prayers  
> And ask you to grant what you gods can give,  
> Be not angry with me, Jupiter, as though  
> I were proud: I ask nothing for myself.  
> I must petition you on Caesar’s behalf;  
> On my own behalf, I must petition Caesar.

Thus the gem must either be referred to Alexius, or its owner was already a Christian under Alexander Severus. HAV.] we invoke the eternal God, the true God, [Note: [Col. 0441B] Paris adds “and.”] the living God, whom the emperors themselves desire to be favorable to them above all others. They know who gave them their empire; [Note: [Col. 0441D] “Gave them their empire”—something the false gods cannot do, since, as Jeremiah says in the book of Baruch, ch. VI.33, “They cannot set a king upon a throne or remove him.” Pliny also says significantly in his *Panegyric to Trajan*: “Even if it had previously been doubtful whether rulers were given to the earth by chance and fortune or by some divine power, it would nevertheless be clear that our emperor was divinely appointed. For he was not discovered by the hidden power of the Fates, but was openly and manifestly found and chosen by Jupiter himself.” HAV.] they know who made them human beings and who gave them a soul. [Note: [Col. 0441B] “Who,” Paris.] [Note: [Col. 0441B] “Souls,” Paris.] They perceive that he alone is God, in whose power alone they stand, in relation to whom they are second and, after whom, first—before and above all the gods.

Why should they not? [Note: [Col. 0441B] “Why should they not? Since—human beings,” absent from Paris.] Since they are above [Col. 0442A] all human beings, who are surely alive and take precedence over the dead. [Note: [Col. 0441B] In the Fulda manuscript and Rhenanus’ edition, the words [Col. 0442B] “Why should they not?—human beings” are missing. Lyons manuscript I and Gelenius: “gods, human beings who,” etc. Aldus: “Who for a long time, since,” etc. In place of “live,” Havercamp, from the Fulda manuscript, reads “living.”] They reflect upon the extent to which the strength of their empire avails, and thus they understand God: they recognize that they prevail through him against whom they cannot prevail.

Let the emperor, in short, conquer heaven; [Note: [Col. 0441D] “Let the emperor conquer heaven.” The argument of Callisthenes proceeds in the same fashion in Curtius, book VIII, ch. 5, where he refutes Cleon’s foul flattery concerning the worship of the living Alexander as a god: “Would you like to test your power? Make someone a king, if you can make a god: it is easier to bestow an empire than heaven.” For “that they prevail,” Rhenanus has “they themselves prevail.” HAV.] let him carry heaven captive in his triumph, [Note: [Col. 0442B] “Carry heaven in his triumph.” The ancient custom is well known whereby triumphing generals had representations of captured cities, and also paintings of memorable battles and victories, carried around on ceremonial carriages. Tertullian alludes to this. Rhenanus’ manuscript did not recognize the next six words. HAV.] let him send guards to heaven, let him impose tribute upon heaven. [Note: [Col. 0442B] “Heaven—impose,” absent from Paris.] He cannot. He is therefore great because he is less than heaven. For he himself belongs to the one to whom heaven and every creature belong. The source from which he is emperor is the source from which he was also a man before becoming emperor; the source of his power is also the source of his breath.

Looking up toward that place, Christians pray with outspread hands, [Note: [Col. 0442B] “Looking there, Christians, with outspread hands.” Lucian’s Tryphon very aptly accords with this Christian meaning and practice: ἡμεῖς δὲ τὸν ἐν Ἀθήναις Ἀγνώστον ἐφεύροντες καὶ προσκυνήσαντες, χεῖρας εἰς οὐρανὸν ἐκτείναντες, τούτῳ εὐχαριστήσομεν (“But having found and worshipped the Unknown One at Athens, and having stretched our hands toward heaven, we shall give thanks to him”). Moreover, we learn from a verse of Lucretius, book V, that even pagans worshipped with outspread hands:

> Nor to fall prostrate upon the ground and spread one’s palms  
> Before the temples of the gods.

[Col. 0442C] RIG.—“Looking upward toward that place.” That very posture, raised toward heaven, signifies contempt for the world. Clement of Alexandria writes beautifully in *Stromata* VII, p. 231 E: “We are raised toward heaven, endeavoring together with the word to draw the body away from the earth; and by desire for better things we compel the upright and elevated soul to advance courageously into holy things, despising the bond of the flesh.”

Maurice of Sens, *On the Mass*, which the often-praised Johann Albert Fabricius appended to his *Bibliography*, writes in verse 60 concerning the congregation standing during the sacred service, etc.:

> That you should be attentive, patient, and upright against the foe  
> Your head, your staff, and the very act of standing teach.  
> For, uncovering your head, you are admonished to listen attentively;  
> When you put aside your staff, you are instructed to be forbearing.  
> Standing, you learn that we are accustomed to fight while standing,  
> Because a grievous battle beneath a watchful enemy awaits you.

John Chrysostom, on Psalm CXXIX, concerning Elijah: “But if you wish also to see him standing upright in prayer, behold him again stretched out toward heaven, and stretched so far that he even brought fire down from heaven.” Thus, while the body stands upright, the spirit bows down; and God demands the rending not of a cloak but of the heart. Hence Jerome, book II, on *Ephesians* ch. 3: “Just as external genuflection edifies the simple, so we teach that true bending of the knee lies in the mind, because many have bent the bodily knee without in any way bending the knee-joint of the soul; and conversely, others, while beseeching God with the body upright, have bowed themselves more deeply in mind.” HAV.
