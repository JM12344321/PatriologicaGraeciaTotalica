Christians abhorred this as something dedicated to the honor of profane divinities, as an affront to nature and reason, and finally as the sign of some new brothel. Our Septimius says in the *Apologeticum*: “Why, on a day of rejoicing, do we not shade our doorposts with laurels, nor violate daylight with lamps?” He adds the reason mockingly: “It is an honorable thing,” he says, “when a public solemnity demands it, to dress your house in the appearance of some new brothel.” And again: “For who compels a philosopher to sacrifice, or to prostitute useless lamps at midday?” It is in this sense that Canon XXXVII of the Council of Elvira prohibits the faithful from lighting lamps publicly—that is, during the day, according to pagan custom; and Canon XXIV of the Second Council of Arles deprives of communion a bishop who permits even unbelievers in his territory to light torches. Canon XXXIV of Elvira also forbids candles to be burned in a cemetery during the day, lest the spirits of the saints be disturbed. Lactantius explained why the spirits of the saints might be disturbed by such observances in a very harsh denunciation of pagan ritual, Book VI, chapter 2. Nevertheless, the use of wax candles even during the day subsequently began in the Church; nor was there lacking, among the holiest and most learned men, Jerome, who contended against Vigilantius that such a rite, if it could not be approved, should at least be excused and tolerated. One may therefore marvel that this man of keen intellect, inflamed by the heat of controversy, after commending the example of the woman who anointed Christ’s feet, did not turn his Vigilantius into Judas Iscariot. The same Jerome testifies that it was the custom of the churches throughout the East to light lamps during the reading of the Gospel even while the sun was shining: “Certainly not to dispel the darkness, but to demonstrate a sign of joy.” This explanation shows the rite to be all the more pagan, since the signs that the nations used even in their more wanton and wicked rejoicings were adapted to the most sacred readings of the Gospels. # TWO BOOKS ON THE ADORNMENT OF WOMEN

[Col. 1303]

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, *ON THE ADORNMENT OF WOMEN* [Note: [Col. 1303D] *On the Adornment of Women*. Until now editions of Tertullian have presented us with one book *On Women’s Dress* and another *On the Adornment of Women*. We have entitled both *On the Adornment of Women*, following the authority of the very ancient Agobard manuscript, in which, although only half of the first book has been preserved, the second concludes intact as follows: “Here ends Book II of *On the Adornment of Women*.” From this it is manifest that the first book preceded under the same title. Both are also cited thus in the catalogue of books: *On the Adornment of Women*. RIG.] (C)

## BOOK ONE

### [ARGUMENT]

[Col. 1303C] **ARGUMENT.—** Since the emperors’ most savage edicts against Christians threatened them with death hanging over them daily, Tertullian, already inclining toward Montanism, composed two books *On the Adornment of Women*. His purpose was to prepare more readily for martyrdom even those Christian women who did not shrink from imitating pagans in bodily adornment and luxury. In these books he shows that they adorn a body which at every moment they ought to be willing to lose for Christ. Hence in them he writes a bitter repudiation of feminine finery.

After prefacing a few remarks about women’s fall in Eve and the extreme humility consequently incumbent upon them, he shows that feminine adornment was instituted by a demon and describes the vanity of gold, silver, and gems glittering upon [Col. 1303D] clothing.

[Col. 1304C] Then, in the second book, he vigorously asserts that women who love ornament hate chastity; that no adornment befits God’s handmaids except simple cleanliness; that one must not glory in the flesh except when it is torn for Christ; and, finally, that cosmetics and pigments, curling irons and eye paints, little stones upon garments, and variegated colors must be avoided entirely, lest a body occupied by chains of pearls and emeralds leave no place for the sword.

## CHAPTER I

If as much faith dwelt upon earth as the reward awaiting it in heaven is great, not one of you, dearly beloved sisters [Col. 1304D], after she had come to know the living God and had learned about her own condition—that is, the condition of woman—would have desired a more cheerful mode of dress, to say nothing of a more glorious one. Rather, she would live in mourning garments and affect a neglected appearance, carrying Eve herself about in her person, grieving and repentant. She would thereby atone more fully, through every habit of satisfaction, for what she derives from Eve—I mean the disgrace of the first transgression and the odium of humankind’s perdition:

> In pains and anxieties you shall bear children [Note: [Col. 1305C] Semler: *paries*, “you shall bear.”], woman; your turning shall be toward your husband, and he shall rule [Note: [Col. 1305C] *Dominatur*, “he rules,” in the Paris edition.] over you.  
> (Gen. 3:16)

And do you not know that you are Eve? God’s sentence upon that sex lives on in this age; therefore her guilt must necessarily live on as well. You are the devil’s gateway. You are the unsealer of that tree. You are the first deserter of the divine law. You are she who persuaded [Note: [Col. 1305C] Semler: “advised.”] him whom the devil was not strong enough to attack. You so easily shattered man, the image of God. On account of what you deserved—that is, death—even the Son of God had to die. And does it enter your mind to adorn yourself over your tunics of skins?

Come now: if from the beginning of creation the sheep of Miletus had been shorn, the Seres had spun from trees, the Tyrians had dyed, the Phrygians had embroidered, and the Babylonians had woven; if pearls had gleamed white and thunderstones flashed [Note: [Col. 1305D] “Thunderstones flashed.” The *ceraunia* is a gem, white and gleaming red, so called as though from thunder, for κεραυνός means “thunderbolt.” Pliny, Book XXXVII, chapter 9: “Among the white gems is also the one called *ceraunia*.” The neuter *ceraunium* is also used; St. Jerome, *Letter to Demetrias*: “with the flames of thunderstones.” LE PR.]; if gold itself had already emerged from the earth accompanied by greed; if even a mirror [Note: [Col. 1305C] Junius: “from a mirror.”] had already been permitted to lie so greatly—then even this Eve, expelled from paradise and already dead, would, I suppose, have desired these things [Note: [Col. 1305C] “Had been permitted; this Eve had desired,” in François; Semler, following Rhenanus: “desired.”].

Therefore, even now, if she desires to live again, she ought neither to seek nor to know the things that she neither possessed nor knew when she was alive. All these things are accordingly the impedimenta of a condemned and dead woman, arranged as though for the pageantry of her funeral.
