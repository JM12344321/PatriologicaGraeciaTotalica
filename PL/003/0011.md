And we began to have no hope in this world. Thus Pontius says of Cyprian, when his future passion was already certain: “But he, with his mind raised toward heaven, had already ceased to regard the world.” And Cyprian himself, Epistle VI: “We had renounced the world when we were baptized; but now we have truly renounced the world, when, tested and proved by God, we leave behind all that is ours,” etc. The same author, *On the Dress of Virgins*: “Among the Martyrs there is no thought of the flesh and the world”; doubtless because, as he likewise says in Epistle I, *To Donatus*: “One who is greater than the world can no longer seek or desire anything from the world.” Hence in Epistle XV he exhorts Moyses and the other Confessors condemned to death in these words: “Already hoping for heavenly things alone and contemplating only divine things, through the very delay of your passion you ascend to loftier heights.” For, as the Confessor Celerinus says in Cyprian’s Epistle XXI: “This is what usually happens to God’s servants, [Col. 0030B] especially to those who are established in the confession of Christ; for I know that each now pays no attention to the things of this world, because he hopes for the heavenly crown.” Lucifer of Cagliari, in the book *That One Must Die for the Son of God*: “We know that this discipline has been divinely handed down to us: that, without any terror of wickedness, we should defend the power of so great a name; for the desire of eternal remembrance long ago withdrew us from the desire for this light, and fellowship with Christ set us apart to trample upon all earthly things.”

Furthermore, this contempt for the world, characteristic of Martyrs—τεθνηξομένων, “those about to die immediately”—was sought in imitation by those who, having been seriously converted, undertook the perfect life. Possidius says of St. Augustine after his initiation into the Church’s sacraments by Ambrose: “Soon, converted to God from the innermost depths of his heart, he abandoned every hope that he had in the world.” And because those who embraced the monastic life were said to die to the world, this disposition and affection were likewise characteristic of them; as Augustine himself declares concerning himself in Sermon 49, *On Various Subjects*: “I was seeking where I might establish a monastery, for I had abandoned every hope in the world.” HOLST.

**CHAPTER II.—I. “Lay aside your spirit.”** *Animi* (“spirits” or “high spirits”) seems to be used here for impulses of spirit—that is, more vehement thoughts or emotions. Thus Tertullian, in *On the Shows*: “They proceed to frenzies, passions, and dissensions.” And in *On Patience*, chapter 7: “Anger forbidden, passions restrained, the insolent hand checked.” Unless perhaps Perpetua’s father here called *animi* the loftiness of her spirit and the vigor of a victorious conscience—that is, the martyr’s noble steadfastness—as Cyprian speaks in Epistle LXXVII. **“He called me not daughter, but lady.”** Thus the Vulgate translator at John 13:13: “You call me Teacher and Lord.” In Greek also the nominative stands for the accusative: ὁ διδάσκαλος καὶ ὁ κύριος, “the Teacher and the Lord.” HOLST.

**“Because he alone, out of all my family, would not rejoice in my passion.”** From these words it appears established that Perpetua’s husband, mother, all her brothers, and her relatives were Christians. This will be useful below for understanding that Dinocrates, Perpetua’s brother, who died at the age of seven, had been baptized in a household that was almost entirely Christian. HOLST.

**“What happens on that platform,” etc.** A *catasta* was properly a raised place upon which slaves for sale were displayed, so called ἀπὸ τοῦ καταστῆναι, that is, “from setting forth.” Pamelius so notes on Cyprian’s Epistle XXXIII, adding that Martyrs, when judged or tortured, were likewise customarily displayed to the people upon a raised place. Hence the words of Cyprian in the already cited epistle concerning the Reader who had confessed Christ upon the *catasta* and was afterward promoted to the Church’s pulpit: “to come to the pulpit after the *catasta*”; for as the pulpit was a more elevated place in the church, so was the *catasta* in the forum. The Martyr Romanus in Prudentius:

> Hear, all of you; I cry aloud and proclaim afar;  
> From the lofty platform I send forth my voice.

It is as though he said: “I use the *catasta* as a pulpit.” The word also has another meaning, namely, an instrument of torture. An old glossary: “*Catasta*: the rack, a kind of punishment.” And Prudentius, Hymn I, περὶ στεφάνων (*On the Crowns*), “After the violence of cracking lashes, after the fiery racks.” But this latter meaning is irrelevant here. **II.—“An immense populace gathered.”** That is, an immense crowd of people assembled. For the word *populus* here signifies a dense and closely packed multitude of people. It is used in this sense also by Apuleius, Book III of the *Metamorphoses*, where he says: “Immediately the entire city, pouring out into a crowd, follows us with astonishing density.” At that passage the acumen of critics who substitute *publicum* (“public place”) or *propatulum* (“open space”) for *populum* is intolerable. HOLST.

**“When questioned, the others confessed.”** They did not confess murders, incestuous unions, injury to the majesty of the Deity or of Caesar, or whatever else a pagan world ignorant of the truth was accustomed to charge against those who professed Christ; they confessed only this: that they were Christians. Justin Martyr, *First Apology*: ἐξεταζόμενοι οὐκ ἀρνούμεθα, διὰ τὸ συνεπίστασθαι ἑαυτοῖς μηδὲν φαῦλον, etc.: “When questioned, we do not deny it, since we are conscious of no evil in ourselves.” This confession sufficed for the formal charge, such as that proclaimed three times by the herald concerning Polycarp, in Eusebius, Book IV, chapter 15: Πολύκαρπος ὡμολόγησεν ἑαυτὸν χριστιανὸν εἶναι, “Polycarp has confessed himself to be a Christian.” So too the inscription upon the placard of Attalus, in the same author, Book V, chapter 1: οὗτός ἐστιν Ἄτταλος ὁ χριστιανός, “This is Attalus the Christian.” HOLST.

**“And he pulled me down from the step.”** She means the higher *catasta*, upon which persons facing a capital charge ascended to plead their case and receive the judge’s sentence—unless we should rather say that upon the *catasta* itself there was a somewhat higher step which the defendants mounted one by one, in order that they might be more conspicuous to judge and people. We have seen this custom observed in Britain. In *Ecclesiastical History*, Book VIII, chapter 9, Eusebius calls τὸ πρὸ τοῦ δικαστοῦ βῆμα “the step or platform erected before the judge.” Translators entirely failed to observe this there, rendering it “the judge’s tribunal.” Eusebius’s words are: ἅμα γοῦν τῇ κατὰ τῶν προτέρων ἀποφάσει, ἐπεπήδων ἄλλοθεν ἄλλοι τῷ πρὸ τοῦ δικαστοῦ βήματι, χριστιανοὺς σφᾶς ὁμολογοῦντες: “As soon as the first had been condemned, others from various quarters leapt upon the raised place erected before the judge, confessing themselves to be Christians.” Likewise in Book V, chapter 1, the governor of Vienne, ἀνῆγεν ἐπὶ τὸ βῆμα θεατρίζων τοὺς μακαρίους μάρτυρας, ἐμπομπεύων τοῖς ὄχλοις: “brought the blessed Martyrs up upon a raised step, presenting them to the multitude as a spectacle, as though in a theater and solemn procession.”

Dionysius of Alexandria, in the same Eusebius, calls it τὸ βάθρον, when speaking of Ammon, Zeno, and the other soldiers who, standing before the tribunal, encouraged Christians who were wavering; and when the cries of the crowd demanded them for execution, φθάσαντες ἐπὶ τὸ βάθρον ἀνέδραμον, “they anticipated them by running voluntarily upon the step,” proclaiming themselves Christians. If, however, anyone prefers to understand this as the tribunal and its steps, I do not stubbornly oppose him. HOLST.

**“Spare your father’s gray hairs.”** Thus the mother of the youthful Origen, when he burned with desire for martyrdom, begged him τῆς περὶ αὐτὸν μητρικῆς διαθέσεως φειδῶ λαβεῖν, that is, in Rufinus’s translation, “to take thought for himself and spare his mother.” HOLST.

**“Offer sacrifice for the welfare of the emperors.”** The Acts of nearly all the Martyrs teach that pagans employed this as the principal means of testing Christians: once the superstition of sacrifice had been rejected, Christians could be convicted of offending both the gods and the emperor. Accordingly, the more insistently the pagans pressed them, the more steadfastly the Christians avoided the guilt of idolatry, as Perpetua steadfastly answers here: “I will not.” “This,” says Tertullian, “is the law peculiar to us Christians, by which we are recognized and tested by the pagans” (*On Idolatry*, final chapter). Hence in *Apology*, chapter 9, he calls “the little hearth and the incense-box” the examiner of Christians. The same author, *Apology*, chapter 10: “You say: ‘You do not worship the gods, and you do not offer sacrifices for the emperors.’ We are therefore arraigned for sacrilege and treason: this is the chief charge—indeed, the entire charge.” He then refutes both heads of this accusation in a long and learned discussion, whose substance he embraces in these few words in the book *To Scapula*: “We sacrifice for the emperor’s welfare, but to our God and his; and in the manner God commands—with pure prayer. Thus we pray all the more for the emperor’s welfare, asking it from the One who can grant it.” HOLST.

**“I will not.”** Thus Polycarp, when they advised him to call Caesar “Lord” and to sacrifice, answered: οὐ μέλλω πράττειν ὃ συμβουλεύετέ μοι, “I will never do what you advise me to do.” Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book IV, chapter 15. HOLST.

**“I am a Christian.”** This is the proper formula of confession. Thus that glorious soldier of God who refused the laurel, in Tertullian, when required to give his reasons, answered: “I am a Christian.” Thus St. Blandina, amid the most savage torments, repeatedly cried: “I am a Christian.” That utterance was an alleviation of pain and a refreshment of spirit. And Sanctus the deacon, Blandina’s companion, answered nothing else to all the torturers’ questions but “I am a Christian”: τοῦτο καὶ ἀντὶ ὀνόματος, καὶ ἀντὶ πόλεως, καὶ ἀντὶ γένους, καὶ ἀντὶ παντὸς ἐπαλλήλως ὡμολόγει, “This served him as name, country, race, and everything.” For Christians regarded this as a πάντιμον καὶ ἔνδοξον καὶ ζωοποιὸν προσηγορίαν, “an all-honored, glorious, and life-giving appellation,” as Eusebius records. HOLST.

**“I see Dinocrates coming out of a dark place, where there were also several dark places,” etc.** This passage seems especially worthy of note because it attests the ancient tradition from which the Scholastic theologians teach with great agreement—as Bellarmine observes in Book II, *On Purgatory*, chapter 6, § “The Eighth”—that there are four distinct places beneath the earth in which the souls of the dead are detained. First is the lowest of all, where the demons and condemned souls are; above it is Purgatory, where souls are purified for a time; next is the Limbo of children, where the souls of those who died in original sin alone are detained; and finally the Limbo of the Fathers, now empty since the death and resurrection of Christ, where the souls of the patriarchs and holy persons who died before Christ were detained, although cleansed of every stain. These appear to have been those several dark places which St. Perpetua testifies in these words that she saw. From one of them—namely, Purgatory—she affirms that she saw Dinocrates come forth: not because he was then being delivered from punishment, for this happened a little later, but because, by God’s permission, he came forth thence with his punishments in order to implore the assistance of his sister’s prayers. Many apparitions of this kind, thoroughly attested, are related in St. Gregory and other authors concerning souls enduring purgatorial punishments. HOLST.

**“He died miserably, his face eaten by cancer, so that his death was hateful,” etc.** She signifies that he died a kind of death which inspired horror in those who saw it, because of the deformity of a foul and fetid ulcer projecting from his face. Moreover, in order to know what *cancer* means, we thought that, in addition to all our other diligence, physicians too should be consulted; from their answers, with certain additions, we have assembled the following.

Among the ancient Latins, *cancer* means the same as gangrene. Thus Nonius Marcellus: “Gangrene is cancer”; the old Glossary follows him. Cornelius Celsus, Book VIII, chapter 10, may also be drawn to this sense, where, by saying that “cancers” arise more readily when a wound of the flesh accompanies a fracture of a bone, he seems to signify what experience shows: that in such a case there is greater danger of gangrene. Yet I would not readily assent to the opinion of a learned physician who, after making this observation, denies that the physicians of later times and of our own age agree with the ancients concerning the meaning of this word, because they understand by the name *cancer* what the Greeks call Καρκίνωμα (*karkinōma*, carcinoma)—as though the ancients too had not expressed the Greek name *carcinoma* by the Latin word *cancer*.

Certainly Celsus, Book V, chapter 28, speaks of cancer projecting from the face in these words: “Καρκίνωμα does not entail the same danger unless it has been aggravated through the imprudence of the person treating it. This disease arises chiefly in the upper parts, around the face, nostrils, ears, lips, and women’s breasts.” Thus far Celsus, who also in Book VI, chapter 15, when treating cancer of the mouth, appears to speak of the ulcer which in the other passage he calls *carcinoma*. I would therefore believe that καρκίνωμα is a general name for more severe ulcers. Hence in Hippocrates ulcers are said καρκινωθῆναι τὰ ἕλκεα, “to become cancerous,” when they grow aggravated and incurable. And in the Hippocratic Lexicon καρκινοῦσθαι, “to become cancerous,” is explained by σκληρύνεσθαι, “to harden,” which means “to become aggravated.”

Therefore gangrene properly so called, if it differs from cancer, seems to share with it the common name *carcinoma*, differing from it perhaps because it causes death very rapidly, whereas cancer creeps along in such a way as to consume a person who may remain alive for many years. Nothing, however, prevents the cancer from which Dinocrates died from having been gangrene; for this may be inferred from the representation of his death at so tender an age. It is characteristic of both—of cancer, I say, as well as gangrene—to creep. Hence the words concerning the teaching of heretics at 2 Timothy 2:17: “And their speech creeps like a cancer.” The expression seems transferred from the animal crab, a many-footed creature accustomed to creep forward and backward.

Relevant here is a passage of Lucifer of Cagliari in the book *On Not Associating with Heretics*, addressed to the Arian emperor Constantius: “Thus Sabellius was cut off from the body of the Church, just as that utterly detestable Arius was cut off. His speech has crept like a cancer in you so powerfully that you are now seen to be wholly cancer; nor will you be able to say that you have not been cancered by Arius’s speech, since the stench of your cancerous corruption has reached every place in your kingdom, and all of us have resolved to avoid you, lest we too be cancered by your Arian plague.”

Thus he speaks. **“For him I had made prayer.”** This too is an African manner of speaking, for the expression “to make a prayer” is also found in Tertullian. As to the substance, however, the Church’s ancient practice and conviction of praying for the dead who pay the penalties of their sins after this life should be noted here. Although there are many other thoroughly trustworthy testimonies to it, this one seems especially important, since it was written by a holy Martyr deemed worthy of divine revelations and already close to her consummation. Nor do the heretics retain any objection concerning the truth and reliability of these Acts after the many splendid testimonies of every kind of author who praises them, which we have appended to this volume. Indeed, they are such that even obstinacy and shamelessness themselves would appear unable to object to them. HOLST.

**“And between me and him there was a great diadem.”** I do not conceal that, if conjecture were permissible, it might appear not without foundation that *diastema* (“interval”) could be restored in place of *diadema*. For one may understand that the Latin writers of that age frequently used the word *diastema* from Julius Frontinus’s book *On the Quality of Lands*, where he writes: “If three equal circles are connected with one another by an equal interval [*diastema*].” And Hyginus, *On the Establishment of Boundaries*: “Furthermore, even in that very region the position of the sun cannot be correctly determined unless the instrument is placed at equal intervals [*diastemata*] from its rising and setting.” Likewise: “The earth is a point of the heavens, and the air sustains it beneath the sun across a vast interval [*diastema*].” HOLST.

—**“And between me and him there was a great diadem, so that neither of us could approach the other.”** Holstenius’s comments here upon his conjecture that *diastema* should be restored for *diadema* appear open to this objection: the passages he cites from the land-surveyors Frontinus and Hyginus certainly do little to persuade us that St. Perpetua used here that word, which was proper to the art of surveying and consequently foreign to common usage, especially that of women in Africa. Accordingly, since—as Holstenius himself acknowledges in the very papers from which we transcribed his note—the manuscript’s consistent and explicit reading gives *diadema*, there is no reason to alter it readily, especially since the word yields a very suitable meaning.

For those dark places which she says above that she saw in their plurality were like certain zones of the sort usefully imagined by cosmographers as embracing heaven and earth—namely, oblong spaces of an appropriate breadth encircling globes. With regard to the lower regions, ancient usage, preserved particularly by Scholastic writers from the old custom of the churches, calls these spaces *limbi*. Now *limbus* is a word of the same meaning as *diadema*. For *diadema*, as is well known, signifies a band longer than it is wide, surrounding the head or bound around it, as the Greek etymology of the name indicates, from the verb διαδέω, “I bind around.”

Therefore Perpetua’s statement that “between Dinocrates and me there was a great diadem” is equivalent to saying that there was a great *limbus*—that is, an oblong space of a certain breadth. In the Gospel it is called a “chasm,” Luke 16:26: “And now between us and you a great chasm has been fixed.” The Greek is χάσμα, which means an opening or gulf, and the added words—“so that those who wish to pass from here to you cannot, nor can they cross from there to us”—agree with what St. Perpetua says here: “So that neither of us could approach the other.” For these spaces were like ditches drawn around fortifications, excluding passage by their very emptiness. This is more readily understood in the case of bodies; but in the case of souls the divine ordinance intervened to keep them apart, as expressed by the verb “to fix”: “A great chasm has been fixed.”

Nor is the foregoing contradicted by the fact that, according to these words, the diadem was not the place in which Dinocrates himself was, but the space lying between him and St. Perpetua; and therefore it does not appear to follow that the name *diadema* or *limbus* is applicable to Purgatory. For since both the subterranean places and the openings or χάσματα by which they were separated possessed the same form and shape, the name *diadema* or *limbus* can equally apply to both.

Although I found nothing concerning the foregoing observations in Holstenius’s adversaria, I nevertheless heard from a trustworthy man that Holstenius was by no means averse to this opinion. Father Jean Besson of our Society reported to me that, when Lucas Holstenius had once shared with him these very Acts of St. Perpetua, which he was preparing for publication, and asked what he thought about this mention of a diadem, Besson replied that the term *limbus* appeared to have been replaced by that word of equivalent meaning. Holstenius indicated that this interpretation pleased him greatly. POSSIN.

**“We were transferred to the camp prison.”** That is, the prison situated within the camp itself. HOLST.

**“For we were to fight in a camp spectacle.”** Since Roman camps outside cities were long-standing and virtually permanent stations for soldiers, the chief amenities of cities were transferred into them so that soldiers would not be deprived of them. Among these were the spectacles, to which the Romans were extraordinarily devoted. Suetonius accordingly mentions “camp games”—that is, games celebrated in the camp to entertain the soldiers—in his *Tiberius*, chapter 72.

But as for the word *munus* (“spectacle”) used here, it signified a combat either of gladiators with one another or of beasts with persons condemned to death, which is properly called θηριομαχία, “combat with wild beasts.” Thus the phrase in Eusebius, Book V, chapter 1, θηριομαχιῶν ἡμέρας, “days of combats with beasts,” is elegantly translated by Rufinus as *munerum dies*, “days of spectacles.” Note here a certain catachresis in the word “fight,” since it is said of persons who, when exposed to beasts, remained entirely passive, as the Martyrs did. Thus Juvenal, after calling the nocturnal assault of drunken men upon unarmed and unresisting passersby a “fight,” adds:

> If it is a fight when you strike and I merely take the beating.

Nevertheless usage prevailed so that those who were delivered, bound and defenseless, to the beasts were said in Greek θηριομαχεῖν, “to fight wild beasts,” and in Latin *pugnare ad bestias*, “to fight against the beasts,” although these expressions properly applied to those who were actually ordered to fight beasts in hunts staged in the amphitheater. Such men did so at the utmost risk to life, yet not without some hope of victory, for we read that lions and whatever other beasts were more savage were sometimes killed by them.

There were therefore two kinds of punishment. The former was the supreme penalty and is called capital punishment in Law 8 of the Digest, *On Penalties*; the latter is reckoned among moderate punishments by Paul, *Sentences*, Book V, title 17, and by Ulpian in Law 8 of the Digest, *On the Cornelian Law concerning Forgeries*. For those condemned to the hunting school could be released, whereas those assigned to the beasts had to be consumed without remission. Hence they were either bound to a stake or enclosed in nets and thrown to the wild beasts, so that they had no ability to resist. Then, as we learn from the conclusion of these Acts, whatever life and breath the beasts’ cruelty had left them was finally taken by the sword. HOLST.

**“It was then Geta’s anniversary.”** Antoninus Geta was the son of Severus. After his father’s death he was killed by his brother Caracalla, with whom he had ruled for a time. But here by *birthday* or “anniversary” we must understand the day upon which Geta had been made Caesar. This day was commonly called the emperor’s birthday, just as a bishop’s “birthday” was the day upon which he was consecrated. For otherwise Geta, according to Spartianus, was born on the sixth day before the Kalends of June, that is, May 27. RUINART.

**IV.—“On the day when we remained in the stocks.”** The laws of the Twelve Tables, as quoted by Aulus Gellius, Book XX, chapter 1, mention two kinds of restraints formerly used to bind criminals in prison: the *nervus* or stocks, and fetters. Other authors also frequently join the two kinds. Cato, in Gellius, Book XI, chapter 18: “Thieves convicted of private thefts spend their lives in the stocks and fetters.” And Livy, Book VIII: “No one was to be kept in fetters or in the stocks, unless he had incurred a penalty, until he had paid it.”

Fetters are iron shackles which restrain the feet of prisoners by a shorter or longer chain, more tightly or loosely, in such a manner that they can not only stand upon their feet but also, with some difficulty, advance a step and remain standing. The *nervus*, however, is properly a wooden apparatus pierced by several holes, which encloses prisoners’ feet at nearer or farther intervals so that, sitting upon the ground with their legs spread apart, they are wholly unable to move from the spot. Spreading the legs through several holes, because of the discomfort, served as a torture and punishment before the execution itself.

I know that learned men of our time do not agree concerning the *nervus*; but among the ancients, when both the object and its name were in use, there is no dispute. On occasion of this mention of fetters, we should not omit to observe that, at least in Africa, they appear to have been used only rarely in the custody of Christians imprisoned for religion’s sake. St. Augustine, Discourse II on Psalm 101, near the beginning, volume 10, page 439: “Why are the Martyrs said to be fettered by the feet? Were they not chained rather than fettered? For we know that the holy Martyrs, led after judges traveling through the provinces, were put in chains; but we do not know that they were put in fetters.” HOLST.

**“Then she understood that he had been removed from punishment.”** Behold: St. Perpetua understands by divine revelation that a soul has been delivered from the flames of purgatory.

—**“That he had been removed from punishment.”** An excellent passage for proving that the dead can be delivered, through the prayers of the living, from the punishments they suffer. Some inferred from this passage that even those who had died before baptism could be aided by prayers. Augustine excellently refutes them in *On the Origin of the Soul*, Book I, chapter 10, and Book III, chapter 9, where he says that Dinocrates endured such punishments because of certain sins committed after baptism. For a boy of seven could have lied or, at the persuasion of his pagan father, done something else against God. This opinion of Augustine is confirmed by Perpetua’s earlier statement that her entire family, with the exception of her father, practiced the religion of Christ. RUINART.

**CHAPTER III.—I. “Pudens, a soldier and *optio*, superintendent of the prison.”** The word *optio* is the name of an office or prefecture. In Gruter’s inscriptions the phrase *optio carceris*, “prison *optio*,” occurs, so that the addition made here—“superintendent of the prison”—appears to have been placed by way of explanation. The Greeks call him δεσμοφύλαξ, “prison-keeper.” At Acts 16:27 St. Ambrose, in his commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians, chapter IV, translates this word as “prison *optio*,” writing: “Nor did Paul and Silas delay the time in which they might baptize the prison *optio*.”

Here the question may arise why this office was entrusted to a soldier. Perhaps it was because, in the camp where the prison then was, as stated above, civic officials could not be employed for duties of this kind, which therefore had to be discharged by soldiers. This may be understood by analogy from Law 4 of the Digest, *On the Office of the Proconsul*, where it is written: “No proconsul may have his own orderlies; rather, soldiers are to perform their service in the provinces in place of them.” Relevant here is an ancient inscription in which a soldier serving as *optio* is mentioned:

> TO AURELIUS CANDIDUS, HIS SWEETEST SON,  
> WHO LIVED FOUR YEARS. AURELIUS CALANDINUS,  
> SOLDIER OF THE EIGHTH PRAETORIAN COHORT,  
> *OPTIO* OF THE CENTURY OF SECUNDUS,  
> ERECTED THIS TO ONE WELL DESERVING.

HOLST.
