## CHAPTER IX

We, however, because we do not invent subject matter from parables but interpret parables from their subject matter, do not labor greatly to force every detail into the exposition, provided that we guard against all contradictions. Why are there a hundred [Col. 0996C] sheep (Luke 15:4)? And why ten drachmas? [Col. 0997A] And what are those brooms (ibid. 8)? It was necessary for one who wished to express how exceedingly welcome the salvation of one sinner is to God to name some numerical quantity, from which he might describe one as lost. It was necessary that the circumstances of the woman searching for the drachma in her house be furnished with the aid of both broom and lamp.

Curiosities of this kind make some things suspect and, through the subtlety of forced expositions, often lead away from the truth. Some details, however, have simply been placed there to construct, arrange, and weave the parable, so that it may be brought to the point for which the example is provided.

The two sons certainly refer to the same point as the drachma and the sheep. Since they are connected with those parables, they have the same cause: namely, the same murmuring of the Pharisees against the Lord’s association with gentiles. [Col. 0997B] Or, if anyone doubts that the tax collectors in Judea were gentiles—Judea having long since been seized

by the hand of Pompey and Lucullus—let him read Deuteronomy: “There shall be no one paying tribute from among the children of Israel” (Deut. 23:19).

Nor would the name of tax collectors have been so accursed in the Lord’s eyes

> **Note [Col. 0997D]: “Nor would the name of tax collectors have been so accursed.”** Septimius curses the sinews of war; in *On the Crown* he stripped the Christian soldier of his military belt. **RIG.**

[Col. 0997C] For an equation of examples must not be admitted, however perfectly it may correspond to the image, if it is exceedingly harmful to salvation. We see the whole condition of salvation, established in the maintenance of discipline, overturned by the interpretation promoted by the opposing side.

For if the Christian is the man who, after receiving from God the Father his substance—namely baptism, the Holy Spirit, and thereafter the hope of eternity—wanders far from the Father and squanders it by living as a gentile; if, stripped of the goods of the mind, he also hands over his service to the ruler of the world—to whom else but the devil?—and is appointed by him to tend swine, that is, to minister to unclean spirits; and if he then comes to his senses and returns to the Father, then by this parable it will be not merely adulterers and fornicators, but idolaters, blasphemers, deniers, and every class of apostates who make satisfaction to the Father. For who will fear to squander what he will afterward be able to recover? Who will take care to preserve forever what he cannot lose forever? Security from punishment is itself an inducement to sin.

The apostate, therefore, will recover his former robe, the clothing [Col. 0998A] of the Holy Spirit; and again the ring, the seal of baptism; and Christ will again be slaughtered for him (Luke 15:22–23). He will recline there upon that couch from which those unworthily clothed are customarily taken by the torturers and cast into darkness (Matt. 22:12–13), much more those who have been stripped bare.

It is therefore a stronger point that the sequence of the prodigal son is not even suitably applicable to a Christian. But if the son’s image does not completely correspond to the Jew either, the interpretation will simply be governed by the Lord’s purpose.

The Lord had surely come to save what had been lost, as a physician more necessary to the sick than to the healthy (Matt. 18:11; 9:12; Luke 19:10; 5:31). This he both represented in parables and proclaimed in sayings. Who among human beings is lost? Who fails in health, except the one who does not know God? Who is safe and sound, except the one who knows God? This parable too will signify these two brotherly kinds within the human race.

Consider whether the gentile possesses in God the Father a patrimony of wisdom and natural knowledge of God. Through this the Apostle also remarks that, in the wisdom of God, the world did not know God through wisdom (1 Cor. 1:21)—a wisdom it had, of course, received from God. The gentile therefore squandered this when, in his conduct, he went far away from the Lord amid the errors, enticements, and desires of the world. There, driven by hunger for the truth, he handed himself over to the ruler of this age. That ruler appointed him over the swine—to feed that livestock so familiar to the demons—where he himself had no share in life-giving food, while at the same time he saw others, engaged in divine service, abounding in heavenly bread.

He remembers God the Father, returns after making satisfaction, and receives his former robe: namely, the condition Adam lost through transgression. He then receives the ring for the first time, with which, when questioned, he seals [Col. 0998C] the covenant of faith. Thereafter he feeds upon the richness of the Lord’s body—that is, the Eucharist.

This will be the prodigal son: one who was never thrifty before, who was prodigal from the outset because he was not a Christian from the outset. It was this man, returning from the world to the Father’s embrace in the persons of tax collectors and sinners, whom the Pharisees mourned. The elder brother’s envy was therefore adapted to this point alone: not because the Jews were innocent and obedient to God, but because they envied salvation to the nations, though certainly they ought always to have remained with the Father.

And the Jew certainly groans at the Christian’s first calling, not at his second restoration. The former shines even upon the gentiles; the latter, however, which is transacted within the churches, is unknown even to the Jews.

I think I have produced interpretations better adapted to the subject matter of the parables, the correspondence of the facts, [Col. 0998D] and the protection of discipline.

But if the opposing side is eager to make the sheep, the drachma, and the son’s dissipation figures of the Christian sinner, so that by repentance they may pardon adultery and fornication, then either pardon must likewise be granted to all the other capital offenses, [Col. 0999A] or adultery and fornication, which are their equals, must likewise be maintained as unpardonable.

More importantly, nothing may be inferred beyond the matter under discussion. Indeed, if it were permissible to transfer the parables elsewhere, we would direct their hope toward martyrdom instead. Martyrdom alone will be able to restore the son after his entire substance has been squandered; will proclaim with joy the drachma found among everything else, though even in dung; and will carry the fugitive sheep back to the flock upon the Lord’s own shoulders through every rough and precipitous place.

But we prefer, if it may be, to understand less in the Scriptures rather than to understand against them. Accordingly, we must preserve the Lord’s meaning and command. Transgression in interpretation is no lighter than transgression in conduct.

## CHAPTER X

[Col. 0999B]

Now that the obstacle to explaining these parables as referring to the gentile has therefore been removed, and once the necessity has been discerned or accepted of interpreting them in no other way than the subject under discussion requires, they contend that the proclamation of repentance does not apply even to gentiles. Their offenses, they say, are not answerable to repentance, since they must be imputed to ignorance, which nature alone makes culpable before God. Nor, they say, can people understand remedies when they do not understand the dangers themselves. The rationale of repentance exists only where sin is committed knowingly and voluntarily, where both guilt and grace are understood. The man who knows both what he has lost and what he will recover if he sacrifices repentance to God—who surely offers it to sons more than to strangers—is the one who should mourn and roll in the dust.

Did Jonah, then (Jonah 3:4), for that reason think repentance unnecessary for the gentile Ninevites when he quibbled over [Col. 0999C] his duty of preaching?

> **Note [Col. 0999C]: “Did not,” Pamelius; “for,” Oberthür, Semler.**

It is well that John too, while preparing the Lord’s ways, was a herald of repentance no less to soldiers and tax collectors than to the sons of Abraham (Luke 3:13ff.). [Col. 1000A] The Lord himself presumed that Sidon and Tyre would have repented if they had seen the evidences of his mighty works (Matt. 11:21).

No doubt the Lord is more kindly to the ungrateful than to the ignorant, and more quickly merciful to those who have already been rejected than to those not yet tested! Thus he is supposedly not more angered by insults to his clemency than he is ready to show favor, and does not more gladly bestow that clemency upon strangers than he has lost it among his sons—even though he adopted the nations precisely while the Jews made sport of his patience.

> **Note [Col. 0999D]: “The Jews make sport of his patience.”** That is, patience so often injured by offenses so often repeated. Thus canon 3 of the Council of Elvira, concerning *flamines* who committed adultery after repentance: “It was resolved that communion should no longer be given to them, lest they appear to have made sport of the Lord’s communion”; and canon 47: “If, after being restored, he again commits adultery, it was resolved that God should no longer be mocked through the communion of peace.” **RIG.**

But this is what the Psychics desire: that God, the just judge, should prefer the repentance rather than the death of the sinner who preferred death to repentance. [Col. 1000B] If that is so, we earn merit by sinning.

Go on, then, tightrope-walker of modesty and chastity and of every holiness concerning sex! You advance along the most slender thread of discipline of this kind, with your steps suspended above the byways of truth, balancing the flesh by the spirit, governing the soul by faith, and restraining the eye by fear. Why, then, are you wholly fixed upon the rope? Go forward, certainly, if you can and if you wish, while you are so secure and as though standing on solid ground. For if some wavering of the flesh, distraction of the mind, or wandering of the eye knocks you from your course, God is good: he holds open his bosom to his own, not to gentiles. A second repentance will receive you;

> **Note [Col. 0999D]: “You will again become a Christian from being an adulterer.”** As though one who fell into adultery after baptism had ceased to be a Christian. **RIG.**

from an adulterer you will again become a Christian.

So much for you, my most benevolent interpreter of God. But I would yield to you if the writing of *The Shepherd*, which alone loves adulterers, had deserved to be inscribed among the divine documents; if it were not judged apocryphal and false by every council of the churches, including [Col. 1000C] your own—being itself adulterous and therefore the patroness of its companions;

> **Note [Col. 0999D]: “By which you are initiated in other respects too.”** By that apocryphal and false writing of *The Shepherd*, of course, rather than by writings that are unquestioned and universally received. **RIG.**

and by which you are initiated elsewhere too.

If perhaps that shepherd depicted upon your cup will patronize that writing, he too is

> **Note [Col. 0999D]: “A panderer of the Christian sacrament.”** That is, of the Eucharist. **RIG.**

a panderer of the Christian sacrament. He is deservedly both an idol of drunkenness and a gadfly inciting the adultery that will follow the cup—

> **Note [Col. 0999D]: “And a gadfly inciting the adultery that will follow the cup.”** The text should read *asilum*, for it signifies *οἶστρον* (*oistron*), “gadfly, frenzy.”  
>   
> “…which bears the Roman name *asilus*,  
> while the Greeks have translated it by calling it *oestrus*.”  
>   
> Pliny XI.16: “Sometimes larger bees are born at the outer edges of the honeycombs, and these drive away the others. This affliction is called *oestrus*.” Again: “It has been decided to call it *asilus* or *tabanus*; its sting is in its mouth. Animals struck by it are driven as though into madness.” From this the word was transferred to sexual frenzies. Hesychius: *οἶστρος, ἐρεθισμός, ὁ λεγόμενος μῦα· μανία ἀφροδισίων, κύρωσις, ἔκκαυσις*—“gadfly; irritation; the so-called fly; sexual madness, excitation, inflammation.” Septimius therefore calls Catholics who condemn dry fasts and restore the Church to repentant adulterers “Psychics”; and he calls the Shepherd depicted on their cup an idol of drunkenness and a gadfly of lust, because, their bodies being well tended, they are supposedly struck as by frenzy and rush into adultery and every kind of wantonness, invited by the flattering confidence that repentance may be obtained. How bitter are these insults against Catholics! **RIG.**

from which you would drink nothing more gladly than the sheep of second repentance.

> **Note [Col. 1000D]: “The sheep of second repentance.”** That is, the lustful sheep, the adulterous sheep: the hope of obtaining pardon even for adultery committed after baptism. **RIG.**

But I drink from the writings of that Shepherd who cannot be broken. John immediately presents him to me together with the washing and duty of repentance, saying:

> “Produce fruits worthy [Col. 1001A] of repentance, and do not say, ‘We have Abraham as our father’”

—lest, of course, they should once again derive inducements to sin from the favor enjoyed by their fathers—

> “for God is able from these stones to raise up children for Abraham” (Matt. 3:8–9).

Thus it follows for us too

> **Note [Col. 1001D]: “So that those who sin up to this point.”** “Up to this point” means “now no longer.” **RIG.**

that those who sin only up to this point must produce worthy fruit of repentance. For what ripens from repentance except the accomplishment of amendment?

But even if pardon is instead the fruit of repentance, pardon too cannot exist without the cessation of sin. Thus the cessation of sin is the root of pardon, so that pardon may be the fruit of repentance.

## CHAPTER XI

Thereafter, as regards the Gospel, the question of the parables has now been examined. But if by his deeds the Lord also performed some such act on behalf of sinners—[Col. 1001B] as when he permitted the sinful woman even to touch his body, while she washed his feet with tears, wiped them with her hair, and with ointment inaugurated his burial (Luke 7:37ff.); or when he revealed even who he was, something he did not readily disclose to anyone, to the Samaritan woman now in her sixth marriage—not an adulteress, but a prostitute (John 4:7ff.)—nothing from this assists our adversaries, even if he had already granted Christians pardon of their offenses.

For now we say: this is permitted to the Lord alone; today the power of pardon belongs to him. Yet concerning those times when he acted on earth, we determine that it establishes no precedent against us if pardon was granted to sinners, even Jewish ones.

For Christian discipline is reckoned from the renewal of the Testament and, as we stated beforehand, from the redemption of the flesh—that is, from the Lord’s Passion. Before [Col. 1001C] the order of faith was discovered, no one was perfect; before Christ was taken back into heaven, no one was a Christian; before the Holy Spirit was manifested from heaven as the one who determines that discipline, no one was holy.
