“Further,” he says (Gal. III, IV, V, and VI), “I speak in human [Col. 0475D] terms: while we were children, we were placed under the elements of the world, to serve them.” Yet this is [Col. 0476A] not spoken in merely human terms, for it is not an illustration but the truth. For what child—child, of course, in understanding, as the nations are—is not subject to the elements of the world, which he regards as God [variant: “receives,” al.]? What he does [variant: “was,” Jun.], however, is that although he had spoken in human terms, nevertheless no one rejects or adds provisions to a human covenant. For by the example of an enduring human covenant he was defending the divine one.

“The promises were spoken to Abraham and to his seed. He did not say, ‘and to seeds,’ as though referring to many, but ‘and to your seed,’ as referring to one, who is Christ.” Let Marcion’s sponge blush—except that it is superfluous for me to reconsider what he removed, since he is more powerfully refuted from what he preserved.

“But when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son”: assuredly that God who is also the God of the very times of which the age consists; who also ordered [Col. 0476B] the signs of the times—the suns, moons, constellations, and stars; and who both appointed and foretold the revelation of his Son for the end of the times (Isa. II, 2): “In the last days the mountain of the Lord will be manifest”; and, according to Joel (Joel II, 28): “In the last days I will pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh.” It belonged to him to wait for the time to be fulfilled, to whom the end of time belonged just as its beginning did.

But that idle god, responsible neither for any work nor for any proclamation, and therefore for no time either—what did he ever do to bring about the fulfillment of time, or even [variant: “or now,” Lat.; Col. 0476D] to endure the time that had to be fulfilled? If nothing, it is quite pointless that he should have waited upon the Creator’s times, thereby serving the Creator.

For what purpose did he send his Son? “To redeem those who were under the law”: that is, to make (Isa. XL, 4) [Col. 0476C] “crooked things into a straight way, and rough places into gentle ways” [variants: “the crooked way,” Fran.; “smooth ways,” Jun.], according to Isaiah; so that old things might pass away and new things arise: “A new law from Zion, and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem” (Isa. II, 3); and “so that we might receive adoption as sons”—we, assuredly, the nations, who were not sons. For “he too will be a light of the nations,” and “in his name the nations will hope.”

> **Note [Cols. 0475D–0476D].** Thus, to make it certain that we are sons of God. This is how he explains the Apostle’s words: “Now because you are sons of God.” The reading of all the Latin witnesses, which add “of God,” is confirmed; this phrase is lacking among the Greeks, according to the consistent reading of the Greek Fathers. Conversely, the Author reads “our hearts,” in accordance with the Greek ἡμῶν (*hēmōn*, “our”), with Blessed Ambrose and Jerome in his Commentary likewise agreeing, whereas others read “your hearts.” Moreover, Rhenanus rightly understands “is” there when the Author says, “whose grace.” —PAM.

Thus, to make it certain that we are sons of God, “he sent his Spirit into our hearts, crying, ‘Abba, Father.’” For he says (Joel II, 28): “In the last days I will pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh.” Whose grace is this, unless it is his whose promise of grace it also was? Who is the Father, unless he who is also the Maker?

After such riches, therefore, there was to be no return to the weak and beggarly elements. Among the Romans too, moreover, the first letters are commonly called “elements.” He was not, therefore, seeking to turn them away from the God of the cosmic elements [Col. 0476D] by disparaging those elements. Even though he had said above, “If, then, you serve those who by nature are not gods,” he was censuring [variant: “censures,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] the error [Col. 0477A] of physical—that is, natural—superstition, which regards the elements as God; yet even so he was not reproaching the God of the elements.

But he himself makes clear which “elements” he wishes to be understood: namely, the first letters of the law. “You observe days, and months, and seasons, and years, and Sabbaths, I suppose, and pure suppers, and fasts, and great days.”

> **Note [Col. 0477C].** “Pure suppers. Days of Preparation. ἐν τῇ ἔκτῃ τῶν ἡμερῶν” (“on the sixth day of the days”). Irenaeus interprets it thus, book I, chapter 10. Glosses: “pure supper,” προσάββατον (*prosabbaton*, “the day before the Sabbath”). —RIG.

For it was necessary to cease from these also, just as from circumcision, by the decrees of the Creator, who says through Isaiah as well (Isa. I, 14): “Your new moons and Sabbaths and great day I will not endure; my soul hates your fast, festivals, and ceremonies”; and through Amos (Amos V, 21): “I hate and reject your ceremonies, and I will not smell [variant: “be adored,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] in your assemblies.” Likewise through Hosea (Hos. II, 11): “I will turn away all her joys, her ceremonies, her Sabbaths, her new moons, and all her assemblies.”

[Col. 0477B] “He abolished what he himself had instituted,” you say? He, more properly than anyone else. Or [variant: “But,” Par.], if someone else abolished them, then that other supported the Creator’s judgment by taking away the things the Creator too had condemned. But the question why the Creator annulled his own laws does not belong to this passage. It is enough that we have proved he would annul them, so that it may be established that the Apostle determined nothing against the Creator, since the removal [variant: “abolition,” al.] of the law itself comes from the Creator.

But [variant: “And,” Par.] just as thieves commonly let something fall from their booty as evidence, so I believe Marcion left behind the final reference to Abraham—the one above all that ought to have been removed—even if he partly altered [variant: “swept away,” Lat.; Col. 0478C] it:

“For Abraham had two children, one by the slave woman and the other by the free woman. But the one [Col. 0477C] by the slave woman was born according to the flesh; the one by the free woman, through [Col. 0478A] the promise. These things are allegorical,” that is, they portend something else. “For these are the two covenants,” or two manifestations, as we find it interpreted: one from Mount Sinai, giving birth into slavery in the synagogue of the Jews according to the law; the other [variant readings: alium, Rhen. Seml. Obert.; alterum, Lat.] “above every principality,” giving birth to power, dominion, and every name that is named, not only in this age but also in the age to come. This is our mother, into whom we have promised the holy Church. Therefore he adds: “Consequently, brothers, we are not children of the slave woman but of the free woman.”

He plainly showed that the noble freedom of Christianity possesses an allegorical mystery in Abraham’s son born of the free woman, just as the legal servitude of Judaism does in the son of the slave woman; and thus that both dispensations belong to that God in whose writings we find [Col. 0478B] the delineation [“delineation,” Pam.; variant: “declension,” Jun.] of both dispensations.

When he says, “With the freedom by which Christ set us free,” does he not establish as the manumitter the one who had been Lord?

> **Note [Cols. 0477C–0478D].** “For not even Galba manumitted another man’s slaves.” This is certainly how the manuscripts read, and nothing should be changed. Marcion’s heresy was to say that Christ was not the Son of God the Creator, and that the God of the Old Testament, or Law, was one God, while the God of the New Testament, or Gospel, was another. The God of the Law was the Creator, fierce and savage; the God of the Gospel, however, had created nothing and was only good and exceedingly indulgent, having freed us through his Christ from the yoke of that savage and tyrannical God. Tertullian refutes this doctrine with many arguments, among them the fact that Paul, in the Epistle to the Galatians, calls Christ our manumitter. Not even Marcion rejected that Epistle. From this it necessarily follows that we were once in servitude under God the Creator, and that Christ is the Son of that God the Creator, since no one can be called or be a manumitter unless he was Lord, nor can anyone be a freedman unless he served in lawful servitude under the one who manumits him.

> We, therefore, as the same Epistle teaches, were for a time servants under the Law of the Old Testament; afterward, however, we were freed from that servitude to the Law by Christ’s beneficence, and are no longer under the Law but under the Gospel. Accordingly, Tertullian argues thus: if we acknowledge Christ as the manumitter, we must necessarily acknowledge that this same Christ belongs to that Creator whose Law it was from which we have been released, having obtained freedom. For unless Christ belonged to the Creator, he would not lawfully have freed us from the servitude in which we served under the Creator, beneath the harshness of his law. We served under the Creator; therefore the manumitter had to come from the Creator. For one who manumits another man’s slaves accomplishes nothing.

> Here, in his customary fashion, Tertullian sprinkles in a little wit. “For,” he says, “not even Galba manumitted another man’s slaves, though he was more likely to release free men.” He had read in Suetonius Tranquillus—an author familiar to him and cited by him more than once—that Nero’s death afforded such public rejoicing that the common people ran throughout the city wearing the caps of freedmen, as though delivered from the grim yoke of slavery. Galba, Nero’s successor, began his reign under the following auspices: as though preparing to conduct a manumission, he mounted the tribunal and, after setting before himself very many images of people condemned and killed by Nero, began to lament the condition of the times; while in that posture or action, he was acclaimed Emperor by the bystanders. Tertullian therefore says that not even Galba, in that state of affairs, manumitted another man’s slaves. He adds, “though he was more likely to release free men”—that is, to restore liberty to Roman citizens who had endured shameful slavery under Nero. —RIG.

“For not even Galba manumitted another man’s slaves, though he was more likely to release free men” [variant: “not Galba,” omitting “even,” Jun.]. Freedom, therefore, will be granted by him under whom there had been the servitude of the law. And rightly so.

> **Note [Col. 0478D].** “It was not fitting for those who had been manumitted to be bound again to the yoke of slavery.” These words are taken from chapter V of Galatians according to all editions. Moreover, he correctly translates the Greek ἐνέχεσθαι (*enechesthai*) as “to be bound.” In the words “he persisted in erasing circumcision itself, the mark of servitude,” he alludes to what follows in the same passage: “Behold, I Paul say to you that if you are circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing,” and so forth through verse 6. Epiphanius, in his third refutation of Marcion from the Epistle to the Galatians, cites the intervening words: “And I testify again that every circumcised man is a debtor to fulfill the whole law.” —PAMELIUS.

It was not fitting for those who had been manumitted to be bound again to the yoke of slavery—that is, of the law—now that the Psalm (Ps. II) had been fulfilled: “Let us burst their bonds and cast their yoke away from us,” after the rulers had gathered together against the Lord and against his Christ.

Thus, for those released from servitude, he persisted in erasing the very mark of servitude [Col. 0478C], circumcision, on the authority, of course, of the prophetic proclamation [Col. 0479A], remembering what was said through Jeremiah (Jer. IV, 4): “And circumcise the foreskins [variant: “foreskin,” al.] of your heart.” Moses too says (Deut. X, 16): “You shall circumcise your hardness of heart”—that is, not the flesh.

Finally, if he came from another god and was excluding circumcision, why does he also deny that uncircumcision has any value in Christ, just as circumcision does not? For he ought to have preferred circumcision’s rival, which he was attacking, if he came from a god who was the rival of circumcision. But because circumcision and uncircumcision alike were assigned to the one God, both were therefore without effect in Christ because of the preeminence of faith: that faith of which it was written (Isa. XLII, 4), “And in his name the nations will believe”; that faith which, by saying it is perfected through love, he thereby also shows to belong to the Creator.

For whether [Col. 0479B] he means love toward God, this too belongs to the Creator (Deut. VI): “You shall love God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength”; or love toward one’s neighbor: “and your neighbor as yourself”—this too belongs to the Creator.

“The one who troubles you will bear judgment.” From what God? From the supremely good one? But he does not judge. From the Creator? But neither will he condemn an advocate of circumcision. Yet if there is no other who judges except the Creator, then the defenders of the law will now be condemned only by the one who himself ordained that it should cease.

What if he also confirms the law in the respect in which he ought? “For the whole law,” he says, “has been fulfilled among you” [“among” omitted in Fran.]: “‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’” Or, if he wishes “has been fulfilled” to be understood as meaning that it is no longer to be fulfilled, then he does not wish me to love my neighbor as myself, since this too would have ceased along with the law. But one must persevere [Col. 0479C] in this commandment forever.

Therefore even by its adversary the Creator’s law was approved. It obtained from him not a diminution but an abridgment, its sum being reduced to a single commandment. Yet this too is more fitting for no one than for its Author. Indeed, when he says, “Bear one another’s burdens, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ,” if this cannot be done unless one loves one’s neighbor [variant: “a neighbor for oneself” (sibi), Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] as oneself, it is evident that “You shall love your neighbor [variant: “the neighbor to you” (tibi), Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] as yourself,” which is heard in “Bear one another’s burdens,” is the law of Christ, which is the Creator’s law. Thus Christ belongs to the Creator, since the Creator’s law is Christ’s law.

“You are mistaken; God is not mocked.” Yet Marcion’s god can indeed be mocked, since he knows neither how to become angry nor how to take vengeance. “Whatever a man has sown [variant: “will have sown” (severit), Rhen. Seml. Ober.], that he will also reap.” Therefore [Col. 0479D] he holds out a God of retribution and judgment. “Let us not grow weary in doing good”; and: “While we have time, let us [Col. 0480A] do good.” Deny that the Creator commanded people to do good, and then a different doctrine may belong to a different divinity. But if he proclaims retribution, both the harvest of corruption and the harvest of life will come from the same God. “In its own time we shall reap,” because Ecclesiastes too [variant: “Ecclesiasticus,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] says (Eccl. III, 17): “There will be a time for every matter.”

Moreover, “the world has been crucified to me,” a servant of the Creator—but not the God of the world; “and I to the world”—but not to the God of the world. By “the world” he meant its manner of life, in relation to which, by renouncing it, we are mutually transformed and die to one another; he calls the persecutors of Christ “the world.”

But when he adds that he “bears the marks of Christ in his body” (bodily marks, assuredly, are appropriate), he has thereby professed Christ’s flesh to be no longer imaginary but true and solid, for he displays its bodily marks.

> **Note [Cols. 0479D–0480D].** “That he bears the marks of Christ in his body,” and so forth. From the fact that, to prove Christ’s true and solid flesh, he adds that he displays its bodily marks, the Author appears to take “marks” not as mere brands of the kind with which slaves were marked by their masters so that they could be recognized as belonging to them, but as wounds such as Christ too bore bodily in himself—as though he were saying that he had suffered the same afflictions as Christ. In this, however, his reading differs: he read “of Christ,” where others have “of the Lord Jesus.” —PAM.

# CHAPTER V

[Col. 0480B]

The preliminary treatment of the preceding Epistle led me not to reconsider its salutation, since I was certain it could be reconsidered elsewhere: namely, the common and identical salutation in all the Epistles. It does not, of course, prescribe “health” to those to whom he writes, but “grace and peace.”

I will not ask what a destroyer of Judaism has to do with a still-Jewish custom; for even today the Jews greet one another in the name of peace, and formerly in the Scriptures they saluted in the same way. But I understand him to have defended, by performing his office, the Creator’s proclamation (Isa. LII): “How timely are the feet of those who proclaim good things, who proclaim peace!” For as a proclaimer of good things—that is, of God’s grace—he knew that grace should be placed before peace.

In announcing these things as coming “from God our Father and the Lord Jesus,” he uses common names, names also compatible with our mystery. I do not think it can be discerned which [variant: “why,” Fran.] God the Father and which Lord Jesus is being proclaimed except from their attributes [Col. 0480C]—which claimant they fit better.

First, I lay it down as a rule that no other should be acknowledged as Father and Lord than the Creator and Founder both of man and of the universe. Further, the name “Lord” accrues to the Father also because of his power, a name that the Son too receives through the Father.

Next, grace and peace belong not only to him by whom they were proclaimed, but to him who had been offended. For there can be no grace except where there has been offense, nor peace except where there has been war. Both the people, through transgression of discipline, and the whole human race, through disregard of nature, had sinned and rebelled against the Creator. But Marcion’s god, both because [variant: qua, Latin.] he was unknown, could not be offended, and because [variant: qua, Latin.] he does not know how to become angry. [Col. 0480D] What grace, then, can come from one who was not offended? What peace from one against whom no rebellion had occurred?

> **Note [Cols. 0480D–0482D].** He says that the cross of Christ is foolishness to those who are perishing, but power, and so forth. Here too the Author differs from the Apostle, perhaps following Marcion. For in Marcion’s text there is first: “The word of the cross of Christ”; then, “to those who perish, but to us who are being saved”; and it does not contain “and wisdom.” When the Author then adds from the Apostle, “I will render understanding void,” the Greek is ἀθετήσω (*athetēsō*, “I will set aside”), for which the Vulgate translator, and Epiphanius as well, has “I will reject.” Epiphanius likewise hurls this against Marcion in his first refutation, from the Epistle to the Corinthians. —PAM.

He says that “the cross of Christ is foolishness to those who are perishing,” [Col. 0481A] but “the power and wisdom of God” to those who will obtain salvation. To show whence this came, he adds [variant: “something,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.]: “For it is written: ‘I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and render the understanding of the prudent void.’”

If these words belong to the Creator, and those matters [“those matters” omitted in Rhen. Seml. Ober.] concerning the cross were reckoned as foolishness [variant: “he reckons them foolishness,” Pam.], then both the cross and, through the cross, Christ will belong to the Creator, by whom that which concerns the cross was foretold. Or if the Creator, as a rival, therefore took away wisdom so that the cross of Christ—his adversary’s Christ, of course—might be reckoned foolishness, how could the Creator have pronounced anything concerning the cross of a Christ who was not his, when he did not know him at the time he made the proclamation?

Moreover, why is it that, under the supremely good Lord of lavish mercy, some receive salvation because they believe the cross to be the power and wisdom of God, while others receive destruction [Col. 0481B] because Christ’s cross is reckoned foolishness by them, unless the Creator had punished some offense committed by both the people and the human race with the loss of wisdom and understanding?

What follows will confirm this, when he says: “Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world?” And when he here adds the reason: “Because the world, in the wisdom of God, did not understand God [variant: “the Lord,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] through wisdom, God deemed it good, through the foolishness of proclamation, to save those who believe.”

> **Note [Col. 0482D].** “God deemed it good.” Thus he translates the Greek εὐδόκησεν ὁ θεὸς (*eudokēsen ho theos*, “God was well pleased”). For just as authors say “to account it good,” and “to regard it as fair and good,” so he said “to deem it good.” —RHEN.

But first I shall discuss “the world,” since the most subtle heretics interpret “the world” here, most emphatically, as “the Lord of the world.” We, however, understand it as the human being who is in the world, in accordance with the ordinary form of human speech, by which we very often put the container for what is contained. “The Circus shouted,” “the Forum spoke,” and “the Basilica roared”—that is, the people who were conducting affairs in those places.

[Col. 0481C] Therefore, because man—not the God of the world—did not know God through wisdom, though he ought to have known him—the Jew through the wisdom of the Scriptures, and every nation through the wisdom of his works—the same God, who had not been acknowledged [variant: adagnitus, al.] in his own wisdom, consequently resolved to strike back at human wisdom by means of foolishness, saving all who believed in the foolish proclamation of the cross.

“For Jews desire signs,” although they ought already to have been certain about God; “and Greeks seek wisdom”—their own wisdom, of course, not God’s wisdom, which they set forth. But if a new God were being proclaimed, what offense had the Jews committed by desiring signs through which they might believe? Or the Greeks by pursuing wisdom in which they might more readily believe?

Thus the requital itself against Jews and Greeks confirms that God is both jealous and judge, since by a retribution marked by rivalry and judgment he made foolish [Col. 0481D] the wisdom of the world. But if both the grounds of the argument and the Scriptures brought to bear belong to the same God, then when the Apostle discusses the God who was not understood, he assuredly teaches that the Creator is to be understood.

Likewise, [Col. 0482A] when he proclaims Christ as “a stumbling block to the Jews,” he seals the Creator’s prophecy concerning him, spoken through Isaiah (Isa. XXVIII, 15): “Behold, I have laid in Zion a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense.” “The rock, moreover, was Christ”—even Marcion preserves this.

“What is foolish in God” and wiser than men, if not the cross and death of Christ? “What is weak in God” and stronger than man, if not the birth and flesh of God? But if Christ was neither born of a virgin nor constructed of flesh, and therefore did not truly suffer either the cross or death, there is [variant: “there was,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] nothing foolish or weak in him.

Nor, then, did “God choose the foolish things of the world to confound wisdom” [variant reading: sapientia (“wisdom”), Par.]; nor did “God choose the weak things of the world to confound the strong”; nor “the dishonorable, the least, and the contemptible things—the things that are not,” that is, that do not truly exist, “to confound the things that are,” that is, that truly [Col. 0482B] exist.

For nothing ordained by God [“is” omitted in some copies] is truly insignificant, ignoble, or contemptible, but only what is ordained by man. [Col. 0482B] Yet in the Creator’s dispensation even the old ordinances [variant: “true things,” Lat.] may be reckoned foolish, weak, dishonorable, paltry, and contemptible. What is more foolish or weaker than God’s demand for bloody sacrifices and smoke-reeking burnt offerings? What is weaker than the purification of little vessels and beds [variant: “platters,” Ciaccon. Jun.]? What is more dishonorable than a further disfigurement of flesh already causing shame? What is so lowly as the pronouncement of retaliation? What is so contemptible as the restriction of foods? Every heretic, so far as I know, ridicules the whole Old Testament.

“For God chose the foolish things of the world to confound wisdom” [variant reading: sapientia (“wisdom”), Par.]. Marcion’s god did nothing of the kind, because he does not even display rivalry by refuting contraries with contraries, [Col. 0482C] “so that no flesh may boast”; so that, “as it is written, ‘Let the one who boasts boast in the Lord.’” In which Lord? Assuredly in the one who commanded this—unless the Creator commanded him to boast in Marcion’s god [variant: “the god” (Deum), Rhen. Seml. Ober.].

# CHAPTER VI

Thus, through all these statements (1 Cor. II and III), he shows which God’s wisdom he speaks “among the perfect”: namely, the wisdom of him who took away the wisdom of the wise and rendered void the understanding of the prudent; who made foolish the wisdom of the world, choosing its foolish things and appointing them for salvation.

He says that this wisdom had been hidden: it had been present in foolish, paltry, and dishonorable things and had also lain concealed beneath figures, allegories, and riddles, afterward to be revealed in Christ, whom the Creator appointed as a light of the nations when he promised through the voice of Isaiah [Col. 0482D] that he would uncover invisible and hidden treasures.

For that the god who performed absolutely no work in which he might be thought to have hidden anything should have hidden something is [Col. 0483A] quite incredible. If he himself existed, he could not have remained hidden, much less any mysteries belonging to him.

But both the Creator himself and his mysteries were known: they were, of course, openly enacted among Israel, but obscured as to their meanings. In these the wisdom of God lay hidden among the perfect, to be declared in its proper time, although it had been determined in God’s purpose before the ages.

Whose ages, unless the Creator’s? For if the ages too are constructed from times, while times are joined together from days, months, and years, and days, months, and years are marked by the Creator’s suns, moons, and stars, which were appointed by him for this purpose—“For they shall be,” he says (Gen. I), “for signs of months and years”—it is evident that the ages too belong to the Creator, and that everything said to have been determined before the ages belongs to no other than him to whom the ages themselves belong.

> **Note [Col. 0483D].** “For they shall be,” he says, “for signs of months and years.” That is, the motions of moon and sun distinguish days, years, ages, and times. Those who enlist this passage from Genesis I in defense of astrology misuse it; this interpretation argues against them. —LE PR.

Or let Marcion prove [Col. 0483B] the ages of his own god: let him also show the world itself in which ages may be reckoned, a vessel, as it were, of the times; and let him show some signs or their origin [variants: Ortaneorum, Rhen. Seml. Obert.; notas eorum (“their marks”), Wouw.; ortam eorum, Jun.; the readings Ortaneorum and ortam eorum are uncertain]. If he demonstrates nothing, I return to make this further point.

Why, moreover, did he determine our glory before the Creator’s ages? He could have been thought to have determined it before the ages if he had revealed it when the age was introduced. But since he does so only after almost all the Creator’s ages have already been prolonged [variant: “extended,” al.], he determined it “before the ages” in vain, rather than “within the ages,” when he was going to reveal it almost after the ages. For eagerness in determining something does not belong to one who delayed revealing it.

Both are fitting for the Creator, however: both to have determined it before the ages and to have revealed it at the end of the ages, because throughout the intervening span of the ages he [Col. 0483C] ministered beforehand [variant: “foreshadowed,” Jun.] in figures, riddles, and allegories what he both determined and revealed.

But because he adds concerning our glory that “none of the rulers of this age knew it—for if they had known it, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory”—the heretic argues that the rulers of this age fastened to the cross the Lord, that is, the Christ of another God, so that this accusation too may rebound [variant: “be redirected,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] upon the Creator.

Yet for one to whom we have shown [variant: “shall have shown,” Rhen. Seml. Ober.] above the ways in which our glory must be assigned to the Creator, it should have been established beforehand that this glory, since it had been hidden with the Creator, was rightly unknown even to all the Creator’s powers and authorities [variant: “all men,” Rig. Venet.]. For servants are not permitted to know their masters’ counsels—still less those apostate angels and the prince of transgression himself, the devil, whom I would all the more insist were alienated, through their fault, from all knowledge of the Creator’s dispensations.

But now it is not even appropriate for me to interpret [Col. 0483D] “the rulers of this age” as the Creator’s powers and authorities, because the Apostle ascribes ignorance to them. Yet [Col. 0484A] according to our Gospel (Matt. IV), the devil too recognized Jesus during the temptation; and according to the commonly held canonical record (Luke IV, 34), the unclean spirit knew that he was the Holy One of God, that he was called Jesus, and that he had come for their destruction.

> **Note [Col. 0484D].** “According to our Gospel”: this distinguishes it from Marcion’s corrupted codex. These things are related in Matthew IV. A little later comes: “For it is written in my text that Satan entered Judas.” That passage is from Luke XXII, which Marcion had cut out of “my” text. —LE PR.

Even the parable of the strong armed man, whom another stronger man overcame and whose goods he seized (Luke XI), if in Marcion’s reading it is understood of the Creator, means that the Creator could no longer have remained ignorant of the God of glory, since he was overcome by him; nor could he have fastened to the cross one against whom he had no power.

It remains, according to my position, credible that the Creator’s powers and authorities, though they knew the God of glory, crucified their own Christ, since through desperation and an excess of malice even the most wicked servants do not hesitate to kill their masters. For it is written [Col. 0484B] in my text (Luke XXII, 3) that Satan entered Judas.

According to Marcion, however, the Apostle does not permit ignorance of the Lord of glory to be ascribed in this passage to the Creator’s powers, because he plainly does not intend “the rulers of this age” to mean those powers. If he does not appear to have spoken of spiritual rulers, then he spoke of secular rulers: assuredly of the people that held primacy—not among the nations—and of that people’s own rulers—King Herod, Pilate as well, and the Roman magistracy by which the higher principality of this age presided.

Thus, when the opposing party’s arguments are demolished, our interpretations are built up. But do you still wish our glory to belong to your god, and to have been hidden with him? Then why do God and the Apostle still rely on [variant: “use,” Lat.] the same instrument? [Col. 0484C] What has he to do everywhere with the sayings of the Prophets?

“For who has known the mind of the Lord? And who has been his counselor?” That is Isaiah (Isa. XL, 13). What has he to do even with the examples belonging to our God? For when he declares himself a wise architect, we find that the Creator, through Isaiah (Isa. III, 2), indicated by this phrase one who makes divine discipline manifest: “For I will remove [variant: “also remove,” Par.],” he says, “from Judaea, among other things, the wise architect.”

Was not [variant reading: “not” (Non), al.] Paul himself then destined to be removed from Judaea—that is, from Judaism—for the building up of Christianity, since he would lay the one foundation, which is Christ? For concerning this foundation too the Creator says through the same prophet (Isa. XXVIII, 15): “Behold,” he says, “I lay in the foundations of Zion a precious and honorable stone, and whoever believes in it will not be confounded.”

Unless, indeed [si (“if”) omitted in Par.], God was professing himself an architect of an earthly work, [Col. 0484D] so that he was not signifying his own Christ, who was to become the foundation of those who believe in him. Upon that foundation, according as each person builds [Col. 0485A] a worthy or unworthy doctrine, if his work is tested by fire and if his reward is repaid to him through fire, that fire belongs to the Creator, because your superstructure is judged [variant: “indicated,” Par. Fran.] through fire—assuredly the superstructure raised upon [“upon” inserted in Rhen. Seml. Oberth.] his own foundation, that is, upon his own Christ.

> **Note [Col. 0485D].** “If his reward is repaid to him through fire.” Almost everyone explains this passage of the Apostle as referring to the fire of Purgatory; among them Francis Turrianus, S.J., discusses it most extensively in his *Defense of the Apostolic Canons*. —LE PR.

“Do you not know that you are God’s temple and that the Spirit of God dwells in you?” If man—his substance, workmanship, image, likeness, flesh through earth, and soul through the Creator’s breath—belongs to the Creator, then Marcion’s god dwells wholly [variant: “your,” Par. Fran.] in another’s property [variant: “Marcion,” Par. Fran.], if we are not the Creator’s temple [variant: “if we are the Creator’s temple,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.].

“If anyone destroys God’s temple, he will be destroyed”—assuredly by the God of the temple. In holding out an avenger, he will be holding out [variant: “you will be holding out,” Rhen. Seml. Ober.] the Creator.

“Become fools, that you may be wise.” Why? “For the wisdom of this world is foolishness before God.” Before what God? Even if nothing in our preceding interpretations [variant: “up to this interpretation,” Par. Fran.] has decided this point beforehand, it is well that [Col. 0485B] here too he supports it: “For it is written, ‘He catches the wise in their wickedness.’” And again: “The Lord knows the thoughts of the wise, that they are futile.”

For it will have been established by us as a general rule that he could not have used any saying belonging to the God whom he was bound to destroy, unless he were teaching on behalf of that God.

“Therefore,” he says, “let no one boast in man.” This too accords with the Creator’s discipline (Jer. XVII, 5): “Wretched is the man who places his hope in man”; and (Ps. CXVII): “It is good to trust in God rather than to trust in man” [variant: “men,” Rhen. Seml. Oberth.]. So too with boasting (1 Cor. IV, X).
