## CHAPTER XVI

**First, that our faith owes obedience to the Apostle, who forbids disputation when he prohibited meeting a heretic after one correction.**

## CHAPTER XVII

## CHAPTER XIX

**The sole matter for dispute, therefore, is this: to whom the faith belongs; whose the Scriptures are; and from whom, through whom, and to whom the discipline by which people become Christians was handed down.**

Therefore, no appeal is to be made to the Scriptures, nor is the contest to be established in matters in which the victory is either nonexistent, uncertain, or equally uncertain [Note: [Col. 0031C] From traces of an ancient manuscript, Seml. and Pam. read “not very uncertain,” or “not very certain.”]. Whose are the Scriptures? From whom, through whom, when, and to whom was the discipline handed down by which people become Christians? [Note: [Col. 0031C] “For where it becomes apparent that the truth exists.” A notable passage in favor of Catholics, because among them is found the truth of the Scriptures and their interpretations, and of all Christian traditions. ## CHAPTER XX

**But Christ handed down to the Apostles the one doctrine of the same faith; they proclaimed it to the nations and founded churches in each city. From these the other churches daily borrow what makes them churches, so that all these many and great churches are that one first Church derived from the Apostles, from which they all come.**

Our Lord Jesus Christ—allow me for the moment to say, whoever he is: the Son of whatever God, man and God composed of whatever substance, teacher of whatever faith, and promiser of whatever reward—while he lived upon earth himself declared what he was, what he had been, what will of the Father he administered, and what he determined that man must do. He declared it either openly to the people or privately to his disciples. From among these he had chosen twelve principal men to remain at his side, appointing them as teachers destined for the nations [Note: [Col. 0031D] “Teachers destined for the nations.” For there was no part of the world, however remote in distance or barbarous in the savagery of its customs, into which the glory of Christ did not most gloriously penetrate. Irenaeus, Justin, Clement, and Origen have treated this matter sufficiently. To this pertains Isaiah’s astonished exclamation in chapter 66, “Who has heard such things?” etc., and that of the royal Prophet: “Their sound has gone out into all the earth.” LAC.].

Accordingly, after one of them had been struck away, as he was departing [Note: [Col. 0031C] “Returning,” Semler. Pam.] to the Father following the resurrection, he commanded the remaining eleven to go and teach the nations, baptizing them [Note: [Col. 0032C] “Baptizing,” Seml.; variant spelling, Pam.] into the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Immediately, therefore, the Apostles—a name meaning “those who are sent”—took Matthias by lot as the twelfth in Judas’s place, on the authority of the prophecy contained in a psalm of David. Having received the promised power of the Holy Spirit for miracles and utterance, they first bore witness throughout Judaea [Note: [Col. 0031D] “First throughout Judaea.” No one disputes that the Church was first founded at Jerusalem from among the Jews; hence among pagan writers there is almost no distinction between Jews and Christians. LE PR.] to faith in Jesus Christ and established churches. Then, setting out into the world, they proclaimed to the nations the same doctrine of the same faith [Col. 0032B], and thereafter founded churches in every city.

From these churches the others subsequently borrowed—and daily continue to borrow—the shoot of faith and the seeds of doctrine, so that they may become churches. [Note: [Col. 0031D] “From whom the shoot of faith.” Those who have written on agriculture, Quintinus says, call them *traduces*, especially in vines, when several little branches spread from one trunk or one arm of a vine and are led to another place while remaining connected with one another; they do not appear to differ greatly from layers. By a particularly elegant metaphor, Tertullian here calls the transference of faith from one church to another a *tradux fidei*, “a shoot of faith.” The churches, that is, handed down the apostolic doctrine to one another, as though from hand to hand. PAM.] On this account these churches themselves are also reckoned [Note: [Col. 0032C] “Will be reckoned,” Seml.] apostolic, as offspring of the apostolic churches. Thus they are all first [Note: [Col. 0032C] “The first churches,” Seml. [Note: [Col. 0032D] “The reciprocal pledge of hospitality.” With elegance, Tertullian, an inventor of words, fashioned the verb *contesserare* from *tessera*, and from it *contesseratio*. It signifies a more intimate association made by means of a token. For among the ancients the *tessera* was a token of hospitality, and if someone happened to meet another and showed it, he was immediately recognized. RHEN.] These rights are governed by no other principle than the one transmission of the same sacrament. [Note: [Col. 0032D] “The one transmission of the same sacrament.” That is, of the same creed or rule, the same faith. ## CHAPTER XXI

**From this he therefore formulates another prescription: no preachers are to be accepted except those whom Christ appointed; and consequently that doctrine is to be accepted which undoubtedly maintains what the Church received from the Apostles both by living voice and by letters, the Apostles from Christ, and Christ from God. The testimony to the truth of our doctrine is therefore this: that we communicate with the apostolic churches.**

From this, therefore, we formulate our prescription. If the Lord Jesus Christ sent the Apostles to preach, no other preachers are to be accepted than those whom Christ appointed, because no one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son has revealed him; and the Son does not appear to have revealed him to anyone other than the Apostles whom he sent to preach—namely, to preach what he had revealed to them.

As for what they preached—that is, what Christ revealed to them—here too I prescribe that it must be proved in no other way than through those same churches which the Apostles themselves founded by preaching to them, both by living voice, as the saying goes, and afterward through letters. If these things are so, it follows that every doctrine agreeing with those apostolic churches, the mother-churches and sources of the faith, must be reckoned true, since it undoubtedly maintains what the churches received from the Apostles, the Apostles from Christ, and Christ from God. Every remaining doctrine [Note: [Col. 0033C] “Every doctrine, however,” in place of “every remaining doctrine,” Venet. Rig.] must be prejudged false if it savors of anything contrary to the truth of the churches, the Apostles, Christ, and God.

It remains, therefore, for us to demonstrate whether this doctrine of ours, whose rule we set forth above, is classified as belonging to the tradition of the Apostles, and [Note: [Col. 0033C] “From this very fact,” Pam. Seml.] by that very fact whether the other doctrines proceed from falsehood. We communicate with the apostolic churches, because our doctrine differs from theirs in no respect [Note: [Col. 0033C] “No doctrine differs,” Pam. Seml.]. This is the testimony of truth.

## CHAPTER XXII

**The Apostles, however—whatever the opposing party may customarily say to the contrary—both knew and handed down everything; for Christ deigned to explain all the Scriptures to them and sent the Holy Spirit to lead them into all truth.**

But because the proof is so readily available [Note: [Col. 0033C] “Then,” Seml.] that, if it were produced immediately [Col. 0034A], nothing would remain to be reconsidered, let us act for the moment as though it had not been produced by us [Note: [Col. 0033C] “As though produced.” That is, “just as though it had been produced.” EDD.], and allow the opposing party an opportunity to see whether they think they can advance anything to weaken this prescription.

They are accustomed to say that the Apostles did not know everything. [Note: [Col. 0033C] “That the Apostles did not know everything.” The heretics of our own time affirm this very thing about the Fathers. For Hyp. says in the *Method* that they necessarily remained ignorant of many things. Wittacherus says that they erred conspicuously in many matters. No one, so far as I know, has explicitly asserted this about the Apostles. Rather, Kimedon, in *On the Word of God*, fastens the charge upon the Catholics—by what right, let him see. Yet these phantoms are hardly dissimilar, as when Bullengerus says in the German *Decades* that the Apostles did not possess plenary authority. LAC.] Driven by the same madness with which they turn what is above downward [*susum jusum*] [Note: [Col. 0034C] Seml. and Pam. read “again” for *susum jusum*.] [Note: [Col. 0033D] “By which they turn it back again.” In Agobard’s copy the reading is, “By which they turn upward back again,” and this is confirmed by the Dijon manuscript. Augustine, *Tractate 8 on the Epistle of John*: “What is upward, you make downward; what is below, you make above. Do you wish to make God downward and yourself upward? In both cases they subject Christ to reproach [Note: [Col. 0034C] “Bring reproach upon,” Seml.], because he sent Apostles who were either insufficiently instructed or insufficiently straightforward.

What person of sound mind, then, can believe that those whom the Lord appointed as teachers were ignorant of anything, when he kept them inseparably with him in companionship, discipleship, and common life, and privately explained every obscure matter to them, saying that it had been granted to them to know the mysteries which the people were not permitted to understand? Was anything hidden [Col. 0034B] from Peter, [Note: [Col. 0033D] “Was anything hidden from Peter?” Simon Bar-Jona was called Peter by the Lord because he was the first, upon recognizing Christ, to declare: “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God.” This utterance was a profession of the true faith; hence Peter merited hearing that he was the rock upon which Christ would build his Church, for here Peter and rock are the same. RIG., as corrected by EDD.] who was called the rock upon which the Church was to be built (Matt. 16:18–19), who received the keys of the kingdom of heaven and the power of loosing and binding in heaven and on earth?

Was anything hidden from John as well [Note: [Col. 0034D] “Was anything hidden from John as well?” Jerome, preface to Matthew: “Last came John, Apostle and Evangelist, whom Jesus loved most greatly; reclining upon the Lord’s breast, he drank the purest streams of doctrine, and he alone merited hearing from the cross: ‘Behold your mother.’” RIG.], the Lord’s most beloved disciple, who reclined upon his breast, to whom alone the Lord pointed out Judas as the betrayer, and whom he entrusted to Mary as a son in his own place? What did he wish those men to remain ignorant of, to whom he displayed even his glory, as well as Moses and Elijah, and furthermore the voice of the Father from heaven? He did this not as though rejecting the others, but because every word shall stand upon three witnesses (Matt. 18:16).

Were even those men, then, ignorant to whom, after his resurrection, he deigned to explain all the Scriptures on the road? He had indeed once said: “I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now” (John 16:12–13). Yet by adding [Col. 0034C], “When that Spirit of truth comes, he will lead you into all truth,” he showed that those to whom he had promised the attainment of all truth through the Spirit of truth were ignorant of nothing. And he assuredly fulfilled his promise, as the Acts of the Apostles proves by recording the descent of the Holy Spirit.

## CHAPTER XXIII

**For in Peter it was not any ignorance that Paul rebuked, but his conduct.**

To brand the Apostles with some kind of ignorance, they therefore put forward the fact that Peter and those with him were rebuked by Paul. “They were so plainly deficient in something,” they say, “that from this it may also be inferred that a fuller knowledge could afterward have supervened, such as came to Paul and enabled him to rebuke his predecessors.”

Here too we can say to those who reject the Acts of the Apostles: you must first show who this Paul is, what he was before becoming an Apostle, and how he became an Apostle—especially since in other questions they make very extensive use of him. For if he himself professes that he became an Apostle after being a persecutor, this is not enough for anyone who believes only after examination [Note: [Col. 0035C] “To examine,” Venet.; Heuman reads, “Nothing is sufficient without examination.”], since not even the Lord gave testimony concerning himself.

[Note: [Col. 0035D] “But let them believe without the Scriptures.” Those who do not accept the Acts of the Apostles nevertheless believe that Paul was made an Apostle after being a persecutor, though once Acts has been rejected they possess no authentic Scripture attesting this. For if they say that they put faith in Paul when he writes this, Paul cannot be his own witness, since not even the Lord gave testimony concerning himself. But let their faith be so perverse that they believe some things without the Scriptures in order to believe other things against the Scriptures; nevertheless, let them teach, from the allegation that Peter was rebuked by Paul, that Paul introduced a different form of the Gospel beyond that which Peter and the others had previously delivered, etc. RIG.]

But let them believe [Note: [Col. 0036C] Others read “they will believe”; Jun. reads “indeed” for “without.”] without the Scriptures, so that they may believe against the Scriptures. Nevertheless, let them demonstrate, from their allegation that Peter was rebuked by Paul, that Paul introduced another form of the Gospel beyond the one Peter and the others had already delivered.

On the contrary [Note: [Col. 0036C] “And indeed,” Pam.; “But indeed,” Seml.], when Paul had been changed from persecutor into preacher, he was conducted to the brethren by brethren, as one of the brethren, and brought by them to those who had put on the faith from the Apostles. Then, as he himself relates, he went up to Jerusalem for the purpose of becoming acquainted with Peter, in accordance, of course, with the duty and right belonging to the same faith and preaching. For they would not have marveled that a persecutor had become a preacher if he preached something contrary to them; nor would they thereafter have glorified the Lord because Paul, formerly his adversary, had come to them. Accordingly, they gave him the right hand as a sign of harmony and agreement, and arranged among themselves a distribution of office, not a division of the Gospel—not that each should preach something different, but that each should preach to different people: Peter to the Circumcision and Paul to the nations. [Note: [Col. 0036D] “Peter to the Circumcision, Paul to the nations.” Certainly the Apostle of the Circumcision and that other Apostle of the Gentiles were each sent in a special manner to one of the two peoples, but not in such a way that one excluded the other from his own territory. For blessed Peter was also pastor and Apostle of the universal Church, while blessed Paul became all things to all people, Jews and Gentiles alike, that he might win them all for Christ. EDD., from the work of D. DE GOURCY.]

Moreover, if [Note: [Col. 0036D] “Peter was rebuked.” The enemies of the Roman See make a remarkable display of this rebuke of blessed Peter; for from it they think it proved that the Supreme Pontiff is truly liable to rebuke. This is entirely false, at least in matters of faith. For in such matters that κρηπίς (*krēpis*, “foundation”) of the Church can in no way err. In the present case, however, the error—if indeed there was one—concerned conduct, not faith. LE PR.] Peter was rebuked because, after having eaten with Gentiles, he afterward began to withdraw from their company out of regard for certain persons, this was assuredly a fault of conduct, not of preaching. For no other God than the Creator was thereby proclaimed, nor any other Christ than the one born of Mary, nor any other hope than the resurrection.

## CHAPTER XXIV

[Col. 0036B]

**And that the practice giving rise to the rebuke was adopted in view of particular times, persons, and circumstances.**

My condition is not so fortunate—or rather, not so unfortunate—that I should set the Apostles against one another. But because these most perverse men parade that rebuke for the purpose of making the earlier doctrine suspect, I shall answer as though on Peter’s behalf. Paul himself said that he had become all things to all people: a Jew to the Jews, a non-Jew to non-Jews, so that he might win everyone [Note: [Col. 0036C] “Might gain,” Semler.]. Thus, with regard to particular times, persons, and circumstances, they censured certain actions which they themselves likewise committed with regard to particular times, persons, and circumstances. It would be as though Peter were also to rebuke Paul because, although forbidding circumcision, he himself circumcised Timothy.

Let those who pass judgment upon the Apostles see to it. [Note: [Col. 0036D] “It is well that Peter is made equal to Paul even in martyrdom.” Do not suppose that these words are put forward directly by the learned man; the context alone shows that he seems to say them by way of concession, as though he were saying: Grant—although this must not in fact be granted—that Peter and Paul were not equals, or held differing opinions; nevertheless, it must be admitted that they were entirely equal in martyrdom, which they bravely suffered for Christ. LE PR.] It is well that Peter [Col. 0037A] is made equal to Paul even in martyrdom.

But even if [Note: [Col. 0037D] “Paul was caught up as far as the third heaven.” This had also become known to Lucian’s Trypho. For he undoubtedly describes Paul in these words: ἡνίκα δέ μοι Γαλιλαῖος ἐνέτυχεν ἀναφαλαντίας, ἐπίρρινος, εἰς τρίτον οὐρανὸν ἀεροβατήσας, καὶ τὰ κάλλιστα ἐκμεμαθηκώς, etc.—“when a Galilean met me, bald in front, hook-nosed, who had traveled through the air to the third heaven and had learned the finest things,” etc. RIG.] Paul was caught up as far as the third heaven and carried into paradise, where he heard certain things, these cannot be thought to have made him better instructed in another doctrine, since their very nature was such that they were to be disclosed to no human being. But if that unknown something passed into the awareness of any person, and some heresy affirms that it follows this teaching, then either Paul is guilty of betraying the secret, or else another person must be shown to have been caught up into paradise afterward and granted permission to utter what Paul was not allowed even to mutter.

## CHAPTER XXV

**Nor, indeed, did the Apostles preach some hidden gospel.**

But, as we have said, it is the same madness when they indeed acknowledge that the Apostles were ignorant of nothing and did not preach doctrines differing [Col. 0037B] from one another, yet refuse to admit [Note: [Col. 0037C] “But did not,” Pam. Seml.] that they revealed everything to everyone. For they say that the Apostles entrusted some things openly and to all, but others secretly and to a few. Their reason is that Paul too used these words to Timothy: “O Timothy, guard the deposit” [Note: [Col. 0037C] “Keep,” Rigalt.]. And again: “Keep the good deposit.”

What is this deposit? Is it something so secret [Note: [Col. 0037C] “So secret,” Fran. Paris.; “so suitable,” Seml.] that it should be reckoned as another doctrine? Or is it the charge of which Paul says: “This charge I entrust to you, son [Note: [Col. 0037C] “Little son,” Semler.] Timothy”? Likewise, is it the commandment of which he says: “I charge you before God, [Note: [Col. 0037D] ‘Who gives life to all things.’ Following Greek usage: ζωοποιοῦντος (*zōopoiountos*, ‘giving life’). Other manuscripts have ‘who justifies,’ as though the text read δικαιοῦντος (*dikaiountos*, ‘justifying’). LE PR.] who gives life [Note: [Col. 0037C] Others read “who justifies.”] to all things, and Jesus Christ, who testified the good confession before Pontius Pilate, that you keep the commandment” (1 Tim. 6:13)?

But what commandment and what charge? From what is written above and below it will be understood [Note: [Col. 0037C] “He had understood,” Seml.; Rhenanus conjectures “it was to be understood.”] that these words do not hint at some unknown matter belonging to a more remote doctrine [Note: [Col. 0037D] “Of a more remote doctrine,” Rhen.]. Rather, they emphatically command that no doctrine be admitted other than the one Timothy had heard from Paul himself—and, I think, openly [Note: [Col. 0038C] Seml., following Pam., deletes “openly.”]. “Before many witnesses,” he says (2 Tim. 2:2). If they refuse [Note: [Col. 0038C] “Wish,” Paris.; “he advises,” Pam. Seml.] to understand those many witnesses as the Church, it makes no difference, since nothing uttered before many witnesses could have been secret.

Nor, because Paul wished Timothy to entrust these things to faithful people capable of teaching others as well [Col. 0038A] (2 Tim. 2:2), is this statement to be interpreted [Note: [Col. 0038C] Pam. and Semler delete “to.”] as evidence for some hidden Gospel. For when he says “these things,” he means the matters about which he was presently writing. With regard to secret things, however—as matters absent but retained in his own consciousness—he would have said not “these things” but “those things.”
