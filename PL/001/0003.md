### On the continence of clerics

“How many men and women, then, in the ecclesiastical orders are esteemed for continence—those who have preferred to be wedded to God, who have established the honor of their flesh, and who have now dedicated themselves as children of that modesty, killing within themselves the desire of lust and all that could not be admitted within paradise. . . . . . . . Accordingly, among us it is prescribed more fully and explicitly that those chosen for the priestly order ought to have been married only once” (*On the Exhortation to Chastity*, vol. II, ch. 7, col. 922; ch. 13, col. 930).

### Man is the image of God through free will

“Man was not fashioned in the likeness of God in respect of his face, but in respect of the substance which he drew from God himself, that is, the soul, [Col. 0030B] which corresponds to the form of God and is marked by the freedom and power of its own choice. The law itself also confirmed this condition of man. For a law would not be imposed upon one who did not have in his own power the obedience due to the law; nor, again, would the threat of death be attached to transgression if contempt for the law were not also attributed to the freedom of man’s choice. . . . And otherwise, what sense would it make for man, possessing the whole world, not first to rule over the passion of his own mind—to be lord of others but the slave of himself?” (*Against Marcion*, book II, chs. 5–6, vol. II, cols. 290–291).

### God is to be loved as Father and feared as Lord

“The justice of God is the fullness of his divinity itself, presenting God as perfect, both Father and Lord: Father in clemency, Lord in discipline; Father in gentle power, Lord in stern power; Father, to be loved dutifully; [Col. 0030C] Lord, to be feared necessarily. He is to be loved because he prefers the repentance of the sinner to his death, and feared because he does not will sinners who no longer repent to belong to him. The law therefore defines both: ‘You shall love God,’ and ‘you shall fear God.’ It set the one before the obedient person and the other before the transgressor. In everything God meets you, the same God striking and healing, putting to death and giving life, humbling and exalting, creating evils and making peace” (*ibid.*, chs. 13–14, cols. 300–301).

Thus far, from all that we have gathered about Tertullian’s doctrine, we seem to have wished earnestly to prove that using him is good and very profitable. But we would now rightly be blamed if we offered no advice for guarding against a bad use of him. Just as we have established how much benefit his doctrine can bring, so from his [Col. 0030D] errors we must infer how much harm they may cause and with what keen judgment his writings must be sifted. Some paradoxes he shares with the Montanists; others are so peculiarly his own that he thereby became a founder of heresy and produced followers of those errors who were called Tertullianists after their author.

It is certain and beyond doubt that Tertullian not only assented to the errors and ravings of the Montanists, but also promoted them with the utmost ardor. Of this kind are all the passages in which he everywhere and shamelessly advertises his Paraclete; those in which he not only condemns second marriages but calls them an enormity greater than every crime; and those in which he teaches that, when persecution rages, it is unlawful either to flee or to buy oneself free with money. Yet my spirit recoils from recounting the insults that arrogant doctor poured out against the Church of God, attacking Catholics, whom he repeatedly calls “psychics,” that is, animal and carnal men. He charges them with indulging their bellies and practicing indecencies while celebrating agapes, and with driving confessors held in prison to intemperance. Here you have the chief errors of Tertullian the Montanist, from which he himself seized an occasion to reveal his hatred and his bitterly hostile disposition toward Catholics.

We come to the errors proper and peculiar to Tertullian as a heresiarch. He teaches that, when persecution rages, it is unlawful either to flee or to redeem oneself with money [Col. 0031A]. To avoid extending the discussion without limit, we refer the detailed weighing of these points to the notes of Pamelius, Rigaltius, and the other commentators that will be appended below [Col. 0031B]. It will suffice to bring forward the more notable ones. First, he dares to profess that the Virgin Mother of God lost her virginity in childbirth. Then, concerning the soul, although we judge that some things should be interpreted favorably, many remain wholly inexcusable—for example, his teaching that the more substantial properties, those in every way proper to corporeality, are also present in the soul; that the soul is distinguished by its own sex, namely male or female; that, like bodies, it has a threefold dimension of length, breadth, and depth; that it displays the form and figure of the human body; and that it is not an empty and void quality, but something tangible, airy in color and luminous. Tertullian errs notably besides when he teaches that the Church exists even where there are two or three, though they be laymen: “Where there are three, there is the Church [Col. 0031C], even though they are laymen!” A truly dreadful proposition, from which many far more ruinous consequences follow for the Church.

I pass over in silence other errors that he did not defend alone, but together with some other early writers, on matters not yet defined by the Church—for example, that those baptized by heretics must be baptized again. To the same class belongs the millenarian opinion and the description of paradise which in *Apology*, ch. 47, he calls a place of divine delight, appointed to receive the spirits of the saints and separated from knowledge of the ordinary world by a kind of wall of the fiery zone. To these must be added what he says about the apostate angels who knew the daughters of men [Note: [Col. 0031D] These and other paradoxes of Tertullian you will find below, supplied with Pamelius’s antidote and elsewhere cured by other commentators’ notes. Tertullian is defended against certain errors wrongly imputed to him by Lumper, *Bibliotheca historico-critica Patrum*, vol. VII, ch. 11, p. 709 ff.].

But, says one author of a life of Tertullian, this seems equal to a prodigy: that a man endowed with so lofty a genius [Col. 0031D] could have lent credibility to errors of this kind because of the visions of women, to which he gave complete faith just as though they were revelations of the Holy Spirit.

Hence we must conclude with the author just praised [Note: [Col. 0031D] Corbinian Thomas, concluding dissertation, work cited, p. 192] that fallen Tertullian now warns us how slippery a thing is the human understanding when it presumes too much upon itself, attributes everything to itself alone, believes itself too much, and disregards [Col. 0032A] the authority of others and especially that of the Church herself; how quickly any private person’s talent can be overthrown; how prone it is to fall into errors; and how truly Blessed Vincent of Lérins pronounced upon Tertullian in chapter 18 of his *Commonitorium*: “Tertullian, insufficiently tenacious of Catholic dogma—that is, of the universal and ancient faith—and far more eloquent than fortunate, afterward changed his opinion and finally did what the blessed confessor Hilary writes of him in one place: ‘By the error that followed,’ he says, ‘he destroyed the authority of his approved writings.’”

## ARTICLE III. THE LITERARY ANNALS OF TERTULLIAN

Having now weighed, as far as was possible, Tertullian’s manifold authority [Col. 0032B], and having offered such counsels as we ventured to give and judged most important concerning the right use of him, we think that we ought not to depart from this order of treatment. We therefore undertake to review briefly, year by year through the course of the centuries, what strenuous labors learned men devoted to him—whether in transcribing manuscripts, producing editions, translating the text into modern languages, or separately elucidating particular points of Tertullianic studies. Nothing shows more clearly than the laborious and tireless rivalry of so many men over the same author how highly all past antiquity and more recent scholarship valued Tertullian, how great his authority was, and with what diligence and prudence each man used his writings. Freely making use, according to our custom and right, of earlier studies on the same subject, especially C. T. G. Schoenemann’s exceedingly rare *Historico-Literary Library*, [Col. 0032C] we shall first touch briefly on the manuscripts whose existence has become known to us; then, as far as possible, give an extensive series of editions, translations, and commentaries under their respective years [Col. 0032D] [Note: [Col. 0032D] Material that we judged should be added to Schoenemann’s most learned notices has been marked by prefixed brackets; the rest we have distinguished only by new headings or a different arrangement.].

### § I. THE MANUSCRIPTS OF TERTULLIAN

“After the very ancient testimonies of Cyprian, Lactantius, Hilary, Eusebius, Rufinus, Jerome, Augustine, and Vincent of Lérins concerning Tertullian’s books, genius, and authority, no traces of him remain for several centuries; he seems buried beneath the celebrated decree of Pope Gelasius, who indiscriminately banished his works among the apocrypha. At last, awakened from that darkness of the apocrypha, he came into the light again around the fortunate age of Charlemagne, when literary studies reemerged from the monstrous flood of the barbarians. These flourished especially at Lyons, the ancient seat of Gallic learning and eloquence, and the archbishops of Lyons, among whom Leidradus and Agobard are named, are remembered as giving them considerable support. Agobard in particular must be thought to have cherished Tertullian above all others, since several manuscripts bearing his name survive. Hence:

[Col. 0033A] **Ninth century**

“**1. Between 814 and 840. I.** The first Agobard manuscript, on parchment, written in elegant, neat lowercase uncials and bound in quarto in 204 folios, torn by great age and gnawed and mutilated at the margins by prolonged damp, survives in the Royal Library under no. 1622. It could scarcely be handled safely when that outstanding codex was at last recently repaired; nevertheless, several translucent scraps of leaves were not preserved, upon which some letters once glimmered but now disappear because the margin is missing or an opaque binding obstructs them. On the second folio, beneath a title written by a more recent hand in square letters, these words appear in an ancient script: ‘BOOK OFFERED AT THE ALTAR OF ST STEPHEN [Col. 0033B] BY VOW OF BISHOP AGOBARD.’ On the verso of the same folio: ‘HERE ARE TWENTY-THREE BOOKS OF TERTULLIAN.’ The following list appears: *To the Nations*, book I; likewise *To the Nations*, book II; *On the Prescription of Heretics*, book I; *Scorpiace*; *On the Testimony of the Soul*; *On the Crown*; *On the Spectacles*; *On Idolatry*; *On the Census [Origin] of the Soul*; *On Prayer*; *On the Dress of Women*; likewise *On the Dress of Women*; *To His Wife*; again *On the Dress of Women*; *To His Wife*; again *To His Wife*; *On the Exhortation to Chastity*; *On the Hope of the Faithful*; *On Paradise*; *On the Veiling of Virgins*; *On Flesh and Soul*. In the other column of the same folio: *On the Flesh of Christ*; *On Patience*; *On Repentance*; *On the Submission of the Soul*; *On the Superstition of the Age*; ‘READ IN CHRIST IIIY.’ The manuscript ends in *On the Flesh of Christ*, at these words of chapter X: ‘likewise, since they presume it to be not of our flesh but of our soul.’ Nothing survives [Col. 0033C] of the books that follow, nor a single word of the four preceding works named in the list. On the red parchment cover, distinguished by golden lines and letters, one reads: ‘JACQUES GODEFROY, JURISCONSULT, A NATIVE OF PARIS, TO THE ROYAL LIBRARY OF PARIS.’ For this is the famous manuscript first handled by Godefroy, from which he first published the two books *To the Nations*, and which, after publishing them, he dedicated as a gift to the Royal Library.

[Col. 0037B] Rhenanus conceived the plan of editing Tertullian when he happened upon an old manuscript from the Transjurane monastery of Payerne, and was soon assisted by the Hirsau manuscript as well. Yet he found both so corrupt and full of errors, and their script so similar, that one seemed copied from the other. He states that he would have abandoned the work altogether had the books, once announced, not already begun to be printed while Froben’s presses were free. He therefore followed this method in completing the labor: “Whenever he found corrupt passages and manuscripts did not come to his aid, knowing that authors’ writings must be treated reverently like sacred things, he changed nothing, but added in the margins [Col. 0037C] the conjectures that happened then to occur to him. Some of them,” he says, “do not greatly please even me, but I wished to assist the reader who would be in doubt. If it did not succeed everywhere, the attempt should not for that reason be condemned.” He continues: “Would that I had had access to the copy preserved at Gorze Abbey, three miles from Metz, and likewise to the one at Fulda or the one said to be at Rome. But shut out by the constraints of time, we could not obtain them. Yet I fear that none is more correct than ours, since the Hirsau and Payerne manuscripts we used agreed so consistently in all respects, though brought from different libraries and copied at different times. We shall entrust the labor of comparing them to others.”

Besides summaries prefixed to the individual books with brief annotations, Rhenanus prefixed a life of Tertullian [Col. 0037D] and a discussion, under the title *Admonition to the Reader Concerning Certain Dogmas of Tertullian*. There follow *Definitions of Ecclesiastical Dogmas*, later attributed to Gennadius, and finally an index of words, propositions, and dogmas. He dedicated the work to Prince Stanislaus Thurzo, bishop of Olomouc. Typographically this edition is quite distinguished, both by its large, clear type and by emblematic, mythological, and historical woodcuts, some surrounding the margins of the title, the first page of the dedication, and then the first page of the text itself, and others incorporated into the larger initials.

From the elegance [Col. 0040A] of its paper and type, this edition must be counted among the handsomest. In the Baluze Library the *Apology* in one copy of this edition had been collated with a manuscript.

**1561.** Paris, at Andreas Wechel, quarto. Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian’s *Book of Prescriptions Against Heretics*, with scholia by Jean Quintin of Autun.

**1562. FOURTH BASEL EDITION.** Basel, folio. Reprint of the 1550 edition. At the end: Basel, in the Froben shop, by Hieronymus Froben and Nicolaus Episcopius, September.

**1562. FIRST FRENCH TRANSLATION.** Tertullian’s treatise *On the Prescription of Heretics*, published at [Col. 0040B] Paris by Aubert de Macère.

**1563. FIRST FRENCH TRANSLATION.** *On the Soldier’s Crown*, by the same, there.

**1566. BASEL–PARIS EDITION.** Paris, at Oudin Petit [Note: [Col. 0040D] Sandius wrongly says this appeared at Robert Estienne. SCHOENEMANN], under the sign of the Golden Lily, rue Saint-Jacques. An accurate reprint of Gelenius, in two octavo volumes. At the end of each: printed at Paris by Andreas Wechel, 1566. Gelenius’s index (“compiled by J. G.”—what that may mean I do not know) was reshaped to fit the contents of each volume.

**1572. FIRST GERMAN TRANSLATION.** *The Certain, Just, and Necessary Prescriptions of Q. S. F. Tertullian Against All [Col. 0040C] Heresies*, translated into German by Lorenz Albert of Franconia and printed at Dillingen.
