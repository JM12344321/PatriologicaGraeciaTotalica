[Note: [Col. 0816C] *Consita* in Rhen. and Seml.] For although the Apostle calls it a vessel, which he commands to be treated with honor, [Note: [Col. 0816C] *Tractare* in Rhen. and Seml.] the same flesh is nevertheless called by the same Apostle the “outer man”: namely, that clay upon which the title “man” was first inscribed—not “cup,” “sword,” or any other “vessel.” It is called a vessel by reason of its capacity, by which it receives and contains the soul; but it is called man by reason of its community of nature with the soul, which renders it not an instrument in operations, but a servant. ## CHAPTER XVII

On the contrary, it will need it to this extent: not because it cannot perceive anything without the flesh, but because it must also perceive together with the flesh. [Note: [Col. 0817C] *Quia* in Rhen. and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0817C] *Quia* in Rhen. and Seml.] For insofar as it is sufficient by itself for action, it is sufficient also for suffering. Yet it is less sufficient by itself for action. For by itself it possesses only the ability to think, desire, and determine; to complete the deed, however, it awaits the flesh’s assistance. Thus, likewise, [Col. 0817B] for suffering it demands partnership with the flesh, so that it may suffer through the flesh as fully as it was unable to act fully without it.

And therefore, concerning those things for which it is sufficient by itself—desire, thought, and will—it meanwhile pays their penalty. But if these were sufficient for the fullness of merit, so that deeds too were not required, the soul would suffice entirely for the completion of judgment, being judged for those things in performing which it alone had sufficed. Since, however, deeds too are bound to merit, and deeds are administered through the flesh, the soul’s being comforted or tormented without the flesh is no longer sufficient for the works of the flesh as well—even if the soul has a body, [Note: [Col. 0817D] “Even if it has a body.” This error of Tertullian can be excused if the multitude of those who fell into it in those times carries any weight. Blessed John Chrysostom agrees with this interpretation in his treatment of Isaiah 6: τὸ ταχὺ, τὸ κοῦφον, τὸ δραστήριον ἡμῖν διὰ τῶν στοιχείων ἐνδεικνύμενος, “indicating to us through these elements their swiftness, lightness, and energetic activity.” Theodoret has almost the same on that same Psalm 103. [Note: [Col. 0817C] “Fully, just as” is absent from Rhen.]

For this reason, in whatever manner it acted, in that same manner [Col. 0818A] it suffers in the regions below, tasting judgment first, just as it first introduced the offense; yet it awaits the flesh, so that through it the soul may also make recompense for the deeds whose conceptions it entrusted to the flesh. Finally, this will be the rationale of the judgment appointed for the final end: that through the presentation of the flesh the whole divine judgment may be completed. Otherwise, what souls even now reap in the regions below would not have been deferred until the end, if it were appointed for souls alone. [Note: [Col. 0817C] *Decerpuntur* in Rhen. and Seml.]

## CHAPTER XVIII

Thus far I have dealt with preliminary constructions designed to fortify the meaning of all the Scriptures that promise the return of the flesh. Since so many authoritative grounds of just advocacy provide for it—by which I mean the honors belonging to the substance itself, then the powers of God, then examples of those powers, then the principles of judgment and its necessities—the Scriptures must surely be understood [Col. 0818B] in accordance with the prior determinations of so many authorities, not according to the inventions of heretics that arise from unbelief alone: namely, because it is held incredible that a substance withdrawn by destruction should be restored, not because the substance itself is undeserving of it, or because it is impossible for God, or because it is unfit for judgment. [Note: [Col. 0817C] *Tota auctoritate si* in Rhen.] [Note: [Col. 0817C] *Rationis judicii et necessitatis* in Fran.] [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Et* in Rhen. and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0818C] Rhen. conjectures *inremeabile*.] [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Inhabitabile* in Rhen.]

It would plainly be incredible if it had not been proclaimed by God—except that, even if God had not proclaimed it, it ought to have been presumed of itself, so that its not having been proclaimed would be because it had already been determined by so many authorities. [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Aut plane* in Rhen.] [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Praejudicatum* in Rhen.] But since it also resounds in divine utterances, it is so far from being understood otherwise than those considerations demand by which it is proved even without divine utterances. [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Personat* in Rhen., Pam., and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Persuadeatur* in Rhen. and Seml.]

Let us therefore first see under what title [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Quoniam* in Ven.] [Note: [Col. 0818C] *Stylo* in Rhen. and Seml.] this hope has been posted publicly. [Col. 0818C] One edict of God, I think, hangs before everyone: [Note: [Col. 0818D] “One edict of God, I think, hangs before everyone.” It hangs on an affixed tablet, from which it may conveniently be read at ground level. I shall address the words themselves and examine them, to determine to which substance they bind themselves. When I hear that resurrection threatens—or awaits—a human being, I must ask what part of him has been allotted the fate of falling. For nothing will await resurrection except that which has first fallen. Anyone who does not know that the flesh falls through death can likewise be ignorant that it stands through life. Nature pronounces God’s sentence: “Earth you are, and into earth you shall go.” Even one who has not heard it sees it. [Note: [Col. 0819C] *Audit* in Rhen. and Seml.] No death is anything other than the collapse of the members. [Note: [Col. 0819C] *Est* absent from Rhen. and Seml.]

The Lord also expressed this fate of the body when clothed in that very substance: “Destroy,” he says (John 11), “this temple, and I will raise it again in three days.” For he showed that what belongs to that which is destroyed, dashed down, and lies fallen is also to be raised and resurrected, [Note: [Col. 0819C] *Cum* absent from Par.] [Note: [Col. 0819C] *Cujus* in Rhen., Rig., Ven., and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0819C] *Revelari* in Ven.] although he also carried about a soul trembling even unto death, but not falling through death. [Note: [Col. 0819C] *Circumferat* in Rhen. and Par.] For Scripture had said, “He spoke concerning his body.” [Col. 0819B]

Indeed, it is so completely the flesh that is overthrown by death that it is thereafter called a *cadaver* from *cadere*, “to fall.” [Note: [Col. 0819C] *Enuntietur* in Par. and Jun.] The soul, however, does not fall even in terminology, because it does not collapse in condition. Rather, it is the soul that brings collapse upon the body when it is breathed out, just as it is the soul that will raise the body from the earth when it enters it. That which raised it by entering cannot fall. That which dashes it down by departing cannot collapse. [Note: [Col. 0819C] “That which raised it by entering cannot fall. It cannot” omitted in Rig. and Ven.] [Note: [Col. 0819D] *Eligit* in Rhen. and Seml.]

I shall state it more closely: the soul does not even fall asleep with the body, nor is it prostrated when the flesh is. For it is active and tossed about in dreams; but it would be at rest if it lay fallen, and it would lie fallen if it had fallen. [Note: [Col. 0819D] *Sedenim* in Rhen. and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0820C] “And it would lie fallen if it had fallen” absent from Rhen. and Seml.] Thus it does not fall into the reality of death, since it does not collapse even into its image. [Note: [Col. 0820C] *Veritate* in Rhen. and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0820C] *Imagine* in Rhen. and Seml.]

Now examine the following word, “dead,” in the same way, to determine upon which substance it rests. In this matter, however, let us grant that mortality is sometimes assigned to the soul by heretics: [Note: [Col. 0819D] “Mortality is assigned to the soul by heretics,” etc. Those who substitute “immortality” give no consideration at all to the matter under discussion. For I think Tertullian is identifying those heretics who arose in Arabia in the time of Origen, who said that souls were corrupted and perished together with bodies and that the bodies would return to life together with those same souls. LE PR.] so that, if the mortal soul is to obtain resurrection, this establishes in advance that the no less mortal flesh will also share in resurrection. But now the proper sense of the word must be vindicated for the lot that belongs to it. [Col. 0820A]

Since resurrection, by the very fact itself, belongs to a thing that falls—that is, to the flesh—the same thing will also be present in the designation “dead,” because the resurrection belonging to a thing that falls is called “the resurrection of the dead.” We also learn this through Abraham, the father of faith and a man intimate with God. For when he asked the sons of Heth for a place in which to bury Sarah, he said: “Give me, therefore, a possession for a tomb among you, and I shall bury my dead” (Gen. 23)—that is, her flesh. [Note: [Col. 0820C] *Heth* in Rhen. and Seml., with the same meaning.] [Note: [Col. 0820C] *Mortuum meum* in Rig. and Ven.] For he would not have desired a place in which to bury the soul, even if the soul were believed mortal and even if it deserved to be called dead. But if the body is the dead thing, the resurrection will belong to bodies when it is called “the resurrection of the dead.”

## CHAPTER XIX

Accordingly, this examination of the title and of the proclamation itself, which certainly defends the faithful meaning of the words, must contribute to this end: if the opposing party introduces confusion under the pretext of figures and riddles, all the clearer passages should prevail, and the more certain should prescribe the interpretation of the uncertain. [Note: [Col. 0820C] *Illic* in Fran.] [Note: [Col. 0820C] *Qua* in Rhen., Seml., and Oberth.] [Col. 0820B]

For certain people, having seized upon the very customary form of prophetic speech—which is often, though not always, allegorical and figurative—distort even the plainly announced resurrection of the dead into an imaginary meaning. They assert that death itself must likewise be understood spiritually: that the real death is not this one that lies before us, the separation of flesh and soul, but ignorance of God, through which a human being, dead to God, has lain in error no less than in a tomb.

Consequently, they contend that the resurrection is that by which someone, having entered into the truth, is reanimated and restored to life for God, the death of ignorance having been dispelled, and bursts forth as though from the tomb of the old man. [Note: [Col. 0820D] “They contend that the resurrection is that.” These are trifles and sophistries employed by Hymenaeus and Philetus and by those heretics who asserted that death itself must be understood spiritually. They abused the Apostle’s words in Romans 6: “Do you not know, brothers, that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? For we were buried with him through baptism into death, so that, just as Christ rose from the dead,” etc. He refutes this in the next chapter. Those heretics, I mean Hymenaeus and Philetus, who had departed from the truth and affirmed, as the blessed Apostle testifies in 2 Timothy 2, that the resurrection had already occurred, called the procreation of children “resurrection.” LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 0820C] *Addita* in Rhen. and Seml.] They argue this because the Lord also likened the scribes and Pharisees to whitewashed tombs. [Note: [Col. 0820C] “Whitewashed” absent from Rig. and Ven.]

Hence, therefore, those who have obtained resurrection through faith are with the Lord once they have put him on in baptism. [Col. 0820C] [Note: [Col. 0820D] *Cum baptisma* in Rhen.; Junius has *cum eam baptismate vel baptismo*.] Indeed, by this very device they have often been accustomed to deceive our people in discussions, as though they too admitted the resurrection of the flesh. “Woe,” they say, “to him who has not risen in this flesh,” lest they be struck down at once if they openly deny the resurrection. [Col. 0821A] Silently, however, according to their own understanding, they mean this: “Woe to him who, while still in the flesh, has not come to know the heretical mysteries.” [Note: [Col. 0821D] “Has come to know the heretical mysteries.” Since heretics love darkness more than light, they employ a secret and obscure tradition, fearing that, if they reveal their shamefulness, their inventions will vanish into smoke. But whoever has once exposed those mysteries trusts that he has gained the victory; for to have exposed heresies is to have defeated them. Tertullian teaches this in chapter 3 of the book *Against the Valentinians*. LE PR.] For this is resurrection among them.

Moreover, many who contend that resurrection occurs when the soul departs interpret “to leave the tomb” as “to escape from the world,” because the world is the habitation of the dead—that is, of those ignorant of God. Or they understand it as escaping from the body itself, because the body, like a tomb, confines the soul and detains it in the death of worldly life. [Note: [Col. 0821C] *Detinebat* in Par.]

## CHAPTER XX

On account of conjectures of this kind, therefore, I shall repel their first preliminary assumption, by which they maintain that the Prophets proclaimed everything through images. For if that were so, even the images themselves could not have been distinguished, unless realities too had been proclaimed, from which the images might be delineated. [Col. 0821B] [Note: [Col. 0821C] *Delinearentur* in Jun.] Indeed, if everything consists of figures, of what will those things be figures? How will you hold up a mirror if there is nowhere any face?

Thus, not everything consists of images, but there are also realities; not everything consists of shadows, but there are also bodies. This is so true that all the more notable things concerning the Lord himself were proclaimed more clearly than daylight. For the virgin both conceived in her womb—not figuratively—and gave birth to Emmanuel, God with us, Jesus—not obliquely. [Note: [Col. 0821C] “Lord” in Rhen. and Seml.] Yet he was to receive the power of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria obliquely (Isa. 8:4), but was manifestly to come in judgment with the elders and rulers of the people (Isa. 3:14). [Note: [Col. 0821C] “Princes” in Rhen. and Seml.]

For “the nations raged” (Ps. 2) in the person of Pilate, and “the peoples devised vain things” in the person of Israel. “The kings of the earth stood up”—Herod—and “the rulers assembled together”—Annas and Caiaphas—“against the Lord and against his Christ.” He also (Isa. 53) “was led like a sheep to slaughter,” [Note: [Col. 0821C] “Victim” in Fran.] and “like a lamb before its shearer”—namely Herod—he was voiceless, and thus “he did not open his mouth”; [Note: [Col. 0821C] Others add *se*, “itself.”] he placed his back before the scourges, [Col. 0822A] his cheeks before blows, and did not turn his face away from the missiles of spittle. [Note: [Col. 0821C] *In* in others.] [Note: [Col. 0821D] “From the foulness of spittle” in the codex of Pithoeus, Pam., and Fran.; “from the stains of spittle” in Jun.; “from the foulness of spit” in the Paris edition.] He was also “reckoned among the lawless”; his hands and feet were pierced (Ps. 21); he endured the casting of lots for his garment, bitter drinks, and the nodding heads of those who mocked him; and he was valued by the traitor at thirty pieces of silver (Zech. 11:12).

What figures are there here in Isaiah? What images in David? What riddles in Jeremiah, [Note: [Col. 0821D] “In Jeremiah.” Here Jeremiah is certainly put for Zechariah, for none of Jeremiah’s words are cited in what precedes; instead, mention is made of the thirty pieces of silver, concerning which Zechariah speaks. The blessed evangelist Matthew, whose feast we celebrate today, used the same freedom. EDD.] when not even his miracles were foretold through parables? Or were the eyes of the blind not opened (Isa. 35:6), and did the tongue of the mute not become clear? Were withered hands and feeble knees not restored, and did the lame not leap like a deer? [Note: [Col. 0822C] *Clodi* in Fran.]

Although we ourselves are also accustomed to interpret these things spiritually, according to an analogy with the soul’s faults that the Lord has healed, [Note: [Col. 0821D] “Although we are also accustomed to interpret these things spiritually.” Paul interpreted it thus in Hebrews 12, alluding to the passage of Isaiah, chapter 53: “Therefore, the hands that hang down,” etc. There he employs, not without reason, the metaphor of the stadium, which the Apostle frequently uses; and Faber discusses it learnedly in his work on athletic contests. For he is not speaking—indeed, he is not even alluding—to weakness arising from disease, but to weakness from struggle and weariness, exhorting us to do what vigorous contestants did, who, however fatigued, persisted bravely and fearlessly. I read this in the English codex in place of *Carthaginensium*, in the aforementioned chapter 23, where according to the Septuagint one reads πλοῖα καρχηδόνος, that is, “ships of Carthage,” in place of what the Hebrew has as “ships of Tarshish,” that is, “of the sea,” according to blessed Jerome. ## CHAPTER XXI

“If, then, it is allegorical sometimes and in certain things,” you say, “why may it not also be understood spiritually in the edict concerning the resurrection?” Because a very great body of reasoning intervenes.

First, what will they do with so many other divine documents that testify so openly to a bodily resurrection [Col. 0822C] that they admit no suspicion of figurative meaning? [Note: [Col. 0822C] *Ac talia* in Fran. and Pam.; *tot alia* in Rhen. and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0822D] “That they admit no suspicion of figurative meaning,” etc. He means “suspicion” as he earlier spoke of a “cloud of allegory.” For suspicion concerns things hidden, obscure, and mystical. Thus George of Baetica, in his book *On Faith*: “Furthermore, if you do not understand from the many expressions set forth above, observe at least from what follows that all suspicions of an impious interpretation have been excluded.” RIG.] [Col. 0823A]

Surely it is equitable, as we also prescribed above, that the uncertain should be prejudged from the certain, and the obscure from the manifest, lest amid disagreement between the certain and the uncertain, the manifest and the obscure, faith be dissipated, truth be endangered, and the divinity itself be branded inconsistent. [Note: [Col. 0823C] “Faith be dissipated, truth be endangered,” etc. If the predictions concerning the resurrection were accepted in discordant senses, so that the conspicuous and explicit testimonies that speak openly of the resurrection of bodies did not compel the more obscure passages to be interpreted concerning bodily resurrection, but these ambiguous passages were instead distorted to refer only to a resurrection of souls, the following absurdities plainly result: God would be regarded as inconsistent, since in the manifest passages he had predicted a bodily resurrection, but in others a resurrection of souls only, as the adversaries maintained. Truth would therefore also be endangered, for since the prophecies would have been contradictory, people would risk error by assenting to a false prophecy; and faith would be dissipated, for all trust would rightly be denied to one who made such predictions. LAC.]

Then there is the fact that it is not credible that that article of the mystery to which the whole faith is committed and upon which the whole discipline exerts itself should appear to have been announced ambiguously and set forth obscurely. [Note: [Col. 0823C] *Tunc* in Rhen. and Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0823D] “That article of the mystery”: that is, the principal doctrine of the Christian faith, the resurrection of the flesh.] [Note: [Col. 0823C] *Annuata* in Rhen. and Seml.; *annuta* or *annutata* in Jun.] For unless the hope of resurrection plainly revealed the peril and reward, it would persuade no one to embrace a religion of this kind, especially one exposed to public hatred and a hostile indictment. No work is certain when its reward is uncertain. No fear is justified when the peril is doubtful. Both reward and peril depend upon the outcome of the resurrection.

[Col. 0823B] Moreover, if prophecy has so openly hurled forth God’s temporal, local, and personal decrees and judgments against cities, nations, and kings, how can it be that his eternal and universal arrangements for the whole human race fled from their own light? [Note: [Col. 0823C] *Sed* absent from Seml.] [Note: [Col. 0823C] *Aperta* in Pam. and Rhen.] [Note: [Col. 0823C] *Humanum* in others.] [Note: [Col. 0823C] *Quae* absent from Rig. and Ven.] The greater they were, the clearer they ought to have been, so that they might be believed to be greater. And I think that neither envy, deceit, inconsistency, nor pandering can be ascribed to God—the faults by which the promulgation of the decrees of the great is commonly mocked. [Note: [Col. 0823D] “By which the promulgation of the decrees of the great is commonly mocked.” “Of the great” means the more powerful, magistrates, and authorities. RIG.]
