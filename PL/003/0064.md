Minucius Felix passes from Saturn to his son Jupiter, and likewise demonstrates that he was a man and by no means God [Col. 0570D]. For Saturn’s “son Jupiter,” he says, “reigned in Crete after driving out his father; there he died, there he had children; the cave of Jupiter is still visited, and his tomb is shown, and in his own sacred rites he is convicted of humanity.” But we discussed these matters a little earlier, and they must be treated again in our subsequent dissertations. Moreover, we shall discuss below the rites of Jupiter which continued to show that he was a man.

Since, therefore, this Jupiter cannot be a god, because he was begotten by another man, it necessarily follows that the others descended from him and from other human beings were by no means gods, but mortal men, infinitely removed from the divine nature. Minucius accordingly declares that he passes over in silence the genealogies of those gods described [Col. 0571A] by the pagans, since it would be as tedious as it would be useless to set them forth. For, he says, “it is pointless to go through them individually and unfold the entire line of that family, since the mortality proved in the case of its first parents has flowed down to the rest in the order of succession.” Did he not take this idea from Tertullian, who had said earlier: “It is also pointless to pursue their titles. . . . What has been established concerning their origin will apply also to their posterity”? (Tertull., *Apologet.*, ch. 10, p. 12.) These, of course, are fictitious gods and mere human beings.

But Minucius presses the pagans more sharply and assails them in these words, nearly all of which Cyprian transcribed: “Why, if they were born, are they not still being born today? Unless perhaps Jupiter has now grown old, Juno’s childbearing has failed, [Col. 0571B] and Minerva has gone gray before giving birth” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 12). Earlier, Theophilus of Antioch had refuted the pagans by the same argument:

> Εἰ γὰρ τότε ἐγέννων, καὶ ἐγεννῶντο, δῆλον, ὅτι ἐχρῆν καὶ ἕως τοῦ δεῦρο γίνεσθαι θεοὺς γεννητούς· ἢ γὰρ ἐγήρασαν διὸ οὐκ ἔτι γεννῶσι· ἢ ἀπέθανον, καὶ οὐκ ἔτι εἰσί.
>
> “For if at that time they begot and were begotten, it is evident that begotten gods ought to have continued coming into being down to the present. If matters are otherwise, the race of gods must surely be considered feeble: either they have been exhausted by old age and have ceased to beget, or they have been utterly removed from the number of the living and reduced to nothing.”

(Theoph., *To Autolycus*, bk. I, p. 81.)

Tatian is briefer, but no less witty: “If,” he says, “you recount a generation of gods, you show that they are mortal: διὰ τί γὰρ οὐ κύει νῦν ἡ Ἥρα; πότερον γεγήρακεν; ἢ τοῦ μηνύσαντος ὑμῖν [Col. 0571C] ἀπορεῖτε; Why does Juno no longer conceive? Has she grown old? Or do you have no one who can disclose the matter to you?” (Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, p. 160.)

Thus Seneca too, as quoted by Lactantius, argues by ridiculing his fellow pagans: “Why is it, then,” he says, “that Jupiter, the most lustful figure in the poets, has ceased to produce children? Has he turned sixty, and has the Papian law fastened a clasp upon him? Or has he obtained the right of three children? Or has it at last occurred to him, ‘Expect from another what you have done to another,’ and does he fear that someone might do to him what he himself did to Saturn?” (Lact., bk. I, ch. 16, p. 88.) That is, he fears that, just as he deprived his father Saturn of his kingdom, so his own sons might deprive him of his. But something must be said below about Lactantius and these words of Seneca. Finally, to omit others, Juvenal too sang:

> [Col. 0571D] Yet who can assert that nothing happens on the mountains  
> Or in the caves? So greatly have Jupiter and Mars grown old.

(Juvenal, *Satires*, VI, v. 62 ff.)

Minucius presses on and utterly destroys false opinions about the gods with yet another argument: “If gods,” he says, “could be created but could not perish, we should have more gods than all the human race; so that by now neither heaven could contain them, nor the air hold them, nor the earth support them.” With this exceedingly sharp weapon, which the Sibyl also employs, Theophilus of Antioch, already cited by us, had thoroughly transfixed the pagans. For from this, he says,

> μᾶλλον καὶ πλείονες θεοὶ ὤφειλον εἶναι τῶν ἀνθρώπων, ὥς φησι Σίβυλλα
>
> “it follows that there ought to be more gods than human beings, as the Sibyl also declares.”

Anyone may readily read the remainder, which is more extensive in Theophilus, as well as similar material in Arnobius, Lactantius—whom we shall discuss in the proper place—and Eusebius (Arnob., bk. III *Against the Pagans*, p. 104 ff.; Lactan., bk. I, ch. 16 ff.; Euseb., *Oration of Constantine to the Assembly of the Saints*, ch. 4, p. 570). [Col. 0572A]

Meanwhile, however, we wish to remind you that this argument possesses such force and weight that Pliny, completely overwhelmed by it, admits that either the number of celestial beings ought to be greater than that of human beings, or the marriages of the gods were not genuine. But it is pleasing to hear the man himself speak:

> “The population of celestial beings,” he says, “may even be understood to be greater than that of human beings, since individuals also make for themselves an equal number of gods out of their own selves by adopting Junos and tutelary spirits; while certain peoples regard animals, and even some obscene things, as gods, [Col. 0572B] and swear by many things still more shameful to mention, including foul foods and other objects. But to believe in marriages among the gods, while throughout so great an age no one is born from them, and that some are always aged and gray-haired, while others are young men and boys, some winged, some lame, some born from an egg, and some living and dying on alternate days, is almost the delirium of children.”

(Plin., *Natural History*, bk. II, ch. 7, p. 142 f.)

What argument, then, I ask you, could have been advanced more effective for abolishing the religions of the pagans than that candid and public confession of Pliny and of other pagans as well?

Nor should you say that Pliny, like those most learned pagan men, rejects these beliefs as fables of an ignorant and uncultivated populace. For this was plainly sufficient for Minucius and the other defenders of the Christian religion utterly to overthrow the impious cults of all pagans, which rested upon these monstrous errors. For who could endure the empty superstitions of those who denied the one eternal God and worshiped and venerated numerous others as gods—mortals, or beings that begot and were begotten? [Col. 0572C] Learned pagans no less than the unlearned rejected the true God and worshiped those ridiculous gods; or, while pretending to worship them, admitted no god at all, or admitted something else still further removed from true divinity because it was created or itself deprived of reason.

Did those men not stray in the same way from the straight path of truth when they foolishly proclaimed that certain mortal human beings had become gods either by virtue of their birth or through the perjury, lust, and arbitrary will of others? Such, however, was “the god Romulus,” as Minucius says, “through the perjury [Col. 0572D] of Proculus,” as we shall explain shortly. “Juba likewise,” he continues, “is a god because the Moors willed it; and the other kings who are deified are called divine, not from belief in their divinity, but to honor their power after its service has ended.” Lactantius subscribed to our author’s statement and committed this to writing:

> “Individual peoples privately worshiped with the highest veneration either the founders of their own nation or city, or men distinguished for courage, or women remarkable for chastity—as the Egyptians worshiped Isis and the Moors Juba.”

(Lactan., *Institutes*, bk. I, ch. 15, p. 81.)

Earlier, however, Tertullian had spoken more generally, suppressing Juba’s name but leaving it understood: “Each province and city has its own god . . . as Mauretania has its own petty kings.” [Col. 0573A] Cyprian follows Tertullian there in his customary fashion and says: “The Moors openly worship kings and do not conceal that name beneath any covering” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 12).

Cellarius, however, suspects that some error lies concealed in the text of our Minucius. For Juba, he says, was king not of the Moors but of the Numidians. We cannot, indeed, deny that Dio Cassius and Plutarch committed this to writing. But did Minucius, or his copyist, or the authors cited by us therefore err (Dio, *Roman History*, p. 193; Plutarch, *Life of Caesar*, p. 732)? Or had Cellarius himself perhaps not examined Strabo’s books carefully enough? For there he could have read these words:

> Νυνὶ εἰς Ἰούβαν περιέστηκεν Μαυρουσία, καὶ πολλὰ μέρη τῆς ἄλλης Λιβύης [Col. 0573B] διὰ τὴν πρὸς Ῥωμαίους εὔνοιάν τε, καὶ φιλίαν.
>
> “Juba now possesses Mauretania and many other parts of Africa, having obtained this because of his goodwill and friendship toward the Romans.”

(Strab., *Geography*, bk. VI, near the end, p. 288.)

Why, then, you will ask, is Juba called king of the Numidians by Dio and Plutarch? Herodian unties the knot of this difficulty. He testifies that Mauretania, where Juba ruled and which was subject to the Romans, was called Numidia by its inhabitants:

> Καπελλιανὸς ἦν τις ὄνομα, ἀπὸ τοῦ συγκλήτου, ἡγεῖτο δὲ Μαυρουσίων τῶν ὑπὸ Ῥωμαίοις, Νομάδων δὲ καλουμένων.
>
> “There was a certain Capellianus by name, a man of senatorial rank, who governed Mauretania, which was subject to the Romans and was called Numidia by its inhabitants.”

(Herodian, *History*, bk. VII, p. 280.)

Cellarius further insists that it is not plausible that Juba, who was defeated by Caesar and laid violent hands upon himself, was placed by the Moors among the gods. [Col. 0573C] But this argument is of the slightest possible weight. For who does not know that a custom became prevalent among the Romans of enrolling their emperors, however wicked and criminal, in the register of the gods by a solemn rite and apotheosis? Nor did they very often await their deaths; rather, while they were still alive, they paid them divine honors, even against their will and resistance. Therefore, immediately after the words cited by us, Minucius added: “Finally, this name is assigned to them against their will: they desire to persevere as human beings, fear being made gods, and, even when already old, do not wish it.” Tertullian, however, presses this point more sharply, as we shall see in our dissertation on his apologetic books. What wonder is it, then, if the Moors, after the example of the Romans, wished their king [Col. 0573D] Juba to be a god?

# CHAPTER XVII  
## HOW WEIGHTY IS ANOTHER ARGUMENT OF MINUCIUS AGAINST THE GODS OF THE PAGANS, DRAWN FROM THEIR RIDICULOUS FORMS AND FIGURES AND FROM THEIR UNWORTHY AND SHAMEFUL DEEDS AND CRIMES

### ARTICLE I  
#### How absurdly Vulcan is imagined by the pagans as lame; Apollo as beardless; Aesculapius as bearded, and sometimes beardless; Neptune as blue-green-eyed, Minerva as gray-blue-eyed, Juno as ox-eyed; Mercury as wing-footed; Pan as hoofed; Saturn as fettered; and Janus as two-faced and four-formed

The ridiculous forms and figures of the gods furnish Minucius [Col. 0574A] with another altogether invincible argument for demolishing their fictitious divinity. “For what,” Minucius asks, “of their very forms and attire? Do they not expose the mockery and disgrace—our manuscripts have *decora*, ‘adornments,’ but wrongly—of your gods?” If you desire to know what those forms were, hear Gregory of Nazianzus describing them in these few verses:

> Ἰθυφάλους, κερόεντας, ἀναύχενας, ἡμιδράκοντας,  
> Θῆρας, θηρομιγεῖς τε γελοῖον εἶδος ἔχοντας.
>
> Ithyphallic and obscene, horned, half-dragons,  
> Beasts without necks and others mingled with beasts,  
> All bearing various laughable faces.

(Greg. Nazianz., *Poem* LXI, p. 142, vol. II.) [Col. 0574B]

Our author, however, pursues this subject far more extensively; and so that the pagans might at last feel shame at worshiping such gods, he enumerates nearly all their monstrous forms and sets them before their very eyes. Although he follows no order based upon their fictitious ages or ranks, yet, because this contributes little or nothing to the force and cogency of the argument, we have thought it better to adhere to his footsteps.

He begins with Vulcan in this way: “Vulcan is a lame and feeble god.” Cotta, in Cicero, uses the same argument to refute the Epicurean Velleius, although he observes more carefully the order of his gods, and objects to him concerning Vulcan in these words:

> “At Athens, indeed, we praise the Vulcan made by Alcamenes, in whom, though he is standing and clothed, a slight lameness is apparent, but without deformity. We shall therefore have a lame god, since this is what we have received concerning Vulcan.”

(Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. I, p. 207, line 27.) [Col. 0574C]

Lucian relates that he became lame when Jupiter hurled him headlong from heaven:

> Εἴγε μὴ Λήμνιοι καλῶς ποιοῦντες, ἔτι φερόμενον αὐτὸν ὑπεδέξαντο, ἐτεθνήκει ἂν ἡμῖν Ἥφαιστος, ὥσπερ ὁ Ἀστυάναξ ἀπὸ πύργου καταπεσών.
>
> “Had the Lemnians not kindly caught him while he was still falling through the air, our Vulcan would have perished, just like Astyanax when he fell from the tower.”

(Lucian, *On Sacrifices*, p. 184; Homer, *Iliad* I, v. 590 ff.; idem, *Iliad* XVIII, v. 304 ff.) There he undoubtedly alludes to two passages of Homer’s *Iliad*, also cited by Clement of Alexandria, where Vulcan is said to have been hurled from heaven onto Lemnos and thereby made lame. Athanasius, moreover, when attacking the pagans in the same manner as our author, charges them with representing Ἥφαιστον ῥιπτόμενον, καὶ χωλαίνοντα, “Vulcan as cast down from heaven and made lame by that fall.” Arnobius reports that he was lame in one foot, Gregory in both. Nor are there lacking those who believe that he was cast from heaven twice, first by Juno and then by Jupiter (Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Pagans*, p. 18; Athanas., *Oration Against the Pagans*, §12, p. 13, vol. I; Arnob., bk. IV *Against the Pagans*, p. 134; Greg. Naz., Orat. 3 *Against Julian*, p. 259, and Orat. 4, p. 308). [Col. 0574D]

The second pair introduced by our author is: “Apollo, smooth-faced through so many ages, and Aesculapius, always bearded.” It was surely for this reason, they say, that Dionysius, tyrant of Sicily, removed Aesculapius’ beard, since he was the son of Apollo, who, as Cicero says, was always beardless. On this subject see further Cicero, Valerius Maximus, Arnobius, Lactantius, and many others. Cicero himself and others assert that the beard was removed not from just any Aesculapius, but from the Epidaurian one [Col. 0575A] (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. I, p. 107, line 26; idem, ibid., bk. III, p. 252, line 40; Valer. Max., bk. II, ch. 1, *Foreign Examples*, §3; Arnob., bk. VI *Against the Pagans*, p. 206; Lactant., *Institutes*, bk. II, ch. 4, p. 157; Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. III, p. 252, line 45). Pausanias, however, when speaking of the Phliasians, records this concerning the image of Aesculapius:

> Κατιόντων δὲ ἐκ τῆς ἀκροπόλεως ἔστιν Ἀσκληπιοῦ ναὸς ἐν δεξιᾷ, καὶ ἄγαλμα οὐκ ἔχον πωγωνεῖα.
>
> “As one descends from the citadel, there is a temple of Aesculapius on the right, containing a beardless image.”

(Pausan., *Corinth*, bk. II, p. 56.) [Col. 0575B]

But if Lucian is to be trusted, Apollo was represented by everyone as beardless, except only by the Assyrians:

> Οἱ μὲν γὰρ ἄλλοι πάντες Ἀπόλλωνα νέον τε ἥγηνται, καὶ πρωθήβην ποιοῦσι· μοῦνοι δὲ οὗτοι Ἀπόλλωνος γενειήτεω ξόανον δεικνύουσι.
>
> “For all others,” he says, “regard Apollo as young and fashion him with the first down of youth; these alone exhibit an image of a bearded Apollo.”

(Lucian, *On the Syrian Goddess*, p. 1071.) Macrobius, however, reports that this was done not by all the Assyrians but by the Hieropolitans: “The Hieropolitans,” he says, “who are peoples of Assyria, reduce all the effects and powers of the Sun to the appearance of a single bearded image, and call that same figure Apollo. His elongated face is shaped by a beard tapering to a point” (Macrob., *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 17). [Col. 0575C]

Third, Minucius says: “Neptune has blue-green eyes, Minerva gray-blue ones” (Minuc., p. 181). Cicero, already cited by us, had said: “It will be permissible to say that Minerva’s eyes are gray-blue and Neptune’s deep blue” (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. I, p. 207, line 24). Although Diodorus Siculus rejects this as an insipid fiction and believes that Minerva was imagined as *glaukopis* because the air has a blue-gray appearance (Diodor., *Library*, bk. I, p. 88), Lucian nevertheless calls her γλαυκῶπις (*glaukopis*, “gray-blue-eyed”), the epithet by which Homer everywhere commonly designates her; and Homer’s Latin translator renders it now as “gray-blue-eyed,” now as “blue-green-eyed” (Lucian, *Dialogue of Vulcan and Jupiter*, p. 74, and *On Sacrifices*, p. [Col. 0575D] 185). Certainly among Latin writers *caesius*, or blue, is the same color as *glaucus*, blue-green or gray-green, such as, they say, the eyes of cats.

What of the fact that Pausanias testifies that Neptune, of whom Minucius speaks, had blue-green eyes like Minerva? “Concerning the image of Minerva,” these are his words, “which has blue-green eyes, γλαυκοὺς ἔχον τοὺς ὀφθαλμούς, I learn a Libyan story: that Minerva is the daughter of Neptune and Lake Tritonis, and that for this reason she has blue-green eyes like Neptune, καὶ διὰ τοῦτο γλαυκοὺς εἶναι ὥσπερ καὶ τῷ Ποσειδῶνι ὀφθαλμούς.” If anyone should maintain and prove that Pausanias is speaking there of a different Minerva from that of the author cited by us, we shall not oppose him. No one, however, will deny that Cicero and our Minucius posited some distinction [Col. 0576A] between the eyes of Neptune and those of Minerva, perhaps because the eyes of one were greener than those of the other. On these eyes of Minerva, see Arnobius, whom we discuss in the proper place (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 14, art. 1).

Juno, moreover, as our author next says, was said by the pagans to have “bovine eyes” (Homer, *Iliad* IV, v. 5). He undoubtedly followed Homer, by whom she is called βοῶπις πότνια Ἥρη, “venerable ox-eyed Juno” (*Iliad* XIV, v. 159). The poet’s Latin translator renders this: “Venerable Juno with beautiful eyes.” If you ask the reason, we shall answer that he rendered the Greek name in this way because bovine eyes were regarded as the most beautiful of all. For Lucian (*Images*, p. 590) regards as a most accomplished painter one who made βοῶπίν [Col. 0576B] τινα ποιήσας αὐτῶν, “a certain one among them ox-eyed”—that is, who painted Juno “with bovine eyes.”

Minucius continues (p. 184): “Mercury has winged feet, Pan hoofed feet, Saturn fettered feet.” Surely no one is unaware that Mercury was imagined as the messenger of the gods and therefore as bearing wings upon his head and feet. Accordingly, Homer sang these lines about him when he was sent by Jupiter:

> Ὡς ἔφατ’, οὐδ’ ἀπίθησε διάκτορος Ἀργειφόντης.  
> Αὐτίκ’ ἔπειθ’, ὑπὸ ποσσὶν ἐδήσατο καλὰ πέδιλα,  
> Ἀμβρόσια, χρύσεια· τά μιν φέρον ἠμὲν ἐφ’ ὑγρὴν,  
> Ἠδ’ ἐπ’ ἀπείρονα γαῖαν, ἅμα πνοιῇς ἀνέμοιο.
>
> Thus he spoke, and Mercury the messenger, slayer of Argus, did not disobey.  
> At once he fastened beneath his feet the beautiful sandals,  
> Immortal and golden, which bore him alike over the sea  
> And over the boundless earth together with the breath of the wind.

(Homer, *Odyssey* V, v. 43 ff.) [Col. 0576C]

Pan, however, was said to have hoofed or goat-like feet, as Eusebius relates in these words:

> “Indeed, they themselves describe the appearance and form of their own bodies, as in the case of Pan himself; what he was like we learn from his oracles: ‘Though I am mortal, I now offer prayers, Πανὶ συμφύτῳ θεῷ, θισσοκέρατι, δισσόποδι τραγοσκελεῖ—to Pan, the god born with me, double-horned, two-footed, and goat-legged, distinguished for sensual pleasure.’”

(Euseb., *Preparation for the Gospel*, bk. III, ch. 14, p. 124.) Herodotus indeed acknowledges that painters and sculptors represent him with a goat’s face and goat’s legs; yet he candidly admits that it is not agreeable for him to explain why this was done [Col. 0576D] (Herodot., bk. II, §46). But you may consult Servius’ notes on Virgil and others (Serv. on Virgil).

Concerning Saturn’s “fettered feet,” as our author calls them, we read these words committed to writing by Cicero: “This ancient belief filled Greece: that Heaven was castrated by his son Saturn, while Saturn himself was bound by his son Jupiter.” Our authors Athenagoras and Athanasius support the Latin orator, as does Epiphanius, besides very many others. In accordance with the thought of our Minucius, Epiphanius argues from it as follows: “But one who is held in custody is not only subjected to someone more powerful, but is also ordinarily restrained with bonds because of some wrongdoing” (*Selections* 2, v. 31; Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. II, p. 223; Athenag., *Embassy for the Christians*, pp. 19 [Col. 0577A] and 20; Athanas., *Oration Against the Pagans*, §11, p. 12; Epiphan., *Ancoratus*, §106, p. 107).

Moreover, in Lucian, when a priest had asked Saturn himself whether he had truly been hurled by his son Jupiter into Tartarus after being placed in chains, Saturn replies:

> “Since I was already old and gout-ridden with age—from which it also came about that most people imagined that I was fettered, τὸ μὲν ὅλον γέρων ἤδη καὶ ποδαγρὸς ὤν, διὸ καὶ πεπεδῆσθαί με οἱ πολλοὶ εἴκασαν—I lacked the strength to punish so many crimes of this age. . . . And so, taking thought for myself, I yielded my place to Jupiter.”

Cicero and Macrobius finally offer other reasons why he was defeated by Jupiter (Lucian, *Saturnalia*, p. 1022 f.; Cicero, loc. cit.; Macrob., *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 8).

[Col. 0577B] Minucius then continues: “Janus, however, bears two faces, as though so that he may walk backward.” To omit Athenaeus (*Deipnosophists*, bk. XV, p. 592) and very many others, Ovid has these verses about him:

> Janus, two-headed source of the silently gliding year,  
> You alone among the gods above can see your own back.  
> . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .  
> Yet what god shall I call you, Janus of double form?  
> For Greece has no divinity equal to you.

(Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. I, §4, v. 65.) If you ask why they called him two-faced, Plutarch, Macrobius, Cyprian, and Vossius will give you differing explanations (Plutarch, *Roman Questions*, p. 279; Macrob., *Saturnalia*, bk. I, chs. 7 and 9; Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 12; Vossius, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, bk. I, ch. 18, p. 71 ff.). Finally, [Col. 0577C] we shall add that this Janus, according to the testimony of the same Macrobius and also of Augustine, was proclaimed by the pagans not only two-faced but also four-faced and four-formed (Macrob., *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 9; Augustine, *City of God*, bk. VII, ch. 4). Who, then, will not lament that dreadful blindness of the pagans, who believed not so much human beings as monstrosities of human beings to be gods, and rendered them divine honors? Yet their insane superstition did not stop there. They worshiped many still more infamous forms belonging to other gods. We must now investigate how our author explains and demolishes these.

### ARTICLE II  
#### With what madness the pagans celebrated Diana as high-girded and a huntress, as the many-breasted Ephesian, and as Trivia with three heads and many hands; Jupiter as sometimes bearded and sometimes beardless; Ammon with horns; Capitolinus bearing thunderbolts; Latiaris drenched in blood; Feretrius not heard—whose monstrosities are as numerous as his names

[Col. 0577D]

Minucius pursues his argument and attacks Diana’s threefold form: “Diana,” he says, “is high-girded and a huntress; as the Ephesian, she is built up with many breasts and udders”—the codex reads *veribus*, “spits”—“and as Trivia she is terrifying with three heads and many hands.” Who does not know that Diana was a huntress? Pausanias [Col. 0578A] (bk. VII, pp. 230 and 234) and others mention this more than once. That in hunting she is rightly said to have been high-girded may be gathered from these verses of Ovid:

> Over ridges, through forests, and across thorny rocks she wanders,  
> Her knee bare and her garment girded up after Diana’s fashion.

(Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, bk. X, fable 12.)

And again elsewhere:

> Such are Diana’s girded-up legs portrayed  
> When, stronger than the strong beasts, she pursues them.

(*Amores*, bk. V, Elegy 22.) There is no need to cite other poems or testimonies of the same Ovid, of Prudentius (epist. IV, hymn on Romanus, v. 257), and of others concerning a matter sufficiently known to all.

Some, however, think that the following words in [Col. 0578B] our manuscript codex are corrupt: “The Ephesian, built up with many breasts and spits.” For certain men could not bring themselves to believe that Diana, being a virgin, had several breasts. They therefore think that the word “Ceres,” who was called many-breasted, must be added. Others judge that instead of *veribus* (“spits”) one should read either *uberibus* (“udders”) or *tuberibus* (“swellings”). But these conjectures are extremely weak, or rather false, and the reading of our ancient codex must not be changed. For Jerome, in his commentary on Paul’s Epistle to the Ephesians, transmitted this concerning both Dianas, the high-girded and the many-breasted:

> “He wrote to the Ephesians, who worshiped Diana—not the huntress who holds a bow and is girded up, but that many-breasted figure whom the Greeks call πολύμαστον (*polymaston*, ‘many-breasted’), so that, by her very image, they might falsely claim that she was the nature of all beasts and living things.”

(Jerome, preface to Paul’s *Epistle to the Ephesians*.) [Col. 0578C]

Nor should you insist that Jerome followed our erring Minucius there and struck against the same rock of error. It is certain that neither erred, since Diana of Ephesus is known to have been mentioned by many, and no one is unaware that she had a most celebrated temple among the Ephesians. But her image was represented there with many breasts, as is evident from the various statues and pictures of her exhibited by Federico Ubaldini in his treatise explaining the symbolic statue of Diana of Ephesus (vol. VII, *Greek Antiquities*, p. 364).

It is no less certain that these statues of Diana, like other statues of the gods, were supported by upright and transverse *verua*, that is, spits or rods, which were supports and stays made of iron. For Lucas Holstenius proves this most explicitly in his letter to Cardinal Francesco Barberini, “On the Supports Affixed to the Image of Diana of Ephesus” (ibid., p. 396). There he exhibits certain images of the same Diana, with her supports and numerous breasts, engraved upon coins. From these facts, therefore, it is clearly established that some critics have unjustly assailed both our author’s text and Jerome’s testimony. [Col. 0578D]

Moreover, Virgil sings in these verses of Diana Trivia as terrifying with three heads, as Minucius says:

> With three hundred mouths she thunders forth the gods, Erebus, and Chaos,  
> And threefold Hecate—the three faces of the virgin Diana.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. IV, vv. 518 and 511.) [Col. 0579A]

In commenting upon these verses, Servius observes that she is called not only Diana but also Lucina and Hecate; that because of her threefold power she is imagined as triple and three-formed; and that her temples were therefore constructed at crossroads. Varro, however, notes from Ennius that she is called Trivia either because she is commonly stationed at a three-way crossing in Greek towns, or because she is called Luna, the Moon, which moves in heaven in three ways: height, breadth, and length (Varro, *On the Latin Language*, bk. VI, p. 74). Undoubtedly for the first reason Ovid said:

> You see Hecate’s faces turned in three directions,  
> So that she may guard the crossroads divided into three ways.

[Col. 0579B] For this very reason she is called by him “three-headed Hecate,” and by Horace and others “the three-formed goddess.” See Orpheus, Pausanias, and what Eusebius reported from Porphyry, and, if you wish, Prudentius also (Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. I, §3, and *Metamorphoses*, bk. VII, fable 3; Horace, bk. II, Ode 22; Orph., *Argonautica* and *Vow to the Muse*, vol. I, *Greek Poets*, pp. 494 and 501; Pausan., bk. II, p. 72; Euseb., *Preparation for the Gospel*, bk. IV, ch. 23, p. 174; Prudent., *Against Symmachus*, bk. I, §174).

It is therefore no wonder that, since she was three-formed, she was also said to have had several hands. Rigault indeed testifies in his note on this passage of Minucius that he had seen such a figure on a bronze coin belonging to Giovanni Battista Altino. Consult further, if you wish, Caelius Rhodiginus, Vossius, Verderius, Giraldus, and others who discuss the same [Col. 0579C] Diana (Rhodig., *Ancient Readings*, bk. XX, ch. 6; Vossius, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, bk. II, ch. 25, p. 216 ff.). Who, then, will not join our Minucius in laughing at and detesting such gods?

He strikes the pagans with no lighter a blow when they claimed that Jupiter had various countenances and differing faces. “What,” he asked, “of your Jupiter himself? At one moment he is set up beardless; at another he is placed as bearded.” Earlier, Cotta had pressed Velleius in Cicero as follows: “By that reasoning it will be permissible to say that Jupiter is always bearded” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. I, p. 207, line 26). But Lucian asks where the pagans had seen this bearded Jupiter (Lucian, *On Sacrifices*, p. 185). If you too should ask this, Prudentius will answer:

> Art, effective in sowing errors,  
> When it wreathes Jupiter with a bristling beard.

[Col. 0579D] (Prudent., *On the Roman Martyr*, v. 243.)

Yet Virgil calls him Anxur:

> And the Circean ridge, over whose fields  
> Jupiter Anxur presides.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VII, v. 799.) The name *Anxur* means the same as “beardless,” as Servius reminds us in his note on these verses of Virgil: “In this region of Campania,” he says, “a boy Jupiter was worshiped, who was called Anxur, as though ἄνευ ξυρᾶς (*aneu xyras*), that is, ‘without a razor,’ because he had never shaved a beard,” and so was beardless. He was represented in this youthful form not only in that region of Campania but elsewhere also. Pausanias records [Col. 0580A] that two such images of Jupiter were set up by the Eleans beside the temple of Pelops. Of one he says: “There is a youthful Jupiter, οὐκ ἔχον γένεια, ‘having no beard,’ among the offerings of Smicythus. . . . A little farther on, proceeding straight ahead, there is another image of a youthful Jupiter, dedicated by the Elcitans” (Pausan., *Eliaca*, bk. V, p. 173). To this may be added Naso, by whom the youthful Jupiter is described as follows:

> Jupiter too is young: behold his youthful face;  
> Then look upon his hands—he holds no thunderbolts.

(Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. III, §6, v. 437.)

After this Minucius enumerates other ridiculous forms of the same Jupiter which the pagans were not ashamed to venerate: “When,” he says, “he is called Ammon, he has horns; and when Capitolinus, he then bears thunderbolts.” Cicero had earlier said: “Indeed, the appearance of Jupiter Capitolinus is one thing among us, while that of Jupiter Ammon is another among the Africans” [Col. 0580B] (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. I, p. 207, line 23). Herodotus explains in these words why Jupiter Ammon was believed to have horns:

> “The Thebans and all who, because of them, spare sheep say that when Hercules wished to see Jupiter and Jupiter was unwilling to be seen by him, he was finally prevailed upon and devised the following expedient: he cut off a ram’s head, clothed himself in the woolly hide he had stripped from it, and thus showed himself to Hercules. For this reason the Egyptians established the practice of making Jupiter’s image with a ram’s face, ἀπὸ τούτου κριοπρόσωπον τὤγαλμα τοῦ Διὸς ποιοῦσι; and the Ammonians received the practice from the Egyptians. They are colonists of the Egyptians and Ethiopians and employ a language intermediate between the two. They also seem to me to have called themselves Ammonians [Col. 0580C] because the Egyptians call Jupiter ‘Ammon.’”

(Herodot., bk. II, §42.)

Hence he is called κερατοφόρος (*keratophoros*, “horn-bearing”) by the Greeks, κριοπρόσωπος (*krioprosōpos*, “ram-faced”) by Lucian, “ram-horned” by Macrobius and Arnobius, and horn-bearing Ammon by the poets Ovid, Claudian, and others (Lucian, *On Sacrifices*, p. 186; Macrob., *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 21; Arnob., bk. VI *Against the Pagans*, p. 197).

Moreover, concerning this same Ammon and also the Capitoline Jupiter, we read these verses of Lucan:

> They had come to the temple which alone the uncultivated Garamantes  
> Possess among the Libyan peoples. There, as tradition tells, stands  
> A horned Jupiter—but not one who brandishes thunderbolts  
> Or resembles ours: it is Ammon with curling horns.

(Lucan, bk. IX, v. 517.)

[Col. 0580D] Jupiter Capitolinus is mentioned very frequently by various writers, especially writers of Roman history. He was called by that name because his temple and image were placed on the Capitol. Titus Livy, Suetonius, Dio Cassius, Plutarch, Pliny, and Valerius Maximus mention him. Finally, his image may be seen upon a very ancient coin of Aurelia Quirina, a Vestal Virgin. On it he sits with the upper half of his body naked and the lower half clothed, bearing a thunderbolt in his right hand and a scepter in his left; around it is inscribed the name: *Jup. Opt. Max. Capitolinus*—“Jupiter Best and Greatest, Capitolinus” (Livy, bk. II, §21; Sueton., bk. II, p. 344; Cassius, *Roman History*, bk. LIV, p. 598 ff.; Plutarch, *Life of Publicola*, p. 104; Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. III, chs. [Col. 0581A] 2 and 8, pp. 91 and 125; Valer., bk. I, ch. 2, §2; Lescalopier on Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, bk. I, §83, p. 118).

Our author mentions two further Jupiters: “Latiaris,” he says, “is drenched in blood,” because human victims, as we have shown, were sacrificed to him; “and Feretrius is not heard.” Plutarch reports various explanations of this surname and sets them forth in these words:

> Φερέτριος Ζεύς, ὡς μὲν ἔνιοι φασίν, ἀπὸ τοῦ φερητρευομένου τροπαίου, κατὰ τὴν ἑλληνίδα γλῶσσαν ἔτι πολλὴν τότε συμμεμιγμένην τῇ Λατίνων. Ὡς δὲ ἕτεροι, Διός ἐστιν ἡ προσωνυμία κεραυνοβολοῦντος, τὸ γὰρ τύπτειν, φερίρε, οἱ Ῥωμαῖοι καλοῦσιν· ἄλλοι δὲ παρὰ τὴν τοῦ πολέμου πληγὴν γεγονέναι τοὔνομα λέγουσι· καὶ γὰρ νῦν ἐν ταῖς μάχαις ὅταν διώκωσι τοὺς πολεμίους, πυκνὸν τὸ [Col. 0581B] φέρι, τουτέστιν παῖε, παρεγγυῶσιν ἀλλήλοις.
>
> “Jupiter is called Feretrius from the trophy that was carried upon a bier (*feretrum*), according to some, the name being derived from the Greek language, which at that time was still extensively intermingled with Latin speech. Others affirm that it is the surname of Jupiter as wielder of the thunderbolt, derived from striking (*ferire*), for the Romans call ‘to strike’ *ferire*. Others say that the name arose from the blows inflicted in war. For even now in battles, when they press upon the enemy to strike them, they repeatedly exhort one another with *feri*, that is, ‘strike!’”

(Plutarch, *Life of Marcellus*, p. 302.) He adds that, in the opinion of certain men, Numa ordered the first spoils and the *spolia opima* (‘spoils of honor’) to be consecrated to Jupiter Feretrius.

Titus Livy, however, attests that this surname was given to him by Romulus. After defeating and killing Acron, king of the Caeninenses, Romulus, “bearing his spoils [Col. 0581C] upon a frame constructed for the purpose”—these are Livy’s own words—“ascended the Capitol; and there, after depositing them beside an oak sacred to the shepherds, he marked out, together with his gift, the boundaries of a temple of Jupiter and added a surname to the god: ‘Jupiter Feretrius,’ he said, ‘I, King Romulus, victorious, bring you these royal arms, and dedicate within the boundaries which I have just mentally marked out a temple as a dwelling place for the *spolia opima* (‘spoils of honor’) which, after kings and commanders of the enemy have been slain, my descendants, following my example, will bring here’” (Livy, bk. I, §10). The same history was committed to memory not only by Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Dio Cassius, Florus, Messalla Corvinus, and others (Dionys. Hal., bk. II, p. 182; Dio Cass., *Roman History*, bk. XLIV, p. 274; Florus, bk. I, ch. 1; Mess. Corv., *On the Progeny of Augustus*, p. 531). [Col. 0581D] But Plutarch himself, already cited by us, also clearly indicates that from this arose the custom of carrying the *spolia opima* of slain commanders to the same Jupiter Feretrius (Plutarch, *Life of Romulus*, p. 27). Hence Propertius too sang:

> . . . . . . . . the derivation of Feretrius:  
> Whenever a commander strikes down a commander with an unerring sword;  
> Or because they bore these conquered arms upon their own shoulders,
