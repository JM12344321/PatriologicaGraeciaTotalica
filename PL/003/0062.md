But Tertullian had treated this much more fully before: “Philosophers affect truth, and in affecting it they corrupt it, since they seek glory. Christians both necessarily desire it and present it in its integrity, since they are concerned for their salvation. Thus we are not on an equal footing, as you suppose, either in knowledge or in discipline” (Tertull., *Apology*, chap. 46, p. 40). He discusses many more matters on this subject there, which must be examined by us in their proper place [Col. 0548D] (*Dissertation on Tertullian*, chap. 12, arts. 3 and 4; and chap. 13, arts. 2 and 3).

Moreover, Minucius says, when the pagan philosophers uttered grand things with the cosmetic coloring of empty eloquence, they were eloquent against their own vices. Earlier, however, they had been called by the same Tertullian “men lustful for glory and eloquence” (Tertullian, ibid., chap. 47, p. 41), because they abused these things to serve their lust. They therefore sold nothing but a cosmetic coloring of words. Nor was unknown even to the pagans themselves what Anaxippus sang in these verses preserved by Athenaeus:

> Alas! You philosophize; but I find philosophers  
> Wise only in their words,  
> [Col. 0549A] While in their deeds I see that they are foolish.

(Athen., bk. XIII, *Deipnosophistae*, chap. 9, p. 610 ff.)

Would you like a celebrated example of this? Rufinus will give you an eminent philosopher who, although he surpassed all the others in eloquence and learning, was nevertheless confounded by a single bishop of the Council of Nicaea through the plain and simple exposition of Christian doctrine, and openly confessed himself defeated (Rufinus, bk. X, *Ecclesiastical History*, chap. 13).

Although Minucius said that truth had neither been sought nor discovered by the pagan philosophers, we nevertheless read that a little earlier he had written: “From the divine proclamations of the Prophets they imitated a shadow of truth that had been adulterated.” By these words, however, he means nothing other than that the philosophers pursued a shadow of the truth drawn from the sacred proclamations of our [Col. 0549B] Prophets, whether handed down in writing or orally, since they corrupted the truth found there with their various errors. This, unless we are mistaken, has already been demonstrated quite clearly more than once in our other dissertations (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 327, 423, 1104, 1112), and will be demonstrated further in those that follow.

Furthermore, the profound difference in doctrine and morals between Christians and pagans did not prevent them from sometimes living in the same house and addressing one another by the name “brother.” For we have seen that the pagan Caecilius lived at Rome with Minucius Felix, and that he was called brother by Octavius, just as Octavius was called brother by him (above, chap. 3, art. 2, pp. 11 [Col. 0549C] and 12).

Nor should you say that Caecilius and Octavius were not real but fictitious persons; for even if that were so, it must nevertheless be certain that Minucius would not have said these things unless this custom of address and shared residence had become established in his own time.

# CHAPTER XIV

## THE ARGUMENTS BY WHICH CAECILIUS ATTEMPTS TO PROVE THAT HIS GODS REALLY EXIST, AND CONSEQUENTLY THAT THE RELIGION OF THE PAGANS IS TRUE, ARE EXAMINED

### ARTICLE I

#### Caecilius’ first argument is explained, by which he attempts to prove that his gods are true gods from ancestral tradition and the most ancient worship of the gods—namely, Eleusinian Ceres, the Phrygian Mother or Cybele, Epidaurian Aesculapius, Chaldaean Bel, Syrian Astarte, Tauric Diana, and Gallic Mercury

[Col. 0549D]

Thus far we have heard Caecilius arguing, always in vain, against the Christian religion, and Octavius triumphing over him. We must now see whether he fought with greater success in defense of his own pagan religion. His first argument, which he considers the strongest, is drawn from its antiquity. For from the very beginning of the world—or, as Caecilius himself says, “while the age was still uncultivated and at the very birth of the world”—our ancestors were privileged to have either “gracious” gods or [Col. 0550A] “kings.” He said “gracious,” following Ovid, who sang:

> You made your wishes, and had gracious gods;

(Ovid, bk. V, *Metamorphoses*, penultimate section), that is, gods who were favorably disposed, benevolent, and readily granted what was desired. From there their worship spread into every part of the earth. The Eleusinians worshiped Ceres; the Phrygians, the Mother, that is, Cybele; the Epidaurians, Aesculapius; the Chaldaeans, Bel; the Syrians, Astarte; the Taurians, Diana; the Gauls, Mercury; and the Romans, all the gods. And because the Romans fortified their city with the sacred rites of every nation, with chaste virgins, and with honors paid to priests, they obtained dominion over the whole world. From these considerations Caecilius concludes that, since everything is uncertain, it is better to follow those religions, rites, and forms of divine worship handed down by our ancestors from the cradle of the world [Col. 0550B] and enduring down to his own age than, with blind fickleness, to surrender oneself to a new religion defiled by secret and utterly corrupt crimes. This is the conclusion of Cotta the Academic in Cicero, of Celsus in Origen, of Symmachus in Ambrose, and of other pagans, as Arnobius and Lactantius attest (Cic., bk. III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, beginning, p. 240; Origen, bk. V, *Against Celsus*, p. 254 ff.; Symmachus, *Memorial*, in Ambrose, §§ 3, 5, and 8, p. 829; Arnobius, bk. II, *Against the Pagans*, p. 30 f.; Lactantius, bk. II, chap. 6, p. 171).

Because Caecilius believed that none of the things concerning his gods which we have just reviewed and which he put forward were unknown to anyone, he did not trouble to prove them individually. But if anyone should wish to know something about them, since they may now perhaps be unknown to him, [Col. 0550C] we shall observe that Eleusis was a city of Attica situated not far from Athens. There Ceres, mentioned by Caecilius, while searching for her daughter, was most kindly received as a guest by Eleusis, who was ruling there. In memory of these events the Eleusinians celebrated rites called the Eleusinian Mysteries, which we have discussed elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. I, chap. 9, art. 2, p. 789 ff.). Herodotus attests that the temple of Eleusinian Ceres there was founded by Philistus, son of Pasicles. You may read more in Diodorus Siculus, Strabo, Mela, and Pausanias, as well as, if you wish, what we have noted elsewhere (Herodotus, bk. IX, §§ 64 and 96; Diodorus Siculus, bk. I, *Library*, p. 117, and bk. V, p. 200; Strabo, bk. IX, *Geography*, p. 395; [Col. 0550D] Mela, *On the Situation of the World*, chap. 3; Pausanias, bk. VI, p. 103, and bk. VIII, pp. 249 and 256; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 759 f.).

From the already cited Pausanias you will learn why “Aesculapius,” as Caecilius says, was worshiped by the Epidaurians and where his temple was situated. Livy writes: “Epidaurus did not equal Sicyon and Argos in resources, but was famous for the renowned temple of Aesculapius, which stood five miles from the city. Now it is rich in traces of offerings that have been torn away; at that time it was rich in the offerings that the sick dedicated to the god as payment for healing remedies.” We have noted something elsewhere about the inscription of this temple (Pausanias, bk. II, p. 68; Livy, bk. XLV, § 18; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1093).

The Phrygians, Caecilius further says, venerated the Mother, [Col. 0551A] as Strabo attests in these words: “The Berecyntes, a Phrygian people, and the Phrygians generally, as well as those of the Trojans who live around Ida, themselves honor Rhea and celebrate orgiastic rites for her, calling her the Mother of the Gods, and Agdistis, and the Phrygian and Great Goddess; and, from places, Idaean, Dindymene, Bylene, Pessinuntian, and Cybele” (Strabo, bk. X, *Geography*, p. 469). See also what we have noted elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. I, chap. 9, art. 2, p. 758).

How great was the reverence with which the Chaldaeans honored Bel may easily be gathered from the enormous size of the temple built in his honor. Herodotus, in his account of Babylon, reports the following about it:

[Col. 0551B]

> In the other enclosure is the temple of Zeus Bel, with gates of bronze; it still existed in my own time. It is a square measuring two stades on every side. In the middle of the sanctuary a solid tower has been built. . . . No human being spends the night there except one woman of the native population, whom the god has chosen from them all, as the Chaldaeans, the priests of this god, relate.

(Herodotus, bk. I, § 181.)

Diodorus Siculus also says more about this temple, which he reports to have been built by Semiramis but to have collapsed from age (Diodorus, *Library*, [Col. 0551C] bk. [number missing], p. 69). Nevertheless, Pliny asserts that the same temple still remained in his own time (Pliny, bk. V, *Natural History*, chap. 26, p. 712). Whatever may be true about the temple’s destruction, we have shown elsewhere that Theophilus of Antioch, praised by Lactantius, wrote that the Assyrians and Babylonians offered worship and veneration to Bel. Read again Herodotus, bk. III, § 158, p. 223; Jerome on Ezekiel 24:11 and Hosea 2:16; Claudian, *Panegyric I in Praise of Stilicho*; Vossius, bk. I, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, chap. 24, p. 93; Giraldus, *History of the Gods*, syntagma 2, p. 108 ff. (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. II, diss. 4, chap. 2, § 1, p. 502).

Why, moreover, should it be surprising if the Syrians worshiped “Astarte,” whom Caecilius also [Col. 0551D] mentions, since Tertullian is our witness: “Every province and city has its own god, as Syria has Astarte.” Elsewhere, however, as we shall see in its proper place, she is called by him “Atargatis,” and by Cicero the fourth Venus: “The fourth,” he says, “was conceived in Syria and Tyre; she is called Astarte, and tradition says that she married Adonis.” Philo of Byblos likewise says from Sanchuniathon, in Eusebius: “The Phoenicians relate that this same Astarte is Venus.” To Lucian, however, she appears to be the same as the moon, although he had learned from a certain priest and from the Phoenicians that she was Europa. From Sacred Scripture, moreover, some infer that she was Astarte, goddess of the Sidonians, whom King Solomon worshiped [Col. 0552A] and for whom he erected high places, afterward destroyed by King Josiah (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 24, p. 26, and bk. II, *To the Nations*, chap. 8, p. 70; *Dissertation on Tertullian’s Apology*, chap. 27, art. 1; Cicero, bk. III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 248, line 30; in Eusebius, bk. I, *Preparation for the Gospel*, chap. 10, p. 38; Lucian, *On the Syrian Goddess*, beginning; 1 Kings 11:5; 2 Kings 23:13). See further, if you wish, Varro and Scaliger’s notes on his words, Eusebius, the *Suda*, Gyraldus, Salmasius, and Vossius (Varro, bk. IV, *On the Latin Language*, p. 14; Eusebius, bk. I, *Preparation for the Gospel*, chap. 10, p. 37; *Suda*, s.v. “Astarte”; Gyraldus, syntagma 13, p. 375; Salmasius, *Exercises on Solinus*, p. 574; Vossius, bk. I, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, chap. 28, p. 88, and bk. II, p. 206).

Caecilius adds that divine honors were rendered to “Mercury” by the [Col. 0552B] Gauls, as is confirmed by these words of Caesar: “Of the gods they worship Mercury above all. He has the greatest number of images. They regard him as the inventor of all the arts, the guide of roads and journeys, and as possessing the greatest power over the pursuit of gain and commerce.” Tacitus supports Caesar, relating that the Gauls sacrificed human victims to this same Mercury, as we noted above (Caesar, bk. VI, *Gallic War*, well after the beginning; Tacitus, *On the Customs of the Germans*, near the beginning). One may also consult Vossius, although he is criticized by some because he attempted to prove from Livy’s testimony that a mound of Mercury Teutates had been found near New Carthage. For the words of Livy that he cited are not found in the very ancient Puteanus manuscript. To have noted this in passing will perhaps not be unwelcome to anyone [Col. 0552C] who wishes to consult Vossius (Vossius, bk. I, *On the Origin of Idolatry*, chap. 13, p. 134; chap. 37, p. 140; and bk. II, p. 238 ff.).

Finally, no one is unaware that the superstition of the Romans was such that they worshiped all the gods about whom Caecilius boasts. For their city, “although it ruled nearly all nations,” as Leo the Great says, “was enslaved to the errors of all nations; and it seemed to itself to have embraced a great religion because it rejected no falsehood” (Pope Leo, sermon I, *On the Feast of the Apostles Peter and Paul*). Whether, however, they merited dominion over the whole world by this pious worship, as Caecilius calls it and contends, we shall consider shortly.

From all these things Caecilius believes it can be concluded [Col. 0552D] that the gods of the pagans truly exist; that the traditions concerning them which arose from the beginning of the world must by no means be abandoned; and that one must instead persevere steadfastly in this religion of our ancestors. We must examine, according to our customary method, in what manner and by what force of reasoning Minucius attacks and refutes him.

### ARTICLE II

#### How Octavius refutes Caecilius’ argument and shows both that the worship of the gods is not of such great antiquity and that the impious and superstitious traditions of the ancestors—such as those concerning Scylla, the Chimera, the Hydra, and the Centaurs—must not be followed

The argument of Caecilius, which the other [Col. 0553A] pagans likewise employed without exception, has two parts. The first concerns absolutely all pagans; the second, specifically the Romans themselves. Each is utterly overthrown by a corresponding answer from Minucius and by various, extremely weighty considerations. Therefore, so that everything may be treated in proper order, we shall begin from the first points of the discussion and afterward proceed to the others.

First, therefore, it must be clear to anyone who considers without a thoroughly prejudiced mind what has been said thus far that both parts of Caecilius’ argument had already been completely weakened and shattered by those arguments with which Minucius, as we have seen, demonstrated most conclusively that there are not many gods but only one, and that he cares for each and every thing by his divine providence.

[Col. 0553B] Accordingly, after he had fortified both these foundations of true religion with an impregnable bulwark of arguments drawn chiefly from the opinions of the pagan philosophers themselves, he then proceeded to refute the first part of Caecilius’ reasoning. Caecilius had said that one must adhere most steadfastly to the most ancient traditions of our ancestors, traditions coeval with the world, since they had worshiped their gods from the beginning of the world.

But our Minucius shows most clearly that this is absurd and wholly contrary to right reason: “For if,” he says, “the world is ruled by providence and governed by the will of one God, antiquity—delighted or captivated by the fables of the unlearned—ought not to carry us away into the error of mutual agreement, since it is refuted by the opinions of its own [Col. 0553C] philosophers, whose authority rests upon both reason and antiquity.” As you see, he there strikes down and overthrows with a twofold battering ram the antiquity of their religion and worship of the gods vaunted by the pagans. First, through the authority of the philosophers, which no pagan could oppose, he makes the falsity of this supposed antiquity manifest. And the things which we have seen related and proved by Minucius himself and by Clement of Alexandria certainly contributed greatly to the confirmation of this assertion: namely, that these same philosophers drew most of their doctrines from our Sacred Scriptures, though they afterward distorted and corrupted them.

Second, however ancient the pagans’ religion might be, everyone was nevertheless obliged to renounce it because it was composed entirely of fables, utterly absurd lies, monstrous opinions, and the dreams [Col. 0553D] of delirious men. For, as Ambrose admirably teaches when refuting the same argument of Symmachus: “Let an old age that cannot correct itself be ashamed. It is not the gray hair of years that is praised, but that of character; there is no shame in passing over to better things” (Ambrose, epistle 18, § 7, p. 834). You may read more on this subject in Clement of Alexandria, Arnobius—whom we shall discuss in his proper place—and Prudentius (Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Pagans*, p. 57; Arnobius, bk. II, *Against the Pagans*, p. 90 ff.; Prudentius, bk. II, *Against Symmachus*).

But we cannot pass over Tertullian, who, proceeding still further, skillfully and sharply rebukes the pagans because they ceaselessly cast at Christians—to the point of nausea—the traditions of their ancestors, vaunted with loud [Col. 0554A] and frequently repeated outcries, although they themselves ruined and scorned them: “Where,” he says, “is your religion? Where is the reverence owed by you to your ancestors? In dress, food, furnishings, wealth, and finally in speech itself, you have renounced your forefathers. You are always praising the ancients, yet day by day you live in novel ways. From this it is shown that, while departing from the good institutions of your ancestors, you retain and preserve what you ought not to have preserved, although you failed to preserve what you ought to have preserved.” But we shall say more about these matters in their proper place, when we discuss his books (Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 6, p. 7; *Dissertation on Tertullian*, chap. 18, p. 15).

Although no one could have been unaware that the ancient beliefs of the pagans were ridiculous and wholly absurd, Minucius nevertheless [Col. 0554B] demonstrates this by several examples, so as to satisfy everyone abundantly. For the pagans believed, he says, that there existed a many-shaped Scylla, a multiform Chimera, a Hydra reborn from its wounds, and Centaurs—horses entwined with human beings. Virgil described Scylla in these verses:

> But Scylla is confined within a cavern’s hidden recesses,  
> Thrusting forth her mouths and dragging ships upon the rocks.  
> Her upper form is human, a maiden with a beautiful breast  
> Down to the groin; below, she is a sea-monster of monstrous body,  
> With dolphins’ tails joined to a womb of wolves.

(Virgil, bk. III, *Aeneid*, v. 424 ff.)

She is represented differently, though no less monstrously, by Homer, whom you may consult, as also Ovid, Ausonius, Natalis Comes, and Vossius (Homer, *Odyssey*, bk. XII, p. 172; Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, bk. IV, fable 1; Ausonius, *Idyll* 4; Natalis Comes, bk. VIII [Col. 0554C], *Mythology*, chap. 12; Vossius, bk. IV, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, chap. 17, p. 34). The Chimera was depicted by Homer, Hesiod, Lucretius, and Ovid; if these do not suffice for you, you may also consult Virgil and Natalis Comes (Homer, *Iliad* VI, p. 106; Hesiod, *Theogony*, p. 469; Lucretius, bk. V, p. 57; Ovid, bk. IX, *Metamorphoses*, § 11; Virgil, bk. IV, *Aeneid*, 28; Natalis Comes, bk. IX, *Mythology*, chap. 4).

There were various opinions concerning the Centaurs, which Diodorus Siculus reports in these words: “Ixion, inflamed with love for Juno, did not hesitate to solicit the goddess for adultery. Jupiter therefore sent forth a cloud fashioned in the image of Juno and placed it before him in her stead; [Col. 0554D] Ixion coupled with the cloud and begot the so-called Centaurs, human in part. . . . Others relate that the Centaurs were raised by the Nymphs on Pelion and, after reaching adulthood, through passion for mares propagated the two-formed race called Hippocentaurs. Some affirm that the Centaurs born from Nephele and Ixion received the name Hippocentaurs because they were the first to undertake the practice of horsemanship, and that from this the fiction of a double nature passed into myth” (Diodorus Siculus, bk. IV, *Library*, p. 189; idem, p. 153). Earlier he had related how Hercules is said to have defeated these same Centaurs in his fourth contest. Cotta notes the first of these opinions in Cicero, where he says: “Even a single cloud is said to have given birth to the Centaurs” [Col. 0555A] (Cicero, bk. III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 247).

The author of the Eusebian *Chronicle* says: “The war of the Lapiths and Centaurs occurred at this time; Palaephatus writes in his book *On Incredible Tales* that the Centaurs were noble Thessalian horsemen” (Eusebius, *Chronicle*, at the year 772). Scaliger, however, in his observations on these words, freely and boldly rebukes him for citing Palaephatus in confirmation of this opinion, from which Palaephatus differs greatly. Others nevertheless attempt to prove it not only by the authority of Palaephatus but also by that of Tzetzes and Orosius. The latter further added that the Lapiths were called Centaurs by the Thessalians because, fighting from horseback, they appeared to form a single body with their horses (Tzetzes, *Histories*, *Chiliad* VII, chap. 99; Orosius, bk. I, chap. 13). Consult Ovid, Natalis Comes, [Col. 0555B] and Vossius (Ovid, bk. XII, *Metamorphoses*, fable 4; Comes, bk. VII, *Mythology*, chap. 4; Vossius, bk. I, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, chap. 15, p. 62).

Finally, Minucius adds that the pagans constantly proclaimed that birds, flowers, trees, and other things of this sort had been made from human beings. No one is unaware that such stories occur throughout Ovid’s books of *Metamorphoses*. Jerome, however, when discussing Nebuchadnezzar, encompasses all these things in a few words: “Greek and Roman histories have related that things far more incredible happened to human beings: their myths also tell that Scylla, the Chimera, the Hydra, and the Centaurs, birds and wild beasts, flowers and trees, stars and stones, were made from human beings” (Jerome, on Daniel 4:1).

You see, therefore, how rightly and deservedly Minucius concludes from these [Col. 0555C] and many other examples, to be reviewed below, that Caecilius is altogether mistaken, and that all the other pagans are in error who taught that it is better for everyone to follow the traditions of our ancestors concerning the gods and religion, and that the gods acknowledged by them ought to be feared rather than examined more closely and made the subject of judgment.

# CHAPTER XV

## THE ARGUMENTS BY WHICH MINUCIUS SHOWS THAT THE GODS OF THE PAGANS ARE FALSE ARE CONSIDERED

### ARTICLE I

#### With what folly the pagans proclaimed that human beings had been made gods because of the merit of their virtue or benefits conferred upon other human beings

[Col. 0555D]

A particularly abundant source and origin of the worship of false gods was the insane conviction of the pagans, by which they not only proclaimed but even boasted that their gods had indeed been human beings, but that, because of benefits conferred upon other human beings and outstanding deeds, they had received divinity as a reward—or, as Minucius says, that they were “regarded as gods because of the merits of their virtue or service.” Cicero is for us a witness to this origin of the gods beyond all objection; in words cited by Lactantius, he confirms it in a remarkable manner: “The life of human beings,” he says, “and common custom adopted the practice of raising men distinguished by benefactions to heaven through reputation and popular will. Hence Hercules, hence Castor and Pollux, hence Aesculapius, hence Liber also” (Cicero, bk. II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 223, line 13; Lactantius, bk. I, *Institutes*, chaps. [Col. 0556A] 8 and 15, pp. 43 and 80). Again, in another book, we read these words of his, likewise transcribed by Lactantius: “In most states it can be understood that, for the sake of increasing virtue, so that each of the best men might more willingly face danger for the republic, the memory of brave men was consecrated by the honor paid to the immortal gods” (Cicero, bk. III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 247, line 8; Lactantius, loc. cit.). Lactantius could certainly have produced a third passage from Cicero, where he speaks no less clearly than in those above: “They relate that brave, famous, or powerful men attained to the gods after death, and that these are the very ones whom we are accustomed to worship, pray to, and venerate” (Cicero, bk. I, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 212, line 34). Consider, I ask you, whether Minucius was aiming especially at these words of Cicero.

[Col. 0556B] That Latin orator did not say what the beginning of this perverse custom was; but Pliny declares that it was extremely ancient: “This is the most ancient custom of returning thanks to those who have deserved well: that they should enroll such persons among the divinities” (Pliny, bk. II, *Natural History*, chap. 7, p. 144). But however ancient that custom was, it did not arise at the very birth of the world, as Caecilius said. For, as Octavius correctly argues, it began only when “each nation venerated its founder, or a renowned leader, or a chaste queen braver than her sex, or the discoverer of some service or art.”

Grant, if you wish, that it was coeval with the world—something that can by no means be the case. Even then you will never thereby prove [Col. 0556C] that these are true gods, since they are neither eternal nor immortal, and since they received from human beings both life itself and a fictitious divinity; or since, as Octavius says, divinity “was given to the dead as a reward and to those yet to come as an example.” He could have proved this by various arguments, as Athanasius and Theodoret did (Athanasius, *Oration Against the Pagans*, p. 18, vol. I; Theodoret, vol. IV, discourse 3, *On the Angels*, p. 510); but he judged it better to send Caecilius and the other pagans to their own writers: “Read,” he says, “the writings of the Stoics, or the writings of the wise; you will acknowledge together with me” that people were “regarded as gods” because of the merits of virtue and bravery, or of service and benefactions. [Col. 0556D] But some suspect that “historians” should there be read instead of “Stoics,” doubtless because that term is more naturally contrasted with the following “wise men.” Yet this is mere conjecture, and certainly does not suffice by itself to reject the reading of our manuscript, which appears to be confirmed by Cicero’s authority. For where he discusses those who “were duly regarded as gods,” as he himself puts it, and the origin of their great multitude, he adds in the same place: “This topic was treated by Zeno and afterward explained at greater length by Cleanthes and Chrysippus” (Cicero, bk. II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 223, line 20 ff.). It is established, moreover, that Zeno was the founder of the Stoics and that these men were his disciples, as Laertius teaches, who wrote their lives, and as we have observed elsewhere (Laertius, bk. VII, *Lives and Doctrines of the Philosophers*, § 1 ff.; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1123).

[Col. 0557A] Two further witnesses are produced by our Minucius. Concerning the first, he says: “Prodicus speaks of those being assumed among the gods who, by wandering about and discovering new crops, benefited humankind.” Did he not excerpt this from these words of Cicero: “Prodicus of Chios said that things beneficial to human life were regarded as belonging among the gods”? In another book Cicero also reports what Xenophon handed down concerning Prodicus, and we have noted this passage of Xenophon elsewhere (Cicero, bk. I, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 212, line 72, and bk. I, *On Duties*, p. 367, line 19; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1245).

The second is called by our author “Perseus,” or Persaeus, “who philosophizes,” as he says, “in the same sense, and links together the crops discovered and the discoverers of the crops themselves under the same names, as the comic [Col. 0557B] saying has it, ‘Venus grows cold without Liber and Ceres.’” The comic poet Terence sang:

> By Hercules, this saying is true:  
> Without Liber and Ceres, Venus grows cold.

(Terence, *Eunuch*, act IV, scene 5.)

But there he indicates that this too had been taken from someone else. From whom, if not Euripides, to whom this Greek verse belongs?

> Venus is dead without Bacchus and Ceres.

The things Minucius related about Persaeus had likewise been drawn, like the preceding material, from the same book of Cicero already cited by us, where we read: “Persaeus, a pupil of Zeno”—as Laertius also says, or his servant, as Athenaeus says—“maintains that those by whom some great benefit for the cultivation of life had been discovered were regarded as gods; [Col. 0557C] and that useful and health-giving things themselves were designated by the names of gods, so that he did not even say that those things had been discovered by gods, but that they were themselves divine” (Cicero, bk. I, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 201, line 18; Laertius, bk. VII, *Life of Zeno*, §§ 13 and 54; Athenaeus, bk. IV, *Deipnosophistae*, chap. 17, p. 162). From the cited verse of the Latin and Greek poets, however, it is readily understood that, following Cicero, our Minucius chiefly means Ceres and Bacchus, who were said to have discovered things extremely useful to the life of all human beings. Hence Athenagoras rebukes those same pagans who “make gods of the elements and their parts” (*ta stoicheia kai ta moria autōn theopoiousi*), and call the fruit of the vine Bacchus, the vine itself Semele, [Col. 0557D] and the lightning bolt the heat of the sun (*ton de tēs ampelou karpon Dionyson, kai Semelēn autēn tēn ampelon, kai keraunon tēn tou hēliou phloga*) (Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, p. 24). How boldly the pagans attributed to Ceres the discovery of grain, to Bacchus that of wine, as Diodorus Siculus relates, and to others the discovery of other things useful to human beings, we shall show in our following dissertations (Diodorus Siculus, bk. III, *Library*, p. 142, and bk. V, p. 232; *Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 9, art. 2; *on Lactantius*, chap. 30, art. 5; *on Tertullian*, chap. 25, art. 3). No less clearly shall we demonstrate how rightly our earliest writers, like our Minucius, proved that no person of sound mind could infer from these or any other benefits conferred upon him that those who discovered but did not create them either are [Col. 0558A] gods or merited divinity. For Cicero himself, immediately after the words concerning Prodicus cited by us a little earlier, at once added: “What religion, then, does that Prodicus leave?” (Cicero, bk. II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 212, line 34). Once the true God has been rejected and false gods introduced, there can plainly be no religion, but only empty superstition. But if the discoverers of things useful to human beings cannot be gods, much less can the things discovered by others. “For what,” Cicero further says, “is more absurd than either giving the names of gods to sordid and ugly things, or restoring among the gods human beings already destroyed by death, whose entire worship would consist in mourning?” (Ibid., p. 201, line 21). Nothing, moreover, is truer than this judgment, since before any things were discovered or any discoverers of them existed, those things had undoubtedly [Col. 0558B] been created by him who founded the world and all creatures, and who alone can therefore be God. Who, then, will not marvel that this was one of the chief and most corrupt sources from which idolatry and the impious worship of false gods flowed out and inundated the whole world?

### ARTICLE II

#### Minucius’ argument is examined by which he proves that the gods of the pagans are false from their birth, native country, death, and tombs, especially those of Dictaean Jupiter, Delphic Apollo, Pharian Isis, and Eleusinian Ceres

By yet another argument Minucius utterly overthrows the divinity of all their deities fabricated by the pagans. He again drew it from the things [Col. 0558C] which they themselves handed down and continually vaunted concerning the births, native countries, and tombs of these same gods. There was doubtless no need for him to undertake to prove what they so remarkably boasted of. Nevertheless, as a completely reliable witness and guarantor, he offered Euhemerus in precisely these words: “Euhemerus traces the subject in detail, enumerates their births, native countries, and tombs, and points them out province by province.” Here, as usual, he appears to follow Cicero as his predecessor, who says: “Euhemerus demonstrates both the deaths and the burials of the gods” (Cicero, bk. I, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 112, line 37). This Euhemerus, if you wish to know, was a Greek writer whom some believed to have been a native of Agrigentum, others of Messene, some of Cos, and even of Tegea. [Col. 0558D] He composed several volumes of histories in which he committed to writing the things recalled by Cicero and Minucius. Read Plutarch, Athenaeus, Aelian, Augustine, Theodoret, and the others cited by us in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus* (Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris*, p. 360; Athenaeus, bk. XIV, *Deipnosophistae*, p. 658; Aelian, bk. II, *Various History*, chap. 32; Augustine, bk. I, *On the Agreement of the Evangelists*, chap. 23; Theodoret, discourse 3, *On the Angels*, p. 506). There we observed that this Euhemerus was numbered by many among the atheists, doubtless because of those histories of the gods. But this did not prevent Ennius, as Cicero and afterward Augustine say, from translating his books, or prevent the things he had narrated concerning the births, deeds, [Col. 0559A] deaths, and tombs of those gods from being true (Cicero, loc. cit.; Augustine, bk. VII, *City of God*, chap. 17, § 1, p. 183).

From what that Greek writer said concerning the origin, native country, and tombs of the gods, Minucius touches only upon a few examples, namely, “Dictaean Jupiter, Delphic Apollo, Pharian Isis, and Eleusinian Ceres.” Dicte is a mountain on the island of Crete. In its cave Ops, or Rhea, the wife of Saturn, gave birth to Jupiter and secretly entrusted him to the Curetes, or Corybantes, and the Idaean Dactyls, to be guarded so that, newly born, he would not be devoured by his father like his other sons (Strabo, bk. X, *Geography*, p. 478). Hence Virgil:

> Come now, I shall explain the qualities which Jupiter himself  
> Bestowed upon the bees, as a reward for their having followed  
> The tuneful clashing of the Curetes and their ringing bronze,  
> And fed the king of heaven beneath the Dictaean cave.

[Col. 0559B] (Virgil, *Georgics*, bk. IV, v. 149 ff.)

If you desire another Latin poet, we shall quickly produce Lucretius for you, who sang similarly:

> They tell of the Dictaean Curetes, who are said  
> Once to have concealed in Crete that wailing of Jupiter,  
> When boys around the boy in swift-paced dance,  
> Armed and moving rhythmically, struck bronze against bronze,  
> Lest Saturn should seize him and consign him to his jaws,  
> And deal his mother’s heart an everlasting wound.

(Lucretius, bk. II, v. 637 ff.)

Read further, if you have leisure, Servius on the cited verses of Virgil and on his other verses; Ovid; Diodorus Siculus; Lucan; Strabo; Theophilus of Antioch; Athanasius; and what we have excerpted elsewhere (Servius, on the cited verses and on bk. III of the *Aeneid*, p. 270; Ovid, bk. IV, *Fasti*; Diodorus, bk. V, *Library*, p. 253; [Col. 0559C] Lucian, *On Sacrifices*, p. 183; Strabo, bk. X, *Geography*, p. 468; Theophilus of Antioch, bk. I, *To Autolycus*, p. 75; Athanasius, *Oration Against the Greeks*, § 10, vol. I; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1164). Nor should it trouble you that some assert that Jupiter was raised not on Mount Dicte but on Mount Ida. For the two mountains were near each other, as Aratus attests, although Strabo believed that they were one thousand stades apart (Strabo, bk. X, *Geography*, p. 478).
