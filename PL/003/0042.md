But how unjust it is to judge matters unknown and unexamined, as you do, believe us on the testimony of our own repentance. For we too once did the same: while still blind and obtuse, we shared your opinions, supposing that Christians worshiped monsters, devoured infants, and mingled in incestuous banquets. We did not understand that these stories were continually being circulated by those men, yet were never either investigated [Col. 0328A] or proved; nor that throughout so long a time anyone had ever appeared who would betray them, though he would have obtained not merely pardon for his own deed but also a reward for giving information; nor that the evil was so far from existing that a Christian on trial neither blushed nor feared, and regretted only that he had not been a Christian before.

Yet although we undertook to defend and protect certain sacrilegious and incestuous persons, even parricides, we thought that Christians should not be heard at all. Sometimes, indeed, out of pity for them, we treated them with greater cruelty: we tortured those who confessed, to make them deny—evidently so that they should not perish—employing against them a perverse interrogation designed not to elicit the truth but to compel a lie. And if anyone weaker, overwhelmed and conquered by suffering, denied that he was a Christian, we favored him, as though by abjuring the name he had cleansed away all his deeds through that denial [Col. 0328B]. Do you recognize that we held and acted upon the same opinions, 42 which you now hold and act upon? Yet if reason, rather than the instigation of a demon, were judging, they should instead have been pressed, not to deny that they were Christians, but to confess to incestuous debauchery, impious rites, and the sacrifice of infants.

For with these and similar tales those same demons have filled the ears of the ignorant against us, inspiring the horror of execration. Nor is that surprising, since human rumor, which is always nourished by lies scattered abroad, is consumed when the truth is disclosed [Col. 0329A]. Such is the business of demons: by them false rumor is both sown and fostered.

Hence comes what you say you have heard: that an ass’s head is an object of worship to us. Who is foolish enough to worship this? Who is more foolish than one who believes it is worshiped? Unless it is because you consecrate even whole asses in your stables, together with your Epona, and religiously devour those same asses together with Isis. Likewise you both sacrifice and worship the heads of cattle and rams; you dedicate as gods beings compounded of goat and man, and faces of lions and dogs. Do you not also worship and feed the bull Apis [Col. 0330A] along with the Egyptians? Nor do you condemn their rites established in honor of serpents, crocodiles, other beasts, birds, and fish—if anyone kills one of these gods, he is even punished with death. Those same Egyptians, together with most of you, fear the pungency of onions no less than Isis, and tremble at noises expelled through the body’s private parts no less than at Serapis.

The man who fabricates against us the story that we worship a priest’s genitals is likewise trying to transfer to us 43 what belongs to his own people. For perhaps those things are rites of their shamelessness [Col. 0331A], among men for whom every sex is prostituted through every bodily member, among whom utter obscenity is called sophistication; who envy prostitutes their license; who lick men about the middle and cling with lustful mouths to their groins—men of evil tongue even when silent, who become weary of their obscenity before they become ashamed of it. Monstrous wickedness! They perpetrate upon themselves an evil deed that neither tender age can endure nor the harshest slavery compel.

## CHAPTER XXIX

> **Note: [Col. 0331D]**
>
> **CHAP. XXIX—“Believed to be God.”** Construe: “either that a guilty man deserved to be believed God, or that an earthly being could be believed God.” LINDNER.
>
> **“The Egyptians indeed.”** For *sane* Jacob Gronovius wishes to restore *Saitae* (“the Saïtes”). Whoever wishes may agree. HEUMANN.
>
> **“Honor more truly.”** Do not delete *verius* with Heumann; it is indispensable here. Two propositions are connected by one and the same verb:
>
> [Col. 0332B] “since honor is bestowed more truly upon an illustrious man;
>
> “since love is bestowed more sweetly upon an excellent man.” LINDNER.
>
> **“We neither worship nor desire crosses.”** There is no reason to abandon the manuscript, which reads *optamus* (“desire”). For just as the ancient Christians did not desire to be subjected to crosses, so they did not refuse to undergo that punishment rather than renounce their religion. Ausonius, *Morning Prayer*, v. 73:
>
> “Let a life with a good conscience neither fear nor desire death.”
>
> The same author, *Periander*, v. 3, p. 290:
>
> “To desire death is evil; to fear it is worse.” DAVIS.—Read *ornamus* (“adorn”). He is arguing against the gentiles, whom he presently confronts with adorned crosses. GRUNER, loc. cit., p. 246.—**“We do not worship crosses.”** Caecilius reproached the Christians for worshiping the cross. Hence above, chap. 9, he called the funereal wood of the cross part of Christian ceremonial. [Col. 0332D] And in chap. 12, objecting still more plainly that Christians worshiped the cross, he says: “See, here are crosses for you, no longer to be worshiped but to be endured.” Accordingly, when Minucius answers Caecilius’s objection by saying, “We do not worship crosses,” his words must clearly be understood of worship in the absolute sense conveyed by Caecilius’s accusation. It is as though Minucius had said: “We Christians do not worship crosses in such a way as to render honor and worship to the crosses themselves, as you gentiles worship your images.” That this is our author’s meaning is clear from what he immediately adds, turning this very accusation back against the gentiles: “But you, who consecrate wooden gods, perhaps worship wooden crosses as components of your gods.” See *Giornale de’ Letterati d’Italia*, vol. XXVII, p. 391 ff.; and add the distinguished Orsi, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VI, § VII, and Trombelli, *On the Worship of the Saints*, vol. II, part III, p. 373. GALLAND.
>
> **“Cantabra, soldiers’ standards.”** See Tertullian, *To the Nations* I, the notes there, and the dissertation by Dom Le Nourry elsewhere in the same volume. EDD.
>
> [Col. 0333B] **“With a pure mind.”** These words have plainly nothing to do with the point. A man forms the sign of the cross when he stretches out his hands and worships God, whether this is done with a pure or an impure mind. LINDNER.

**ARGUMENT.—** Nor is it any more true that they worship a mere man affixed to a cross for his crimes; for they rightly believe him to have been not merely innocent but also God. The gentiles, by contrast, invoke the divine powers of kings whom they themselves have enrolled among the gods, supplicate their images, and implore their guardian spirits [Col. 0331B].

Such disgraces and the like it is not lawful for us even to hear; and to defend them at greater length is shameful. For you invent against the chaste and modest things that we would not believe could be done, had you not proved them of yourselves.

As for your ascribing to our religion a guilty man and his cross, you wander far from the neighborhood of truth in supposing either that a guilty man deserved to be believed God or that an earthly being could be so believed. Truly [printed *nec*, “not”] pitiable is the man whose entire hope rests upon a mortal man, for all his aid ends when that man dies [Col. 0332A].

The Egyptians, indeed, choose a man for themselves to worship; they propitiate him alone, consult him concerning everything, and slaughter victims to him. Yet the man who is a god to everyone else is certainly a man to himself, whether he likes it or not; for though he deceives another’s conscience, he does not deceive his own. Even toward princes and kings, false adulation shamefully fawns upon them not as great and eminent men, as is proper, but as gods [printed *de eis*, “concerning them”], 44 although honor would be rendered more truly to an illustrious man and love more sweetly to an excellent one. Thus they call upon their divinity, supplicate their images, and implore their *genius*—that is, their demon; and among them it is safer to perjure oneself by Jupiter’s genius than by the king’s.

We neither worship nor desire crosses. But you, who consecrate wooden gods, perhaps worship wooden crosses as components of your gods. For what else are the standards themselves, the *cantabra*, and the military banners but gilded and ornamented crosses [Col. 0332B]? Your victorious trophies imitate not merely the appearance of a simple cross but also that of a man affixed to it. We naturally see the sign of the cross in a ship when it is driven along with swelling sails or glides forward with its oars outspread. When a yoke is raised, it is the sign of a cross; and so it is when a man worships God with a pure mind and outstretched hands. Thus either natural reason rests upon the sign of the cross, or your own religion is fashioned by it.

## CHAPTER XXX

> [Col. 0333A] **Note: [Col. 0333B]**
>
> **CHAP. XXX—“Concerning the slaughter.”** I delete *de* (“concerning”), for it is un-Latin. HEUMANN.
>
> **“The fates of wounds.”** I restore *tanta* (“so many,” that is, “so numerous”) *vulnera*. HEUMANN.—Since Valerius Flaccus, *Argonautica* VI.652, said *fatale vulnerum* (“a fatal wound”), I think our author could correctly have said *fata vulnerum*. LINDNER.
>
> **“No one can believe this.”** Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9: “You who believe such things of a human being are also capable of doing them.” ELMENHORST.
>
> **“Fresh blood.”** Without offering even the slightest reason, the Lyon editor smoothly introduced what he regarded as the more delicate reading, *roseum sanguinem* (“rosy blood”)! EDD.
>
> **“Children they have begotten.”** Not foolishly, Nicolaus Heinsius, *Adversaria*, book III, chap. 2, p. 409, suspects that one little word has fallen out here, and reads: *modo procreatos*, “children only just begotten.” HEUMANN.—Lactantius V.9.15: “Who either strangle the children born from themselves or, if they are excessively dutiful, expose them.” LINDNER.
>
> **“By drugs and potions.”** So the Royal manuscript reads, not *epotis* (“drunk down”), as in most common editions. Whether *et potis* is one word or the same expression, let experts determine. EDD.
>
> **“To the Taurians of Pontus.”** Heraldus printed *Pontificis* (“of the pontiff”). LINDNER.
>
> **“And to Mercury the Gauls.”** The word *constat* (“it is established”), or something similar, has dropped out. It is harsher to restore *Gallis* (“among the Gauls”) with Wopken and understand *ritus fuit* (“it was the custom”) ἀπὸ κοινοῦ, “in common,” from the previous clause. HEUMANN.—In his notes on these words, Wower cites the dialogue of Aeneas of Gaza entitled *Theophrastus* to show that the Getae sacrificed their servants to Zalmoxis. But since nothing of the kind is found there, Caspar Barth, in his notes on that dialogue, p. 125b, believes that Wower’s memory failed him. LINDNER.
>
> [Col. 0333D] **“Human or inhuman victims.”** Either *vel inhumanas* is a gloss, or *vel* is used for “or rather.” Thus Cicero, *Philippic* II, chap. 12: “But observe the insensibility of the man—or shall I say, of the beast?” The same author, *On Friendship*, chap. 12: “Gracchus attempted to seize the kingship—or rather, he actually reigned for a few days”; and *Letters to Friends* IV.13, at the end: “But you understand all this better than anyone.” HEUMANN.
>
> **“To bury them alive for the sacrifice.”** Gelenius printed *sacrifici viventes obruere*; but the manuscript has *sacrificii*, as Rigaltius testifies, and this is the true reading, for *causa* (“for the sake”) is understood. Terence, *The Brothers* IV.4.6: “Nor do I say this more for the sake of flattery than because I have reason for you to think me grateful.” Apuleius, *Apology*, p. 187: “So that, under cover of his tender age, he himself might not be punished for the sake of the false accusation.” See also the commentators on Sallust, *Catiline*, chap. 6. DAVIS.—**“For the sacrifice.”** Since the manuscript had *sacrificii*, Davis accepted this reading, understanding *causa*. But his examples do not appear by any means to have proved this ellipsis sufficiently Latin. Jacob Gronovius read *sacri vice* (“in place of a sacred offering”). An easy change with a plain sense would be *sacrificiis* or *in sacrificiis* (“at sacrifices”). Tacitus, *Annals* XV [Col. 0334B].47: “Two-headed offspring … were found in sacrifices at which it is customary to slaughter pregnant victims.” Paulinus of Nola, *Letter* XI.7: “Let us slaughter as sacrifices, in our vices, those animals which were commanded by the Law.” In the same way *lustratione, ludis, comitiis*, and so forth, are used of the time when such things occur, as critics long ago noted from *Observations on Prodigies*, chap. 130, Livy II.36, and elsewhere. WOPKEN.—This word seems to me to have proceeded from another hand and therefore should be expelled. Indeed, since the Roman edition—which Heraldus also followed—has *sacrifici*, it will readily be believed that someone wrote this word in the margin as a gloss on *Romani*. HEUMANN.—I greatly prefer the Roman edition’s reading, taking *Romani* as an adjective and *sacrifici* as a substantive. A *sacrificus* is one who performs sacred rites. Ovid, *Fasti* VI:
>
> “Martia—a name derived from Ancus the sacrificer.” LINDNER.
>
> [Col. 0334C] **“Buried.”** This is not an infinitive but the indicative perfect. HEUMANN.—In the Royal manuscript most paradigms of this kind are represented only by the abbreviation *er*, *obruer*; hence the abbreviated word *ere*, *obruere*, is commonly introduced in printed editions. EDD.
>
> **“Of a wicked and guilty man.”** That is, of a condemned combatant with beasts. Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9: “Look: in that most religious city of the pious descendants of Aeneas there is a Jupiter whom at his games they drench with human blood. ‘But it is the blood of a bestiary,’ you say. This, I suppose, is less than the blood of a man. Or is it more disgraceful because it is the blood of an evil man?” LINDNER.
>
> **“Bellona her sacred servant.”** So the exemplar reads. Minucius attributes to Bellona’s rite what Tertullian, *Apology*, had said of those consecrated to and serving Bellona. RIGALTIUS.—Salmasius, *Plinian Exercises*, p. 130, likewise retains the Royal manuscript’s reading and confirms that *sacrum* is used for “sacrifice.” I do not accept the very learned man’s opinion, since Minucius would have been guilty of ἀκυρολογία, “improper diction,” if he spoke in this way. For mention of the person who is initiated by drinking blood seems altogether necessary [Col. 0334D]. Here, therefore, *sacer* has the force of *sacratus*, “consecrated.” Virgil, *Aeneid* VI.484:
>
> “Polyboetes, sacred to Ceres.”
>
> On which Servius comments: “*Sacrum*, ‘consecrated’: a noun for a participle.” Prudentius, *Peristephanon*, Hymn XI.2:
>
> “We have seen you, Valerian, consecrated to Christ.”
>
> There is therefore no need to substitute either *servum* (“servant”) with Gelenius or *sacratum* (“consecrated”) with Heraldus. DAVIS.—**“Bellona her sacred rite by a draught.”** *Sacrum* comes from the manuscript; the common text has *servum*. But *sacrum* means “sacrifice,” and the entire cult of the savage goddess, whose celebrants bloodied their forearms and arms. Lucan, book I, v. 565:
>
> “Then those whom savage Bellona rouses by their slashed arms  
> prophesied of the gods.” CELLARIUS.—The remarks of a learned man in *Miscellaneous Observations*, vol. IV, tome II, p. 224, on Aelius Lampridius’s *Commodus*, chap. 9, deserve notice: “In his eagerness for cruelty he commanded those serving Bellona actually to cut their arms.” Casaubon says: “Observe the word ‘actually’; from it we learn that the wounds of Bellona’s devotees were generally only feigned and simulated.” It is clear from a thousand testimonies that the Bellonarii were accustomed truly to cut their arms. But they skillfully gave themselves slight wounds, as Seneca says in *On the Happy Life*, chap. 27. That monster compelled the wretched men to inflict deep wounds upon their bodies. LINDNER.
>
> **“Not unlike them.”** That is: “Not unlike these devotees of Bellona are those who,” etc. LINDNER.
>
> **“Nor hear.”** Delete this disgraceful addition. Minucius says: “For us it is unlawful even to see a killing”—that is, to see one at all. HEUMANN.—I read *audere* (“dare”). For Caecilius had accused Christians of being initiated through the killing of an infant. Octavius answers this slander by saying that a Christian is so far from daring to commit a killing that he thinks it unlawful even to see one. I could defend here the construction of *audere* with the accusative, were I not aware that every dictionary has already done so. I add only two passages. Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* VI.20.25: “If it is in no way permissible to commit homicide, neither is it at all permitted to be present at one.” The same author, *Epitome* (IV), LXIII.4: “But if to be present at homicide is complicity in crime, and the spectator is bound by the same guilt as the perpetrator, then at these gladiatorial slaughters the man who watches is no less drenched in blood than the man who does the deed.” LINDNER.
>
> **“Edible livestock.”** He calls livestock that may be eaten *edulia pecora*, just as Horace, *Satires* II.4.43, called roe deer *edules*, “edible.” Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 9, calls them “edible meals,” and Pliny VIII.55, “edible animals.” HEUMANN.

**ARGUMENT.—** Octavius also proves utterly shameless the slander that Christians drink the blood of an infant whom they themselves have killed. Gentiles, however, he says, cruelly expose newborn children and, before they are born, kill them by cruel abortion. Christians are not permitted even to see or hear a killing.

I should now like to confront the man who says or believes that we are initiated through the slaughter and blood of an infant. Do you think it possible that so tender, so tiny a body could sustain fatal wounds? That anyone could slaughter, pour out, and drain the fresh blood of a newborn and scarcely human creature? No one can believe this unless he is capable of daring it.

For I see you now exposing the children you have begotten to wild beasts and birds, now strangling and crushing them to death by a miserable kind of death [Col. 0333B]. There are women who extinguish in their very wombs, by drugs and potions [Col. 0334A], the origin of the human being to come and commit parricide 45 before they give birth.

These practices assuredly descend from the teaching of your gods. Saturn did not expose his sons but devoured them. With good reason, therefore, in certain parts of Africa infants were sacrificed to him by their parents, while caresses and a kiss suppressed their wailing, lest a weeping victim be sacrificed. The Taurians of Pontus and the Egyptian Busiris likewise had a rite of sacrificing guests; and the Gauls slaughtered human—or rather inhuman—victims to Mercury. The Romans buried a Greek man and Greek woman, and a Gallic man and Gallic woman, alive as a sacrifice. Even today Jupiter Latiaris is worshiped by them through homicide and, in a manner worthy of Saturn’s son, is fattened on the blood of a wicked and guilty man.

It was he, I suppose, who taught Catiline to bind his conspiracy by a covenant of blood, and Bellona to initiate her consecrated servant [Col. 0334B] with a draught of human gore; and to cure epilepsy [Col. 0335A] with a man’s blood—that is, with a more grievous disease. Not unlike them are those who devour beasts from the arena, smeared and saturated with blood or fattened on human limbs and entrails. For us it is unlawful even to see or hear a killing; and we are so wary of human blood that we do not even regard the blood of edible livestock as food.

## CHAPTER XXXI

> **Note: [Col. 0335C]**
>
> **CHAP. XXXI—“Conspiracy.”** The manuscript has *cotio*. Minucius wrote *coitio*, that is, faction or conspiracy. HERALDUS.—The Roman edition, according to Heumann, has *concio* (“assembly”).
>
> [Col. 0335D] **“Deflection.”** I restore *adspersione*, or, as monks wrote it, *aspersione* (“bespattering”). Wower has already emended it thus. Cornelius Nepos, *Alcibiades*, chap. 3.6: “He was also bespattered with the infamy that,” etc. Cicero, *For Caelius*, chap. 10: “He was not bespattered even by suspicion of that deed, much less by infamy.” Similarly Curtius X.10.18: “Those whom rumor had bespattered.” Again Cicero, *Against Vatinius*, final chapter: “Lest any stain from your supreme unworthiness be spattered upon his dignity.” Jacob Gronovius, who thought Minucius had undoubtedly written *accessione* (“addition”), was undoubtedly mistaken. HEUMANN.—I agree with the very learned man. Someone wrote *aversione* because the verb *averteret* follows. But *aspersione* is preferable because the subsequent phrase is “as an orator, he bespattered.” LINDNER.
>
> **“By terror.”** I see that, if one attends to grammatical rules, Heumann rightly restores *terror*; whether he does so rightly if one attends to the author’s thought, I doubt. Minucius surely wrote *errore* (“by error”) and intended “the conspiracy of demons” to be repeated ἀπὸ κοινοῦ, “in common.” For the demons’ conspiracy turns men away from Christians through terror at the abominable opinion. By an equal right I can say [Col. 0336B]: “The cunning of demons turns men away from virtue through terror at its difficulty”; and: “Terror at difficulty turns men away from virtue.” LINDNER.
>
> **“Your Fronto.”** From the word “your,” Dallaeus sought to prove that Minucius was Fronto’s contemporary, against Bellarmine, who had maintained that Minucius was later than Tertullian. But that otherwise learned man is mistaken. For “your” signifies not merely a contemporary but also a fellow citizen, an adherent of the same religion, and one whom somebody cites as a witness to prove his opinion. LINDNER.—See the note above on chapter 9. EDD.
>
> **“Have arisen.”** Read *nota sunt*, “are well known.” HEUMANN.
>
> **“Among the Egyptians and at Athens.”** Read either “among the Egyptians and Athenians” or “in Egypt and at Athens.” LINDNER.
>
> **“Your records and tragedies glory in acts of incest.”** *Memoriae* are writings and books by which things are handed down to memory, or literary monuments, as above, chap. 7: “Examine the records in your books.” Gellius IV.6: “We read it written in ancient records.” The same author V.5, at the beginning: “It is written in books of ancient records.” Tertullian, *Against the Jews*, chap. 1: “According to the records of the divine Scriptures, the elder—that is, more ancient—Jewish people abandoned God and served idols.” There is therefore insufficient reason to substitute *comoediae* (“comedies”) with Fulvius Ursinus and Wower. CELLARIUS.
>
> **“With his mother.”** This refers to Jupiter, to whom Juno was both sister and wife, as Virgil says, *Aeneid* I.51. But that is well known; less well known is that this common king of the gods also had intercourse with his mother and daughter. The commentators are silent about this—not, I suppose, because the enormity of the crime has struck them speechless. We shall demonstrate what we have said by ancient testimonies. Clement of Alexandria, *Protrepticus*, p. 8D: Δηοῦς δὲ μυστήρια καὶ Διὸς πρὸς μητέρα Δήμητραν ἀφροδίσιοι συμπλυκαί, “The mysteries of Deo, and Zeus’s sexual embraces with his mother Demeter.” Athenagoras, *Plea for the Christians*, chap. 27: Χρῆν δ᾽ αὐτοὺς … τὸν Δία μεμισηκέναι ἐκ μητρὸς μὲν Ῥέας θυγατρὸς δὲ Κόρης πεπαιδοποιημένον, “They ought … to have hated Zeus, who fathered children by his mother Rhea and by his daughter Kore.” [Col. 0336D] The same author in chap. 16 mentions θυγατέρα τοῦ Διὸς ἣν ἐκ τῆς μητρὸς Ῥέας ἢ Δήμητρος ἐπαιδοποιήσατο, “a daughter of Zeus whom he begot by his mother Rhea or Demeter.” Arnobius also relates the matter at length in book V, p. 170 ff. DAVIS.
>
> **“You scatter promiscuously.”** So the manuscript reads both here and above in chap. 9; for the ancients said both *promiscus* and *promiscuus*. Gellius XI.16: “It deters us from varied and promiscuous thought”; II.24: “To distribute promiscuously and without limit”; and VI.14: “As it is commonly said.” CELLARIUS.
>
> **“While you even expose children born at home.”** By all means consult the highly learned jurist Gerard Noodt’s *Julius Paulus, or Commentary on Law IX of the Digest, Concerning the Acknowledgment and Support of Children*. GALLAND.—**“Born at home.”** Heraldus wrongly reads *dominatos*. LINDNER.
>
> **“Return among your own.”** Johannes Pricaeus, on Apuleius’s *Metamorphoses* VII, p. 379, substitutes *incurrere* (“run into”), and Jacob Ouzel followed him. But there is no need to listen to them. DAVIS.
>
> [Col. 0337B] **“Stray unwittingly among your sons.”** The very learned Jacob Perizonius prefers *in filias inerrare*, “stray among your daughters.” I do not agree, since *filii* denotes children of either sex. Tertullian, whom our author follows, *Apology*, chap. 9: “First of all, you expose your children.… The memory of an alienated family line must eventually be dispersed. And once error has caused a collision, thereafter the offshoot of incest will advance, as the family line creeps onward together with the crime.” Arnobius IV, p. 137, after speaking of Minerva’s birth from Jupiter’s head, adds: “He begets children conceived from his head.” Javolenus, *Digest* I, title XVI.84: “Under the designation ‘sons’ we understand all children.” Add Laws 116, 121, and 201. DAVIS.—Perizonius proposed *in filias inerrare*, “stray among your daughters,” while Davis defended the other reading because daughters are commonly included under the name “sons.” But Perizonius himself had already noted that very point on Sanctius, *Minerva* IV.10.5, as had many others. Since the matter is thus so commonplace, that celebrated scholar seems to have employed this emendation to give the sentence a more elegant variation, making Minucius refer *recurrere* to females and *inerrare* to males. Yet we have seen in chap. 1 and elsewhere that Minucius was by no means averse to the appearance of this kind of ταυτολογία, “repetition.” WOPKEN.—Unless I am greatly mistaken, our Minucius wrote: *Necesse est vos vestras incurrere in filias, in sorores*, “You must run into your own daughters and sisters.” Pricaeus had already restored *incurrere*, and Ouzel followed him, supporting Pricaeus’s emendation sufficiently by producing other authors who spoke in this way. HEUMANN.—Pricaeus ought not so violently to have introduced here the verb *incurrere*, used by others of lustful frenzy, because Minucius sometimes favors compounds such as *recurrere* even though the prefix’s meaning contributes nothing and wholly disappears, as above in *recognoscere*, and shortly afterward in *vera religio reservetur*. Elsewhere he has *refidamus*, where the expression is likewise strained and grating [Col. 0337D]. Here, however, the prefix’s full meaning is required, as though he said that while you drive away some of your blood in this fashion and retain others, these, ignorant of their kinship, form love affairs with one another; and Venus thus returns upon your own, overflows, and circles back among them. GRONOVIUS.
>
> **“Thus you weave the story of incest.”** He means the story of Oedipus, of whom Lactantius VI.20.23 says: “Who does not understand, who does not know, what may or commonly does happen to either sex through error? The example of Oedipus alone, confounded by a double crime, makes this clear.” He could have said “you enact the story,” but preferred “weave” because above, chap. 9, Caecilius had said: “The bonds of unspeakable lust entwine them.” Wower’s conjecture *fibulam nectitis*, “you fasten a clasp,” departs too far from the truth for us to assent to it. LINDNER.—The Lyon editor likewise strays in conjecturing *labem nacti estis*, “you have incurred a stain.” EDD.
>
> **“By the desire of procreation.”** So all editions and the Royal manuscript read. The sense, however, requires *cupiditatem*, “as regards the desire.” HERALDUS.—Jacob Gronovius rightly rejected Heraldus’s correction. John Davis printed *cupiditatem* in the text, but in his notes more correctly judged the manuscript reading sound. *Unam, nullam* (“one, none”) depend upon the understood word *mulierem* (“woman”) or something similar, just as in Ovid, *Art of Love* II.399:
>
> “While Atreus’s son was content with one, and she  
> Was chaste.”
>
> Plautus spoke more fully, *The Merchant* IV.6.8:
>
> “For a good wife is content with one man;  
> Why should a man be less content with one wife?” GRUNER.
>
> **“Disdainful.”** Almost all printed editions and the Royal manuscript have this. We nevertheless dare affirm that one must read *nec factiosi sumus*, “nor are we factious.” For Christians were accused especially on the ground that they entered into conspiracies and factions, a charge Caecilius had not omitted in chap. 8. Octavius now refutes that accusation in the same manner as Tertullian in the *Apology*, from which this material is taken, chap. 39: “We are the same when assembled as when dispersed; the same all together as individually,” etc. See our observations there. HERALDUS.
>
> **“We are of one mind concerning the good.”** Fulvius Ursinus rejects *bonum* (“the good”) as a gloss. CELLARIUS.—He repeated, that is, the words of Paul in Romans 15:5 and Philippians 2:2, τὸ αὐτὸ φρονεῖτε, “think the same thing,” rendered thus in the Old Latin version: *Idem sapiatis*, “Be of the same mind.” HEUMANN.
>
> **“Our number.”** I write *noster numerus*. HEUMANN.—There is no need. Thus chap. 32: “Shall I offer the Lord sacrifices and victims which he produced for my use?” Chap. 34: “See how all nature contemplates the future resurrection for our consolation.” LINDNER.
>
> **“In this way of life one of its own both excels and perseveres.”** I emend thus: *viventibus et perstat suus*, “among those living thus, one of their own persists.” Someone had explained τὸ *perstat* in the margin by *perseverat*. An “outsider” is one who was not yet a Christian. Now it is also clear who is called “one of their own.” HEUMANN.
>
> [Col. 0338D] **“Not by a bodily mark.”** So the books read, using an unusual word. Yet the old glosses have: “*NOTACULUM*: mark, sign.” The Roman book has *nota occulta*, “a secret mark,” as Caecilius said above, chap. 9: “They recognize one another by secret marks and tokens.” WOWER and CELLARIUS.
>
> **“By mutual love, which pains you.”** *Amore* should not, it seems, be removed. Horace, *Epodes* XV.10:
>
> “That this love would be mutual.”
>
> Terence: “The prostitute excluded the young man by whose mutual love she was held.” LINDNER.—The words of Terence which Lindner cites here are incorrectly assembled from an old lexicographer and are given only with a mark of doubt in Robert Estienne’s *Thesaurus*, under *MUTUUS*. EDD.
>
> **“As human beings under the one God as Father.”** Cast *Dei* (“of God”) into the margin, from which it was transferred into the text. HEUMANN.—Cellarius printed *omnes* (“all”), from what source I do not know. LINDNER.
>
> **“For parricide.”** Rigaltius interprets this, following Petronius’s Crito, of the destruction of a human being who exists in the seed; Heumann, of Romulus and Remus, Eteocles and Polynices. I approve the latter. LINDNER.

**ARGUMENT.—** The charge that Christians pollute their banquets with incest is as wholly removed from every appearance of truth as it is certain that the gentiles are themselves truly guilty of incest. Christian banquets are not merely chaste but also sober. Finally, they are so far from desiring incest that some are ashamed even of a chaste union.

[Col. 0335B] Concerning an incestuous banquet, moreover, the demons’ conspiracy [Col. 0336A] has fabricated an enormous tale against us, so that it might stain the glory of chastity by bespattering it with hideous infamy, and turn people away from us through terror at this abominable belief before the truth had been examined. Thus, concerning this matter, 46 even your Fronto bore witness not as one making an assertion but, as an orator, cast an insult upon us.

Such things have instead arisen among your own nations. Among the Persians it is lawful to unite with one’s mother; among the Egyptians and at Athens marriages with sisters are legitimate. Your records and tragedies glory in acts of incest, and you gladly both read and hear them. Thus too you worship incestuous gods joined with mother, daughter, and sister. With good reason, therefore, incest is often detected among you and always committed.

Even unknowingly, wretched people, you can rush into unlawful unions: while you scatter Venus promiscuously, while you sow children everywhere, and while you frequently expose even children born at home to another’s compassion [Col. 0336B], Venus must inevitably return among your own people [Col. 0337A] and stray among your children. Thus you weave the story of incest even when you have no consciousness of it.

Among us, modesty is displayed not merely in the face but in the mind. We gladly adhere to the bond of a single marriage; for the purpose of procreation, we know either one woman or none. The banquets we celebrate are not merely chaste but also sober. We neither indulge in feasts nor prolong the banquet with unmixed wine, but temper merriment with dignity, with chaste conversation and a still chaster body. Most enjoy rather than boast of the perpetual virginity of an inviolate body. Finally, the desire for incest is so far removed from us that some are ashamed even of a chaste union.

Nor do we consist solely of the lowest dregs of the people merely because we refuse your offices and purple robes. Nor are we disdainful [printed correction: “factious”] because, assembled together, we are of one mind concerning the good, with the same peace that we possess individually. Nor are we chatterers in corners merely because you are either ashamed or afraid to hear us publicly.

The daily increase of our number [Col. 0338A] is not proof of error but testimony to merit. For in a noble manner of life one of its own both excels and perseveres, while an outsider joins and increases it. Finally, we are readily recognized not by a bodily mark, as you suppose, but by the sign of innocence and modesty. Thus we love one another with mutual love—which pains you—because we do not know how to hate. Thus, to your envy, we call ourselves **BROTHERS**, as human beings under the one God as Father, as partners in faith, and as coheirs of hope. For you neither acknowledge one another nor cease raging in mutual hatreds; nor do you recognize yourselves as brothers except, indeed, for the purpose of parricide.

>
> **CHAP. XXXII—“I may dwell more widely.”** Heumann wishes to read *laxius habeam*, “I may have more spaciously,” an opinion he advanced in his *Critical Byworks*, published in 1712, p. 47, and in the new *Leipzig Miscellanies*, vol. VIII, part I, p. 127. But Davis and Wopken defend the manuscript reading which we have had printed.—For *latius* is used of things that are broad or extended, whether they are such through breadth properly so called or even through length, as Davis proves on Lactantius, *Epitome*, chap. 58, and Cicero, *On the Laws* II.1. Wopken proves *manere* in the sense “to dwell,” as do all dictionaries and the Greek μένω, which occurs in the same sense in John 1:38 and Matthew 10:11. But the strongest argument is drawn from Cyprian, Minucius’s epitomator, who writes in *On the Vanity of Idols*, chap. 5: “And although man dwells more widely.” LINDNER.
>
> **“Within.”** Cellarius incorrectly prints *inter*, “among.” LINDNER.
>
> **“Indeed, in our.”** Heumann, who understood *immo* as “innermost,” deletes *nostro*. I take it as an adverb and am confirmed in my conjecture by the authority of the Roman edition, which gives *immo*. LINDNER.
>
> **“Victims … with an uncorrupted conscience.”** Jacob Gronovius did not understand Minucius’s meaning and wrote the following on these words: “He plainly alludes to Paul’s words in 1 Timothy 4:4: οὐδὲν ἀπόβλητον μετ᾽ εὐχαριστίας λαμβανόμενον, ‘nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving.’ Here *rejicere* does not mean ‘disapprove’ but must be taken in its proper sense, ‘throw back’ or ‘return.’ For to offer sacrifices and victims to God is the same as wishing thoughtlessly to return to him what he himself gave us. This, however, is ungrateful, because God gave us animals so that we might use them to the glory of his name, not misuse them to his dishonor—as those certainly do who suppose that God needs victims and can be appeased by their slaughter.” GRUNER.—Heumann rejects *ingratum est*, “it is ungrateful,” as a manifest gloss. LINDNER.
>
> **“Since it is a propitiatory victim.”** Lactantius also uses *victima litabilis* in *Divine Institutes* I.21, that is, a victim pleasing and acceptable to God; and in the *Epitome*, chap. 5, *victima litabilior*, “a more propitiatory victim.” CELLARIUS.—Cellarius is right as to the meaning, but not as to the force of the word. A *litabilis hostia* is a victim by which God can be propitiated. For *litare*, a pontifical term, means “to have appeased the divinity by offering sacrifice,” as Macrobius says in *Saturnalia* III.5. Add Servius on Virgil, *Aeneid* II.118. Minucius appears to have Psalm 50:18–19 in view. GRUNER.
>
> **“An uncorrupted judgment.”** The Roman edition has *conscientia*, “conscience.” WOWER.
>
> **“Justice.”** Heraldus incorrectly prints *injustitiam*, “injustice.” LINDNER.
>
> **“The best victim.”** Isaac Vossius, on Mela III.2, emended this to *optimam victimam* (“the best victim”), as Heraldus also printed it. LINDNER.—But one must by all means adhere to the alternative reading. For in the preceding clauses, “to supplicate,” “to pour a libation,” [Col. 0340B] and “to propitiate” mean doing fully and perfectly what was done imperfectly by literal supplications and the like; *opimam victimam caedere*, “to slaughter a choice victim,” must therefore be understood in precisely the same manner. And who does not know that choice victims were commonly regarded as most pleasing to God? Lactantius VI.2: “They therefore slaughter the finest and fattest victims for God, as though he were hungry.” WOPKEN.—By a similar error, Lactantius, *Epitome* 25.7, used to be read: “Can the man who slaughters the finest victims for such beings be thought to be in his right mind?” But Pfaff, Davis, and Bünemann vindicate *opimas*, “choice,” in that passage. LINDNER.
>
> **“Before the eyes.”** Read *sub oculos*, “before one’s eyes.” Seneca, *On Benefits* I.5: “A benefit is not that which comes before the eyes.” Lactantius III.9.12: “Nor do they see more than what comes before their eyes.” The same author, VII.9.2, uses the same argument as our Minucius and at the same time—even the same distinction, as I believe may perhaps have happened: “God,” he says, “does many things whose force is apparent although their substance is not seen, as with the voice, scent, and wind; so that by the proof and example of these things we might perceive God also from his power, effect, and works, even though he did not come before our eyes.” HEUMANN.—Add the same author VII.1.9; chap. 9.27; chap. 11.9. LINDNER.
