But here it is enough to show that this god is utterly perverse in the very proclamation of his solitary goodness, by which they refuse to ascribe to him the sorts of emotions that they censure in the Creator. For if he neither feels rivalry, nor grows angry, nor condemns, nor punishes—as one who does not present himself as judge—I do not see how his system of discipline can stand, especially one supposedly more complete. For what sort of thing is it that he should establish precepts which he will not enforce? that he should forbid offenses [Col. 0277C] which he will not avenge, because he will not judge them? He is, of course, a stranger to every feeling of severity and punitive correction. Why, then, does he forbid the commission of something whose commission he does not punish? It would have been far more consistent not to forbid what he would not punish than not to punish what he had forbidden. Indeed, he plainly ought to have permitted it, since he would forbid it without cause, seeing that he would not [Note: [Col. 0277D] *Not* Semler; *not even* Junius.] punish it. For even then that which is forbidden without vengeance is tacitly permitted. And certainly he forbids the commission of nothing except what he does not wish to be done. He is therefore utterly senseless if he is not offended by the doing of what he does not wish to be done, since offense accompanies a frustrated will. Or, if he is offended, he ought to grow angry; if he grows angry, he ought to avenge. For vengeance is the fruit of anger, anger is owed to offense, and offense, as I have said, accompanies a frustrated will. But he does not avenge; therefore he is not offended. [Col. 0277D] But he is not offended; therefore his will is not injured when that is done which he did not wish to be done. And so the offense is committed in accordance with his will, because what does not injure his will is not done against his will. [Col. 0278A]

Or if this will be characteristic of divine power or goodness—to be unwilling, indeed, that something be done, and to forbid its being done, but nevertheless not to be moved if it is done—we reply that he who was unwilling has already been moved; and it is vain for one not to be moved by the deed when he had been moved to prevent its being done, since he did not wish it to be done: for by his unwillingness he forbade it. Did he not also judge? By being unwilling that it be done, and therefore forbidding it, he judged that it ought not to be done and pronounced that it ought to be forbidden. Therefore he too already judges. If it is unworthy of God to judge—or if it is worthy of God to judge only so far as he is merely unwilling and forbids, but does not also [Note: [Col. 0278D] *For* Paris.] punish the offense once committed—yet nothing is so unworthy of God as failing to enforce what he did not wish and had forbidden to be committed. First, because he owes vengeance to any sentence and law of his own, whatever it may be, in order to give it authority and make obedience necessary. [Col. 0278B] Second, because he must necessarily oppose what he did not wish to be committed and by his unwillingness forbade. But for God to spare evil is more unworthy than to punish it—and particularly for the best God, who cannot otherwise be fully good unless he is the adversary of evil, exercising his love of good through hatred of evil, and [Note: [Col. 0278D] *So that* Semler, Oberthür.] fulfilling his protection of good through the overthrow of evil.

## CHAPTER XXVII

But plainly he judges evil by being unwilling that it occur, and condemns it by forbidding it; yet he pardons it by not avenging it, and acquits it by not punishing it. O God who betrays truth and annuls his own sentence! Listen, sinners—and you who are not yet sinners, so that you may become so! A better God has been discovered, one who is neither offended nor angry nor vengeful, for whom no fire is kindled in Gehenna and no gnashing of teeth shudders in the outer darkness. He is merely good; in short, he forbids sinning, but only in writing. It is in your power, if you wish, to sign your obedience over to him, so that you may seem to have shown God honor; for he does not desire fear. Indeed, the Marcionites openly boast that they do not fear their god at all. “But an evil god,” they say, “will be feared, whereas a good one will be loved.” Fool, you call him “Lord” and deny that he is to be feared, though this name denotes power—and power that is also to be feared. Yet how will you love him unless you fear not loving him? Plainly he is not even your father, toward whom both love because of filial devotion and fear [Col. 0278D] because of authority would be fitting; nor is he your lawful master, whom you should love because of his kindness and fear because of his discipline. Thus, in fact, kidnappers are loved but not [Col. 0279A] feared. For dominion will not be feared unless it is just and lawful; but even an illegitimate dominion can be loved. It rests on enticement, not authority; and on flattery, not power. What, finally, is more flattering than not prosecuting offenses?

Come then, you who do not fear God because he is good: why do you not boil over into every kind of lust—the highest fruit of life, as far as I know, for all who do not fear God? Why do you not attend the customary pleasures of the raging circus, [Note: [Col. 0279D] *The pleasures of the raging circus.* Nearly all the ancients teach that frenzy and madness were attributed to the circus. Augustine, Book II, *On the Creed, to Catechumens*: “What delights one in the circus? To see the charioteers competing? the peoples panting in insanity and frenzy?” St. Jerome to Marcella: “Let the circus rave.” St. Gregory Nazianzen, Oration 27: περὶ τοὺς ἱππικοὺς καὶ τὰ θέατρα, καὶ τὰ στάδια, καὶ τὰ κυνογέσια τοσοῦτον μεμηνέναι, ὥστε ταῦτα ποιεῖσθαι βίον, “He was so mad about horse-races and spectacles, races and hunts, that he made his life consist in them.” The same Theologian also, *Poem* 3, in iambics. LE PRIEUR.] [Note: [Col. 0279C] *those raging around the circus* Rhenanus, Oberthür.], the savage amphitheater, [Note: [Col. 0279D] *The savage cavea.* *Cavea* is often used for the theater and amphitheater, as here. [Note: [Col. 0279D] *The wanton stage.* Spectacles were forbidden to Christians because of their obscenity and shamelessness; the canons proclaim this everywhere. Tertullian himself, *Apology* 38: “We renounce your spectacles entirely”—at baptism, evidently. Hence St. Chrysostom, Oration 19, *To the People*, testifies that Christians ought not to be seen “on the stage and amid the antics of mimes,” ἐν σκηνῇ καὶ μίμων παιδιαῖς. LE PRIEUR.] Why, during persecutions, do you not immediately save your life by denial when the incense-box is offered? “God forbid,” you say, “God forbid!” Therefore you already fear the offense, and by fearing it you have proved that he who forbids the offense is to be feared. It would be another matter if, with the same perversity as your god, whom you do not fear, you obey him just as he forbids what he does not avenge. [Col. 0279B]

It is still more futile when, upon being asked, “What will happen to each sinner on that day?” they answer [Note: [Col. 0279C] *They will answer* in their Latin; *they answer* Semler. *Of their god* Rhenanus.] that he will be cast away, as though out of sight. Is not this too effected by judgment? For he is judged worthy of being cast away, and certainly by a judgment of condemnation—unless the sinner is cast away [Note: [Col. 0279C] *Unless into* Rhenanus, Oberthür.] for salvation, so that even this may befit the best God. And what will it mean to be cast away except to lose what he would have obtained had he not been cast away—that is, salvation? Therefore he would be cast away to the loss of salvation; and this cannot be decreed except by one who is angry, offended, and the prosecutor of the offense—that is, a judge.

## CHAPTER XXVIII

But what end awaits the man thus cast away? “He will be seized by the Creator’s fire,” they say. Does he possess no element of his own, provided even for this purpose, by which he might banish his sinners [Col. 0279C] without cruelty, rather than hand them over to the Creator? What, then, will the Creator do? I suppose he will prepare [Note: [Col. 0279D] *Has prepared* Semler, Junius.] for them a Gehenna richer in sulfur, precisely because they are his blasphemers—unless perhaps the jealous God will spare the deserters from his adversary [Note: [Col. 0280C] *Does he spare his adversary?* Paris, Frankfurt.].

O God perverse in every respect, irrational everywhere, futile in all things, and therefore no god at all! I see neither his status, nor his condition, nor his nature, nor any order belonging to him as coherent; [Col. 0280A] by now not even the sacrament of his faith itself. For to what purpose is baptism required even under him? If it is the remission of offenses, how will he appear to forgive offenses when he does not appear to retain them? For he would retain them if he judged. If it is release from death, how would he release from death one whom he did not bind over to death? For he would have bound him over if he had condemned him from the beginning. If it is the regeneration of the human being, how does one regenerate who did not first generate? Repetition does not belong to one by whom a thing was not done even once. If it is the attainment of the Holy Spirit, how will he bestow the spirit when he did not previously confer the soul? For [Note: [Col. 0280D] *The soul is, in a manner, to serve as a substitute for the spirit.* He calls it a substitute, as though placed beneath the spirit in order to sustain it, just as, with the opposite meaning, we speak of a “prefecture.” Thus he assigns substitution to the soul, so that we may understand that prefecture was given to the spirit. So too in the book *On the Soul*, he said that the *animus* was supplied to the *anima*. RIGAULT.] the soul is, in a manner, to serve as a substitute for the spirit.

He therefore seals a human being who was never unsealed under him; he washes a human being who was never unsealed under him; he washes a human being who was never defiled under him; [Col. 0280B] [Note: [Col. 0280D] *And in this he immerses the whole sacrament of salvation—the flesh.* Augustine, Book I, *On the Merits of Sins*, against Pelagius, chapter 24: “The Punic Christians,” he says, “most aptly call baptism itself nothing other than salvation.” RIGAULT.] and in this he immerses the whole sacrament of salvation—the flesh, which has no share in salvation. Not even a farmer will water land from which he will reap no fruit, unless he is as futile as Marcion’s god. Consequently, why does he impose so great a burden—or so great a glory—of holiness upon flesh that is utterly weak or unworthy? [Note: [Col. 0280C] *As Marcion’s god; consequently* with Semler, Oberthür.] What am I to say, moreover, about the futility of a discipline by which he sanctifies a holy substance? Why does he either [Note: [Col. 0280C] *Moreover* Semler.] burden one that is weak or adorn one that is unworthy? Why does he not reward with salvation that which he burdens or adorns? Why does he defraud it of the reward for its work, not repaying [Note: [Col. 0280C] *Rebuking* Rhenanus.] salvation to the flesh? Why does he allow even the honor of holiness to die in it?

## CHAPTER XXIX

Under him the flesh is not baptized unless it belongs to a virgin, a widow, an unmarried person, or one who has purchased baptism by divorce [Note: [Col. 0280D] *Divorce*, Junius.], [Col. 0280C] as though it had not been born from marriage even in the case of eunuchs. Without doubt this institution will rest upon the condemnation of marriage. Let us see whether it is just—not as though we intended to destroy the blessedness of holiness, like certain Nicolaitans, advocates of lust and luxury, but as people who have learned to recognize, pursue, and prefer holiness without condemning marriage: not as a good over an evil, but as a better thing over a good one. For we do not cast marriages away, [Note: [Col. 0280D] *But we lay marriages aside*, etc. The author certainly did not condemn marriages; rather, he commended holiness within them. But he rejected the obscenity already gaining strength under the pretext of marriage. LE PRIEUR.] but lay them aside; [Col. 0281A] nor do we impose holiness as a binding rule, but recommend it, preserving both the good and the better by pursuing each according to a person’s powers. Only then, indeed, do we expressly defend marriage, when it is attacked in a hostile fashion under the name of filthiness, for the destruction of the Creator, who accordingly blessed marriage because of the honorableness of the thing and for the increase of the human race, just as he blessed the whole creation for sound and good uses.

Foods will not, however, be condemned merely because, when sought out with excessive elaboration, they contribute to gluttony; nor [Note: [Col. 0281C] *Then* Rhenanus, Oberthür.] will clothing be accused merely because, when obtained at greater expense, it swells into ostentation. Likewise the institution of marriage must not be despised merely because, when indulged without restraint, it blazes into lust. There is a great difference between cause and fault, between an established condition and its excess. Thus it is not the institution of such a thing but its deviation that must be condemned, in accordance with the judgment of its Founder himself, to whom belong both “Increase and multiply” (Gen. 1:28) and “You shall not commit adultery” (Exod. 20:14), and “You shall not desire your neighbor’s wife” (Lev. 20:10)—he who punishes by death even incestuous [Note: [Col. 0281C] *Incestuous also* Junius.], sacrilegious, and monstrous madness of lust directed toward males and beasts.

But even if the impulse to marry is now being laid aside—a rule which among us the spiritual discipline, under the authority of the Paraclete, indeed defends, prescribing one marriage in the faith—it will belong to the same one to impose a limit who had once extended that limit. He who scattered will gather; he who planted the forest will cut it down; he who sowed the crop will reap it. He will say, “It remains that those who have wives should be as though they had none” (1 Cor. 7:29), to whom the earlier saying also belonged, “Increase and multiply.” The end belongs to the same one [Col. 0281C] as the beginning. [Note: [Col. 0281C] *Yet the forest is not cut down as something deserving accusation.* By a comparison he teaches that marriages must not be condemned; and, as he was skilled in Roman law, he speaks of a forest meant for cutting, which is frequently mentioned in law, where every forest is divided into one meant for cutting and one not meant for cutting. The former is preserved in order to be cut; the latter is assigned to the grazing of livestock and is commonly called pasture. LE PRIEUR.]

Yet the forest is not cut down [Note: [Col. 0281C] *As* omitted by Semler, Oberthür.] as something deserving accusation, nor is the crop severed as something deserving condemnation, but as something obedient to its season. So too the institution of marriage admits the axe and sickle of holiness, not as though it were evil, but as though it had ripened for completion and had been reserved for holiness itself, to which, by being cut down, it grants even the possibility of existence [Note: [Col. 0281C] *May grant existence* Semler; *would grant existence* Rhenanus.]. From this [Col. 0282A] I shall now say that Marcion’s god, by rejecting marriage as an evil and a business of immodesty, acts against the very holiness which he seems to cultivate. For he uproots its material: if there are no marriages, there is no holiness. The testimony of abstinence becomes meaningless when permission is removed, since certain things are proved by their opposites. Just as “virtue is perfected in weakness” (2 Cor. 12:9), so abstinence from marriage is recognized where the capacity to marry exists [Note: [Col. 0281C] *Distinguished* Rigault, Frankfurt.]. Who, finally, will be called abstinent when that from which one must abstain has been removed? What restraint of gluttony exists in famine? What repudiation of ambition in poverty? What bridling of lust in castration?

But as for wholly suppressing the sowing [Note: [Col. 0281C] *Seed* Junius.] of the human race [Note: [Col. 0282C] Semler deletes *in*.], I do not know whether this too is compatible with the best God. For how will he desire the salvation of a human being [Col. 0282B] whom he forbids to be born by taking away that from which he is born? How will he have anyone upon whom to set the seal of his goodness, when he does not allow him to exist? How does he love one whose origin he does not love? Perhaps he fears an excess of offspring, lest he be burdened with liberating more people, lest he make many heretics, lest he have Marcionites of more illustrious lineage born from Marcionites.

The cruelty of Pharaoh, the destroyer of newborn children, will not be more savage [Note: [Col. 0282C] *More humane* Semler, Oberthür; *more inhuman* in other editions.]. For Pharaoh takes away lives; this god does not give them. The former removes people from [Note: [Col. 0282C] *From* Semler, Pamelius.] life; the latter does not admit them into life. In respect of homicide there is no difference between the two: under both a human being is killed—under the one, after being born; under the other, when he is about to be born.

You would have been grateful, O heretical god, had you been favorably disposed toward the Creator’s arrangement, in that he joined male and female; for your own Marcion was certainly born from marriage. Enough, then, concerning Marcion’s god, whom both the definitions of the one Godhead and the conditions of his states [Note: [Col. 0282C] *State* Rhenanus, Oberthür.] confirm to have no existence at all [Note: [Col. 0282C] *Confirming* Rhenanus.]. But the course of the entire little work also proceeds toward this conclusion. Accordingly, if anyone thinks that we have done [Note: [Col. 0282C] *We shall seem to have done* Frankfurt.] too little in any respect, let him expect it to have been reserved for its proper time, as is also the examination of the Scriptures which Marcion uses.

## ARGUMENT

[Col. 0281D]

I. Since in the preceding book Tertullian determined that Marcion’s god is not God, because certain rules prescribe that there is one Godhead, in this book he defends the proposition that God the Creator is worthily God, since he is good, not evil.

II. He first sets forth an invective against the Marcionites, who by their curiosity [Col. 0282D] cause trouble for God, weighing God according to the judgment of their own understanding.

III. First of all, however, he teaches that the goodness of God the Creator is eternal,

IV. Especially from the making of the human being and the world, and from the law which he prescribed for the human being;

[Col. 0283A]

V. For the fall of the human being must be imputed not to God but to the human being’s free will;

VI. And indeed the goodness of God and the rational nature of the human being required that the human being be constituted with free will, since the reward either of good or of evil could not justly be paid to one who was found good or evil by necessity rather than by will;

VII. God’s dignity, faithfulness, and patience also required this very thing;

VIII. Thus the offense must be imputed entirely to the human being, whom God had created to live rightly;

IX. Nor must it therefore be imputed to God that the soul which sins is the breath of God, because the soul indeed possesses those lineaments of God insofar as it is immortal, free in its own choice, often foreknowing, and rational; [Col. 0283B] nevertheless, even in these respects it is only the image of God, not equal to the very power of divinity itself.

X. Nor, even if the fault were transferred to the devil as the instigator of the offense, should it therefore be directed against the Creator as the author of the devil, since the latter was made by God as an angel and made good—not as the devil, that is, the slanderer.

XI. Furthermore, if after the human being’s offense God was a judge and severe, this pertains not to malice but to justice;

XII. Since this justice has always acted in concert with goodness in God, it is wrong to reckon separately a good God and separately a just God;

XIII. Indeed, every work of justice serves the goodness of God, since fear of judgment contributes to good, not to evil;

XIV. Therefore, if God is ever read to create evils, this must be understood of the evils of punishment, not of guilt; [Col. 0283C]

XV. Accordingly, what appears to be severity is the justice, and thus the goodness, of God;

XVI. Nor should those things that attend justice be reproached in a judge, any more than a physician should be reproached for instruments that cut, cauterize, amputate, and constrict;

XVII. Finally, the entire order of God’s goodness is shown by his mercy toward those who err, his patience toward those who do not come to their senses, and his beneficence toward those who deserve it.

XVIII. Then, coming to Marcion’s *Antitheses*, he proves that the punishment of retaliation, the prohibition of unclean foods, and the burdens of sacrifices also pertain to the goodness of God;

XIX. And likewise each of the disciplines of the Law, so that they should not for a moment be free from regard for God;

XX. Indeed, even the plundering of silver which God commanded the Hebrews to carry out against the Egyptians; for the labor of six hundred thousand people over so many years, and the loss of the sons whom the Egyptians had drowned, were of greater value; [Col. 0283D]

XXI. Nor is there any contradiction in God’s forbidding work on the Sabbaths and commanding the ark to be carried around even on the Sabbath;

[Col. 0284A]

XXII. Nor do the bronze serpent or the golden images of Cherubim and Seraphim conflict with the law forbidding images, since they had causes far different from idolatry, concerning which, after forbidding an image to be made, he adds: “You shall not worship them or serve them.” For God’s institution of sacrifices was also rational, since it called people away from idols, although he subsequently rejected those sacrifices.

XXIII. Nor is it right to call God fickle because he sometimes rejects those whom he approved or at times approves those whom he would reject; for nothing is more fitting even for a good judge than both to reject and to choose according to present merits.

XXIV. Accordingly, Marcion wrongly interprets repentance in God, for even in its Greek sound—μετάνοια (*metanoia*)—the term “repentance” is composed not from confession of an offense but from a change of mind.

[Col. 0284B]

XXV. No less wrongly does he understand the saying, “Adam, where are you?” and that addressed to Cain, “Where is your brother?” Likewise the statement concerning Sodom and Gomorrah, “I shall go down and see,” etc.;

XXVI. Likewise God’s oath by himself and those words to Moses: “Let me be, and, angered with wrath, I shall scatter them.”

XXVII. Finally, the remaining instances of pettiness which the Marcionites charged against God are not so great as those which they themselves acknowledge in Christ, to whom those appearances of the Old Testament must be ascribed.

XXVIII. Lastly, against Marcion he constructs rival antitheses of his own, continuing to the end of the book. PAMELIUS.

## ARGUMENT

Whatever was made by God the Father and Creator, he teaches, was made with the greatest wisdom; therefore God the Creator is good, not evil.

## CHAPTER I

[Col. 0284C]

The occasion for revising this little work—what happened to it we stated in the preface to the first little book—has also afforded us this advantage: in reconsidering Marcion’s two gods, we could distinguish for each his own title and volume in accordance with the division of the subject matter, determining that the one god has no existence at all and defending the other as worthily God, since it pleased the man from Pontus to introduce the one and exclude the other. For he could not build falsehood without demolishing truth. He had necessarily to undermine one thing in order to construct what he wished. Thus does one build [Note: [Col. 0283D] *One who lacks material of his own.* Rhenanus says that here too he calls material *paratura*. Thus in *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 8: “I ought,” he says, “to refute them from their own material”; likewise in the preceding book, chapter 11: “He provided himself no material through recognition.” Latinius further notes similar passages from Book IV *Against Marcion*, near the beginning: “and all the material of the impious and sacrilegious Marcion”; and again in the same book, chapter 2: “He certainly possessed authentic material.” PAMELIUS.] who lacks material of his own.

It would, however, have been proper to dispute this point alone: that this god who is superimposed upon the Creator is no god at all. Once the false god had been driven away by certain rules prescribing that the Godhead is both one and perfect, nothing further ought to have been investigated concerning the true God. For the more certain it was that he existed, inasmuch as no other was established to exist, the more fitting it would have been that he should be accepted without controversy, whatever his character—worshiped rather than judged, and won over rather than reconsidered or even feared because of his severity. [Col. 0285A] For what more is necessary to a human being than concern for the true God, upon whom, so to speak, he had happened, because there was no other God?

## CHAPTER II

[Note: [Col. 0285D] *But now Almighty God is put to trouble*, etc. It is as though he wished to say that God is put to trouble by the rashness and audacity of the Marcionites, who fashioned for themselves another God distinct from the Almighty. LE PRIEUR.]

But now Almighty God, the Lord and Founder of the universe, is put to trouble—only, I suppose, because he has been known from the beginning; because he was never hidden; because he has always shone forth, even before Romulus himself, let alone before Tiberius. The only exception is that he has not been known by the heretics, who put him to trouble. For this reason they think another God must be presumed: because they can more readily criticize than deny the one whose existence is established, certain people weighing [Note: [Col. 0285C] *They weigh* Latinius, Junius.] God according to the judgment of their own understanding. It is just as though a blind man, or one [Note: [Col. 0285D] *With wavering eyes, to presume another sun.* Thus Book I: “To bleary eyes even a single lamp is manifold.” RIGAULT.] whose eyes are wavering, should therefore wish to presume another sun, milder [Col. 0285B] and more wholesome, because he does not see the one he sees.

There is one sun, O man, which regulates this world; and even when you do not think so, it is best and useful. Even when it is harsher and more hostile toward you, or even more foul and corrupted, it is nevertheless consistent with its own governing principle. If you are unable [Note: [Col. 0285C] *Were unable* in other editions.] to perceive that principle, you would certainly have been unable to endure the rays of any [Note: [Col. 0285C] *That* Semler, Oberthür.] other sun, had one existed—especially a greater one [Note: [Col. 0285C] *Greater rays* in other editions.]. For if you are blind toward [Note: [Col. 0285C] *Toward* absent in Rigault, Venice.] an inferior god, what would you be toward a higher one? Why do you not rather spare your weakness and refrain from stretching yourself into danger, since you possess a certain and undoubted God, sufficiently seen by this very fact: that your first perception was that he exists, although you know him only to the extent that he himself has willed?

Yet while acknowledging God, you do not deny him; while not knowing him, you reconsider him—indeed, you even accuse him as though you knew him. If you knew him, you would not accuse him; indeed, you would not even reconsider him. In giving him the name, you deny the substance of the name—that is, the greatness by which [Note: [Col. 0286C] *Of the greatness, which* Rhenanus, Venice, Pamelius.] he is called God—because you do not acknowledge it to be so great that, if a human being could know it in every respect, it would no longer be greatness.

Isaiah, already at that time [Note: [Col. 0286C] *He himself even then* Pamelius, Frankfurt; *he himself already* Rhenanus.], an apostle foreseeing heretical hearts, says: “Who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor? [Note: [Col. 0286C] *Who was his counselor?* Pamelius, Semler, Oberthür.] [Col. 0286A] Or whom did he consult? Or who showed him the way of understanding and knowledge?” (Isa. 40:13–14). With him the Apostle will agree: [Note: [Col. 0285D] *The Apostle will agree: O depth*, etc. The blessed Apostle agrees with Isaiah when he says in Romans II: “O depth,” etc.; in the Greek it is ὦ βάθος, “O depth.” LE PRIEUR.] “O depth of the riches and wisdom of God! How unsearchable are his judgments”—certainly those of God the Judge—“and how untraceable are his ways!”—certainly the ways of understanding and knowledge which no one showed him, unless perhaps these censors of divinity did so, who say: “God ought not to have acted thus,” and “He ought rather to have acted thus,” as though anyone knew the things within God except the Spirit of God.

But since they possess the spirit of the world and do not recognize God through wisdom within the wisdom of God (1 Cor. 1:21), they seem to themselves wiser in counsel than God. For just as the wisdom of the world is foolishness before God, so the wisdom of God is foolishness before the world. But we know that the foolishness of God is wiser than human beings, and the weakness of God stronger than human beings (1 Cor. 1:25). [Col. 0286B] And thus God is greatest precisely when he is petty to a human being; and best precisely when he is not good to a human being; and most truly one precisely when to a human being he is two or more.

But if from the beginning [Note: [Col. 0285D] *The natural human being. Or who [Col. 0286C] will hesitate to pronounce Adam’s very offense a heresy, since he committed it by choosing his own judgment rather than the divine judgment? Except that Adam never said to his potter: “You fashioned me unwisely.” He confessed his deception; he did not conceal the woman who deceived him. He was an altogether unformed [Note: [Col. 0286C] Earlier editions read *still*.] heretic. ## CHAPTER III

Therefore, as we enter upon an examination of the known God, if the question is under what condition he is known, we must begin from his works, which are prior to the human being. Thus his goodness, discovered immediately together with himself and thereafter established and laid down as a presumption, may furnish us with some means of understanding how the subsequent ordering of things came about. Moreover, Marcion’s disciples, upon recognizing the goodness of our God, can acknowledge it as worthy of God through the same grounds by which we showed the goodness of their god to be unworthy.

Now this very thing, which is the material for the knowledge of him, [Note: [Col. 0287D] Earlier editions read *very fact*.] he did not find in another’s possession, but made for himself from his own resources. The Creator’s first goodness, then, was that by which God was unwilling to remain hidden forever—that is, unwilling that there should be no being to whom God might be known. For what is so good as the knowledge and enjoyment of God? Even though it was not yet apparent that this was good, because as yet there was nothing to which it could appear, God nevertheless foreknew how good it would prove to be. And therefore [Note: [Col. 0287D] *He entrusted it to the sum of his own goodness.* *Entrusted* here means “stored away,” namely, to be brought forth in its own time. RIGAULT.] he entrusted it to the sum of his own goodness, which would bring forth the good destined to appear. That goodness was certainly neither sudden, nor accidental, nor awakened by provocation, as though it were to be dated only from the point at which it began to operate.

For if goodness itself established the beginning, then, at the point when it began to operate, it did not itself receive a beginning, since it made the beginning. Once the beginning had been made, the reckoning of times was also born from it, since the stars and heavenly lights were arranged to distinguish [Col. 0287C] and mark those times: “For they shall be,” he says, “for seasons, months, and years” (Gen. 1:14). Therefore the goodness which made time had no time before time, just as the goodness which established the beginning had no beginning before the beginning. Thus, lacking both an order of beginning and a measure of time, it will be reckoned as belonging to an immeasurable and indeterminable eternity. It cannot be regarded as sudden, accidental, or provoked, since there is nothing from which it could be so reckoned—that is, no form of time. Rather, it must be presumed [Note: [Col. 0287D] *And* omitted by Semler, Oberthür.] eternal, unbegotten together with God, and perpetual, and therefore worthy of God. From this point already it puts Marcion’s god’s goodness to shame—not merely because that goodness is later in its beginnings and times, but because it is later even than the Creator’s malice, if indeed malice could have been brought forth by goodness [Note: [Col. 0287D] *Could entrust* in other editions.].
