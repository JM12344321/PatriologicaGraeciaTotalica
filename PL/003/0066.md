What Minucius reports—that temples of the gods were built by victorious Romans out of the spoils taken from enemies and captured cities—is clearly confirmed by Florus’s testimony. For we read that he recorded the following concerning Tarquinius Superbus: “From the spoils of captured [Col. 0593B] cities he erected a temple” (Flor. *Roman History*, bk. I, ch. 7). We have just heard Tertullian say that the Romans’ spoils were the images of captive gods. This is asserted no less explicitly in the following verses of Prudentius:

> Thereafter countless gods did Rome beget  
> For herself from splendid triumphs, when cities had been subdued by valor.  
> Amid the smoking ruins of temples, the armed  
> Right hand of the victor seized the enemy’s images  
> And carried the captive divine powers home, worshiping them.
>
> . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
>
> As often as renowned Rome received with applause  
> The chariot of a triumphant general, so often did she  
> Add altars of gods, and from the spoils make new divine powers for herself.

(Prudent., bk. II, *Against Symmachus*, § 2.)

Lactantius, however, seems to have indicated both temples and images more briefly. For where he recalls that the cities of their enemies were overthrown by Roman arms, [Col. 0593C] he immediately adds: “They consecrate to the gods their spoils of war and their plunder” (Lact. bk. I, *Institutes*, ch. 6, p. 170; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 33, art. 4). From the spoils, indeed—that is, from the money raised by selling the booty taken from their enemies, as we shall explain elsewhere—they built new temples of the gods; and the images of the gods that they had seized and carried away with them to Rome they consecrated anew and adorned after placing them in their own temples.

Cicero too is our witness that Dionysius the tyrant stripped away the cloak with which the tyrant Gelo had adorned Jupiter from the spoils of the Carthaginians. Moreover, in one of his speeches he says: “Fulvius, who fought against the Aetolians with Ennius as his companion, did not hesitate to consecrate the spoils to the Muses” (Cicer. bk. III, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 252, line [Col. 0593D] 87; and *Speech for Archias*, p. 390, line 25).

Octavius proceeds and, in his own name and that of the Christians, asks his adversary and the other pagans which gods came to favor the Romans and assist them in wars undertaken to extend the boundaries of their empire: foreign and immigrant gods, or their own native gods? Yet none of them could have brought assistance to the Romans. Certainly not the former. For how could those gods have favored and assisted the Romans when they had never been able to protect their own dependents and worshipers against the assaults of their enemies? “For,” says Minucius, “neither Thracian Mars, nor Cretan Jupiter, nor Argive Juno—now Punic Juno—nor Tauric Diana, nor the Idaean Mother, nor the Egyptian one, helped the Romans against their own peoples, by whom those gods were worshiped: these were not [Col. 0594A] divine powers, but monstrous prodigies.” Indeed, no one is unaware that those peoples were conquered and routed by Roman arms. Therefore Cyprian, who repeats the same argument from our author, immediately added: “If these had possessed any power at all, they would assuredly have preserved their own kingdoms and those of their worshipers” (Cypr. *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 13).

If anyone wishes to know who those are whom Minucius called monstrous prodigies rather than divine powers, we shall say that Mars is surnamed “Thracian” because some believed that he originated in Thrace, as we have observed elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. I, ch. 9, art. I, p. 753). Ammianus Marcellinus, moreover (Ammian. bk. XXVII, ch. 4, p. 482), testifies that Mars was held in the highest honor and worship among the Thracians.

As for Cretan Jupiter, who was said to have been born and buried in Crete, [Col. 0594B] and likewise Juno, both Argive and Samian, we have discussed them more than once (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, ch. 7, art. 4, p. 730; ch. 8, art. I, p. 735; and ch. 14, art. 5, p. 827).

To this may be added Cicero (Cicer. bk. I, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 207), who handed down the following concerning the first Juno: “But she is not the same as Sospita or Argive Juno, nor as Roman Juno. Therefore the Argives have one form of Juno, the Latins another.” Concerning this Juno we read the following verse of Virgil:

> To Argive Juno we duly offer rightful honors.

See Pausanias (Pausan. bk. II, p. 59, and bk. III, p. 95), Lucian (Lucian, *On Sacrifices*, p. 185), and Strabo (Strab. bk. V, p. 215, and bk. VI, p. 252), who mention her more than once, as do a great many more recent writers.

[Col. 0594C] Concerning Samian Juno, Aethlius relates the following in Clement of Alexandria (Clement of Alexandria, *Exhortation to the Gentiles*, p. 30): Τῆς Σαμίας Ἥρας, ὥς φησιν Αἴθλιος, πρότερον μὲν ἦν σανίς ὕστερον δὲ ἐπὶ Προκλέους ἄρχοντος, ἀνδριαντοειδὲς ἐγένετο. “The Samian Hera, as Aethlius says, was at first a wooden plank; later, during the archonship of Procles, it was fashioned in the form of a statue.” From this it is evident how ancient her veneration among the pagans was. Read further, if you wish, Pausanias (Pausan. bk. VII, p. 209) and Origen (Orig. bk. IV, *Against Celsus*, p. 196). Virgil mentions both of those Junos, and a third, who was called Punic or Carthaginian, in these verses:

> There was an ancient city—Tyrian colonists held it—  
> Carthage. . . . . . . . .
>
> [Col. 0594D] . . . . . . . . . . . .
>
> Which Juno is said to have cherished alone above all lands,  
> Even preferring it to Samos: here were her arms,  
> Here was her chariot; that this should be the goddess’s kingdom  
> Over the nations, if the fates should somehow permit,  
> Even then she strove for and fostered.
>
> . . . . . . . . . . . .
>
> Chiefly because of what she had done at Troy for her beloved Argos.

(Virg., *Aeneid*, bk. I, v. 16.)

Finally, the Idaean Mother will be discussed below, just as we have already discussed above the monstrous divine powers of the Egyptians. These, then, are the gods and goddesses who, like any others whatever, as Minucius rightly asserted, brought no assistance at all to the Romans. Ambrose later argued against Symmachus in much the same way: “Let them choose which they prefer. If the sacred rites prevailed among the Romans, then they were defeated among the Carthaginians; but if they were triumphed over among the [Col. 0595A] Carthaginians, they certainly did not benefit the Romans either” (Ambrose, Epist. 18, § 6, p. 834).

Minucius therefore rightly concludes from what has been said that nothing could be more absurd than to claim that worshipers abandoned and crushed by gods and goddesses of this kind thereby caused the boundaries of the Roman Empire to be extended. Ambrose argued in precisely the same manner against Symmachus: “Why should I speak of the Senones,” whom Minucius above, as we have seen, indicated without naming them, “who penetrated the hidden places of the Capitol, and whom the Roman remnant could not have endured had not a goose betrayed them by its frightened cackling? Behold what sort of guardians the Roman temples had. Where was Jupiter then? Was he speaking through the goose?” (ibid., § 5; above, art. 1). He similarly pressed the pagans with Hannibal’s victories: “Hannibal long insulted [Col. 0595B] the Roman sacred rites and, though their gods were fighting against him, advanced victorious as far as the city walls. Why did they allow themselves to be besieged, when the arms of their gods were fighting on their behalf?” (Ambrose, ibid., § 4). Consult, if you please and have leisure, what we have annotated there. The Romans, therefore, could neither obtain nor hope for any aid from foreign gods by which their empire might be enlarged. Nor could they obtain it from native gods of their own, as must now be examined.

## ARTICLE III

**The native gods—Romulus, enrolled among the gods through Proculus’s perjury; Picus, Tiberinus, Pilumnus, Picumnus, Consus, Cloacina, Fear, Pallor, Fever, Disease, Acca Laurentia, and Flora—were of no assistance to the Romans in extending the boundaries of their empire; with a discussion of the Larentalian rites and the Consualian and Floralian games.**

[Col. 0595C]

Minucius passes to the Romans’ native gods and plainly declares that they provided the Romans themselves with no greater assistance than did the foreign gods. Indeed, he thought this so certain that it needed no proof, and that nothing more was required than to call those gods by their names. Thus the first listed by him, and afterward by Cyprian—though without observing chronological order—are Romulus, Picus, Tiberinus, Consus, Pilumnus, and Picumnus (Cypr. *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 11).

Romulus, the founder of the Roman people and City, as no one is unaware, was celebrated by Ennius in Cicero as enrolled in the number of the gods, in this single verse:

> [Col. 0595D] Romulus spends eternity in heaven with the gods.

(Cic., *Tusculan Disputations*, bk. I, p. 119, line 33.)

Yet Romulus became a god only “through the perjury” of Proculus, as our author had said earlier. Titus Livy relates the matter as follows. When various rumors circulated concerning the death of Romulus, and some spread in hushed murmurs that he had been torn apart by the senators:

> Proculus Julius, while the city was troubled by longing for the king and hostile toward the Fathers, came forward before the assembly. A man of weight, as tradition relates, and a witness even to so momentous a matter, he said: “Romans, Romulus, the father of this city, suddenly descended from heaven at the first light of dawn today and presented himself before me. When I stood there filled with awe and reverence, praying that it might be lawful to look upon him face to face, he said: ‘Go and announce [Col. 0596A] to the Romans that the heavenly gods so will it that my Rome should be the capital of the world. Therefore let them cultivate the art of war, and let them know, and hand down this knowledge to posterity, that no human power can withstand Roman arms.’ Having spoken these words,” he said, “Romulus departed on high.” It is astonishing how much credence was given to the man who announced these things, and how greatly the people’s and army’s longing for Romulus was eased once belief in his immortality had been established.

(Livy, Decade I, bk. 1, § 16.)

Ovid (Ovid, bk. II, *Fasti*, p. 492 ff.), Dionysius of Halicarnassus (Dionys., bk. II, *Roman Antiquities*, pp. 118 and 124), and Plutarch (Plutarch, *Life of Romulus*, p. 35) reported this somewhat more fully; Florus (Florus, bk. I, ch. 1), Lactantius (Lactant., bk. I, ch. 15, p. 87), and Augustine (August., bk. III, *City of God*, ch. 15, p. 70) more briefly. Cyprian (Cyprian, *On the [Col. 0596B] Vanity of Idols*, p. 13), as usual, follows in our author’s footsteps. Accordingly, Tertullian (Tertull., *Apology*, ch. 21), speaking of Christ’s ascension into heaven, says: “With far better grounds than your Proculuses are accustomed to make assertions.” For this reason Tatian (Tatian, *Oration against the Greeks*, p. 149), suppressing Romulus’s name, said: Τοὺς βασιλέας, μισθοῦ ἢ δι᾽ ἐπιορκίας τις τοὺς θεοὺς καταγελῶν, εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν ἀνεληλυθέναι φήσας, πεπίστευται. “Someone, plainly hired for payment or committing perjury and making a mockery of the gods, found people who believed him when he said that certain kings of old had ascended into heaven.”

As for the other Roman gods, we learn from Augustine that Picus and Tiberinus were seriously incorporated into their false and fictitious order (August., bk. IV, *City of God*, ch. 23, p. 104; bk. VI, ch. 10, [Col. 0596C] p. 159; bk. XVIII, ch. 15, p. 499). This is subsequently confirmed by Augustine in these words of Varro: “Romulus consecrated Picus and Tiberinus.” Who Picus was, at what time he reigned, and how he was entered on the roll of the gods Augustine himself relates in another book as follows:

> Picus, son of Saturn, was the first to receive the kingdom of Laurentum, when the woman Deborah was judging among the Hebrews. . . . As for Saturn, the father of this Picus, let the worshipers of gods of this kind consider what they think, for they deny that he was a man; yet others have written that he himself reigned in Italy before his son Picus.

(Ibid.)

He informs us, however, that this second opinion is accepted by its advocates and by Virgil, whom we shall presently cite. Finally, he added: “Picus begot Faunus, the second king of Laurentum; this man too is, or was, a god among them. Before the Trojan War, they bestowed divine honors upon these dead men.” [Col. 0596D]

Virgil, whom Augustine commended, sang the following about Picus:

> . . . Faunus’s father was Picus, and he  
> Traces you, Saturn, as his father.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VII, v. 48.)

And again:

> Picus himself sat there with Quirinal augural staff and short  
> Striped robe drawn close, and bore a sacred shield in his left hand—  
> Picus, tamer of horses, whom his wife Circe, seized by desire,  
> Struck with a golden wand, transformed by her drugs  
> Into a bird, and sprinkled his wings with colors.

(Ibid., v. 187 ff.)

[Col. 0597A] Ovid says more concerning Picus’s transformation into a bird; you may read him, as also Servius on those verses of Virgil (Ovid, bk. XIV, *Metamorphoses*, § 7, p. 517 ff.; Serv. on Virgil, p. 457).

The same poet composed these verses concerning Tiberinus:

> Here the god of the place himself, the river Tiberinus,  
> Rose as an aged man amid the pleasant poplar leaves.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VIII, v. 31 ff.)

Anyone who desires more concerning him should consult Dionysius of Halicarnassus, bk. I, *Roman Antiquities*, p. 57; Livy, bk. I, p. 2; the author of the book *On the Origin of the Roman People*; and others.

Concerning Pilumnus, let us listen again to Virgil, singing as follows:

> . . . Then Turnus happened to be sitting  
> In the sacred valley within the grove of his father Pilumnus.

(*Aeneid*, bk. IX, vv. 3–4.)

And again in the following book:

> It is shameful that Italians should surround a newborn Troy  
> With flames, while Turnus stands upon his native soil—  
> Turnus, whose grandfather is Pilumnus, whose mother is the goddess Venilia.

(Bk. X, v. 74 ff.)

Servius, moreover, comments on the former verses that Pilumnus—who is doubtless the same as Picumnus and is so named by our author with a slight alteration—was the brother of Pithumnus. The latter, the same Servius says, is reputed to have discovered the practice of manuring fields, and for this reason the name Sterquilinius was given to him. He is also said to have first devised the art of “pounding grain,” and therefore to be worshiped by bakers. Servius nevertheless added that some regarded both Pithumnus and [Col. 0597C] Pilumnus as identical with Castor and Pollux, while others regarded them as *Laudum* gods [reading uncertain], whom Varro likewise conjectures to be gods of marriage. Then, on the poet’s later verses, Servius again observes that Varro calls both of them gods of infants, and that a bed was spread for them on behalf of a woman in childbirth while it was determined whether the newborn child was viable. Finally, he reminds us that some think Pilumnus was given his name because he drives away the evils of infancy; but he rejects this derivation as openly as he more readily accepts the other.

[Col. 0597D] Finally, as regards the god Consus, we read in Plutarch that the solemn games at which the Sabine maidens were abducted were proclaimed by Romulus after he had discovered buried beneath the earth an altar of a certain god whom they called Consus. Plutarch thinks him either the guardian of counsels, surnamed from *consilium* (“counsel”) or from *consules* (“consuls”), or else equestrian Neptune (Plutarch, *Life of Romulus*, p. 25). Livy, for his part, declares that these same games, called the Consualia, were prepared by Romulus in honor of Equestrian Neptune (Livy, bk. I, § 9). Varro, however, says: “The Consualia are so called from Consus, because at that time public holidays are held for this god, and those games at which the Sabine maidens were abducted are celebrated by the priests at his altar in the circus” (Varr., *On the Latin Language*, p. 50). Yet do not too readily suppose that there was a great disagreement among these writers. For Dionysius of Halicarnassus is our witness that the one whom the Romans called Consus was called by the Greeks Ποσειδῶνα σεισίχθονα, “Neptune, Shaker of the Earth,” [Col. 0598A] and that this was the very god in whose honor Romulus instituted the games mentioned above (Dionys. Halic., bk. II, *Roman Antiquities*, p. 100).

Tertullian reconciles these same writers still more fully in the following words:

> From that time the games were called the Consualia, though at first they honored Neptune. . . . They assign the Consualia to Romulus because he dedicated them to the god Consus, whom they regard as the god of counsel—that counsel, namely, by which he then devised the abduction of the Sabine maidens as wives for his soldiers. . . . Even now an altar to that Consus lies buried in the circus, beneath the earth near the first turning-posts, with an inscription of this sort: “Consus mighty in counsel, Mars in war, the Lares in assembly.” At it the public priests sacrifice on the Nones of July; and on the twelfth day before the Kalends of September, the Flamen Quirinalis and the virgins sacrifice.

(Tertull., *On the Shows*, ch. 5, p. 92.)

[Col. 0598B] Read further, if you please, Arnobius, bk. III, *Against the Gentiles*, p. 113; Jerome, *Life of Hilarion*, p. 81; Augustine, bk. IV, *City of God*, ch. 11, p. 97; and Servius on Virgil’s *Aeneid*, bk. VIII, p. 525.

Come now, and let Tertullian appear once more; he joins to Consus the other Roman gods named by Minucius: “Thus,” he says, “a man will invent a god, just as Romulus invented Consus, Tatius Cloacina, and Hostilius Fear” (ibid., bk. I, *Against Marcion*, ch. 18). For Minucius said: “Tatius both discovered and worshiped Cloacina; Hostilius, Fear and Pallor; shortly afterward Fever was dedicated by someone or other.” These words were transcribed word for word by Cyprian (Cypr., *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 10). Varro (Varr., bk. IV, *On the Latin Language*, p. 18 ff.), as quoted by Augustine (bk. IV, *City of God*, ch. 10, p. 159), mentions these ridiculous divine powers, and also the aforementioned [Col. 0598C] Picus and Tiberinus. Lactantius speaks specifically of Cloacina: “Tatius,” he says, “consecrated an image of Cloacina found in the Great Sewer; and because he did not know whose likeness it was, he gave it a name derived from the place” (Lactant., bk. I, *On False Religion*, ch. 20, p. 104). Consult further, if you wish, Tertullian in the passage just cited (Tertull., loc. cit.; and *On the Pallium*, ch. 4) and in another book, and Augustine (bk. IV, *City of God*, ch. 8, p. 93, and ch. 23, p. 104), who discusses not only Cloacina, but also Fear, Pallor, and Fever, noted by our Minucius.

There are also those who think that this Cloacina [Col. 0598D] is the same as the one Pliny calls Venus Cloacina in these words: “It is handed down that the Romans and Sabines, when they were about to fight over the abducted maidens, laid down their arms and were purified with myrtle sprays in the place where the statues of Venus Cloacina now stand. For the ancients used *cluere* to mean ‘to purify’” (Plin., bk. XV, *Natural History*, ch. 29, p. 212). But since this Venus was a purifier, she seems wrongly to be confused with the Cloacina with whom we are dealing.

We have discussed elsewhere the other monstrous divine powers mentioned there by Minucius and Lactantius—Fear, Pallor, Fever, and Disease (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. I, ch. 9, art. 4, p. 764 ff.)—and have shown that the pagans, with the utmost foolishness, numbered them among the *Averrunci*, the gods who warded off [Col. 0599A] evil.

The entire troop of such ridiculous gods is brought to a close by “Acca Laurentia and Flora, disgraceful prostitutes,” who, as our author says, “must be counted among the Romans’ diseases and gods.” To understand this, one must observe, or recall from memory, what the ancients handed down: that Romulus and Remus were born to the Vestal Rhea Silvia through an adulterous union with Mars and were reared, as some say, by a she-wolf, or, as others more truthfully [Col. 0599B] relate, by Laurentia. Livy says that, because she prostituted her body openly, they believed she had been called a *lupa* (“she-wolf” or “prostitute”) among the shepherds (Livy, Decade I, bk. 1, § 4). Tertullian therefore calls her a public harlot (Tertull., *Apology*, ch. 13, p. 15). But this did not prevent her from being admitted into the number of the goddesses, or a festival and sacred rites called the Larentalia from being instituted in her honor. Should you ask us to produce witnesses to all these things, in addition to Livy we shall give you Dionysius of Halicarnassus, bk. I, *Roman Antiquities*, pp. 26 and 70; Plutarch, *Life of Romulus*, p. 19, and *Roman Questions*, p. 272; Ovid, bk. III, beginning of the *Fasti*; Servius on Virgil’s *Aeneid*, bk. I, v. 278 ff.; Virgil himself, *Aeneid*, bk. VIII, v. 630 ff.; Pliny, bk. VIII, ch. 17, p. 163, and bk. XV, ch. 18, p. 197; Macrobius, bk. I, *Saturnalia*, ch. 10; Aulus Gellius, bk. VI, *Attic Nights*, ch. 7; Lactantius, bk. II, ch. 20, pp. 101–102, as we shall show in its proper place; Augustine, bk. XVIII, *City of God*, ch. 21, [Col. 0599C] p. 503; and Aurelius Victor, or the author of the book *On the Origin of the Roman People*, near the end, and *On Illustrious Men*, ch. 1.

Flora, says Minucius, was another disgraceful prostitute who, according to Marcus Cato as quoted by Gellius, “openly earned money with her body” (Gell., bk. IX, *Attic Nights*, ch. 20, p. 485). Lactantius says that, after she had amassed great wealth through that most shameful trade, she appointed the Roman people heirs to it on condition that the income from it should fund an annual celebration of her birthday by the staging of games called the Florales or Floralia (Lactant., bk. I, *Institutes*, ch. 10, p. 103). But because, with the passage of time, this most shameful and infamous origin of the games appeared discreditable, they invented the story that she was a goddess who presided over flowers—or, as [Col. 0599D] Augustine says, over grain in bloom—and, changing her former name Chloris, called her Flora (August., bk. IV, *City of God*, ch. 8, p. 94). Since they could not entirely abolish the memory of her trade as a prostitute, they fabricated the story that she was violated not by a man but by the god Zephyr. As though violation by a god could be said to be less disgraceful than violation by a man! Yet Ovid celebrates these matters, no less than the others, as altogether worthy of his gods:

> I was Chloris, who am now called Flora; the Greek letter  
> Of my name was corrupted by its Latin sound.  
> I was Chloris, a nymph of the happy field, where, as you have heard,  
> The abode of fortunate men once was.  
> What beauty I possessed is hard for a modest woman to relate;  
> Yet it obtained a god as son-in-law for my mother.  
> It was spring; I wandered. Zephyr saw me; I turned away.  
> [Col. 0600A] He pursued; I fled; he was the stronger.

(Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. V, v. 195 ff.)

Nor should you doubt that this notorious prostitute was numbered among the goddesses by the pagans, and that her aid was invoked lest the crops ever be spoiled. For Varro recorded the following concerning himself: “Fourth, I invoke Robigus and Flora, by whose favor neither does mildew spoil the grain and trees, nor do they flower out of season. Therefore the public festival of Robigus, the Robigalia, and the games of Flora, the Floralia, were instituted” (Varr., bk. I, *On Agriculture*, beginning). [Col. 0600B] These must be discussed in the following dissertation. Finally, Cornelius Tacitus recorded that Tiberius Caesar dedicated beside the Circus Maximus “the temple of Flora, which had been founded by Lucius and Marcus when they were plebeian aediles” (Tacit., bk. II, after the midpoint).

Who, then, if he possesses any soundness of mind, upon hearing of that utterly ridiculous and infamous rabble of gods and goddesses, would not immediately acknowledge how rightly Minucius concludes his enumeration of them with this not inelegant irony? “It was these, no doubt, who extended the Roman Empire against all the other gods worshiped among the nations?” But come, and let us see by what further weighty arguments he overthrows and disperses this absurd opinion of the same Romans.

## ARTICLE IV

**How false it is that the boundaries of the Roman Empire were enlarged by the gods because honors and privileges had been granted to the Vestals and priests; how great the incontinence and crimes of both were; and concerning adulteries and sacrileges in the temples themselves.**

[Col. 0600C]

With no gentler force Minucius repels the claim we observed Caecilius advancing: that the gods came to the Romans’ aid and gave them dominion over the entire world because of the honors granted and privileges bestowed upon the Vestals and priests. For by what title, Minucius argues, did the Vestals or priests deserve this? Was there greater chastity among the former than among others, or holier religion among the latter? By no means. Indeed, very many Vestals paid the penalty for violated chastity, though others of their company, no less unchaste but more secretive, escaped it.

For the Vestal Rhea Silvia, as we observed shortly above and as Augustine aptly recalls after others, was said to have borne Remus and Romulus through shameful adultery (August., bk. XVIII, *City of God*, ch. 21, p. 503). Why, then, should other Vestals not have believed that they could imitate her wicked example with impunity? Suetonius, moreover, relates that Domitian punished in various and severe ways the unchastity of Vestals that had been neglected by his father and brother: earlier offenses with capital punishment, later ones in the ancient manner. “For,” he adds, “after he had allowed the Ocellata sisters, and likewise Varonilla, freedom to choose the manner of their death, and had banished their seducers, he afterward ordered Cornelia, the chief virgin”—that is, the one placed over the other Vestals—“who had once been acquitted but, after a long interval, was prosecuted again and convicted, to be buried alive, and ordered her violators to be beaten to death with rods in the Comitium” (Sueton., bk. VIII, *Life of Domitian*, § 8). [Col. 0601A] Hence Eusebius notes that, during Domitian’s reign, “three Vestal virgins were condemned for fornication” (Euseb., *Chronicle*, at year 2099). Concerning Cornelia, Pliny writes to Minucianus: “She was led to punishment—I do not know whether innocent, but certainly as though guilty” (Plin., *Letters*, bk. IV, letter 11). Yet Suetonius had no doubt whatever concerning her fornication and just condemnation. Plutarch mentions three other Vestals—Aemilia, Licinia, and Marcia—who, he relates, paid the penalties for unchastity after they had long maintained an unlawful intimacy with men (Plutarch, *Roman Questions*, p. 284). You may also consult Lipsius, if you have leisure (Lips., *Syntagma on Vesta and the Vestals*, ch. III, vol. III, p. 606).

[Col. 0601B] Let us add, if you please, Ambrose, who replies to Symmachus, when the latter argued in the same manner as Caecilius against the Christians and for the pagans, that only seven Vestals survived in his own time, and says of them: “That is not virginity which is purchased for a price and possessed without devotion to virtue” (Ambrose, Epist. 18, *Against Symmachus*, p. 836).

Our author asserts that the shamelessness of pagan priests was no less than that of the Vestals: “For where,” he says, “are acts of fornication more commonly arranged by priests than among altars and shrines? Where are procurers’ negotiations conducted, adulteries planned? Burning lust is more frequently discharged in the chambers of temple attendants than in the brothels themselves.” But consider, I ask, whether in that passage he was not referring to these words of Tertullian: “Adulteries are arranged in temples, procurers’ negotiations conducted among altars; lust is very often discharged in the very [Col. 0601C] lodgings of temple attendants and priests, beneath the same fillets, priestly caps, and purple garments, while incense burns” (Tertull., *Apology*, ch. 15, p. 16). Therefore, in another book, Tertullian represented idolatry itself as speaking thus: “I, idolatry, most frequently provide an opportunity for adultery. My groves know it, and my mountains and living waters, and the temples themselves in the cities: they know how much we contrive for the overthrow of chastity” (Tertull., *On Modesty*, ch. 5, p. 719). What of the fact that Phoenician women, as Athanasius says, believed that by prostituting their bodies before the idols they would make the gods favorable to them (Athanasius, *Oration against the Gentiles*, § 26, p. 24)? But Tertullian must be discussed further in the proper place (*Dissertation on Tertullian’s Apology*, ch. 24, art. 3).

[Col. 0601D] Nor should you say that these things were perhaps either gratuitously fabricated or maliciously exaggerated by Christian writers driven by implacable hatred of the pagans. For they can be proved by almost innumerable testimonies of the pagans themselves, which we omit for brevity’s sake. It will therefore suffice to report some here, beginning with these verses of Ovid:

> Near the temple was a recess of scanty light,  
> Like a cave and roofed with natural pumice,  
> Sacred by ancient religious awe; there the priest  
> Had gathered many wooden images of the ancient gods.  
> She entered it and polluted the sanctuary with forbidden disgrace.

(Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, bk. X, penultimate section.)

Juvenal attacks these most shameful crimes and sacrileges still more vehemently:

> For lately, as I recall, the shrine of Isis, and Ganymede,  
> The secret palaces of Peace and of the Mother brought to Rome,  
> And Ceres—for in what temple does a woman not sell herself?—  
> You, the adulterer better known than Aufidius, used to frequent  
> For what I leave unspoken, and even to make husbands themselves submit.

(Juvenal, *Satire* IX, v. 22 ff.)

You may read still more in his sixth satire, where, among very many other things, he said:

> . . . And now she is awaited in the gardens,  
> Or rather at the sanctuary of the bawd of Isis.

(Ibid., *Satire* VI, v. 485.)

Nor are poets alone, but other pagan writers as well, most certain witnesses to such great crimes. Does Cicero not complain that Clodius was “that man” who “introduced fornication upon the couches of the Good Goddess, and violated by male gaze and by disgraceful fornication those rites which it is unlawful for the eyes of any man, even an unwitting one, to behold”? (Cicer., Oration 30, *On the Response of the Haruspices*, p. 429, line 34.) Did Suetonius not commit the following to writing concerning the emperor Tiberius?

> It is also said that, while sacrificing on one occasion, he was captivated by the appearance of an attendant carrying the incense-box before him and was unable to restrain himself: almost before the sacred rite had been completed, he immediately took the attendant aside on the spot and violated him, along with his brother, a flute-player; and soon afterward, because they reproached one another with the shameful act, he broke the legs of both.

(Sueton., bk. III, *Life of Tiberius*, § 4.)

We certainly should never finish speaking if everything had to be set down that each of these writers and others have repeatedly handed down concerning these detestable crimes.

[Col. 0602C] But even if all those acts were not perpetrated by the priests themselves—as they were by that priest to be discussed below who played the part of Hercules—nearly all of them were certainly privy to such acts. See, therefore, how falsely and shamelessly the pagans claimed that, because honors and privileges had been granted to these priests and Vestals, the Romans had gained dominion over the whole world.

## ARTICLE V

**How Minucius shows that the Roman Empire was neither founded nor enlarged by piety toward the gods, because the Assyrians, Medes, Persians, Greeks, and Egyptians reigned before the Romans and before their pontiffs, Arval Brethren, Salii, Vestals, and augurs.**

Minucius proceeds and refutes, with another argument of no less weight, all the claims that Caecilius and the pagan [Col. 0602D] Romans foolishly boasted concerning dominion over the whole world having been handed to them because of their supreme piety toward their gods. For these gods, whom we have shown to be false, neither gave kingdoms to their worshipers nor extended their boundaries; rather, the one true and omnipotent God alone grants such things to whom and when he wills. Nor is this difficult to prove. For, as Minucius rightly argues and everyone knows: “Before those Romans, by God’s dispensation, the Assyrians, Medes, Persians, and also the Greeks and Egyptians long possessed kingdoms, although they had no pontiffs, Arval Brethren, Salii, Vestals, or augurs.” Tertullian had earlier said that kingdoms are dispensed by that supreme God and that the Romans did not receive theirs from their fictitious gods. For, he says, “the Babylonians reigned [Col. 0603A] before the pontiffs, the Medes before the Fifteen Men, the Egyptians before the Salii, the Assyrians before the Luperci, and the Amazons before the Vestal virgins” (Tertull., *Apology*, ch. 26, p. 28; and bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 17, p. 79).

Cyprian, however, puts it somewhat differently: “Kingdoms do not fall to men by merit, but vary by lot. In any case, before the Romans the Assyrians, Medes, and Persians possessed empire; and we know that the Greeks and Egyptians also reigned. Thus, in the alternating succession of powers, the time for ruling came to the Romans also, as it had to others” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 13). That is, kingdoms have shared in the instability and vicissitude of human affairs and, just as they had a beginning, so too they had an end. The Roman Empire began long after many others [Col. 0603B] and, as we have said, was founded and extended by wars, impiety, crimes, and sacrileges. Yet the omnipotent and most equitable God permits these things for perfectly just reasons; and, by his permission, all kingdoms are dispensed to whom and when he wills.

Since there is no room for even the slightest doubt that the Assyrians and the other nations named by our writers possessed their kingdoms before the Romans, it must be certain that neither the Roman Empire nor the kingdoms of other peoples were ever given by their own gods. For why would those gods have given things that were afterward taken away, or upon which the Romans and others encroached with the greatest impiety and the utmost contempt for those gods? If anyone still desires more, he should consult Ambrose and Prudentius, who utterly overthrow this same Roman argument, absurdly repeated by the pagan Symmachus. See also Augustine, who pursues the matter more fully and crushes and routs the same foolish boasting of the pagans (Ambrose, Epist. 18, p. 834 ff.; Prudent., bk. II, *Against Symmachus*, § 4; Augustine, bk. III, *City of God*, chs. 8, 9 ff.; and bk. IV, ch. 3 ff.).

But someone will doubtless ask us who those Arval Brethren and Salii were whom our Minucius mentioned. We shall therefore answer him in these very words of Pliny: “Romulus was the first to institute the priests of the fields, and he called himself the twelfth brother among them, born of Acca Laurentia, his nurse. He gave them, as the most sacred badge of their priesthood, a crown of grain-ears bound together by a white ribbon; [Col. 0603D] this was the first crown among the Romans” (Plin., bk. XVIII, *Natural History*, ch. 2, p. 433). If you ask for another witness, Sabinus Massurius will quickly appear, whose words were transcribed by Aulus Gellius as follows:

> That woman lost one of her twelve sons to death. In his place Romulus gave himself to Acca Laurentia as a son, and called himself and her other sons the Arval Brethren. From that time the college of the Arval Brethren continued at the number of twelve; the badge of this priesthood is a crown of grain-ears and white fillets.

(Gell., bk. I, *Attic Nights*, ch. 7, p. 377.)

Varro, moreover, said that they were called Arval Brethren because they performed public sacred rites so that the fields (*arva*) might bear crops; and for this reason, as Festus says, the sacrificial victims slain by them [Col. 0604A] were called *Ambarvales* (Varro, bk. IV, *On the Latin Language*, p. 21; Festus, ch. 20, *On the Meaning of Words*, and in the fragments).
