These statements were not made thoughtlessly by Caecilius, as is proved by the fact that Cicero likewise committed to writing the following concerning the same Socrates [Col. 0415B]:

> Socrates seems to me—as is universally agreed—to have been the first to call philosophy away from hidden matters and from things veiled by nature itself . . . and to have brought it into ordinary life, so that it might inquire into virtues and vices, and generally into good and evil things; while he judged that heavenly matters either lay far beyond our consideration or, even if they were fully known, nevertheless contributed nothing to living well. In nearly all the discourses variously and copiously recorded by those who heard him, he argues in such a way that he himself affirms nothing and refutes others; he says that he knows nothing except that very fact, and that he surpasses others because they think they know what they do not know, whereas he knows this one thing, that he knows nothing. For this reason he believed that Apollo had declared him the wisest of all, because [Col. 0415C] all wisdom consisted in this alone: not thinking oneself to know what one does not know.  
> (Cicero, *Academic Questions*, Book I, p. 5, line 41 ff.)

Who would not readily allow himself to be persuaded that these and other words of Cicero—quoted by us elsewhere when we were discussing the same Socrates—agree so closely with Caecilius’s statements that we seem to have grounds for the by no means wholly absurd suspicion that Caecilius drew his own material from them? (*Apparatus*, vol. II, Book III, dissertation 2, chapter 17, article 5, p. 1133 ff.)

Moreover, Cicero again mentions this oracle of Apollo in praise of Socrates in other books (Cicero, *On Old Age*, p. 417, line 12; *On Friendship*, p. 419, line 43, and p. 429, line 22), as do Pausanias (Book I, p. 20), Pliny (*Natural History*, Book VII, chapter 31), Solinus (chapter 1, p. 11), Valerius [Col. 0415D] Maximus (Book III, chapter 4), and very many others. Laertius, indeed, testifies that it was delivered by the Pythia in these words:

> Ἀνδρῶν ἁπάντων Σωκράτης σοφώτατος·  
> “Of all mortals, Socrates is the wisest.”  
> (Laertius, Book II, *Life of Socrates*, §37.)

The martyr Justin (*Exhortation to the Greeks*, p. 33) offered the same explanation of this oracle as Caecilius and Cicero. Lucian, however (*On Dancing*, p. 507; *Dialogues of Love*, p. 583; and *Dialogue on the Sects*, p. 304), after mentioning it more than once, speaks of Socrates himself in these words:

> Ὃς ἐκεκράγει πρὸς ἅπαντας, οὐχ ὅπως μὴ πάντα ἀλλὰ μηδ’ ὅλως εἰδέναι τι, ἢ τοῦτο μόνον, ὅτι οὐκ οἶδε.  
> [Col. 0416A] “He proclaimed before everyone that he knew not merely not everything, but absolutely nothing at all—or only this, that he did not know.”

Theodoret, moreover (*Cure of the Greek Maladies*, discourse 2, p. 488; discourse 4, p. 531), who attributes this oracle to the Pythia, somewhere quotes Xenophon’s words (Xenophon, *Memorabilia*, Book I, p. 710; Book IV, p. 815), in which he relates how Socrates discouraged all men from investigating heavenly and divine matters, and asserted that those who attempted to scrutinize them were either fools or in the greatest danger of folly. You will find Xenophon’s complete words in his own books, and also cited by Cyril of Alexandria (Book V, *Against Julian*, p. 180) and Gellius (Book IV, *Attic Nights*, [Col. 0416B] chapter 4). Nevertheless, Laertius (Book II, §§37 and 45) proves from the testimony of the same Xenophon (Xenophon, *Memorabilia*, Book I, p. 726) that Socrates sometimes discussed the providence of God; and the passage of Xenophon cited by him is indeed found in Xenophon’s own books.

Finally, the saying of Socrates cited by Caecilius, “What is above us is nothing to us,” is given by Lactantius (*On False Wisdom*, Book III, chapter 20, p. 308) in these words:

> Socrates had this celebrated proverb: “What is above us is nothing to us.”

Jerome, however, employs it against Rufinus as follows:

> You set the whole of natural philosophy against me, so that Socrates passed over to ethics in vain when he said: “Things above us are nothing to us.”  
> (Jerome, *Apology Against Rufinus*, Book II, [Col. 0416C] well past the middle.)

Caecilius concludes from these considerations that the safe suspension of judgment practiced by Arcesilaus, Carneades, Simonides, and the Academics in the highest questions—and especially concerning the gods—must be imitated. You can certainly see the foundations on which this argument of Caecilius, or of the pagan spokesman, rests. Let us now examine Octavius’s response to it.

## ARTICLE III  
### The Replies by Which Octavius Dissolves and Overthrows Caecilius’s Foregoing Arguments Are Set Forth

Octavius indeed admits, and concedes to Caecilius, that a man [Col. 0416D] ought to know himself and carefully consider what he is and whence he comes: whether he has been compounded from elements or atoms, or was made and created by God. But this, he says, cannot be known by him unless he knows God. Furthermore, just as other animals are bent toward the earth and so formed as to look upon the ground and their food, man has an uplifted and erect countenance so that he may not crawl upon the ground, but, by raising his eyes to heaven and contemplating the entire adornment of the world, may attain to knowledge of God. What, moreover, of the fact that it is not right for him to remain ignorant of the divine splendor that thrusts itself upon our eyes and senses? For if he surveys the things below, around, and above himself, the things set before his feet and in the regions of heaven, [Col. 0417A] he will immediately discover with perfect clarity that there exists a divine power of surpassing intelligence, by which all nature was created and is moved, nourished, and governed. We have already seen that Octavius excerpted this from Cicero’s first book *On the Nature of the Gods*. Christians, therefore, in asserting this with the greatest constancy, were entangling themselves in no vain or fearful opinions.

He very easily extricates himself from the authority of Socrates advanced against him, diminishing it in very few words and utterly disposing of it. For after he had shown that the many arguments by which he had proved God’s existence were certain, he immediately added:

> Let Socrates, then, that Attic buffoon, look to it: he confessed that he knew nothing, although he gloried in the testimony of a most deceitful demon.

He says no more there, but enough to destroy completely [Col. 0417B] everything drawn from Socrates’ authority. For he freely grants Caecilius that Socrates did indeed confess that he knew nothing, but in the same place asserts that he was “an Attic buffoon.” If you ask on whose authority he said this, he will answer that Cicero reports that Zeno called him so:

> Zeno, he says, assailed with abuse not only those then living—Apollodorus, Syllus, and the rest—but Socrates himself, the father of philosophy, whom, employing a Latin expression, he used to call an Attic buffoon.  
> (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book I, p. 209, line 9.)

Accordingly, Lactantius, on this very subject and citing the same Zeno, attacks Socrates in these words:

> He used to swear by the dog and the goose. What a buffoon the man was—as Zeno the Epicurean says—what a foolish, ruined, desperate man, if he intended to mock religion; what a madman, if he did this [Col. 0417C] seriously, so as to regard a most shameful animal as God!  
> (Lactantius, *On False Wisdom*, Book III, chapter 20, p. 309.)

He thus plainly proves that Socrates was rightly and deservedly called a buffoon by Zeno, although Zeno was an Epicurean: for if Socrates playfully swore by the dog and the goose, he ridiculed and mocked religion; but if he did so seriously, he was certainly insane, since, no differently from the Egyptians, he regarded a most shameful animal as God. Hence, without doubt, Timon portrayed this philosopher in his *Silloi* in the following verses:

> Ἐκ δ’ ἄρα τῶν ἀπέκλινε λιθοξόος, ἐννομολέσχης,  
> Ἑλλήνων ἐπαοιδός, ἀκριβολόγους ἀποφήνας,  
> Μυκτὴρ ῥητορόμυκτος, ὑπαττικός, εἰρωνευτής.
>
> From these there issued the stone-polisher, the chatterer about laws,  
> The prophet of the Greeks and master of imposture,  
> A scoffer, a snuffling rhetorician, sub-Attic, and a dissembler.  
> (Laertius, Book II, *Life of Socrates*, §19.)

[Col. 0417D] Nor is there any ground for someone to object that, as Cicero testifies, Socrates was called εἴρων (*eirōn*) by the Greeks—that is, witty, humorous, and elegant (Cicero, *On the Orator*, Book II, p. 167; *Brutus*, p. 240; *Academic Questions*, Book II, p. 14; *On Duties*, Book II, p. 365)—because with Attic wit, or words seasoned with gravity, he constantly attacked other philosophers who boasted that they had laid claim to wisdom for themselves; whereas the Epicureans, taking greater offense at this, struck him with the most insulting reproach of “buffoon.” For who would deny that a man who professed to know nothing—not even God himself—was rightly called by other pagan philosophers, and by Minucius and Lactantius, a “buffoon,” or, more mildly, an “Attic buffoon”?

[Col. 0418A] Furthermore, Minucius rebukes with great sharpness and wit that Socrates who gloried in Apollo’s testimony. For it was the oracle of a most deceitful demon—an oracle which, like the others vaunted by the pagans, he demonstrates, as we shall see below, to have been delivered and produced by impious demons. Hence Tertullian speaks exceedingly well against this very oracle of Apollo, by which, he says, “Apollo proclaimed Socrates the wisest of all,” exclaiming:

> O thoughtless Apollo! He gave testimony of wisdom to the man who denied that the gods existed.  
> (Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 46, p. 40.)

Once Socrates’ authority has thus been overthrown, the testimony of Arcesilaus, Carneades, and the other Academics subsequently cited by Caecilius must necessarily collapse completely [Col. 0418B] as well. For these men, as Caecilius himself admits, were Socrates’ disciples and proponents of the same opinion:

> From this source—these are Caecilius’s words—flowed the safe suspension of judgment practiced by Arcesilaus, and much later by Carneades and very many Academics, in the highest questions—a kind of philosophizing by which the unlearned may proceed cautiously and the learned gloriously.

Cicero explains this opinion of Arcesilaus, Carneades, and the other Academics with the greatest clarity in *On the Orator*, Book III, p. 193; *On Ends*, Book II, p. 58; *On Duties*, Book III, p. 390; *On Divination*, Book II, p. 301; and especially *Academic Questions*, Book I, p. 10, and Book IV, pp. 15, 14 ff. So too does Diogenes Laertius in Book IV of *Lives and Doctrines of the Philosophers*, where he wrote the lives of both Arcesilaus and Carneades; Augustine in *The City of God*, Book XIX, chapter 18, and *Against the Academics*, Book III, [Col. 0418C] p. 284; Gellius in *Attic Nights*, Book XI, chapter 5, where he clearly elucidates the difference between the Academics and the Pyrrhonists; and Vossius, Jonstonus, and others who committed many observations about the philosophers and their schools to writing. We ourselves discussed the vain school of the Academics, and Arcesilaus and Carneades by name, in volume I of this *Apparatus*, Book III, dissertation 2, chapter 17, article 5, p. 1134. Who, moreover, does not understand by what right Minucius, after refuting and rejecting Socrates, regards the opinions and testimony of all these men as worthless and utterly spurns them?

The point that Caecilius presses at somewhat greater length from Simonides is of no greater value in Minucius’s estimation:

> What of Simonides the lyric poet? Is not his hesitation admirable to all and worthy of everyone’s imitation? When the tyrant Hiero asked Simonides [Col. 0418D] what and of what sort he believed the gods to be, he first requested one day for deliberation; on the next day he extended it to two; then, when reminded, he added an equal period; and at last, when the tyrant inquired into the reasons for so long a delay, Simonides answered that the longer his inquiry proceeded, the more obscure the truth became to him.

He appears to have borrowed this from Cicero, for the Latin orator relates the same matter as follows:

> You ask me what or of what sort God is? I shall employ the authority of Simonides. When the tyrant Hiero asked him this same question, Simonides requested one day for deliberation. When Hiero asked him again the next day, he requested two days; and when he repeatedly doubled the number of days, Hiero asked in amazement why he did so. “Because,” he said, [Col. 0419A] “the longer I consider it, the more obscure it appears to me.”  
> (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book I, p. 204, line 12 ff.)

In that passage Simonides is called by the same Cicero “a charming poet”; and hence, as Suidas says, he was called Μελικέρτης, *Melicertes*, because of his sweetness. Accordingly, the royal manuscript of our Minucius corruptly reads *Simonidis Melchi*, and the restoration *Melici* in the printed editions is not incorrect. Pausanias mentions him, and Aelian testifies that his native land was Ceos. We have already discussed him (Pausanias, *Attica*, Book I, p. 2; Aelian, *Various History*, Book IV, chapter 15; Book IX, final chapter; and Book XII, chapter 25; our *Apparatus*, vol. I, Book III, dissertation 2, final chapter, final article, p. 1284).

How cheaply Minucius values not only this Simonides, or Arcesilaus and Carneades, but [Col. 0419B] also Pyrrho and all the other Academic and pagan philosophers, may be gathered from these words of his:

> Let Arcesilaus, Carneades, and Pyrrho deliberate—as to Pyrrho, see Laertius, Eusebius, and others, as well as our discussion elsewhere—and let the entire multitude of Academics deliberate; let Simonides even postpone his decision forever. We despise the haughty brows of the philosophers, whom we know to be corrupters, adulterers, tyrants, and eloquent against their own vices. We display wisdom not in our attire but in our minds; we do not utter great things, but live them. We glory that we have attained what they sought with the utmost exertion and could not find.  
> (Laertius, Book IX, *Life of Pyrrho*; Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, Book XIV, chapters 17 and 18, p. 350.)

Cyprian similarly transferred some of these words [Col. 0419C] into his book *On the Good of Patience*:

> We see that philosophers are neither humble nor gentle, but highly self-satisfied; yet by the very fact that they satisfy themselves, they displease God. . . . But we, who are philosophers not in words but in deeds, display wisdom not in clothing but in truth; we know the consciousness of virtues rather than ostentation; we do not speak great things, but live them.  
> (Cyprian, *On the Good of Patience*, shortly after the beginning, p. 210 ff.)

Tertullian, moreover—whom we discuss in his proper place (*Dissertation on Tertullian*, chapter 4, article 4)—relates about Thales the same story that Minucius tells about Simonides:

> What certain answer did that Thales, the chief of the natural philosophers, give to Croesus when questioned about the Divinity, after repeatedly failing to make use of the periods granted him for deliberation?  
> (Tertullian, *Apology*, [Col. 0419D] chapter 46, p. 40.)

Minucius therefore rightly concludes that neither human weakness nor the philosophers’ professed ignorance could ever prevent Christians from discussing God and heavenly matters. For before our Minucius, Tertullian had said in the passage already cited:

> Any Christian artisan both finds God and demonstrates him, and from that point assigns in fact everything that is sought concerning God, although Plato declares that the maker of the universe is not easy to find and, once found, is difficult to explain to everyone.

Finally, Tertullian there proves, as we shall demonstrate more fully in its proper place, what Minucius said concerning the vices of the philosophers.

## ARTICLE IV  
### The Arguments by Which Caecilius Denies That God Exists and Octavius Demonstrates His Existence Are Examined

[Col. 0420A]

Since Caecilius, as we have said, sustains in his disputation the role and office of the Academics, it is no wonder that from the very beginning of his speech he confidently affirms that it can be proved with no difficulty at all that “all things in human affairs are doubtful, uncertain, and unresolved, and that all things are more probable than true.” Although the falsity of so absurd and foolish a proposition has been amply demonstrated by us, following Octavius, in the preceding articles, Caecilius nevertheless attempts to infer from it that no one thus far has possessed certain and established knowledge whether God exists:

[Col. 0420B]

> Suppose that in the beginning the seeds of all things, as nature gathered into itself, were compressed together: what God was the author here? Suppose that the members of the whole world were joined, ordered, and formed through accidental collisions: what God was their craftsman? Suppose that fire kindled the stars, that heaven was suspended by its own material, that the earth was established by its weight, and that the sea flowed in as liquid: whence comes this religion, whence this fear, what is this superstition?

Yet with these words Caecilius, after the opinion of Democritus and Epicurus had been rejected by other philosophers—an opinion according to which, as Cicero and others say, they believed that all things were formed by the motion and collision of atoms—endeavors to undermine the true Christian teaching about God (Cicero, *On Ends*, Book I, p. 41 ff.; *Academic Questions*, Book I, p. 4; *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book I, pp. 203, 204 ff.; Laertius, Books IX and X, *Lives of Democritus and Epicurus*; Theodoret, [Col. 0420C] discourse 2, p. 487, and discourse 4, p. 528; Gassendi, vol. I, p. 256 ff.; *Syntagma of Epicurean Philosophy*, part 2, §1, chapter 15; and our *Apparatus*, vol. I, pp. 435 and 1136). For in Cicero himself, the Epicurean Velleius tried to refute Plato’s position concerning God by the same argument:

> With what eyes could your Plato have beheld the construction of that mighty work by which he makes the world be constructed and built by God? What was the method of construction? What tools, what levers, what machines, what assistants were employed in so great a task? How, moreover, could air, fire, water, and earth obey and submit to the architect’s will? And whence arose those five forms from which the remaining things are formed, so aptly suited to fashioning the mind and producing the senses? It would take too long to proceed through all such matters, which make them appear to have been wished for rather than discovered.  
> (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 198, line 22.)

[Col. 0420D] Caecilius undoubtedly believed that by this mine of Cicero’s all the philosophers’ opinions concerning God could be demolished. For if everything was composed by the accidental motion and collision of atoms, and the elements flow and revolve back into themselves without any craftsman or author, then assuredly no God exists—or at least his existence is doubtful and uncertain.

Octavius rightly replies that those who think the world and all its adornment were massed together out of certain fragments or atoms adhering to one another blindly and accidentally appear to possess neither mind, perception, nor eyes. [Col. 0421A] Cicero had previously said:

> Should I not therefore wonder that anyone exists who persuades himself that certain solid and indivisible bodies are borne along by force and weight, and that this most orderly and beautiful world is produced from their accidental collision? If someone thinks that this could have happened, I do not understand why he should not likewise think that, if innumerable letters in the twenty-one forms of the alphabet, whether made of gold or of any material, were thrown together somewhere and shaken out upon the ground, the *Annals* of Ennius could be produced from them in a form that might subsequently be read. I doubt whether chance could accomplish so much even in a single verse. . . . But if a collision of atoms can produce the world, why can it not produce a colonnade, a temple, a house, or a city, which are less laborious works and indeed much easier? Certainly they so recklessly [Col. 0421B] babble about the world that they seem to me never to have looked up at this marvelous adornment of heaven, which is nearest at hand.  
> (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book II, p. 228, line 6.)

Not content to say this only once, he repeats it in another book more briefly, indeed, but no less clearly:

> The beauty of the world and the order of heavenly things compel mankind to confess that there exists some surpassing and eternal nature which must be revered and admired.  
> (Cicero, *On Divination*, p. 301, line 22.)

Nor is there any reason for some atheist or obstinate unbeliever to object that Cicero followed the Academic school, which held that all things were uncertain, and that he actually doubted whether the true God or the other gods of the pagans existed. For we are not asking [Col. 0421C] what he himself believed, nor whether some fear prevented him from openly confessing the true God whom he knew. Rather, we are weighing the force of his argument. Yet no man of sound mind can deny that it proves most invincibly the existence of one God, the creator and ruler of all things. For wherever Octavius says a man turns his eyes—upward or downward, this way or that—he will discover as easily as certainly that there exists a divine power of surpassing intelligence, by which all things were made and are governed.

Both Cicero and Octavius demonstrate this most plainly through a by no means brief enumeration of created things. Octavius does so from heaven and its swift motion, and from the fixed and settled movements of the sun and moon; from the moon’s old age and travail—that is, its various wanderings [Col. 0421D] or increases and decreases, as may be understood from Cicero, whom we shall cite presently, and from Virgil (*Georgics*, Book II, verse 478); likewise from the continuous alternation of light and darkness; the order and arrangement of the stars; the stable variety of crops and of the four seasons of the year; the sea enclosed within the bounds of its shores and coasts; the ebbing and flowing tides of the Ocean; the perpetual course of rivers and streams; straight-rising mountains, curving hills, and outstretched plains; the natural protection afforded to living creatures; and especially the beauty of man and his limbs, none of which was formed except for the sake of necessity or adornment; and from the very appearance of all human beings, by which, although they seem similar to one another, each is nevertheless found dissimilar in particular features; [Col. 0422A] and finally from the principle of birth or the desire to reproduce implanted in each creature.

Cicero pursues this induction at far greater length and devotes a very large part of the whole second book *On the Nature of the Gods* to explaining and elucidating it (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book II, p. 228 ff.). It would indeed be too lengthy and of little use to transcribe here everything that he stated with his customary elegance and that our Minucius excerpted, especially since anyone may easily consult it in Cicero himself. It will therefore suffice for us to observe that Cicero, although a follower of the Academic school, advises us there that this argument was not only handled by certain philosophers, and by Cleanthes in particular, but ought also to have convinced all philosophers completely:

> For the philosophers, if perhaps they had initially been bewildered by the first sight of the world, ought afterward, when they had observed its finite and regular motions, [Col. 0422B] and all things governed by settled orders and immutable constancy, to have understood that in this heavenly and divine house there dwells not merely an inhabitant, but also a ruler and governor, and, as it were, the architect of so great a work and so great a task.  
> (*On the Nature of the Gods*, Book II, p. 227; ibid., p. 216.)

He had already made other entirely similar statements in the same book. But you may also consult Philo the Jew, author of the book *On Monarchy* and of the *Greek Questions*, as well as Athenagoras, Theophilus of Antioch, Irenaeus, Lactantius, Athanasius, and others.

What of the fact that not only these pagan, Jewish, [Col. 0422C] and Christian writers, but also the more recent scholastics—and especially St. Thomas—employ this argument to demonstrate, as they say, the existence of God, and regard it as more compelling than others insofar as it may be more easily perceived and understood by everyone? Accordingly, the Apostle confidently declares from it that no man can have any excuse whatever for his ignorance of God:

> For his invisible things, from the creation of the world, are perceived and seen through the things that have been made; his eternal power also and divinity, so that they are without excuse.  
> (Romans 1:20.)

Hence Tertullian says:

> And this, he says, is the culmination of the sin of those unwilling to acknowledge what they cannot fail to know.  
> (Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 17, p. 18.)

Cyprian says in almost the same words:

> [Col. 0422D] And it is the culmination of sin to be unwilling to acknowledge him whom you cannot fail to know.  
> (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 14.)

We have seen that this was asserted before both of them by Clement of Alexandria (*Apparatus*, vol. I, dissertation 2, chapter 6, article 1, p. 948).

## ARTICLE V  
### Another Argument of Minucius Is Considered, by Which He Demonstrates That God, Known Through the Innermost Sense and Very Conscience of Human Beings, Truly Exists

Our Minucius recalls the pagans from created things and the altogether marvelous adornment of the whole world to themselves, to their innermost sense, and to the individual conscience of each person. [Col. 0423A] For the idea and knowledge of a God who truly exists, he says, is so deeply planted and innate in their minds that not only philosophers and educated people, but even common and uneducated people, with nature as their guide, openly and plainly confess on various occasions that he exists:

> What of the fact that I have universal agreement on this matter? I hear the common people, when they stretch out their hands toward heaven, say nothing other than “God”; and “God is great”; and “God is true”; and “If God grants it.” Is this the natural speech of the common people, or the utterance of a Christian making confession?

Furthermore, he immediately added there that the pagans, under the false name of Jupiter and of their gods, in reality invoke the true God himself:

> Those who would have Jupiter as chief are mistaken in the name, but [Col. 0423B] agree concerning the divine power.

Cyprian, as we have seen above, committed precisely the same thought to writing. But both were preceded by Tertullian, who argues against the same pagans in an entirely similar manner:

> Do you wish us to prove from the testimony of the soul itself

—that is, that only one God of all things exists?—

> which, although confined in the prison of the body, although hemmed in by corrupt training, although enervated by lusts and desires, although enslaved to false gods, nevertheless, when it comes to its senses as though from drunkenness, sleep, or some illness, names God for this reason alone: because properly speaking this one God is true, good, and great—

so the older and better manuscripts read, as we shall see in the proper place—

> and “What God grants” is the voice of all. It also calls him to witness as judge: “God sees”; and [Col. 0423C] “I commend myself to God”; and “God will repay me.” O testimony of a soul naturally Christian! Finally, in making these declarations, it looks not toward the Capitol but toward heaven. For it knows the dwelling place of the living God. From him and from there it descended.  
> (Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 17, p. 18.)

He pursues these matters still more fully in another book. Since it would be too lengthy to transcribe them all, we shall present some, especially those relevant to our Minucius’s second passage:

> I hear you publicly and with all the freedom that is not permitted to us, at home and abroad, pronounce these words: “What God grants,” and “If God wills.” By that expression you both indicate that someone exists and confess all power to belong to him, upon whose will you wait. At the same time, you deny that the others are gods, since you call them by their own names—Saturn, Jupiter, [Col. 0423D] Mars, and Minerva. You affirm God alone when you call only him “God,” so that, when you sometimes call those others gods, you appear to use a designation belonging to another, as though on loan. Nor are you ignorant of the nature of God which we proclaim. “God is good”; “God does good”: these are your words.  
> (*On the Testimony of the Soul*, chapter 2, p. 81.)

Likewise, in his book *To Scapula*:

> The people, acclaiming the God of gods, who alone is mighty, bore testimony under the name of Jupiter to our God.  
> (*To Scapula*, chapter 4, p. 87.)

Yet this, he says, is “the testimony of a soul naturally Christian,” namely, inasmuch as it possesses by nature an implanted knowledge of the true God of the Christians. For elsewhere, as we shall see in the proper place, he affirms no less clearly that Christians are made, not born. [Col. 0424A] Accordingly, our Minucius said:

> Is this ordinary natural speech, or the utterance of a Christian confessing—

namely, confessing that God exists? After proving this first and principal teaching of the true religion by various testimonies of pagan philosophers, he concludes from them that “either the philosophers are now Christians, or the philosophers were Christians even then”—insofar as they acknowledged the true God, but not the other tenets of the Christian religion. More on this, however, in our dissertation on Tertullian’s *Apology*.

Arnobius, moreover, argues in exactly the same manner as Tertullian and our Minucius:

> Render a true judgment: as you look about upon all these things that we see, [Col. 0424B] you will sooner doubt whether the other gods exist than hesitate concerning God, whom we all know by nature—whether when we exclaim, “O God!” or when we appoint him witness of the wicked and, as though he saw us, raise our faces toward heaven.  
> (Arnobius, *Against the Pagans*, Book II, beginning, p. 43.)

Finally, Lactantius similarly argues against the pagans:

> For when they swear, he says, and when they make wishes and give thanks, they name not Jupiter or many gods, but God: so completely does truth itself, under nature’s compulsion, burst forth even from unwilling hearts. . . . But if some grave necessity presses upon them, then they remember God. If the terror of war roars, if a long drought denies nourishment to the crops, if a savage storm or hail bears down upon them, they flee to God; help is sought from God; God is entreated to bring aid. If anyone is tossed upon the sea [Col. 0424C] while the wind rages, he invokes him. If anyone is afflicted by some violence, he immediately implores him. If anyone, reduced to the extremity of begging, seeks sustenance through entreaties, he calls upon God alone and appeals by his divine and unique power.  
> (Lactantius, *Institutes*, Book II, chapter 2, p. 138.)

Moreover, that knowledge of God is so deeply engendered in the human mind and innermost senses that no one can be ignorant of him without great guilt. For “it is neither right nor lawful,” our author says, “to remain ignorant of the heavenly splendor that thrusts itself upon our eyes and senses.” Accordingly, he afterward confidently asserts that all who are ignorant of God must, like the impious, be punished with the penalties and torments they deserve. If you wish to hear him speak, here are his very words:

> No one who is not profane deliberates whether those who [Col. 0424D] do not know God ought deservedly to be punished as impious and unjust, since it is no lesser crime to be ignorant of the Parent and Lord of all than to injure him.

But you have already heard Paul the Apostle, Tertullian, and Cyprian, cited by us, making the same pronouncement concerning those who neither recognize nor wish to recognize him from created things or from that knowledge of God implanted in their minds.

How greatly even pagan philosophers favor this opinion concerning the knowledge of God innate in man may be learned from their Latin Orator:

> What nation or what race of men is there that does not possess, without instruction, a certain anticipation concerning the gods, which Epicurus calls πρόληψις (*prolēpsis*), that is, a certain conception of a thing grasped beforehand by the mind, [Col. 0425A] without which nothing can be understood, investigated, or discussed? . . . For since this belief was established by no institution, custom, or law, and the firm and unanimous agreement of all remains, it must be understood that gods exist, because we possess implanted—or rather innate—knowledge of them. But that on which the nature of all agrees must necessarily be true. It must therefore be confessed that gods exist.  
> (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, Book I, p. 202, line 5 ff.)

What could be truer than this confession and argument of a pagan man, had he only said “the true and one God” in place of his gods? But consider, I ask, whether he corrected this error when, on the testimony of Ennius, he shows that this true God, as Minucius and Tertullian say, is invoked by everyone under the name of Jupiter:

> For if this were not [Col. 0425B] so, how could Ennius, with the assent of all, have said:
>
> Behold this  
> Lofty, gleaming vault, which all invoke  
> As Jupiter.
>
> And indeed call him Jupiter, ruler of things, governing all by his nod, and, as the same Ennius says,
>
> Father of gods and men,
>
> and the present and supremely powerful God? Whoever doubts this, I truly do not understand why he could not likewise doubt whether there is a sun or no sun at all.  
> (*On the Nature of the Gods*, Book II, p. 214, line 12 ff.)

# CHAPTER IV  
## ON THE ONE GOD

## ARTICLE I  
### The Argument Is Examined by Which Octavius Shows, on the Authority of the Pagan Poets Homer and Virgil and of the Philosophers Thales, Anaximenes, Diogenes of Apollonia, Anaxagoras, Pythagoras, and Xenophanes, That There Is Only One God

[Col. 0425C]

Just as Minucius demonstrates by the strongest arguments of reason and by the common agreement of all peoples that God truly exists, so too he clearly shows, from the testimony of the pagans’ own poets and philosophers alike, that God is plainly one. He begins with the poets as follows:

> I hear the poets also proclaiming one Father of gods and men, and saying that the mind of mortals is such as the Parent of all has declared it to be.

[Col. 0425D] Someone may perhaps ask us whether these are the words of one Greek or Latin poet, or of several. Yet at the end of the preceding article we saw that Jupiter, whom all invoke, is called by the poet Ennius “the Father of gods and men.” Cicero there introduces the Stoic Balbus citing these very words to affirm the existence of a divine power of surpassing intelligence. Augustine further testifies that the same Cicero (*The City of God*, Book V, chapter 8, p. 121), when proving that the Stoics called Jupiter “the supreme God,” as they thought him to be, cited the following verses of Homer and translated them into Latin thus:

> Such are the minds of men as the Father himself,  
> Jupiter, when he has surveyed with his light the fruit-bearing earth.

[Col. 0426A] These verses are presented in Greek by Homer himself as follows:

> Τοῖος γὰρ νόος ἐστὶν ἐπιχθονίων ἀνθρώπων,  
> Οἷον ἐπ’ ἦμαρ ἄγησι πατὴρ ἀνδρῶν τε θεῶν τε.  
> (Homer, *Odyssey* XVIII, verse 135.)

A Latin translator rendered them thus:

> For such is the mind of earth-dwelling men  
> As the Father of men and gods directs it each day.

We know, indeed, that Lactantius declared that nothing pertaining to truth could have been supplied by Homer, since he wrote about human rather than divine matters (Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, Book I, chapter 5, p. 21). But apart from the fact that Cicero cited these verses as accepted by the Stoics, they agree so closely with our Minucius’s purpose [Col. 0426B] that he plainly seems to have aimed at them. Yet if anyone properly proves to us that Minucius cited either Ennius or other poets there, we shall immediately assent to him.
