Therefore, since the children of Israel affirm that we are in error by accepting Christ, who has already come, let us establish against them from the Scriptures themselves that the Christ who was proclaimed has already come—even though we have already proved, from the chronology proclaimed by Daniel, that the Christ who was announced has come. For he had to be born in Bethlehem of Judah; thus it is written in the prophet:

> And you, Bethlehem, are not least among the rulers of Judah; for from you shall come forth a ruler who shall shepherd my people Israel (Mic. 5:1).

[Note: [Col. 0633D] “And you, Bethlehem, are not least.” Thus from the manuscript and the Greek sources of Blessed Matthew, in place of “of Judah”; likewise “shall shepherd” instead of “shall rule,” for in Greek it is ποιμανεῖ, “shall shepherd.” Although the Old Latin translator renders it “shall rule,” both there and in the final chapter of John the same author uses the same word as here; it is therefore evident that in antiquity the Fathers understood “to shepherd” in the sense of “to rule.” This is worth noting against those who wish all governing authority to be removed from the Church. What follows, moreover, appears to contain a hyperbaton. Whether a little later one reads “foreigners,” as in the printed editions, or “outsiders,” as in the manuscript, the sense is the same; for above he translated it as “aliens.” PAM.]

But if the one who was announced as the ruler destined to proceed from the tribe of Judah and from Bethlehem has not yet been born—for he must proceed from the tribe of Judah and from Bethlehem—yet we observe that today no one of the stock of Israel remains in the city of Bethlehem, and furthermore that it has been forbidden for any Jew to reside within the boundaries of that region, so that this too might be fulfilled through the prophet:

> Your land is desolate; your cities have been burned with fire—

that is, what happened to them in time of war—

> foreigners will devour your territory before your eyes, and it will be desolate and overthrown by alien peoples (Isa. 1:7).

And elsewhere it is said through the prophet:

> You will see the king in splendor—

that is, Christ performing mighty works in the glory of God the Father—

> and your eyes will see the land from afar (Isa. 33:17).

This has been permitted to you in accordance with your deserts after the conquest of Jerusalem: being forbidden to enter your land, you may see it only with your eyes from afar.

> Your soul, he says, will meditate upon fear (ibid., 18),

namely at the time when they suffered their own destruction. How, then, will a ruler be born from Judea, and how will he proceed from Bethlehem, as the divine books of the prophets announce, when to this very day no one at all of Israel has been left there from whose lineage Christ could be born?

For if, according to the Jews, he has not yet come, when he begins to come, from where will he be anointed?

[Note: [Col. 0634C] “Christ. According to the Jews … begins.” Thus Oberthür and Semler.—“Could be born? Christ, according to,” etc., Latinus.]

[Note: [Col. 0634D] “For the Law commanded,” etc. I do not know to what passage he is alluding, unless perhaps to Deuteronomy 28, and, in his customary paraphrastic manner, he extracted this statement from several verses; or perhaps he used another edition, whether Aquila’s or Theodotion’s. In that case, “commanded” would have to be understood as “foretold.” PAM.]

For the Law commanded that in captivity it was not permissible to prepare the anointing oil of royalty. But if there is now no anointing among them either, as Daniel prophesied—for he says:

> The anointing shall be destroyed (Dan. 9:27)—

then there is now no anointing there, because there is no temple either, in which was kept the horn from which kings were anointed.

[Note: [Col. 0634C] Semler does not add “no,” omitting “either” as well.]

[Note: [Col. 0634D] “Nor the temple in which was the horn.” He alludes to 1 Kings 1:39: “And Zadok the priest took the horn of oil from the tabernacle and anointed Solomon”; from this it is inferred that it had been kept in the temple. PAM.]

If, therefore, there is no anointing, from where will the ruler who is to be born in Bethlehem be anointed? Or how will he proceed from Bethlehem, when there is no one at all of the stock of Israel in Bethlehem?

[Note: [Col. 0634D] Others read “seed.”]

Let us therefore show once again that, according to the prophets, Christ has already come, has suffered, has already been received into heaven, and will come from there in accordance with the proclamations of the prophets. For we read in Daniel that after his advent that very city was destined to be destroyed, and we recognize that this is what happened. Scripture says:

> Thus both the city and the sanctuary shall be destroyed together with the ruler (ibid.),

undoubtedly the one who was to proceed from Bethlehem and from the tribe of Judah. Hence it is also manifest that the city was to be destroyed at the same time as its ruler was destined to suffer in it, according to the writings of the prophets, who say:

> I stretched out my hands all day long to a defiant people who contradict me, who walk in a way that is not good, but after their own sins (Isa. 65:2).

[Note: [Col. 0634D] “According to the writings of the prophets.” Again he adduces Scriptures in which it was foretold—as Blessed Cyprian writes in imitation of this passage, book II *Against the Jews*, chapter 20—that the Jews would fasten him to the cross. PAM.]

And in the Psalms he says:

> They pierced my hands and feet; they counted all my bones. But they themselves gazed upon me and saw me;

and:

> In my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink (Ps. 21:17–18; 68:22).

David did not suffer these things, such that he might rightly be thought to have spoken them about himself, but Christ, who was crucified, did.

[Note: [Col. 0635D] “But Christ, who was crucified.” That barbarous nation of the Jews demanded by the violent force of their votes that Christ the Lord be delivered to the cross, as in chapter 21 of the *Apology*. On this subject Cyril of Alexandria says in Homily IV, *On the Paschal Feast*: σταυρῷ προσηλώκασιν οἱ πάντολμοι τῶν Ἰουδαίων λαοί, καὶ πᾶν εἶδος ἐπενεγκόντες κακίας τε ὁμοῦ καὶ ἀτιμίας, θανάτῳ παραδεδώκασιν: “The utterly audacious people of the Jews fastened him to the cross and, after subjecting him to every kind both of wickedness and of dishonor, delivered him over to death.” LE PR.]

Hands and feet, moreover, are not pierced except in the case of one who is suspended from wood. Hence David himself also used to say that the Lord would reign from the wood (Ps. 95:10); for elsewhere too the prophet proclaims the fruit of this wood, saying:

> The earth has yielded its blessings (Ps. 66:7).

That earth, assuredly, was the virgin earth, not yet watered by rains nor made fruitful by showers, from which the first man was then fashioned, and from which Christ has now been born according to the flesh from a virgin.

[Note: [Col. 0636D] “That virgin earth.” Irenaeus also speaks thus: γῆ παρθένος, “virgin earth.” SEML.]

And:

> The tree, he says, has brought forth its fruit (Joel 2:22).

Not that tree in paradise which brought death to the first-formed human beings, but the wood of Christ’s passion, from which the life hanging there was not believed by you (Deut. 28:66).

For this wood was then present in mystery when Moses sweetened the bitter water, from which the people who were dying of thirst in the wilderness drank and revived (Exod. 15:23–26). So too we, who have been drawn out of the calamities of the present age in which we dwelt, and who were dying of thirst—that is, deprived of the drink of the divine word—have drunk the water of baptism sweetened by the wood of Christ’s passion, or the water which consists in faith in him, and have revived.

[Note: [Col. 0635D] Oberthür and Semler read: “given the divine word to drink.”]

From this faith Israel fell away, according to Jeremiah, who says:

> Send and inquire very diligently whether such things have been done. Will nations change their gods? Yet those are not gods. But my people have exchanged their glory for that from which they will derive no benefit. Heaven was appalled at this (Jer. 2:10–12).

And how and when was it appalled? Undoubtedly when Christ suffered:

> And it shuddered, he says, exceedingly, and the sun was darkened at midday (Amos 8:9).

And when did it shudder exceedingly, if not at Christ’s passion, when the earth too trembled, the veil of the temple was torn, and the tombs were broken open (Matt. 27:51)?

> For my people have committed these two evils: they have abandoned me, he says, the fountain of the water of life, and have dug for themselves broken cisterns that will not be able to hold water (Jer. 2:13).

Undoubtedly, by not accepting Christ, the fountain of the water of life, they began to possess broken cisterns—that is, synagogues scattered among the nations—in which the Holy Spirit no longer dwells as he formerly dwelt in the temple before the advent of Christ, who is the true temple of God.

For the prophet Isaiah had also said that they would suffer this thirst for the divine Spirit:

> Behold, those who serve me will eat, but you will hunger; those who serve me will drink, but you will thirst, and from affliction of spirit you will howl. For you will leave your name for my chosen ones to use as a designation, but the Lord will put you to death. Yet for those who serve me a new name will be given, which will be blessed upon the earth (Isa. 65:13–16).

[Note: [Col. 0635D] The same editors read “unto affliction.”]

The mystery of this wood is further celebrated, as we read, in the Books of Kings. For when the sons of the prophets were cutting wood with axes beside the river Jordan, the iron flew off and sank in the river. Then, when the prophet Elisha arrived, the sons of the prophets asked him to retrieve from the river the iron that had sunk. Elisha took a piece of wood and threw it into the place where the iron had sunk; immediately the iron floated, while the wood sank, and the sons of the prophets recovered it (2 Kings 6:4–7). From this they understood that the spirit of Elijah had been manifested in him.

[Note: [Col. 0636D] Others read “into which.”]

What could be more manifest than the mystery of this wood? The hardness of this age, sunk in the depths of error, is delivered in baptism by the wood of Christ—that is, by his passion—so that what had once been lost through the tree in Adam might be restored through the wood of Christ.

We, therefore, who have succeeded to the place of the prophets, endure today in this age the things that the prophets always suffered on account of divine religion. For they stoned some, drove others into exile, and delivered still more to be put to death—something they cannot deny.

[Note: [Col. 0636D] Wouwer reads: “We, assuredly.”]

Isaac, the son of Abraham, himself also carried this wood for his own sacrifice when God had commanded that he be made an offering (Gen. 22:6). But because these things were mysteries reserved for fulfillment in the times of Christ, Isaac was spared together with the wood, while a ram caught by its horns in a thicket was offered; and Christ in his own time carried the wood upon his shoulders, fastened to the horns of the cross, with a crown of thorns encircling his head.

For he had to be made a sacrifice for all nations, he who was:

> led like a sheep to the slaughter, and like a lamb before its shearer is silent, so he did not open his mouth (Isa. 53:7).

For when Pilate questioned him, he said nothing (John 19:9):

> For in his humiliation his judgment was taken away. But who shall recount his generation (Isa. 53:8)?

[Note: [Col. 0636D] “But who shall recount his generation?” There is no doubt that this is how it must be read, in accordance with all editions of Isaiah, and not “recounted,” especially since Blessed Cyprian also reads it thus in book II *Against the Jews*, chapter 15, and Tertullian below in book III *Against Marcion*. PAM.]

For absolutely no human being was privy to Christ’s generation at his conception, when the Virgin Mary was found pregnant by the word of God.

> And because his life would be taken away from the earth (ibid.).

For after his resurrection from the dead, which took place on the third day, heaven received him, in accordance with the prophecy delivered by Hosea in this form:

> Before dawn they will arise to me, saying: Let us go and return to the Lord God, for he himself will rescue and deliver us; after two days, on the third day (Hos. 6:1–3)—

[Note: [Col. 0636D] “After two days, on the third day.” We have corrected the punctuation in accordance with the Vulgate reading, although all the printed editions begin another phrase with the words “After two days,” as though Christ had been received into heaven on the third day, rather than after the completion of forty days following his resurrection (Acts 1:3, 9). We were moved to make this correction by the author’s meaning, since he employs this prophecy of Hosea to demonstrate that Christ’s resurrection occurred three days after his death. EDD.]

—that is, his glorious resurrection from the earth into heaven, when heaven received him, whence the Spirit himself had also come to the Virgin. Neither his birth nor his passion was recognized by the Jews.

Therefore, since the Jews still maintain that their Christ—whom we have proved in so many ways to have come—has not yet come, let the Jews recognize the fate that it was foretold they would suffer after Christ’s advent because of the impiety with which they both despised and killed him.

[Note: [Col. 0637C] “Their” is absent from Rhenanus and Oehler.]

[Note: [Col. 0637D] “It was foretold that they would suffer because of their impiety.” The author uses this epithet more than once; for in chapter 3 of *On the Shows* he says: “For if at that time he called the few Jews an assembly of the impious,” etc. Blessed Augustine also uses the characterization “impious” for the Jews in Epistle 54. Blessed John Chrysostom attributes ἀσέβειαν, “impiety,” to them in Oration II against them, and παράνομον πολιτείαν, “a lawless manner of life.” Nearly all the ancient Fathers do likewise, as does the Council in Trullo, canon 1, where it speaks of Nestorius: καὶ τὴν Ἰουδαϊκὴν ἀνανεοῦντος δυσσέβειαν, “and renewing Jewish impiety.” LE PR.]

First, from the day when, according to Isaiah’s saying:

> A man cast away his abominations of gold and silver, which they had made for the worship of vain things and owls (Isa. 2:20)—

that is, from the time when the nations, namely we ourselves, cast away our idols after the truth had been made clear through Christ—

[Note: [Col. 0637C] Oberthür and Semler read: “our hearts having been enlightened through the truth of Christ”; the Fulda manuscript reads “our hearts having been enlightened.”]

let the Jews observe that what follows was also fulfilled.

[Note: [Col. 0637C] Oberthür and Semler punctuate: “the idols. Let the Jews observe,” etc.]

For the Lord Sabaoth removed from the Jews and from Jerusalem, among other things:

> the wise architect (Isa. 3:1, 3),

who builds the Church, the temple of God, the holy city, and the house of the Lord. For from that time the grace of God ceased among them.

[Note: [Col. 0637D] “For from that time the grace of God ceased among them.” The Jews, once God’s beloved people, fell from grace because of the stubborn impiety and cruelty that they exercised against the Redeemer of the whole world. In chapter 18 of the *Apology* they are called by this author “God’s household nation.” Christians, however, were grafted and inoculated into this wild tree. LE PR.]

And the clouds were commanded not to rain upon the vineyard of Sorek (Isa. 5:6)—that is, the heavenly benefits were commanded not to come upon the house of Israel. For it had produced thorns (ibid., 4), with which it crowned Christ; and not righteousness but an outcry (ibid., 7), by which it extorted his condemnation to the cross. Thus, when the former gifts of grace had been withdrawn, the Law and the Prophets lasted until John (Luke 16:16), and the pool of Bethesda (John 5:2), which until Christ’s advent had healed Israel’s infirmities, thereafter ceased from its benefits, when, through the persistence of their fury, the Lord’s name was blasphemed because of them, as it is written:

> Because of you the name of God is blasphemed among the nations (Isa. 52:5; Ezek. 36:20).

[Note: [Col. 0637C] Junius reads “Bethesda.”]

[Note: [Col. 0637C] Oberthür and Semler read “good health.”]

[Note: [Col. 0637C] Junius punctuates: “from its benefits. Thereafter.”]

[Note: [Col. 0637C] “From” is absent from Rhenanus and Oberthür.]

For the defamation began with them, and the intervening period extended from Tiberius to Vespasian.

[Note: [Col. 0638C] “For the defamation began with them.” So too in book I *To the Nations*, chapter 14, where he likewise says of the Jews: “For what other race is the seedbed of the slanders against us?” RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0638C] Latinus reads “began.”]

When they had committed these things and had failed to understand that Christ was to be found in the time of their visitation, their land was made desolate and their cities burned with fire, while strangers devoured their territory before their eyes:

> The daughter of Zion was left like a watchtower in a vineyard or like a hut in a cucumber field (Isa. 1:7–8).

For Israel did not know the Lord, and the people did not understand him, but rather abandoned him and provoked the Holy One of Israel to indignation (ibid., 3–4).

Thus also the conditional threat of the sword—

> If you are unwilling and do not obey, the sword will devour you (ibid., 20)—

proved that Christ was the one through their refusal to hear whom they perished.

[Note: [Col. 0638C] Rhenanus and Oberthür read “Likewise.”]

[Note: [Col. 0638C] Oberthür and Semler read: “will devour you. From this we prove that Christ was the sword.”]

In the Psalm he also asks the Father for their dispersion, saying:

> Scatter them by your power (Ps. 58:12).

[Note: [Col. 0638D] “He asks for their dispersion,” etc. No one doubts that they were scattered because of the abomination of the punishment and disgrace that they inflicted upon Christ the Lord. They were not only scattered and wandering but also hated by all, to such an extent that the council cited above, canon 11, forbids the following: “Let no cleric or layman eat unleavened bread among the Jews, associate with them, summon them in illness, receive medicine from them, or bathe with them in a bathhouse. If anyone presumes to do this, if he is a cleric, let him be deposed; if a layman, deprived of communion.” LE PR.]

And again through Isaiah he prays for their burning:

> Because of me, he says, these things have happened to you; you shall lie down in anguish (Isa. 50:11).

Therefore, since it is proclaimed that the Jews would suffer these things because of Christ, and since we find that they have suffered them and see that they remain in dispersion, it is manifest that these things happened to the Jews because of Christ, the meaning of the Scriptures agreeing with the outcome of events and the sequence of times.

[Note: [Col. 0638C] Junius reads “sequence.”]

Or, if Christ has not yet come—the one because of whom it was proclaimed that they would suffer these things—then they will suffer them when he comes. And where then is the daughter of Zion to be abandoned, when she does not exist today? Where are the cities to be burned, when they already lie burned in heaps of rubble? Where is the nation to be dispersed, when it is already in exile? Restore the condition of Judea that Christ is to find, and then contend that another Christ is coming.

## CHAPTER XIV

Now learn, from the superabundance of your error, the course of Christ’s leadership. We say that two conditions of Christ were shown by the prophets, marking beforehand the same number of his advents: one in humility, namely the first, when he was destined to be:

> led like a sheep to the slaughter, and like a lamb before its shearer, voiceless, so he did not open his mouth (Isa. 53:7),

without even comeliness of appearance:

> For we proclaim concerning him, he says, that he is like a little child, like a root in thirsty ground; and there was in him neither form nor glory. And we saw him, and he had neither form nor beauty, but his appearance was dishonored, deficient in comparison with the sons of men—a man under affliction, knowing how to bear infirmity (ibid., 2–4).

[Note: [Col. 0638C] The same editors read “marked beforehand.”]

[Note: [Col. 0638D] “And like a lamb before its shearer.” The ancients employed this manner of speaking with remarkable effect—“sheep,” “beast,” “lamb”—when speaking of Christ’s betrayal. The Council in Trullo, canon 1, says: ὁ θεὸς θυόμενος, “God being sacrificed.” LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0639D] “Without even comeliness of appearance.” This is what he said in *On Patience*: “And moreover he is an object of reproach against himself.” RIG.—Rigaltius understands these words in such a way that he believes them to mean the same as the statement in *On Patience*: “And moreover he is an object of reproach against himself.” But in my judgment, in that passage understand those words as referring to Christ’s silence, which was imputed to him as a fault, as though he were a man of little dignity and distinction who did not dare to speak; or, if you prefer, as referring to his face, covered and bruised with blood and blows. LE PR.]

Thus he was appointed by the Father as a stone of stumbling (Isa. 8:14), and, having been made by him a little lower than the angels (Ps. 8:6), he declares himself to be a worm and not a man, the reproach of mankind and the rejection of the people (Ps. 21:7).

These tokens of lowliness belong to the first advent, just as those of exaltation belong to the second. When that advent occurs, he will no longer be a stone of stumbling or a rock of offense, but the chief cornerstone, taken up after his rejection and exalted to consummation (Isa. 28:16); and indeed that stone in Daniel, cut from the mountain, which will crush and grind to pieces the image of the kingdoms of this age (Dan. 2:34ff.).

[Note: [Col. 0639D] “Just as those of exaltation belong to the second.” The second advent of Christ must not be judged in the same way as the first. In the first he assumed the form of a servant and, everywhere taking upon himself human weakness, was exposed to injuries and torments of every kind. In the second, however, he reveals himself as the Son of God and, by the excellence of his glory, raises up from contempt the humbler condition beneath which he was once overwhelmed. LE PR.]

Concerning this second advent the same Daniel said:

> And behold, one coming with the clouds of heaven like a son of man came as far as the Ancient of Days and stood in his sight. And those who stood by brought him forward; and royal authority was given to him, and all the nations of the earth according to their peoples, and every glory, will serve him. His authority is eternal and will not be taken away, and his kingdom will not be corrupted (Dan. 7:13–14).

[Note: [Col. 0639C] Semler and Oehler read “of the same.”]

[Note: [Col. 0639C] Franeker reads “led him away.”]

Then, assuredly, he will possess an honorable appearance and unfailing beauty beyond the sons of men:

> For you are distinguished in beauty beyond the sons of men. Grace has been poured upon your lips; therefore God has blessed you forever. Gird your sword around your thigh, O mighty one, in your splendor and beauty (Ps. 44:3–5).

For the Father, after making him a little lower than the angels, subsequently crowned him with glory and honor and subjected all things beneath his feet (Ps. 8:6–8; Heb. 2:7).

Then they will recognize the one whom they pierced, and the tribes will beat their breasts, tribe by tribe (Zech. 12:10; John 19:37), precisely because they did not recognize him previously, when he was placed in the humility of the human condition. Jeremiah says:

> And he is a man, and who will recognize him? (Jer. 17:7, according to the Septuagint).

[Note: [Col. 0639C] “And” is absent from Semler and Oehler.]

For Isaiah too says:

> Who shall recount his generation?

Thus also in Zechariah 12, in his own person—indeed, even in the mystery of his very name—the true high priest of the Father, Christ Jesus, is portrayed in a twofold condition corresponding to his two advents.

[Note: [Col. 0639C] Semler and Oehler insert “he says.”]

[Note: [Col. 0639C] The same editors read “most true.”]

[Note: [Col. 0639D] “Jesus” is absent from Semler and Oehler.]

At the first he is clothed in filthy garments—that is, in the indignity of passible and mortal flesh—while the devil opposes him, namely as the instigator of Judas the betrayer, who had also tempted him after his baptism. Then, stripped of his former filth, he is adorned with a long robe, a mitre, and a clean turban, signifying the second advent, because he is shown to have obtained glory and honor.

[Note: [Col. 0640C] The same editors read “filth.”]

[Note: [Col. 0640C] The same editors read “a clean turban.”]

Nor will you be able to call him the son of Jehozadak, who was never adorned with any filthy garment but was always adorned in priestly vestments (Zech. 6:11), nor was he ever deprived of the priestly office.

[Note: [Col. 0640C] The same editors read “Joseph.”]

Rather, this Jesus is Christ, the high priest of God the Father, who at his first advent came in human form and capable of suffering, in humility extending even to the passion. He himself was also made in every respect an offering for us all; and after his resurrection he was clothed in the long robe and named the priest forever (Ps. 109:4; Heb. 5:6) of God the Father.

Thus too I shall interpret the two goats that were offered at the fast (Lev. 16). Do not those two also show both stages of the Christ who has already come? They were equal and similar, because of the same appearance of the Lord, who will not come in a different form, since he must be recognized by those who also injured him.

[Note: [Col. 0640C] The same editors read “because he must be recognized.”]

One of them, however, was wrapped in scarlet, accursed, spat upon, maltreated, pierced, and cast out by the people beyond the city to destruction, marked by the manifest emblems of Christ’s passion.

[Note: [Col. 0640C] Semler reads “marked by emblems.”]

For Christ was clothed in a scarlet garment, spat upon, afflicted with every insult, and crucified outside the city.

[Note: [Col. 0640D] “Who was clothed in a scarlet garment.” The scarlet garment is the same as a purple or blood-colored garment, dyed with the grain of the kermes insect; that color is also the color of slaughter. Hence, to demonstrate Christ’s most bitter punishment, he calls the garment both blood-spattered and scarlet. In chapter 4 of *On the Pallium*, this color is called “Galatian red,” because that insect is produced in the greatest abundance in Galatia. LE PR.]

The other, however, was offered for sins and given solely to the priests of the temple for food. It signified the marks of the second manifestation, in which the priests of the spiritual temple—that is, the Church—having been cleansed from all sins, would enjoy, as it were, a certain distribution of the Lord’s grace, while all others fasted from salvation.

[Note: [Col. 0640D] Franeker reads “having been cleansed”; others read “to those having been cleansed.”]

[Note: [Col. 0640D] “The priests of the spiritual temple, that is, the Church.” He calls Christians “churches”; having been cleansed from all sins through Christ, they are priests of the spiritual temple. RIG.]

[Note: [Col. 0640D] “As though by a distribution of the Lord’s gracious bounty.” He signifies the Eucharist, given to the churches—that is, to the Christian faithful—as a distribution and as food. RIG.—*Visceratio*, as Rhenanus noted in the margin, was a distribution of meat customarily made either at a solemn sacrifice or at the funeral of some distinguished person. PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0641B] “While all others fasted from salvation”—namely the pagans and unbelievers. RIG.]

Therefore, since the first advent was proclaimed as being both obscured by numerous figures and cast down in every deformity, whereas the second was proclaimed as manifest and worthy of God, they looked only to the advent that they could readily both understand and believe—that is, the second, which is attended by honor and glory. Thus it is not without reason that they were deceived concerning the less worthy, or certainly the more obscure advent—that is, the first.

[Note: [Col. 0641B] “And in every deformity.” Rhenanus remarks, both here and below in book III *Against Marcion*, that he uses “deformity” in the sense of ugliness, just as a little earlier he used “comely” in the sense of beautiful. Above, however, when he said in *To Scapula*, “How many honorable men,” I noted there that “honorable men” means men held in honor. PAM.]

And so to this very day they deny that their Christ has come, because he did not come in exaltation, while they are unaware that he was first to come in humility.

For the present, it is enough to have traced the course of Christ thus far in these matters, whereby he is proved to have been such as he was announced to be. From this agreement of the divine Scriptures we may now understand that the things proclaimed as destined to occur after Christ should also be believed to have been fulfilled according to the divine arrangement. For if the one after whose coming they were to be fulfilled had not come, the things proclaimed as destined to occur at his advent could in no way have happened.

[Note: [Col. 0641B] Semler and Oberthür read “would have come.”]

Therefore, if you see all the nations emerging thereafter from the depths of human error toward God the Creator and his Christ, you do not dare deny that it was prophesied.

[Note: [Col. 0641B] Semler and Oehler add “which.”]

For even if you denied it, the Father’s promise in the Psalms would immediately confront you, as we have already stated beforehand:

> You are my Son; today I have begotten you. Ask of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance and the ends of the earth as your possession (Ps. 2:7–8).

Nor will you be able, with respect to this proclamation, to claim Solomon, David’s son, rather than to declare Christ the Son of God; nor to claim that the ends of the earth were promised to David’s son, who reigned only within Judea, rather than to Christ, the Son of God, who has already illuminated the whole world with the rays of his gospel.

[Note: [Col. 0642B] “Declare” is absent from other editions.]

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, *ON THE SOUL* (C)

## [SUMMARY]

[Col. 0641D] Tertullian had written, as he himself says, a book against Hermogenes on the provenance or origin of the soul. That book, now lost, is mentioned, insofar as can be determined, in the following section. In the present treatise, however, the Author—by now a Montanist—contends against the philosophers concerning the condition of the soul and the remaining questions pertaining to it; nevertheless, Tertullian himself has interspersed false principles and introduced various errors.

**I.** First of all, he teaches that the immortality of the soul must be learned not from Socrates but from God.

**II.** For although many things are suggested by nature, or were even drawn from the prophets, the philosophers nevertheless handed down many falsehoods mingled with truths.

**III.** If only no heresy existed, there would be no need at all to enter into conflict with the philosophers, the patriarchs of the heretics; nor would it then be necessary to demolish those things by which heretics are captivated by philosophy, or to repel those things by which the faithful are shaken by heresy. [Col. 0642D]

**IV.** Then, approaching his subject, he first declares against Plato that the soul was born and made, since it came from the breath of God.

**V.** He states that several philosophers also asserted the soul to be a body, although Aristotle and Plato deprived it of corporeality.

**VI.** Following that paradox here as well, the Author refutes rather frivolously the arguments by which the Platonists maintained that the soul is incorporeal.

**VII.** Indeed, he improperly attempts to prove the corporeality of the soul from the Gospel.

[Col. 0643A] **VIII.** He responds in turn to other arguments of the Platonists.

**IX.** What is more, he does not shrink from assigning a form even to the immortal soul, and one of an airy and luminous color.

**X.** He next teaches, more correctly, that soul and spirit are one, as are living and breathing; and, on the basis of what pertains to the state of faith, he defines the soul as simple, in accordance with Plato.

**XI.** Yet the soul is called “spirit” in such a way that it is neither the Spirit of God—which Hermogenes appeared to believe—nor endowed with some indefinable “spiritual seed” of the Valentinians.

**XII.** Likewise, the mind is not separated from the soul, since it is nothing other than the soul’s impulse or equipment by which it understands. He maintains this against Anaxagoras and Aristotle, the former of whom denied that the mind could be mingled, and the latter that it could be affected. [Col. 0643B]

**XIII.** He also proves this from the fact that preeminence is attributed to the soul and not to the mind; moreover, God always addresses the soul, not the mind.

**XIV.** When the soul is divided into parts by Plato or others, different powers, efficacies, and operations are to be understood, as Aristotle too indicated concerning some of them, since he himself, together with most others, recognized the soul’s unity.

**XV.** Meanwhile, Christians too recognize with Plato and Aristotle the *hegemonikon* (ἡγεμονικόν)—that is, the supreme faculty of understanding in the soul—contrary to the opinions of Asclepiades and Dicaearchus; and they locate it in the innermost chamber of the heart.

**XVI.** It likewise belongs to the faith that Plato divides the soul into the rational and irrational; yet in such a way that the rational is attributed to its original nature, while the irrational is attributed to transgression. Nevertheless, he does not approve the division of the irrational faculty into the irascible and the concupiscible, since both are also applicable to Christ and to a rational soul. [Col. 0643C]

**XVII.** Plato and the Academics are also rejected in this respect: they declare the sensory activity of the five senses to be irrational. For it is not permissible to call those senses into doubt, lest their trustworthiness in Christ also become a matter for deliberation.

**XVIII.** Nor should the heretics be heard who, just as Plato did with his Ideas, make intellectual things the truths themselves, but sensory things mere images of truth. Rather, the soul perceives bodily things through the body just as it understands incorporeal things through the mind—yet in such a way that it also perceives when it understands. [Col. 0643D]

**XIX.** Thus understanding is always present in the soul, just as the soul’s other natural properties are.

**XX.** Although the soul is uniform, it is nevertheless multiform in the production of dispositions, according to the condition of climate and soil, the character of occupation and concern, the occurrence of circumstances, the freedom of accidents, and even the health of the body—to say nothing of the necessity of fate, which he treated under its own heading.

**XXI.** Accordingly, contrary to the opinion of the Valentinians, the soul was also capable of change through the power of free choice, as he had already demonstrated against Marcion and Hermogenes.

[Col. 0644A] **XXII.** The soul is therefore defined as born from the breath of God; immortal; corporeal—behold, once again the Author’s paradox; endowed with form; simple in substance; capable of suffering in its own right; proceeding in various ways; free in choice; subject to accidents; mutable through its dispositions; rational; sovereign; and divinatory.

**XXIII.** When the question then arises as to where the soul comes from, certain heretics incorrectly assert that it descends from heaven and will undoubtedly return there; the purveyor of all these notions is Plato.

**XXIV.** He therefore destroys a further argument of Plato: that acts of learning are acts of recollection—that is, that souls coming from heaven forget the things among which they formerly existed, and subsequently, from the visible things here, recollect those same things.

**XXV.** He next argues against the Stoics, and at times against Plato himself, who introduce the soul into the body after birth, as a result of the impact of cold air or of the first breath. [Col. 0644B]

**XXVI.** He attacks their opinion, also on the basis of the Christian Scriptures.

**XXVII.** At the same time, he determines that both substances—that of the soul and that of the body—are conceived, formed, and perfected.
