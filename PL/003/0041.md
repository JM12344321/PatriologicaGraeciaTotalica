For now I come to those Roman auspices and auguries which, you declared, were collected with the greatest diligence, omitted to one’s regret, and observed with happy results. Clodius, Flaminius, and Junius, we are to suppose, lost their armies [Col. 0320B] because they did not think it necessary to await the chickens’ most solemn ritual dance? What of Regulus? Did he not observe the auguries—and was he not captured? Mancinus respected the religious rites, yet he was sent under the yoke and handed over to the enemy. Paulus too had chickens that fed greedily at Cannae; nevertheless, he was struck down together with the greater part of the republic. [38] C. Caesar disregarded the auguries and auspices that sought to prevent him from sending ships to Africa before midwinter; and thus he sailed and conquered all the more readily. [Col. 0321A]

What, then, and how much am I to recount about oracles? After his death Amphiaraus gave answers concerning things to come, although he did not know that his wife would betray him for a necklace. The blind Tiresias could see the future, though he could not see the present. Ennius fabricated the responses of Pythian Apollo concerning Pyrrhus when Apollo had already ceased composing verses; and that guarded and ambiguous oracle of his failed precisely when people began to be more refined and less credulous. And Demosthenes, because he knew the responses were contrived, complained that the Pythia was “Philippizing” (*philippizein*, φιλιππίζειν), that is, taking Philip’s side.

Yet auspices or oracles have sometimes touched upon the truth. Although among so many falsehoods chance might appear to have imitated deliberate skill, [Col. 0321B] I shall nevertheless approach the very source of error and depravity from which all this darkness has flowed, and shall dig more deeply and disclose it more plainly.

There are impure, wandering spirits, weighed down from their heavenly vigor by earthly stains and desires. After these spirits, burdened and submerged in vices, had lost the simplicity of their own substance, they did not cease—in consolation for their own calamity, being themselves already ruined—to ruin others; being depraved, to instill the error of their depravity; and being estranged from [Col. 0322A] God, to separate others from God by introducing corrupt religions.

The poets know that these spirits [39] are demons; philosophers discuss them; Socrates knew them, for at the nod and discretion of the demon that attended him he either avoided or undertook particular affairs. The magi likewise not only know demons but also perform through demons every marvel with which they make sport: through the demons’ inspiration and influence they produce illusions, causing things that do not exist to appear, or things that do exist not to appear.

Sosthenes, foremost among those magi both in eloquence and practice, proclaims the true God with the majesty he deserves. He also knows that angels—that is, the ministers and messengers of the true God—attend upon his worship, so that they tremble in terror at the Lord’s mere nod and countenance. The same Sosthenes also revealed that demons are earthly, wandering, and hostile to humanity. [Col. 0322B]

What of Plato, who believed it a difficult task to discover God? Does he not speak without difficulty about both angels and demons? In his *Symposium* he even endeavors to define the nature of demons. He maintains that their substance is intermediate between mortal and immortal—that is, between body and spirit—formed from a mixture of earthly weight and heavenly lightness. From this, he warns, a certain predisposition to love (*procupidinem amoris*) is also formed and glides into human hearts, stirring sensation, shaping affections, and infusing the ardor of desire. [Col. 0323A]

## CHAPTER XXVII

> **Note: [Col. 0323B]**
>
> **CHAP. XXVII.—“Demons.”** I expel this as a gloss. For the word “demons” (*daemones*), written in the margin by some reader as an interpretation, was afterward admitted into the text by others. [Col. 0323C] **MEURSIUS.**—Fulvius Ursinus and Johannes Meursius wrongly reject this word as a gloss, and Chr. Cellarius, following them as his guides, eliminated it altogether. It must certainly be retained, however, and authors frequently speak in this manner. Justin Martyr, *Apology* I, 71, Oxford ed.: Ἤκουσαν διὰ τοῦ ἄλλου προφήτου ΗΣΑΙΟΥ λεχθέν, “They heard what was spoken through the other prophet, ISAIAH.” And *Apology* II, 6: πολλοὶ τῶν ἡμετέρων ἀνθρώπων ΤΩΝ ΚΡΙΣΤΙΑΝΩΝ, “many of our people, THE CHRISTIANS.” See also Athenagoras, *Embassy for the Christians*, chap. 22, p. 101. **DAVIS.**
>
> **“By philosophers.”** Heumann says that this should be removed as a gloss. But it deservedly maintains its place, since mention is made not only of the magi and Plato but also of Socrates in § 8. I add that “and by Plato” must be explained as “and especially by Plato.” Mark 16:7: Εἴπατε τοῖς μαθηταῖς αὐτοῦ ΚΑΙ Πέτρῳ, “Tell his disciples, AND Peter.” Acts 1:14: Σὺν γυναιξὶ ΚΑΙ Μαρίᾳ, “With the women, AND Mary.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Lie hidden, consecrated.”** Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, chap. 4, reads “lie hidden beneath consecrated things” (*consecratis delitescunt*). Johannes Meursius thought that Cyprian’s manuscripts should be corrected according to the text of Minucius; but Fulvius Ursinus emends Felix from the Bishop of Carthage, and he has my agreement, for statues had to be consecrated in a particular manner if they were to possess any power. Related are the things Eastern writers relate about talismans. See Thomas Stanley, *Philosophy of the East*, book I, section 2, chap. 27, Le Clerc’s edition. **DAVIS.**—For my part, I think neither text should be corrected from the other. For who, other than demons, is consecrated beneath statues? In the same way I can say: “Poison, swallowed under cover of food, lies hidden,” and “Poison lies hidden beneath food that has been eaten.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“While they are inspired . . . prophets.”** Read, without doubt, “while they inspire.” Thus chap. 16: “When demons inspire and influence them, the magi produce illusions.” Lactantius II, 14, 10: “All the art and power of the magi consists in the inspirations of demons.” Apuleius, *On the God of Socrates*, p. 45: “Demons take care either to fashion dreams . . . or to instruct omens from birdsong, or to inspire prophets.” **HEUMANN.**—From these passages it is likewise clear that the reading should be: “while in the meantime they inspire prophets.” But if so, I do not see why the passive cannot stand just as well: “while prophets are inspired”—by them. [Col. 0324B] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“So that both, not knowing.”** I am surprised that, among so many interpreters, no one saw that the particles *ut et* must be transposed. The sense is: the demons are deceived and deceive others—deceived as not knowing the unadulterated truth, that is, as beings who do not know it; and deceiving others because they do not confess what they do know, to their own destruction. The Fathers attributed to demons a knowledge not only not superior to human knowledge but not even equal to it. Augustine, book I, *Against the Academics*, chap. 6: “I grant that we can be surpassed in acuteness and subtlety of sense by certain utterly base creatures of this air, whom they call demons; but in reason, I deny it.” And elsewhere: “Far be it from a genuinely religious mind, subject to the true God, to consider demons better than itself merely because they have better bodies. . . . But just as we are better than all these animals in reasoning and understanding, so too ought we to be better than demons in living well and honorably.” We infer from this chapter that demons take great care not to do anything leading to their own ruin and disgrace: “The demons, overcome by pain, declare what they are; and certainly they do not lie to their own disgrace, especially when some of your own people are present.” [Col. 0324C] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“They weigh men downward from heaven.”** By the earthly weight of vices implanted in the soul, they drag it down so that it cannot rise to God. **CELLARIUS.**
>
> **“They divert them to material things.”** Thus Heraldus printed it, and it is believed that he found this reading in the Royal manuscript. Heumann falsely testifies that the manuscript has the singular *materiam* (“matter”); indeed, I do not know his source—unless, perhaps, he derived it from Rigaltius. **LINDNER.**
>
> **“They disturb everyone.”** So the ancient parchments read, as Nicolas Rigaltius testifies. But Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 14, reads “they disturb dreams” (*somnios inquietant*), with whom Meursius, Ouzelius, and Cellarius agree; and apparently quite correctly, for Lactantius, who modeled his own words upon our author’s, says in *Divine Institutes*, book II, chap. 14: “Because they”—the demons—“are subtle and imperceptible spirits, . . . they cause diseases and terrify minds through dreams.” [Col. 0324D] **DAVIS.**
>
> **“By releasing what they had constricted.”** They cease doing harm so that they may appear to have cured the victims. Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 22. Thus Cyprian read it in his manuscript—not *remissus*—as Sigismund Gelenius and others also printed it. **RIGALTIUS.**
>
> **“These are also the frenzied,” etc.** Minucius did not mean the demons themselves, but people possessed and tormented by those foul spirits. Perhaps, therefore, the reading should be: “Hence come also the frenzied.” Our author afterward calls them “prophets outside a temple,” because, just as prophets raved in temples, so these people went mad and whirled about in streets and public squares. See the *Recognitions of Clement*, book V, chap. 13; the *Homilies*, IX, chap. 7; [Col. 0325A] Origen, *Against Celsus*, book VIII, pp. 333 ff.; and Prudentius, *Peristephanon*, v. 1063. **DAVIS.**—**“These are also the frenzied.”** This is an exceedingly vexed passage, for which I offer the following remedy: “These”—of whom I have thus far spoken, whose limbs the demons contort—“are the frenzied whom you see rushing out in public”—he means the possessed, but they are not the only people driven by demons. “The prophets themselves in a temple”—who do not rush out in public—“likewise rave, revel madly, and whirl around”—that is, they behave in the same manner as the possessed. “The instigation of the demon is alike in both, but the purpose of their frenzy is different.” In the possessed its purpose is that, by releasing what they had constricted, they may appear to have cured them; in inspired prophets, that by their breath they may obtain the authority, as it were, of a present divinity and accustom to themselves people curious about the future. [Col. 0325B]
>
> Come, let us examine the interpretations of others. Heumann reads: “They themselves are also prophets, although they grow heated in a temple under the demon’s agitation.” A most elegant jest; but you should know, reader, that the punctuation here must be corrected, placing a comma after “rush out” and a colon after “temple.” **WOPKENIUS.**—I think it should be punctuated: “prophets. They themselves, even without a temple, . . . whirl around.” Furthermore, because Minucius is speaking not of the demons themselves but of possessed people, Davis read: “Hence come also the frenzied,” etc. But I think the author is plainly speaking about the demons themselves, and that the words “they themselves, even without a temple,” etc., can refer to nothing else. Nothing is more customary than for authors to speak about spirits and demons as though speaking about the people whom they drive; see Mark 3:3. Cellarius says: “If demons who lie hidden beneath statues in temples, and agitate their people and move their priests—or if the latter pretend to be moved by them—are regarded as gods, then all madmen who do the same thing outside temples by whirling and tossing themselves about could equally be regarded as gods.”
>
> Heraldus: “I should think that one must read, ‘The prophets themselves in a temple.’ For we noted agitation of prophets of this kind within the temples themselves, in our remarks on Tertullian’s *Apology*, from Julius Firmicus.”
>
> Grunerus: “I change only the punctuation, as follows: ‘These are also the frenzied whom you see rush out in public; the prophets themselves are without a temple: thus they rave,’” etc. [Col. 0325C] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Most of you—a part.”** I am thoroughly dissatisfied with Rigaltius’ conjecture, which reads: “Most people know all these things; a portion of your own people know that the demons,” etc. Gelenius, Heraldus, and Cellarius printed: “Most of you know everything.” Badly, for they omit a useful word. The text must certainly be restored: “The greater part of you knows all these things” (*Haec omnia sciunt pleraque pars vestrum*). Sallust, *Jugurtha*, chap. 18: “The greater part of Lower Africa is possessed by the Numidians.” So too Juvencus, *Gospel History*, book I, p. 9:

> This man will persuade the greater part of that people to return  
> To the true path.

> Nor should anyone be surprised at the construction, for “part” is a collective noun. Virgil, *Aeneid* I, 212:
>
> Some cut them into pieces.
>
> Ovid, *Metamorphoses* I, 244:
>
> Some approve Jupiter’s words aloud.
>
> See Adsonius on Thales VI, 27. [Col. 0325D] **DAVIS.**—The ordinary construction of these words is: “Most of you know that the demons themselves confess all these things about themselves.” Such interweaving of two clauses is not uncommon among the best writers. Yet since the manuscript reads *plerique pars vestrum*—which no one who knows Gronovius will be surprised that he defended—[Col. 0326A] Davis directs that it be read *pleraque pars vestrum*, “the greater part of you.” But this produces an excessively harsh expression, which ought not to be attributed to our Minucius. Perhaps Rigaltius was mistaken when he taught that the manuscript reads so. Certainly Heraldus, who inspected the same codex with his own eyes, did not see the little word *pars* there—unless perhaps he was unwilling to report an obvious error. [Col. 0326B] **HEUMANN.**
>
> **“Tortures of words.”** The earlier reading was “and by the merits of words,” which presented no difficulty. But besides appearing more suitable, the reading of the Royal manuscript also has Cyprian as a supporter, since he imitates Minucius in this matter. *To Demetrianus*: “When they are adjured by us and tortured with spiritual scourges and tortures of words, they are cast out of possessed bodies.” Lactantius V, 22: “Thus, wrenched and tortured by the power of the divine majesty, they howl.” **HERALDUS.**
>
> **“With themselves as witnesses.”** According to a learned man in *Miscellaneous Observations*, 1734, vol. IV, p. 423, the manuscript has *credite f:ssis* [uncertain reading], although this *f:ssis* has been partly erased; and the words “confessing the truth about themselves” occur only between the lines—although, certainly, in the same hand. From this he conjectured: “Believe, with themselves as witnesses, that they are those demons who have confessed.” But in such a reading *testibus*, “as witnesses,” would be far better omitted, for it seems excessively tautological and harsh. I therefore prefer: “Believe, with themselves as witnesses, that they are demons who have confessed the truth about themselves.” [Col. 0326C] **WOPKENIUS.**—“That they are demons” is an obvious gloss. **HEUMANN.**
>
> **“Unwillingly, the wretches shudder within bodies.”** Thus the manuscript: demons, when adjured, shudder within bodies. This is the same as what James 2:19 says: Καὶ φρίσσουσι, “And they shudder.” This is the first effect of exorcism. Then they leap out, and do so unwillingly, as in Luke 9:39: Καὶ ἐξαίφνης κράζει καὶ σπαράσσει αὐτὸν μετὰ ἀφροῦ καὶ ΜΟΓΙΣ ἀποχωρεῖ, “And suddenly it cries out and convulses him with foam, and SCARCELY departs.” And our author, chap. 12: “Unwillingly, wretch, you are convicted of weakness, yet do not confess it.” **LINDNER.**—I should prefer: “Unwillingly they shudder within the wretched bodies.” Cyprian spoke of “possessed bodies.” It is also surprising that the learned man in *Miscellaneous Observations*, at the place cited, thought the text should read: “the wretches shudder and either leap out of the bodies,” etc. For just as the phrase “shudder within bodies” is used here in a plain and natural sense, so in the words “and either leap out,” etc., “from there” or “from them” is very conveniently understood; nor did Cyprian IV, 7 prefix anything of the kind. [Col. 0326D] **WOPKENIUS.**—It should be read thus: “Overcome, they shudder within the wretched bodies.” **HEUMANN.**—Others read *inhaerescunt*, “cling within.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“And to attack one whom you fear.”** The sentence lies utterly limp unless it is restored as: “and to attack one whom you hate,” as Johannes Freinsheim conjectured in his note on Curtius, book X, chap. 8. This emendation is deservedly approved by Jacob Gronovius. Consult Hieronymus Columna on the *Fragments of Ennius*, p. 298, and Janus Gruter on Publilius Syrus, v. 37. **DAVIS.**—I agree. The origin of the error is obvious. Observe a nearly similar maxim of Tacitus in the *Life of Agricola*, chap. 42: “It is peculiar to human nature to hate the person whom one has injured.” Ennius speaks in precisely the same manner as our Minucius in Cicero, *On Duties*, book II, [Col. 0327B] chap. 7: “Whom they fear, they hate; and each person desires the death of the one he hates.” **HEUMANN.**

**SUMMARY.—**In order to establish this point firmly, Octavius traces the matter more deeply, back to its first origin. The source of the error, namely, is this: demons lie hidden beneath statues and images, dwell in shrines, animate the fibers of entrails, govern the flights of birds, direct lots, and utter oracles wrapped in false responses. Nevertheless, when adjured by Christians through the true God and driven from possessed bodies, they are compelled to confess that these things were done not by God but by themselves. Hence they flee from Christians when nearby, and stir up so much hatred against them among the pagans that the latter begin to hate them before knowing them, lest, once they know them, they should either be able to imitate them or be unable to condemn them.

[Col. 0323B] These impure spirits, then—the demons, as has been shown [Col. 0324A] by the magi, by the philosophers, and especially by Plato—lie hidden, consecrated beneath statues and images. By their breath they obtain the authority, as it were, of a present divinity: meanwhile prophets are inspired by them; they dwell in shrines; they sometimes animate the fibers of entrails; govern the flights of birds; direct lots; and produce oracles wrapped in many false responses.

For they are both deceived and deceivers: they do not know the unadulterated truth, and what they do know they do not confess, to their own [40] destruction. Thus they weigh men downward from heaven, divert them from the true God to material things, disturb life, and trouble everyone. Secretly creeping even into bodies, subtle spirits as they are, they fabricate diseases, terrify minds, and contort limbs, in order to compel people to worship them. Then, fattened by the smoke of altars or by animal victims, they release what they had constricted [Col. 0324B] so that they may appear to have cured it.

These are also the frenzied whom you see [Col. 0325A] rushing out in public; the prophets themselves, even without a temple, rave in this way, revel madly in this way, and whirl around in this way. The demon’s instigation is alike in both, but the purpose of the frenzy is different.

Those incidents mentioned to you shortly before also concern these spirits: that Jupiter ordered the games to be repeated because of a dream; that the Castors appeared with their horses; and that the little ship followed the matron’s girdle.

Most of you know all these things: the demons themselves confess them about themselves whenever they are driven from bodies by us, through the tortures of words and the fires of prayer. Saturn himself, [Col. 0326A] Serapis, Jupiter, and whatever host of demons you worship—overcome by pain—declare what they are. And surely they do not lie to their own disgrace, especially when some of your people are present. Believe the truth about them on the testimony of the demons themselves, who confess it concerning themselves.

For when adjured through the true and only God, they unwillingly shudder within the wretched bodies and either leap out immediately or vanish gradually, according as the sufferer’s faith assists or the healer’s grace inspires. Thus they flee from Christians when close at hand, though at a distance they attacked them in your assemblies through your agency.

Therefore, once they have implanted themselves [Col. 0327A] in the minds of the ignorant, they secretly sow hatred of us through fear; [41] for it is natural both to hate the person whom you fear and, if you can, to attack the person whom you dread. Thus they preoccupy minds and obstruct hearts, so that people begin to hate us before they know us—lest, once they know us, they should either be able to imitate us or be unable to condemn us.

## CHAPTER XXVIII

> **Note: [Col. 0327B]**
>
> **CHAP. XXVIII.—“How unjust it is.”** Thus, most excellently, in the Royal manuscript. The earlier reading was: “How great a fault it is.” Minucius touches upon the injustice which Tertullian attacks at greater length in the *Apology*. **HERALDUS.**
>
> **“Things unknown and unexamined,”** that is, the matters themselves. The expression occurs in full in Cicero, *For Caecina* 29: “When he had wished to judge the matter without examining it.” Virgil, *Aeneid* I, 519: “But the unknown circumstance disturbs their minds.” Accordingly there was no reason for Ludovicus Carrio, *Emendations* II, chap. 18, to restore *de incognitis*, “concerning unknown things,” or for Meursius to write *in incognitis*, “among unknown things.” [Col. 0327C] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“We did the same.”** Thus the manuscript; the printed editions have “We were.” **EDITORS.**—I write *iidem*, “we, the same people.” **MEURSIUS.**—Write “We did the same.” For “what you do” precedes. **WOPKENIUS.**
>
> **“Circulated.”** Heumann writes *venditari*, “offered for sale,” and deletes the following “and.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“The reward for information.”** Gelenius had printed this reading, most correctly. But the Roman edition has *judicii*, “of judgment.” Badly. The Royal manuscript confirms Gelenius’ edition. Is this, however, what Tertullian meant in *Apology*, chap. 7: “Who, after discovering such crimes, concealed them, or sold them while dragging the people themselves forward?”—so that “sell” there means “to inform in exchange for payment”? We have said that this is not clear to us. **HERALDUS.**
>
> **“The evil, however.”** That is: “nor did we understand that the evil was so far from existing that, on the contrary, a Christian on trial neither blushed,” etc. For bad actions possess a certain natural shame joined with fear. [Col. 0327D] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Regretted only one thing . . .”** Although this construction may seem unusual, it is not unknown among good authors. Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations*, book V, chap. 28: “For it is peculiar to the wise man to do nothing that he could regret.” The same author, *On Invention*, book II, p. 66: “Whether that is a deed which it was necessary to regret.” See Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book XIII, chap. 2. **DAVIS.**
>
> **“Some persons.”** This word apparently should be deleted. Lactantius V, 1, 2. **HEUMANN.**
>
> **“We undertook.”** A forensic term. For clients were “undertaken.” Servius, on *Aeneid* VI, 609:
>
> And fraud entwined around a client:
>
> “He wishes,” Servius says, “us to understand prevaricators who are patrons of clients, whom we now call those ‘undertaken’ (*susceptos*).” **WOWER.**
>
> **“Taking pity on them.”** Moved by compassion toward those [Col. 0328B] whose lives we were striving to preserve. Heumann writes *miserentes*, “pitying.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“So that we might torture them.”** Thus the manuscript. But already in the Roman edition it was restored as “so that we pressed them.” Correctly; and immediately afterward this verb “to press” is used again. **HEUMANN.**
>
> **“Of course, lest they should perish.”** He indicates the benefit which ought to accrue to them from that denial. A surgeon too, taking pity on the sick person, amputates, cuts, and cauterizes his limbs—of course, lest he perish. [Col. 0328C] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Pressed and overcome by suffering.”** There is no need to restore with Heumann “oppressed and overcome by pain.” The learned man was evidently thinking of evil not in the physical but in the moral sense. Yet all dictionaries attest that *malum*, “evil,” can mean punishment and pain. Quintilian too used *premere*, “press,” for *opprimere*, “oppress,” in *Institutes of Oratory*, book III, chap. 2, p. 407 of my edition: “Varenus, who was oppressed amid every public calamity; for he was also condemned.” Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 2, 17: “But who is not wretched precisely when he is pressed by the greatest pains, if the highest evil consists in them?” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“That we thought and acted as you think and behave.”** Either *gessisse*, “behaved,” must be restored with the anonymous Parisian because *geritis*, “behave,” follows; or—and I prefer this—*agitis*, “act,” must be written instead of *geritis* because *egisse*, “acted,” precedes. **HEUMANN.**—One or the other would indeed be necessary if one equivalent verb could not be substituted for another. [Col. 0328D] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“When, if reason and not the instigation of a demon were judging, they ought rather to be pressed.”** The manuscript has *urguendi*, “to be pressed”; it had wrongly been printed *arguendi*, “to be accused.” Supply: “when Christians were—or had—to be pressed.” **CELLARIUS.**—Heumann expels *magis*, “rather,” which has the force of “more properly,” as in chap. 5, and writes *urgendum esset*, “it would have been necessary to press.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Nor is it, however, surprising, since everyone’s report.”** If nothing has been omitted here from the exemplar, the entire period must be arranged in the following way if any sense is to be extracted: “For with these and similar tales the same demons have filled the ears of the ignorant against us, to produce the horror of execration. Nor, however, is this surprising. Just as the reputation of human beings, which is always nourished by falsehoods scattered into it, is destroyed when the truth is disclosed, so is the business of demons; for false rumor is both sown and fostered by them.” The words “since human reputation” must be joined to “so is the business of demons,” so that the conclusion is understood thus: “Nor, however, is it surprising. Such is the business of demons with the reputation of human beings, which,” etc. [Col. 0329A] By “human beings” understand Christians. The manuscript reads “everyone’s.” **RIGALTIUS.**—Rigaltius rightly replaces *omnium*, “everyone’s,” with *hominum*, “human beings”; but he wrongly wishes this to be understood of Christians, as the distinguished Johannes Fredericus Gronovius has already observed in *Observations on Ecclesiastical Writers*, chap. 7. There he judges that the entire passage should be restored thus: “Nor, however, is it surprising, since the reputation spread by people’s mouths, which is always nourished by falsehoods scattered into it and is destroyed when the truth is disclosed, is also the business of demons; for false rumor is both sown and fostered by them.” [Col. 0329B]
>
> According to his interpretation, this means: “That report, tossed about and propagated by human mouths, which is always nourished and enlarged by falsehoods and disappears when the truth is revealed—that same human report is the business and work of the demons themselves. The authority and instigation belong to the latter; the service belongs to the former.” **OUZELIUS.**—**“Nor, however, is it surprising.”** Rigaltius was the first to apply a healing hand to this passage when, for *omnium*, which is in the manuscript, he restored *hominum*, meaning Christians, in this sense: “Nor, however, is it surprising. Such is the business of demons with the reputation of human beings, which,” etc. J. F. Gronovius came closer to the truth in chap. VII of his *Ecclesiastical Observations*, p. 81. He likewise restores *hominum* for *omnium*, but understands it not of maliciously disposed pagans but of human beings universally, which I approve. Yet when he restores *sit et*, “is also,” for *sic est*, “so it is,” he does not have my agreement. I therefore think *si est*, “if it is,” was written.
>
> For in Terence especially, *si*, “if,” regularly follows “it is not surprising.” Thus *Hecyra* IV, 4, 87: “The wife acted unsurprisingly, if she took this badly.” *Andria* IV, 1, 27: “What is so surprising in that, if he takes his example from you?” The sense is: If it were only human report that spoke such abominations about us, someone might perhaps wonder how it came about that it was not at some point destroyed when the truth was disclosed. But it is not surprising. For it is the business of demons, by whom false rumor is not only sown—as we know also happens in human report—but fostered as well, lest it ever be destroyed. [Col. 0329C]
>
> Heumann restores: “Since common report, which is always nourished by falsehoods scattered into it and is destroyed when the truth is disclosed, is the business of demons.” Grunerus: “Since the report of everyone, which is always nourished by falsehoods scattered into it, is likewise destroyed when the truth is disclosed, so is the business of demons.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Even with your Epona”:** thus it is written in the Royal manuscript. The Roman edition has “or Hippona.” I certainly think Minucius wrote “with your Epona,” and that someone noted “or Hippona” in the margin. Adrianus Turnebus shows by many examples in his *Adversaria* that the ancients wrote Epona. [Col. 0329D] **HERALDUS.**—Gronovius the younger therefore ought not to have written *Vesta* for *vestra*, “your.” That Minucius mentioned Epona alone here is readily established by the passage of Tertullian just cited, which he had before his eyes. Examine also another very similar passage in Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 16. On Epona see also Tomasini’s book *On Votive Gifts*, chap. 1. Barthius, with intolerable audacity, maintained that this goddess was called not Epona but Equona, in *Adversaria*, book XXVI, chap. 2, and book XXXVII, chap. 1. **HEUMANN.**—Because the interpreters failed to perceive the force of the particle *vel*, “even,” they indulged their ingenuity in various ways. The following examples will make its force clear. Tertullian, *Apology* 23: “This ought to be understood even from their fodder. . . .” Terence, *The Self-Tormentor* III, 3, 7: “Even yesterday, in your wine, how immoderate you were!” Cicero, *Letters to Friends* VII, 15: “How captious lovers are can be understood even from this.” Perhaps no one would have doubted the integrity of the passage if Minucius had written: “even with Epona, your goddess.” But this word order ought not to detain us, since above, chap. 11, he similarly writes “even so that we might believe from an example” in place of “so that we might believe even from an example.” Cicero speaks almost so in *On the Orator* II, 27: “These things all belong to talent even of a moderate degree,” in place of “even to talent of a moderate degree.” [Col. 0330A–B] **GRUNERUS.**—Johannes Georgius Keyssler, *Travels*, letter 19, p. 123, supplies an inscription concerning this goddess found at Solothurn in Switzerland. **LINDNER.**—Whatever survives concerning the goddess of the stable, whether in ancient testimonies or in monuments—inscribed, painted, cast in bronze, or numismatic—has been collected most learnedly by the same already deservedly praised Dr. Greppo, in his notes to the Lyon edition, pp. 275–288. **EDITORS.**
>
> **“Consecrated.”** The manuscript has *devoratis*, “devoured,” which could have a place if anyone showed that Isis devoured asses, or that asses were used in sacrifices, since those performing sacrifices ate a portion of the offerings. Since, so far as I know, no one has done this, Meursius restored *devotatis*, which means either to consecrate or to show oneself devoted to the asses and to Isis. Heraldus reads *denotatis*, “marked out”—that is: “You worship the ass, Isis’ bearer, together with Isis, by a religious mark of distinction.” Nicolas Heinsius, *Collection of Letters*, vol. IV, p. 260, reads *adoratis*, “worshiped.” Wopkenius reads *religione decoratis*, “honored with religious observance”—that is, “you deem them worthy; you honor them.” [Col. 0330C] Rigaltius and Heumann read *decoratis*, “adorned,” because less costly ornaments were placed around the ass that bore the richly adorned Isis; Ovid too, *Fasti* VI, 311 and 317, mentions such asses crowned and adorned. **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Heads of oxen.”** This is not in foreign and Egyptian religions but in Roman rites, whence we see all altars covered with these heads, and indeed garlands fashioned from them, as it were, between the columns of temples. Thus we see the head of a ram, eagle, or lion used to adorn fasces, and that of an ox for other decorations, in the tomb of Marcus Antonius Antius Lupus reproduced by Bellori in *Tombs*. Such an ox-head also appears stamped on a coin of the Sardinians under Gordian, in Erizzo, p. 512. But the Roman practice is confirmed by the appearance of the monument of Caecilia Metella, daughter of Creticus, which is well known. **GRONOVIUS.**
>
> **“Faces.”** Thus the manuscript reads, and it can be explained as an apposition. For Anubis, or Cynocephalus, had a canine face. The “Frugifer” mentioned by Arnobius, book VI, p. 196, in the passage cited by Cellarius, had a lion’s face. [Col. 0330D] Others read “combined with the face of lions and dogs,” supplying “faces.” Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 16, supports this emendation: “They ought immediately to have worshiped a two-formed divinity, since they accepted gods combined with both canine and leonine heads, horned from both goat and ram,” etc. **LINDNER.**
>
> **“The same Egyptians, together with most of you.”** I am surprised that they wished to publish this passage in so shamefully corrupt a form, when other editions are correct here—for example, the Paris edition, which reads “together with most of you” (*cum plerisque vobis*), not *vobiscum*. **SCHEFFER.**
>
> **“Noises expressed through the shameful parts of the body.”** The Roman edition has *crepitus*, “breaking of wind.” Petronius, however, called an obscene noise a *strepitus*. Alexander of Aphrodisias, book I, *Problems* 164: ἀποπορδή, “the breaking of wind.” This ridiculous superstition is also known from Cato’s words quoted by Festus under “To prohibit the assemblies.” **WOWER.**
>
> **“They tremble.”** The Roman edition has *extremiscunt* [a corrupt form]. Badly. **HEUMANN.**
>
> **“This much shamelessness,”** for “this shamelessness.” A little below: “they admit into themselves that degree of the worst crime”; chap. 2, § 4: “that time”; chap. 3, § 6: “that place.” Heumann declares that *impudicitiae*, “of shamelessness,” should be removed. [Col. 0331B] **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Every sex.”** I delete “every,” which easily crept in because “all” follows. Take care not to change “every” to “female,” for our author is speaking of both sexes. **HEUMANN.**—Lactantius VI, 23, 10: “The sex given by God does not suffice unless they also profanely and wantonly mock their own sex.” The same author I, 20, 25: “There is no chastity, since unbridled lusts pollute every sex and every part of the body.” **LINDNER.**
>
> **“Shamelessness is called refinement.”** Similarly Curtius V, 1, 38: “Among Persian matrons and maidens, the promiscuous degradation of the body is regarded as affability.” **HEUMANN.**
>
> **“Crime.”** Rigaltius testifies that the exemplar has *mali*, “evil.” Heraldus, however, notes nothing. Heumann approves neither reading and writes: “they admit into themselves that degree of crime.” **LINDNER.**

> **“Nor is a harsher servitude imposed.”** Ulpian, Digest, law 2, *Concerning Those Who Are Under Their Own or Another’s Jurisdiction*, etc.: “If a master has raged against his slaves or compels them to shamelessness and degrading violation, the duty of the governor will be made clear from the rescript of the deified Pius to Aelius Marcianus, proconsul of Baetica. The words of that rescript are as follows: [Col. 0331D]
>
> > ‘The power of masters over their slaves ought indeed to remain unimpaired, nor should any person be deprived of his right; but it is in the masters’ own interest that assistance against cruelty, starvation, or intolerable injury not be denied to those who justly petition for it. Therefore investigate the complaints of those from the household of Julius Sabinus who fled to the statue; and if you find that they were either treated more harshly than is just or subjected to a disgraceful injury, order them to be sold, on the condition that they do not return into their master’s power.’

**SUMMARY.—**They not only inflict hatred upon Christians but also charge them with abominable crimes which no one has yet been able to prove. Such is the business of demons, for false rumor is both sown and fostered by them. Octavius then demonstrates with unanswerable force that Christians are falsely accused of sacrilege, incest, sexual outrage, and parricide, just as it was certain and true that precisely the same crimes—or deeds altogether [Col. 0327B] similar and even worse—were actually committed by the pagans themselves.
