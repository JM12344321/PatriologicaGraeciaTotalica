We are indeed not unaware that Valerius Maximus records in writing that M. Aquilius preferred to serve Mithridates shamefully rather than to die gloriously and courageously. But Valerius himself there passes over in complete silence the manner of his death (Val. Max., book IX, chap. 13, § 1). Yet even if he held a different opinion concerning Aquilius’ death from Appian and others, that certainly could not prevent Minucius from following their opinion rather than that of Valerius Maximus. To this may be added that this Aquilius is believed to be the man of whom Cicero speaks as follows in one of his speeches: [Col. 0459B] “Will you allow that king Mithridates to go unpunished, who killed a consular legate of the Roman people after torturing him with chains, scourging, and every kind of torment?” (Cic., Speech 13, *For the Manilian Law*, p. 243.) Finally, Arnobius, whom we shall discuss in the appropriate place, employed the example of this same Aquilius against the pagans, just as our Minucius did (Arnob., book I, p. 23).

Now, I ask you to consider how correctly our author argues against the pagans from these examples. First, he reasons from the example of Scaevola as follows: “And how many of our people have endured not merely having their right hand burned, but having their entire body set on fire and consumed, without uttering any cries, especially when it lay within their own power to be released?” Then, more forcefully, gathering all the examples together, he presses the point in this manner: “I compare men with Mucius, [Col. 0459C] or with Aquilius or Regulus. Our boys and little women, through divinely inspired endurance of pain, make sport of crosses and tortures, wild beasts, and all the terrors of execution.” Lactantius reasons in precisely the same way, and from the same examples of Mucius and Regulus: “Not to speak of our men,” he says, “our boys and little women silently overcome their torturers, and not even fire can force a groan from them. Let the Romans go and boast of Mucius or Regulus. . . . Behold, the weaker sex and fragile youth willingly endure being torn throughout their entire bodies—not from necessity, since they could avoid it if they wished, but voluntarily, because they trust in God” (Lactant., book V of the *Divine Institutes*, chap. 13, p. 495 ff.). Other pagans, however, though vigorous in body, cried aloud and uttered groans when tortured, because, as he says, [Col. 0459D] “they lack divinely inspired endurance.” As you see, our Minucius had already used these very words. Lactantius therefore reproduced not only our author’s sense and thought, but even some of his actual words.

Furthermore, we think that no one will find it unwelcome or disagreeable if, from those two passages of Minucius and Lactantius, we observe that the number of martyrs in their time was not small, as some falsely suppose. Do they not plainly and openly declare that there was a great multitude of them when they say that not only men but even boys and little women, with the greatest endurance and without any groan, suffered their bodies to be burned and consumed? And what must be said of the various other kinds of torment about which they were silent—crosses, [Col. 0460A] wild beasts, and, as Minucius himself says, “all the terrors of execution”? Were not Christians, by no means few in number, cruelly killed by so many and such diverse torments, and did they not thereby obtain the palm of martyrdom?

It must further be noted that Minucius, like Lactantius, was persuaded that without the divine aid of victorious grace no one could, in confessing Christ, endure those most cruel torments all the way to death with such great steadfastness (Lactant., passage cited). For first, both expressly affirm that endurance was inspired in them. But how was it inspired except by the assistance of divine grace? Minucius then adds that “there is no one who would wish to undergo punishment without reason, or who could endure tortures without God.” Lactantius subsequently wrote: “Nor can endurance itself, [Col. 0460B] without God, overcome such great tortures” (Lactant., passage cited). Does he not in these words most clearly assert and vindicate the necessity of divine grace itself for undergoing death amid so many and such great torments? Something more concerning Lactantius must still be said below. (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 28, art. 1.)

## ARTICLE V. How Octavius answers Caecilius’ final argument against divine Providence, an argument drawn from the Christians’ opinion concerning fate

Caecilius’ final bulwark against divine Providence was, as he claimed, the common opinion not only of the pagans but also of all Christians, [Col. 0460C] who believed that everything happened by fate. What then? How could Caecilius so confidently assert that this was the common position of all Christians? Did they not oppose it with all the powers of mind and intellect? If he had said this of certain heretics, we would readily concede the point to him. But who can tolerate his asserting it universally of all Christians? Listen, however, I beg you, to the reasoning by which he attempts to prove it. “Whatever we do,” he says, “just as others ascribe it to fate, so you ascribe it to God; thus you say that the members of your sect act not of their own accord, but as chosen persons”—that is, you Christians embraced your sect and religion not voluntarily and of your own accord, but by divine election. In making this objection, he appears to have had in view the words of Christ which he had either heard from Christians [Col. 0460D] or read in John’s Gospel: “You did not choose me, but I chose you” (John 15:16). From these words he concludes: “You therefore represent God as an unjust judge, who punishes men’s lot, not their will.”

But Minucius quickly and easily overturns this futile and inept argument. For although we have indeed been chosen by God, it is nevertheless certain that the human will always remains free and that, consequently, men are punished for their own free acts, if those acts are wicked, rather than for a lot assigned them by God. “The mind,” as he says, “is free, and therefore it is a person’s action, not his condition, that is judged.” God is therefore a just and equitable judge, who rewards men for good actions and punishes them for evil ones.

[Col. 0461A] Yet Octavius deals more generously with Caecilius. He grants him what he wants: that all Christians believed everything to happen by fate. But what, he asks, is fate? “Fate (*fatum*) is what God has pronounced (*fatus est*) concerning each one of us.” Augustine does not disagree with this definition of fate. For he says: “We neither deny the order of causes, in which the will of God has the greatest power, nor call it by the name ‘fate,’ unless perhaps we understand *fatum* as derived from *fando*, that is, from speaking” (August., *City of God*, chap. 9, p. 123). You will find more on this subject in Vossius’ *Etymologicon*.

Having established this definition of fate, Octavius maintains that God imposes no fatal necessity upon a person’s actions. For God, he says, “since he can foreknow the material”—that is, all future works of men, whether good or evil—[Col. 0461B] “determines their fates according to the merits and qualities of each.” By these words he plainly teaches that God, who through his divine foreknowledge knows perfectly all future things and whatever each individual will do either well or badly, yet always with great freedom, determines the fates of each according to their merits, whether good or bad—that is, determines eternal punishments or rewards, or establishes and decrees them by his eternal decree. From this he concludes as follows: “Thus in us it is not our birth that is punished, but the character of our disposition”; that is, it is not our birth or manner of being born, but our vices and all the sins we have committed by disobeying God’s commands, that are most justly punished by him.

Moreover, because Caecilius openly asserts that many pagans defended the impious opinion concerning fate or fatal necessity, someone will doubtless ask us who those pagans were. We believe that anyone who asks this will be fully satisfied by the following words of Cicero: [Col. 0461C] “It seems to me,” he says, “that, although there were two opinions among the ancient philosophers—one belonging to those who thought that everything happened by fate in such a way that fate imposed the force of necessity, an opinion held by Democritus, Heraclitus, Empedocles, and Aristotle; the other belonging to those who thought that the voluntary motions of minds occurred without any fate—Chrysippus wished to take a middle position, like an honorary arbiter. Yet he inclines rather toward those who wish the motions of minds to be freed from necessity. But while employing his own terminology, he slips into such difficulties that he unwillingly confirms the necessity [Col. 0461D] of fate” (Cic., *On Fate*, p. 307, line 48 ff.). Whoever wants more should consult Cicero’s own book *On Fate*, from which these words were taken. He should also read, among the more recent Christian writers, Lipsius and Vossius; and, among the ancient Fathers of the Church, Augustine, Eusebius, and Tatian, who in his *Address to the Greeks*, as we have observed elsewhere, thoroughly attacks and refutes this error (Voss., book II, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, chap. 45 ff.; Lips., *On Constancy*, chap. 29 ff.; August., book V of *The City of God*, chaps. 2 and 20; Euseb., book VI of the *Preparation for the Gospel*, chaps. 1, 2, 3 ff.; and Tatian, vol. I of our *Apparatus*, p. 543).

# CHAPTER VII. ON OTHER DOGMAS OF THE CHRISTIAN RELIGION

[Col. 0462A]

## ARTICLE I. The arguments by which Caecilius attacks the resurrection of our bodies and Octavius defends it are examined; with a discussion of metempsychosis and of Pythagoras and Plato, the authors and defenders of this opinion

Caecilius attempts to overthrow certain other teachings of the Christian religion, but with no greater success. One of the chief among them is undoubtedly the teaching which asserts that our bodies will in the future return to life. For if this is removed, our faith, as the Apostle says, will be vain [Col. 0462B] and empty. Caecilius sought to overturn this foundation of our faith and hope by the following dilemma: Either, he says, we shall rise without a body, or with a body. Not the former, certainly; for without a body there is neither mind, nor soul, nor life. This was how the pagans argued, since they prattled that nothing could exist unless it were corporeal. Nor, Caecilius continues, shall we rise with a body, whether with our very own or with another. Certainly not with our own, for it will have been dead for so long that absolutely no remnant of it could remain. Nor with another, since in that case the person restored to life would not be numerically the same but another person than the one who died.

Caecilius next continues his attack upon the raising of our dead body to life with a second argument: [Col. 0462C] “What single person has ever returned from the underworld, even with the grant of a few hours’ leave, as befell Protesilaus, so that we might at least believe on the basis of an example?” We have already observed that certain persons conjecture, without reason, that in this text of Minucius the adjective “few” must be added after the word “hours.” It is sufficiently understood there, since the matter concerns the fate of Protesilaus, who is said to have been the first of the Greek princes killed by Hector in the Trojan War. His wife Laodamia, as soon as she learned that he was dead, is said to have obtained from the gods, through earnest prayers, permission to enjoy conversation with him for three hours after he had been restored to life. See Propertius, Hyginus, Catullus, and Ovid, who wrote an epistle to this Protesilaus in the name of his wife (Propert., book I, Elegy 19; Hygin., *Fable* 103; Catull., [Col. 0462D] Epistle 63 to Mallius; Ovid, Epistle 13).

This line of pagan argument was common and frequently repeated. Justin Martyr presents it as follows: διὰ τὸ μήπω ἑωρακέναι ὑμᾶς ἀναστάντα νεκρὸν, ἀπιστία ἔχει. “Because you have not yet seen a dead man rise, disbelief in the resurrection possesses you” (Justin, *Apology* II, p. 65). Theophilus of Antioch similarly writes: ἀλλὰ καὶ τὸ ἀρνεῖσθαί σε νεκροὺς ἐγείρεσθαι φὴς γὰρ, Δεῖξόν μοι κᾄν ἕνα ἐγερθέντα ἐκ νεκρῶν, ἵνα ἰδὼν πιστεύσω. “But you also deny that the dead are raised. For you say: ‘Show me at least one person raised from the dead, so that, when I have seen him, I may believe your words’” (Theoph., book I *To Autolycus*, p. 77). To these, apart from the others whom it would take too long to cite, [Col. 0463A] may be added Lactantius, who likewise introduces the pagans making this objection: “So many ages have now passed; what single person has ever risen from the underworld, so that we might believe on the basis of his example?” (Lactant., book VII, chap. 22, p. 719).

But why, Minucius asks, do you, Caecilius, a pagan, dare to reject as an old woman’s tale this Christian teaching concerning the resurrection of our bodies? Why do you demand that it be proved to you by the example of one person? Have you perhaps forgotten that at least half of it is steadfastly defended by Pythagoras and Plato, who asserted that our souls migrate from dead bodies into others? But if this has slipped from your memory, read, I ask you, the first book of Diodorus Siculus, and there you will find it written that Pythagoras received this opinion from the Egyptians (Diod. Sic., [Col. 0463B] book I of the *Library*, p. 62). Turn through the books of Herodotus, and there you will see that he likewise said that the Greeks—under whose name he undoubtedly includes Pythagoras and Plato—drew that doctrine from the same Egyptians (Herod., book II, § 123). Consult Laertius, and from the writers cited by him you will learn what Pythagoras proclaimed concerning the various transmigrations of his own soul (Laert., book VIII, §§ 4–5). If all this is not sufficient for you, you will doubtless not withhold credence from Laberius, who, as quoted by Tertullian, openly testifies that this very doctrine of metempsychosis was defended by Pythagoras (Tertull., *Apologeticum*, chap. 48, p. 42). Tertullian himself, Ambrose, and others, however, just like our Minucius, rightly conclude from this that the Christian teaching concerning [Col. 0463C] the resurrection of our body ought not to appear incredible to the pagans (Ambrose, book II *On Faith in the Resurrection*, § 65, vol. II, p. 1151).

What is more, this same Tertullian elsewhere unhesitatingly asserts that this absurd dogma concerning the transmigration of souls was defended not only by Pythagoras, but also by Plato, as our Minucius said (same author, *On the Soul*, chap. 28). But as far as Plato is concerned, no witness at all is needed to prove the point. For he may be read to have taught it clearly and more than once in his books (Plato, at the end of the book *On the Soul*, vol. III, p. 104, and book X of the *Republic*, vol. II, p. 620).

Before we proceed further, two matters must be noted here. First, concerning this ridiculous error of the philosophers, Minucius said: “This opinion deserves not philosophical [Col. 0463D] study, but a mime’s reproach.” It is truly astonishing how zealously no small number of learned men have attempted to emend this passage, which they consider corrupt, and how many different corrections they have devised for it. But they offer no certain reason why the authentic reading of the royal manuscript should be changed. Retaining it, therefore, Rigaltius conjectures that by these words Minucius was aiming at mimes who sometimes transformed themselves into beasts upon the stage and assumed their forms. Others think that Minucius is referring to Laberius, whom Tertullian praised, as we noted shortly before, and to the following verses of his transcribed by Crinitus:

> And I hear that many evils arise even from good things  
> [Col. 0464A] through that doctrine by which the philosophers transform us,  
> making now a human being from a mule, a snake from a woman,  
> and various other things from different forms.  
> (Crinit., book XI, *On Honourable Learning*, chap. 3.)

Laberius, moreover, was both a poet and a mime. Among others, Macrobius says of him: “Caesar induced Laberius, a Roman knight of uncompromising independence, by an offer of five hundred thousand sesterces, to appear upon the stage and personally perform the mimes which he habitually wrote” (Macrob., book II of the *Saturnalia*, chap. 7). There Macrobius quotes several verses by Laberius, who was compelled to perform the mimes he himself had written. Horace too sang of him:

> And the mimes of Laberius, which I am expected to admire as beautiful poems.  
> (Horat., book II, *Satires*, Satire 10.)

Laberius therefore had undoubtedly ridiculed these philosophers’ ravings about metempsychosis [Col. 0464B] not only in the writings cited shortly before, but also in his mimetic performances. Minucius could accordingly infer that this error was worthy of “a mime’s vice” (*mimico vitio*), or “a mime’s reproach” (*mimico convitio*), or “the reproach of the mime” (*mimi convicio*)—namely Laberius—or, if you prefer, of any pantomime.

Second, it must be noted that Pythagoras is called by Minucius the first person to introduce this opinion, or rather this delusion. But by what right, or on whose testimony, did he affirm this so confidently? For Diodorus Siculus, quite explicitly, as we have already said, and Herodotus somewhat less clearly, testify that the first authors of this opinion were the Egyptians, from whom Pythagoras received it. Consequently, either Minucius was mistaken, or, more probably, he meant nothing more than that this doctrine was disseminated [Col. 0464C] by Pythagoras as its first inventor and author among the Greeks. If he meant only the latter, he will undoubtedly have Herodotus himself as his witness, whose words are these: Τούτῳ τῷ λόγῳ εἴσι οἳ Ἑλλήνων ἐχρήσαντο, οἱ μὲν πρότερον, οἱ δὲ ὕστερον, ὡς ἰδίῳ ἑαυτῶν ἐόντι· τῶν ἐγὼ εἰδὼς τὰ οὐνόματα, οὐ γράφω. “There are some among the Greeks who have employed this doctrine as though it were their own, some earlier and others later; though I know their names, I do not think they should be written down” (Herod., book II, § 123). Jerome, however, wrote much more clearly: “And because I had said that I had read the Pythagorean doctrines, hear what Pythagoras was the first among the Greeks to discover: that souls are immortal and pass from some bodies into others” (Jerome, *Apology Against Rufinus*, book III, near the end, p. [Col. 0464D] 470; vol. I of the *Apparatus*, pp. 522, 620, and 1136; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 29, art. 1; *Dissertation on Tertullian’s Apologeticum*, chap. 11, art. 2). We have already spoken of this opinion of his and Plato’s, and shall discuss it further below.

## ARTICLE II. Other arguments of Octavius for proving the resurrection of our bodies, and the contrary arguments of Caecilius, are examined

Against Octavius’ first line of argument, Caecilius and other pagans objected that they could never assent to the utterly ridiculous opinion either of Pythagoras and Plato or of the Christians concerning the restoration of our body to life, [Col. 0465A] since, by whatever reasoning might be devised, it could never happen. But Octavius checks the foolish rashness of his adversary, who doubts the divine power, as follows: “Who is so foolish or brutish that he dares deny that a dead man could be restored to life by God?” For just as God fashioned and created a human being from nothing, so he can just as easily raise him from the dead. Justin Martyr, however, presses this argument more forcefully:

“If anyone, when you did not yet exist in your present form or originate from such things, had pointed to human seed and to a painted image and asserted that from that very seed a nature of this kind could arise, would you have believed him before it happened? I certainly do not think anyone would dare say that he would. But [Col. 0465B] just as in the beginning you would not have believed it possible that people such as yourselves could arise from a tiny drop, although you now see that they have arisen, in the same way consider it not impossible that human bodies, after being dissolved and cast into the earth like seeds, should at the proper time, by God’s command, arise and put on an incorruptible nature” (Justin, *Apology* II, p. 65).

With the same weapon not only Tatian and Athenagoras, whom we shall cite shortly, but also Tertullian strikes the pagans as follows:

“Consider yourself, O human being, and you will discover confidence in the fact. Think back upon what you were before you existed—nothing, certainly; for you would remember if you had been anything. You, therefore, who were nothing before you existed, and who will likewise have become nothing when you cease to exist, why could you not exist again from nothing by the will of the same author who willed you to exist [Col. 0465C] from nothing? You who did not exist were made; when you do not exist again, you will be made. Explain, if you can, the manner in which you were made, and then demand to know the manner in which you will be made. And yet you will certainly be made more easily, since you once existed, seeing that you were just as easily made when once you had never existed. Doubt will be cast, I suppose, upon the powers of God, who composed this vast body of the world from that which had not existed—from no less than the deathlike absence and emptiness of being—and animated it with the spirit of all living things” (Tertull., *Apologeticum*, chap. 48, p. 42).

He pursues these matters more fully in that book (same author, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chap. 11 ff.). Lactantius later states it more briefly: “If in the beginning God formed man in some ineffable manner unknown to me, let us believe that he who made a new man can restore the old one” (Lactant., book VII of the *Institutes*, chap. 23, p. 721).

[Col. 0465D] The pagans nevertheless insisted that it was absolutely impossible for a human body that had been swallowed by water, consumed by fire, reduced to dust, ashes, and embers, or transformed into another body to be raised, restored, and reconstituted as numerically the same body. Minucius replies, however, that although that body “is withdrawn from us, it is nevertheless preserved for God, the guardian of the elements.” Minucius states this briefly, while Tatian treated it at greater length and pressed the point as follows:

“We believe that this will happen, caring nothing meanwhile for however much you may suppose us to jest and babble. For just as I did not know what I was before I was born, when I did not yet exist at all but existed only in the underlying matter of the flesh, and then, once born, though formerly I had not existed, possess in that very generation assurance of my being; [Col. 0466A] so, having been born and then reduced through death to non-being, I shall again come into existence and once more become visible to the eyes. For even if all my flesh is consumed by fire, the world nevertheless receives its evaporated matter; although I dissolve in rivers or the sea, or am torn apart by wild beasts, I am still stored in the treasury of a most wealthy master. Although the poor and impious do not know this, God who reigns nevertheless will, whenever he wishes, restore to its former integrity the substance visible to him alone” (Tatian, *Address Against the Greeks*, p. 146).

Tertullian likewise answers in the same manner: “Wherever you may have been dissolved, whatever material may have destroyed, swallowed, abolished, and reduced you to nothing, it will return you. Nothingness itself belongs to him to whom [Col. 0466B] the whole belongs” (Tertull., *Apologeticum*, chap. 48, p. 43).

Athenagoras presses and explains these points against the pagans at far greater length. There he himself, like the other writers already cited by us, says: Εἰ μὴ ὄντα κατὰ τὴν πρώτην σύστασιν, ἐποίησε τὰ τῶν ἀνθρώπων σώματα, καὶ τὰς τούτων ἀρχὰς, καὶ διαλυθέντα, καθ’ ὃν τύχῃ τρόπον, ἀναστάσει μετὰ τῆς εὐμαρείας· ἐπίσης γὰρ αὐτῷ καὶ τοῦτο δυνατόν. “If God made human bodies when they did not exist at their first constitution, and created the principles from which they derive their origin, he will also restore them with the same ease after they have been dissolved, in whatever manner that may happen. For this too is equally possible for him” (Athenagor., *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, p. 43 ff.). You should not begrudge the effort of reading in his book the remainder, which it would take too long to transcribe here.

[Col. 0466C] Although Minucius utterly overturned the pagans’ frivolous quibbles with so many arguments of such great weight, he nevertheless further refutes and destroys them with various examples:

“See, then, how all nature rehearses the future resurrection for our consolation. The sun sinks and rises; the stars pass away and return; flowers die and revive; after old age the trees put forth leaves; seeds become green again only after decay. Thus the body in this present age is like trees in winter: it conceals a vitality not yet apparent, under the false appearance of dryness. Why are you in haste for it to revive and return while winter is still unripe?”

Theophilus of Antioch uses the same method to prove the future resurrection of our body:

“God,” he says, “supplies arguments from very many things by which he may drive away our unbelief in the matter of the resurrection. [Col. 0466D] Observe, if it is not troublesome, the passing away of seasons, days, and nights. Do you not notice how these things die and rise again? Why, moreover, should I mention the resurrection of fruits and seeds, all of which serve our will? For if, by way of example, you scatter a grain of wheat or of some other crop upon the earth, it first dies and is dissolved, and only then is born and rises into a fruit-bearing stalk. The nature of trees and oaks, in accordance with the Lord’s command, brings forth fruit at appointed times by a certain hidden power invisible to us. Sometimes a sparrow or another little bird devours the seed of an apple, a fig, or some other thing, flies to some elevated, rocky place, and expels it into [Col. 0467A] some hollow through another part of its body. What happens? That seed grows up and becomes a great tree, although it was swallowed and passed through such great heat. . . . But if you wish to behold a more wonderful spectacle, one suited to proving the resurrection of the dead with greater clarity, I bid you raise your face not only toward earthly things but also toward the resurrection of heavenly things. Look at the moon, which dies and is reborn every month, and consider the other stars as well. Moreover, recognize in yourself the operation of resurrection, O human being, although you do not understand it. I do not doubt that, when at some time afflicted by illness, you lost your flesh, appearance, and strength; yet God, showing you mercy and granting healing, [Col. 0467B] caused you to recover the flesh, appearance, and strength you had lost. Just as you do not know where your flesh went when it left you, so you do not know where it returned from or whence you received it. ‘But in truth,’ you say, ‘my flesh grew again from nourishment and flavours converted into blood.’ Well said; but do not be mistaken: this is God’s benefaction and must not be credited to another” (Theoph., book II *To Autolycus*, p. 77 f.).

Many of the most learned Fathers of the Church also confirmed the future resurrection of our bodies by these same, or very similar, examples. But because their words are too lengthy to be reproduced here in full, we shall indicate them for all curious readers who wish to inspect everything with their own eyes. Let them therefore read Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 48, and [Col. 0467C] *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, chap. 12; Ambrose, book V of the *Hexaemeron*, chaps. 22–23, p. 109 ff., and especially book II *On Faith in the Resurrection*, § 53 ff., p. 1147 ff.; Theodoret, Oration 9, *On Providence*, near the end, p. 436 ff.; Nilus, Oration 1, *On Easter*, in Photius, *Bibliotheca*, p. 1530. They should also consult Augustine, book XXII of *The City of God*, chap. 21 ff., p. 676 ff., where he explains whether and how bodies are to be restored to life, whether those which have been reduced to embers and ashes, those eaten by other people, or even the bodies of infants. If they have the leisure and inclination, let them also turn through the pseudonymous work *Questions and Answers to the Greeks*, ascribed to Justin Martyr, p. 208 ff., published among the works of Justin himself.

[Col. 0467D] But some pagan, or some unbeliever or other, will rise up and object that the examples thus far proposed to prove the resurrection of our body are of little, or rather of no, weight. For neither the sun nor the stars die, nor do the numerically identical flowers and fruits that have died revive. Yet the most learned men praised by us were not so dull and obtuse as either to be ignorant of these points or fail to notice them. They certainly knew that all comparisons, as the saying goes, limp in some respect. Accordingly, they set forth these comparisons in order to make plain that many examples are available to us which have something in common with, and similar to, our dead bodies when they are someday restored to life, and by which [Col. 0468A] God’s power to restore them—the only matter at issue—is clearly and distinctly proved. For do not the setting and rising of the sun and stars, and fruits and other things of this kind growing again from their decaying grains, present us with some likeness of our rising body? But if these things continually happen by God’s customary power, how did the pagans dare deny that our dead bodies could be raised to life by the same divine power? Therefore, for them to return to the breezes of the light they have lost, nothing is required except God’s own will. That God wills this is gathered most clearly from our sacred Scriptures, as we shall show below from other writers of ours. Since, however, the pagans’ dispute concerned not this will of God but only his power, Minucius [Col. 0468B] and the others thought it sufficient to have demonstrated that power by various examples and even by certain arguments *ad hominem*, as they call them.

Moreover, once these points had been established and confirmed, our author thought that nothing further needed to be said in answer to Caecilius’ inept quibble, by which he demanded that some dead person be produced who had ever returned to us alive. For what need was there to prove the divine power to raise the dead by the example either of Protesilaus or of anyone else, when that power had already been demonstrated by the most certain force of reasoning? Theophilus of Antioch, however, in order to blunt that dull and feeble pagan weapon, presents examples other than that of Protesilaus—examples handed down by the pagans themselves—in the following manner:

“But what remarkable thing do you do if you see and then believe? Εἴτε [Col. 0468C] πιστεύεις τὸν Ἡρακλέα καύσαντα ἑαυτὸν ζῆν, καὶ Ἀσκλήπιον κεραυνωθέντα ἐγηγέρθαι, τὰ δὲ ὑπὸ τοῦ Θεοῦ σοὶ λεγόμενα ἀπιστεῖς, ἴσως κἂν ἐπιδείξω σοι νεκρὸν ἐγερθέντα, καὶ ζῶντα, καὶ τοῦτο ἀπιστεύσεις. If you believe that Hercules, who burned himself, is alive, and that Asclepius, who was struck by lightning, was restored to life, what madness is it to refuse belief to the things God tells you? Doubt would still possess you even if I presented to you someone summoned back from the dead and alive; could I obtain credence from you?” (Theoph., book *To Autolycus*, p. 77.)

He might further have adduced the example, presented by Plato, of Er the Armenian, whom we discuss elsewhere, and many other such stories circulated by the pagans; but our writers rejected them as superfluous. If, however, anyone desires still more concerning the future resurrection of the dead than what we have related [Col. 0468D] from the authors cited, let him consult Tatian, Ambrose, and others who published entire treatises on this subject (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation 2, chap. 16, art. 1, p. 1116).

## ARTICLE III. The arguments are considered by which it is shown that wicked people will be tormented with eternal punishments and everlasting fire, while the devout and righteous will be endowed with perpetual happiness; with a discussion of the fires of Mount Etna and Vesuvius, the Stygian marsh, and the fiery river of hell

Just as Caecilius rejects and repudiates, not without mockery, the dogma of the Christian faith concerning dead people’s return to life, so too does he reject its teaching concerning the everlasting punishments of the wicked [Col. 0469A] and the eternal happiness of the devout. “Deceived,” he says, “by this error”—the future return from death to life—Christians “promise themselves, as good people, a blessed and perpetual life after death, but everlasting punishment to all others as unrighteous. . . . While you live in misery, judge what awaits you after death. . . . You who dream of posthumous immortality: when you are shaken by danger, consumed by fevers, and torn by pain, do you still not perceive your condition?” Finally, with equal boldness and impiety, he declares: “As we are born, so we perish”; that is, just as we were nothing before we were born, so after death, reduced to the same nothingness, we shall be absolutely nothing. Such, then, was Caecilius’ argument. But it must incidentally be noted [Col. 0469B] that in this text the words *periculo quateris*, “you are shaken by danger,” have displeased some, who conjecture that one should read *querquero*, that is, “you are shaken by cold and trembling,” or by a chill fever. But the royal manuscript contradicts this conjecture, and the words that follow also prevent its acceptance.

Octavius, however, modestly replies to Caecilius, who casts these objections—or rather these arrogant reproaches—at the Christians, that very many people, “because of the consciousness of their deserts,” that is, because their sins and vices condemn their conscience, more strongly wish than actually believe that a person is extinguished utterly by death and is not preserved for any subsequent punishment. There can indeed be no doubt that the more slowly God’s most just judgment is brought against those who deceive themselves by this error and sin with greater licence, the more severely it will come. [Col. 0469C] Hence Valerius Maximus says concerning Dionysius, tyrant of Syracuse: “Divine wrath proceeds with a slow step toward exacting vengeance for itself, and compensates for the delay of punishment by its severity” (Val. Max., book I, chap. 1, Foreign Examples § 3, p. 21).

But if, by the admission of a pagan, divine vengeance does not permit those guilty of more serious crimes to go unpunished, others certainly cannot deny that wicked, criminal, and impious people who die still stained by their crimes must undergo their due punishments after death. For then, in the other life, which will be eternal, they will pay with everlasting fire the penalties they did not pay in this life. For, Minucius says, “a discerning fire burns and restores the limbs, consumes and nourishes them.” Nor could the pagans answer that this was a fabrication invented by certain persons who wished [Col. 0469D] to frighten others. For this is no more difficult for almighty God than raising a dead person to life. It is therefore sufficient that he should will it. That he does so will is declared not only by sacred Scripture, but also by his divine justice, which can leave no crime unpunished.
