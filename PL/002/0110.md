A change of dress comes close to being blameworthy only if it changes not custom but nature.

[Note: [Col. 1040D] “To change one’s dress.” This is equivalent to saying that a change of dress becomes a disgrace only under this condition. Yet one garment is not a disgrace to another garment; it is a disgrace to the person. **SALM.**]

[Note: [Col. 1040B] “A disgrace of attire,” **Fran.**; “close to blame,” **Saml.**]

There is a considerable difference between deference to an age and religious obligation. Let custom keep faith with time, and nature with God.

[Note: [Col. 1040D] “Let custom keep faith with time.” Salmasius splendidly restored this passage in conformity with Tertullian’s meaning and hand. Certainly custom ought to keep faith with time, since custom itself is nothing other than long-standing usage through time. Thus one who changes custom despises only time. But nature owes fidelity to God, namely, to its author; for God created nature, and the majesty of the divine hands is impressed upon it. Man himself is the foremost part of nature. Human nature itself therefore owes fidelity to God its creator. If it breaks faith, if it violates or defrauds its own nature by the effeminization of its male character, or by any other falsification, then it is truly guilty against nature and the divine majesty. **RIG.**]

For according to Festus, *crepitulum* signifies an ornament of the head which makes a rattling sound when the head moves. Turnebus says that *caliendrum* signifies a covering for the head, from the Greek καγγανον (*kanganon*); it is also used by Arnobius in book VI *Against the Nations*. **PAM.**]

and now even the litters [Col. 1044B] and chairs in which, even in public, they were kept as though at home and in privacy. But one man extinguishes his own eyes; another kindles eyes that are not his.

Look at the she-wolves who are marketplaces for common lusts—even the procuresses and sorceresses themselves.

[Note: [Col. 1044D] “Even the procuresses and sorceresses themselves.” He joins the *fictrix* to the she-wolf, the procuress to the prostitute. A *fictrix* is a dissembler, one who fashions wax images for the winning of love or for destruction: a witch and procuress. **RIG.**]

And although it is better to avert one’s eyes from such disgraceful exhibitions of chastity murdered in public, nevertheless look, even with your gaze raised above them,

[Note: [Col. 1044D] “Even with your gaze raised above them.” That is, with the eyes looking sideways. **RIG.**]

and you will now see matrons. When the attendant of the latrines makes silk flutter, consoles with necklaces a neck more unclean than its place,

[Note: [Col. 1044D] “Consoles with necklaces a neck more unclean than its place.” With the shamelessness of the most absurd luxury, she consoles her neck, condemned to the wretched service of cleaning a latrine. **RIG.**]

thrusts hands privy to every shameful part into bracelets which even matrons would have been reckless to assume from the gifts awarded to brave men, and puts a clean, perhaps even a red patrician, shoe upon an unclean leg—

[Note: [Col. 1044D] “Upon an unclean leg a clean,” etc. Since Roman shoes covered part of the leg, they were high boots; hence Ovid, *Art of Love*, book III:

> Let an ugly foot always be concealed in snow-white leather.

Of red shoes, some were boots, others hooked shoes. Those who had held magistracies wore red shoes; soldiers wore hobnailed shoes; everyone else wore boots and shoes of ὠμοβύρσινος (*ōmobyrsinos*), “raw hide.” **LE PR.**]

why do you not look at those people, or [Col. 1045A] likewise at those forms of dress which falsely claim a religious sanction for their novelty? Thus persons are initiated into the rites of Ceres because of clothing entirely white, and because of the distinguishing headband and the privilege of the ceremonial cap;

[Note: [Col. 1045C] “Because of the distinguishing headband and the privilege of the ceremonial cap.” The headband and ceremonial cap were the marks or privileges of matrons—specifically, matrons initiated into the rites of Ceres. The cap should be understood as white. **RIG.**]

because of a different association with gloomy clothing

[Note: [Col. 1045C] “Gloomy clothing.” Black clothing. **RIG.**]

and a forbidding fleece above the head,

[Note: [Col. 1045C] “A forbidding fleece above the head.” A grim one: Bellona, or Minerva, shod and wearing a helmet of dark form. Tertullian himself, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, p. 82. **RIG.**]

minds are put to flight in Bellona’s temple;

[Note: [Col. 1045C] “Minds are put to flight in Bellona’s temple.” The fanatic loses his mind when seized by frenzy in Bellona’s shrine. A Bellonarian is ὁ θεοφορούμενος (*ho theophoroumenos*), “one possessed by a god.” **RIG.**—The common reading is “they flee to the hills of Bellona.” Even before the first appearance of our edition of Tertullian, we knew that Augustine, in the acts concerning Caecilian’s acquittal, mentioned a hill of Bellona near Carthage, to which certain persons withdrew during the persecution. But since no one has yet shown either that there were several hills of Bellona or that the Bellonarians were driven into the hills of Bellona—and since, on the contrary, the most ancient authors have reported that they were called Bellonarians because fanatics were seized by violent frenzy in Bellona’s sanctuary—we readily allowed ourselves to be persuaded that the common reading could in no way be defended here and that the text must therefore read: “minds are put to flight in Bellona’s temple.” In Bellona’s temple, namely, minds are put to flight: the minds of those from whom mind and reason are taken away by frenzy. Minds are said to be put to flight in the same way as reason is said to flee, or minds to wander, become alienated, be moved, hurled headlong, or agitated—and, in the opposite sense, to return. Salmasius approves “minds are put to flight,” corresponding to the Greek construction ἐξιστάναι φρενός (*existanai phrenos*), “to drive out of one’s mind.” Septimius said “minds are put to flight” just as at col. 1034 he said, “Campania was taken away from Pompey.” **LE PR.**]

[Note: [Col. 1045B] “Hills,” **Fran.**]

the ostentation of a broader purple stripe

[Note: [Col. 1045D] “The ostentation of a broader purple stripe.” A broader strip of purple surrounds and clothes Saturn. **RIG.**]

and the overlay of Galatian red commend Saturn;

[Note: [Col. 1045D] “Galatian red.” Scarlet. Pliny: “The red berry of Galatia is cochineal.” **RIG.**]

and this very *pallium*, arranged with rather affected precision and made Greek by its sandal,

[Note: [Col. 1045D] “Made Greek by its sandal.” “Greekified by the sandal” means “wearing a Greek sandal.” **RIG.**]

[Note: [Col. 1045B] “Chalked in the Greek manner,” **Fran.**]

flatters Aesculapius. How much more, then, should you arraign it and press it upon the eyes, if—though now guilty only of simple and unaffected superstition—it were nevertheless guilty of superstition?

But when it first clothes that wisdom which rejects the most empty superstitions, then most certainly the *pallium* is a more majestic garment than every hide and robe, and, above all priestly conical caps

[Note: [Col. 1045B] “Conical caps and titles,” **Seml.**]

and ritual headdresses, a priestly array.

[Note: [Col. 1045D] “A priestly elevation.” Priestly adornment. Thus Persius, Satire I:

> See how we now teach heroic sentiments  
> To men accustomed to chatter trifles in Greek—

that is, “heroes,” or “heroic sentiments.” **RIG.**]

Lower your eyes, I advise you; [Col. 1045B] revere the dress which, for the present, proclaims your renunciation of your one error.
