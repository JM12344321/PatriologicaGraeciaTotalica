The same Octavius says a little later: “That we experience the defects of the human body is not punishment, but military service . . . adversity is very often the training-ground of virtue.” Seneca, however: “Fortune beats and lacerates us. Let us endure it: it is not cruelty, but a contest.” And shortly before these words: [Col. 0403D] “Adversity is the opportunity of virtue” (Senec. ibid., ch. 4, p. 409).

Octavius continues: “God . . . in adversity tests and examines each person; [Col. 0404A] he weighs the character of each through dangers; he investigates him even to the point of death, secure in the knowledge that nothing belonging to him can perish.” Now, I ask you, listen to Seneca: “Therefore, those whom God approves and loves he hardens, scrutinizes, and exercises; but those to whom he seems indulgent and whom he seems to spare, he keeps soft for evils to come. For you are mistaken if you judge anyone exempt. His share will come to the man who has long been fortunate. Whoever appears to have been dismissed has merely been deferred” (Senec. ibid., ch. 4, p. 408).

Octavius adds: “Therefore, just as gold is tested by fire, so are we proved by ordeals.” And Seneca, after the words of his that we have cited: “Fire tests gold; misery tests brave men” (Senec. ibid., ch. 5, p. 391).

Why say more? Octavius proceeds and says: “What a beautiful spectacle it is for God when a Christian grapples with pain, when he sets himself against threats, punishments, and tortures!” Earlier Seneca had said in the same book: “Behold a spectacle worthy of God’s turning his gaze from his work to look upon. Behold a contest worthy of God: a brave man matched against ill fortune” (Senec. ibid., ch. 2, p. 492). Do you see how aptly [Col. 0404B] Minucius turned the things written by Seneca—the best and most honorable man among the pagans—against the pagans themselves and toward the defense of Christian teaching, which is far holier than pagan teaching?

If we pass from pagan writers to Christian ones, we shall also notice many things that Minucius excerpted from Tertullian’s books, especially from his *Apologeticum*. To prove this, it will suffice for now to adduce only some of them. Thus Octavius said this concerning God: “Whatever things exist, he commands by his word, orders by reason, and brings to completion by power.” Tertullian likewise says of him: “That which we worship is one God, who produced this entire mass . . . from nothing, by the word with which he commanded it, the reason with which he ordered it, and the power by which he was able” (Tertull., *Apologet.*, ch. 17, p. 18).

Again Octavius says of the same God: “What of the fact that [Col. 0404C] I have the agreement of everyone concerning him? I hear the common people, when they stretch their hands toward heaven, say nothing other than ‘God’; and ‘God is great,’ and ‘God is true,’ and ‘If God grants it.’ Is this the natural speech of the common people, or the confession of a Christian?” Tertullian similarly: “The soul . . . calls God by this name alone, because it is the proper name of the true God: ‘God is great,’ ‘God is good,’ and ‘Whatever God grants’; this is the voice of all. It also appeals to him as judge: ‘God sees’; ‘I entrust it to God’; and ‘God will repay me.’ O testimony of the soul naturally Christian! Finally, when pronouncing these words, it looks not toward the Capitol but toward heaven” (Tertull., ibid.). He said the same thing, though more briefly, in two other books (the same author, *On the Testimony of the Soul*, ch. 2, p. 81, and *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 3, p. 381). No one should doubt that Minucius, in the statements we have quoted, had in view these words of Tertullian himself.

Was he not also aiming at other words of Tertullian when [Col. 0404D] he justly and deservedly cast this charge against the pagans: “Where are acts of fornication arranged more often by priests than among altars and shrines, procuring is negotiated, and adulteries planned? [Col. 0405A] Indeed, blazing lust is satisfied more often in the chambers of temple-keepers than in the brothels themselves.” For Tertullian had earlier cast precisely the same charge against those same pagans: “The consciences of all will acknowledge that adulteries are arranged in temples, procuring negotiated among altars, and lust discharged, often in the very chambers of temple-keepers and priests, beneath the same sacred bands, priestly caps, and purple robes, while incense burns” (Tertull., *Apologet.*, ch. 15, p. 16).

Moreover, concerning the utterly cruel savagery of gladiators, these are the words of Octavius: “No different are those who devour beasts from the arena, smeared and steeped in blood, or fattened on human limbs and entrails.” Tertullian says of the same people: “Those who dine on wild-beast dishes from the arena, who ask for some of the boar and some of the stag. The boar [Col. 0405B] wiped away, in struggling, the blood of the man it had bloodied. The stag lay in a gladiator’s blood. The bellies of the bears themselves are sought after, while they still belch with human entrails. Thus man belches up flesh that was fed upon man” (Tertull., *Apologet.*, ch. 9, p. 10).

Octavius also says: “Nor are we seditious, if we all share the knowledge of the one good, gathered together in the same peacefulness in which we live as individuals.” Tertullian, however: “This assembly of Christians . . . if anyone complains of it under the same charge by which complaint is made against factions: for whose destruction have we ever assembled? Gathered together, we are the same as when dispersed; all together, the same as individually—injuring no one, grieving no one” (Tertull., *Apologet.*, ch. 39, p. 36).

Finally, to pass over for now the many other passages that must be cited below, [Col. 0405C] Octavius says: “The victor is the one who has obtained what he contended for.” Tertullian, however: “Victory is to obtain that for which you have contended” (Tertull., *Apologet.*, ch. 50, p. 44).

Let us now come to the passages that Cyprian copied from our Minucius: “When,” says our author, “has a partnership in kingship ever begun in good faith, or ended without blood?” Cyprian in exactly the same words: “When has a partnership in kingship ever begun in good faith, or ceased without blood?” (Cyprian, *On the Vanity of Idols*, p. 14). From this one may perhaps conclude that in Minucius’s text too *desiit* (“ceased”) should be read instead of *discessit* (“ended”), since it is better opposed to *coepit* (“began”).

Minucius: “Bees have one king, flocks one leader, and herds one ruler.” Cyprian: “Bees have one [Col. 0405D] king, flocks one leader, and herds one ruler” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius: “He who commands whatever things exist by his word, orders them by reason, and brings them to completion by power, cannot be seen, for he is brighter than sight; cannot be comprehended, for he is purer than touch; cannot be estimated, for he is greater than the senses . . . and therefore we estimate him worthily when we call him inestimable.” Cyprian, however: “He who commands whatever things exist by his word, orders them by reason, and brings them to completion by power, cannot be seen, for he is brighter than sight; cannot be comprehended, for he is purer than touch; cannot be estimated, for he is greater than sense; and therefore we estimate him worthily when we call him inestimable” (Cypr., loc. cit.). There, however, Cyprian omitted certain words of Minucius—omitted by us and marked [Col. 0406A] by dots—doubtless for brevity’s sake.

Minucius: “Do not seek a name for God: ‘God’ is his name. Names are needed where a multitude must be distinguished person by person through the particular marks of appellations. For God, who alone exists, the word ‘God’ is his whole designation . . . I hear the common people . . . say ‘God is great,’ and ‘God is true,’ and ‘If God grants it’; this is the natural speech of the common people.” Cyprian: “Do not seek God’s name: ‘God’ is his name. Names are needed where a multitude must be distinguished through the particular marks of appellations. For God, who alone exists, ‘God’ is his whole designation . . . In many sayings the common people naturally confess God . . . We frequently hear it said: ‘O God!’ and ‘God sees’; and ‘I entrust it to God’; and ‘God will repay me’; and ‘What God wills’; and ‘If [Col. 0406B] God grants it’” (Cypr., ibid., p. 15). Do you see how Cyprian inserted his own words among those of Minucius? Minucius had furthermore committed this to writing concerning images of the dead: “Those things that had been adopted as consolations became sacred rites.” Cyprian says of the same things: “Those things that had first been adopted as consolations became sacred rites” (Cypr., ibid., p. 11).

Minucius: “That great Macedonian Alexander wrote to his mother in a notable volume that, through fear of his power, a priest had disclosed to him the secret that the gods were men.” Cyprian: “Alexander the Great writes to his mother in a notable volume that, through fear of his power, a priest had disclosed to him the secret that the gods were men” (Cypr., ibid., p. 12).

Minucius: “The Castors die by turns so that they may live; [Col. 0406C] Aesculapius is struck by lightning so that he may rise into godhood; Hercules is burned in the fires of Oeta so that he may cast off the man.” Cyprian, still in the same words: “Castor and Pollux die by turns so that they may live; Aesculapius is struck by lightning so that he may rise into godhood; Hercules is burned in the fires of Oeta so that he may cast off the man” (Cypr., ibid., p. 11).

Minucius: “Apollo pastures the flock of Admetus. Neptune constructs walls for Laomedon, and the unfortunate builder receives no payment for his work.” Cyprian: “Apollo pastured the flock of Admetus. Neptune constructed walls for Laomedon, and the unfortunate builder received no payment for his work” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius later records this concerning Saturn and Janus: “He taught men to stamp letters and coin money . . . Accordingly he preferred Latium to be named from his hiding place (*latebra*) . . . and the Janiculum [Col. 0406D] from Janus.” Cyprian: “Hence Latium received its name from his hiding place. He was the first in Italy to institute the stamping of letters and the coining of money . . . From the name of Janus the Janiculum was named” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius: “The cave of Jupiter is visited, and his tomb is shown.” Cyprian reproduced this in exactly the same words: “The cave of Jupiter is visited in Crete, and his tomb is shown” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius: “Through Proculus’s perjury, Romulus is a god.” Cyprian, however: “There is Romulus, made a god through Proculus’s perjury” (Cypr., ibid., p. 12).

Minucius, concerning the gods: “Why, if they were born, are they not still being born today, unless perhaps Jupiter has now grown old and Juno’s childbearing has failed?” Cyprian, concerning the same: “But if [Col. 0407A] the gods were once born, why are they not still being born today, unless perhaps Jupiter has grown old or Juno’s childbearing has failed?” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius: “We know the native-born gods of the Romans: Romulus, Picus, Tiberinus, Consus, Pilumnus, and Picumnus are gods. Tatius both discovered and worshiped Cloacina; Hostilius, Fear and Pallor; soon Fever was consecrated by someone or other . . . and, of course, Acca Larentia and Flora were prostitutes.” Cyprian: “We know the native-born gods of the Romans. There is Romulus . . . Picus, Tiberinus, Pilumnus, and Consus . . . Tatius also both discovered and worshiped the goddess Cloacina; Hostilius, Fear and Pallor; soon Fever was consecrated by someone or other; and Acca and Flora were prostitutes” (Cypr., ibid., p. 12).

[Col. 0407B] Minucius: “Thracian Mars, Cretan Jupiter, Juno now Argive, now Samian, now Punic, now Taurian Diana, or the Idaean or Egyptian Mother—these are not divine powers but monstrosities.” Cyprian: “Thracian Mars, Cretan Jupiter, Juno either Argive or Samian or Punic, Taurian Diana, and the Idaean and Egyptian Mother of the Gods are monstrosities, not divine powers” (Cypr., ibid., p. 13).

Minucius: “Before them, by God’s ordering, the Assyrians, Medes, and Persians long held kingdoms, as did also the Greeks and Egyptians.” Cyprian: “Before them the Assyrians, Medes, and Persians held empire; and we know that the Greeks and Egyptians reigned” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius: “What of Regulus? Did he not observe the auguries and yet was captured? Mancinus kept the religious observances, and was sent [Col. 0407C] under the yoke and surrendered. Paulus had chickens that ate greedily at Cannae; nevertheless he was laid low with the greater part of the commonwealth. Gaius Caesar disregarded the resistance of the auguries and auspices against sending ships to Africa before midwinter; and for that very reason he sailed and conquered more easily.” Cyprian: “Regulus observed the auspices and was captured. Mancinus kept the religious observances and was sent under the yoke; Paulus had chickens that ate greedily, and yet was slain at Cannae. Gaius Caesar disregarded the resistance of the auguries and auspices against sending ships to Africa before midwinter, and for that very reason sailed and conquered more easily” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius, concerning demons: “Impure and wandering spirits, weighed down from heavenly vigor by earthly stains and desires . . . Already ruined, they do not cease ruining others; depraved, they infuse [Col. 0407D] the error of their depravity.” Cyprian similarly: “Impure and wandering spirits, who, after being immersed in earthly vices and withdrawing from heavenly vigor through earthly contagion, do not cease, though ruined, to ruin others, and, though depraved, to infuse the error of their depravity” (Cypr., ibid.).

Minucius, still concerning the same: “These impure spirits, therefore . . . hide beneath consecrated statues and images, and by their breath . . . prophets are inspired . . . Sometimes they animate the fibers of entrails, govern the flight of birds, direct lots, and produce oracles wrapped in many falsehoods. For they are both deceived and deceive others . . . They disturb life and trouble sleep; secretly creeping even into bodies . . . they terrify minds and contort limbs, [Col. 0408A] so as to compel people to worship them; so that, fattened by the stench of altars or by animal victims, they may release what they had constricted and appear to have effected a cure.” Cyprian: “These spirits, therefore, hide beneath consecrated statues and images; by their breath they inspire the breasts of prophets, animate the fibers of entrails, govern the flight of birds, direct lots, and produce oracles, always wrapping falsehoods in truths. For they are both deceived and deceive others; they disturb life and trouble sleep. Secretly creeping even into bodies, they terrify minds and contort limbs . . . so as to compel people to worship them; so that, fattened by the stench of altars and the pyres of cattle, they may release what they had constricted and appear to have effected a cure” (Cypr., ibid., p. 14).

Minucius, again concerning these same demons: “Thus they seize minds, so that people begin to hate us [Col. 0408B] before they know us, lest, once they know us, they should either be able to imitate us or be unable to condemn us.” Cyprian likewise: “Hence they drive the common people to hatred of our name, so that people begin to hate us before they know us, lest, once they know us, they should either be able to imitate us or be unable to condemn us” (Cyprian, ibid.).

Minucius: “What temple shall I build for him, God, when this whole world, fashioned by his works, cannot contain him? And when I, a human being, dwell more spaciously, shall I enclose the power of such majesty within a single little shrine? Is he not better dedicated in our mind, consecrated in the depths of our heart?” Cyprian, however: “What temple could God have, when the whole world is his temple? And when a human being dwells more spaciously, shall I enclose the power of such majesty within a single little shrine? He must be dedicated [Col. 0408C] in our mind, consecrated in our heart” (Cypr., ibid., p. 15).

Still other passages occur in this book of Minucius that resemble things Cyprian wrote either in the book already cited by us so often, or in his works addressed to Donatus and Demetrian, and elsewhere; to transcribe them now would take too long. We shall therefore adduce them below whenever they are needed to confirm our author’s opinion.

Yet it will doubtless seem astonishing that Cyprian, so eloquent and learned a man, copied word for word so many passages from a single little book composed by Minucius. Many, therefore, will readily persuade themselves that Minucius borrowed his material from Cyprian rather than Cyprian from Minucius. But if Minucius published [Col. 0408D] his book earlier, as we have already shown, then certainly he did not take his material from Cyprian; Cyprian took his from him. What, moreover, of the fact that our Minucius was neither of the lowest nor of an obscure reputation? For Lactantius calls him a distinguished advocate endowed with no inconsiderable learning, as was observed above, and did not hesitate to draw many things from his book. Why, then, should Cyprian not have done so as well?

# CHAPTER III  
## NEW ANNOTATIONS ON THE BOOK OF MINUCIUS FELIX; AND FIRST, AN EXAMINATION OF THE ARGUMENTS BY WHICH CAECILIUS DENIES THAT GOD EXISTS AND OCTAVIUS PROVES IT

[Col. 0409A]

## ARTICLE I  
### What Will Be Treated in These Notes and How; and First, Whether Christians Should Be Excluded from This Disputation

As we have already said, nothing else occurs throughout this whole book of Minucius—if one excepts the prologue, the epilogue, and Minucius’s own judgment—except the disputation of Caecilius and Octavius, in which the former strives with all the powers of his intellect to defend [Col. 0409B] and uphold the religion of the pagans, and the latter that of the Christians. But the arguments of each, and especially Octavius’s answers to Caecilius’s unjust accusations, are separated from one another by such long intervals of discourse that they may so confuse the reader’s mind that he can scarcely perceive their force and weight unless he reads and rereads the same things repeatedly. Moreover, several matters are touched upon briefly and summarily in them that, although clear and evident in Minucius’s time, are now obscure to many and must therefore be explained by us.

Accordingly, we have judged that three things must be accomplished in our observations. First, we shall present and examine together, in connected form, each argument of Caecilius and Octavius’s answers and refutations, which are separated from one another. Second, we shall show [Col. 0409C] what in them is sound or invalid, true or false. Third, we shall attempt to explain what has been stated too briefly and compactly, or too obscurely.

In his first argument against the Christians, Caecilius contends that they should in no way whatsoever be admitted to this disputation concerning the gods, and should be heard by no one. He thinks that his argument can be derived from two conditions of those same Christians, one peculiar to them and the other common to them with the rest of humankind. For he says that they were gathered from the lowest dregs of the populace, wholly ignorant of the liberal arts, illiterate, altogether unlearned, and mere simpletons, and therefore entirely unworthy to be heard when they dispute and decide “concerning the highest principle of things and its majesty,” that is, concerning whether God exists and who and what sort of being he is. [Col. 0409D] What is more, it is not merely unworthy but even, as he himself says, “a matter for indignation” that men of this kind have arrived at such audacity that they should determine something certain concerning God, about whom the most learned philosophers had thus far dared to determine nothing. Do you wish Caecilius’s own words to be presented to you? Here they are: “All should be indignant and deeply grieved that certain persons should dare—even persons so ignorant of learning, strangers to literature, and inexperienced even in the meaner arts—to decide anything certain concerning the highest principle of things and its majesty, concerning which philosophy itself, among very many schools, has continued to deliberate through all the centuries down to the present.” Again, he presses the charge that Christians were “men of a deplorable, unlawful, and desperate faction, who, gathering the more ignorant from the lowest dregs [Col. 0410A] and women who, through the weakness of their sex, easily slip, establish a rabble of profane conspiracy”; and finally, he says, “a furtive, light-shunning nation, mute in public and chattering in corners.”

Nor, moreover, was Caecilius alone in reproaching Christians with this condition—the lowest of all mankind—of being last in rusticity and ignorance. For Celsus too, himself a pagan, inveighs against them in Origen as though they were the vilest and lowest of all and dared to teach anything only before children, petty women, and fools:

> Ὁρῶμεν δὴ ποῦ καὶ τοὺς ἐν ταῖς ἀγοραῖς ἐπιῤῥητότατα ἐπιδεικνυμένους, καὶ ἀγείροντας, εἰς μὲν φρονίμων ἀνδρῶν σύλλογον οὐκ ἄν ποτε παρελθόντας, οὐδ` ἐν τούτοις τὰ ἑαυτῶν κατατολμήσαντας ἐπιδεικνύειν· ἔνθα δ` ἂν ὁρῶσι μειράκια [Col. 0410B] καὶ οἰκοτρίβων ὄχλον, καὶ ἀνοήτων ἀνθρώπων ὅμιλον ἐνταῦθα ὡθουμένοὺς τε, καὶ καλλωπιζομένους.

“We certainly see those itinerant mountebanks displaying their much-vaunted wares in the marketplaces and gathering crowds—not before assemblies of prudent men, for they would never dare to enter them or venture to display their teachings among them; but wherever they notice a crowd of youths, household drudges, and foolish people, there they thrust themselves in and make a show of themselves.”

And a little later:

> Ὁρῶμεν καὶ κατὰ τὰς ἰδίας οἰκιας ἐριουργοὺς, καὶ σκυτοτόμους, καὶ κναφεῖς, καὶ τοὺς ἀπαιδευτοτάτοὺς τε καὶ ἀγροικοτάτους, ἐναντίον μὲν τῶν πρεσβυτέρων, καὶ φρονιμωτέρων δεσποτῶν οὐδὲν φθέγγεσθαι τολμῶντας· ἐπειδὰν δὲ τῶν παίδων ἰδίᾳ λάβωνται, καὶ γυναίκων τινῶν σὺν αὐτοῖς ἂν ἁνοήτων, θαύμασι` ἄττα διεξιόντας, ὼς οὐ χρὴ προσέχειν τῷ πατρὶ καὶ τοῖς διδασκάλοις, σφίσι δὲ πειθεσθαι.

“One may see wool-workers, [Col. 0410C] shoemakers, fullers in their own houses, and every most uneducated and rustic person, daring to say nothing in the presence of the older and more prudent masters of the household. But whenever they get their children alone, together with certain ignorant women, they expound marvelous claims: that parents and teachers should not be heeded, but that they themselves should be believed” (Origen, bk. III, *Against Celsus*, pp. 141, 144, quarto ed.).

For this reason Gregory Nazianzen thus mocks the emperor Julian the Apostate: “These are our tales—that is, the tales of the Galileans, those despised and abject men. This is what we are, we who adore Christ. This is what we are, disciples of fishermen and, as they themselves say, of uneducated men. This is what we are, we who sit with old women and sing psalms. This is what we are, wasted by long fasts and half-dead. [Col. 0410D] This is what we are, we who keep vigils in vain and babble during nocturnal watches, and yet we overthrow you. Where are the grammarians? Where are the counselors? For I am pleased to borrow a triumphal hymn from one of our uneducated people, as indeed you regard them” (Gregory Nazianzen, Orat. 4, *Against Julian*, p. 298 ff.).

Nevertheless, Julian himself, in Cyril of Alexandria, reproaches not so much the Christians of his own age as those of the Church’s first century with these things:

> μηδὲ ἤλπισαν εἰς τοῦτο ἀφίξεσθαί ποτε δυνάμεως ὑμᾶς. ἡγάπων γὰρ εἰ θεραπαίνας ἐξαπατήσουσι καὶ δούλους, καὶ διὰ τούτων τὰς γυναῖκας, ἄνδρὰς τε ὄιους Κορνήλιος καὶ Σέργιος, ὧν εἶς ἐὰν φανῇ τῶν τηνικαῦτα γνωριζομένων ἐπιμνήσθεις, ἐπὶ Τιβεριου γὰρ ἤτοι Κλαυδίου ταῦτα ἑγένετο, περὶ πάντων [Col. 0411A] ὅτι ψεύδομαι νομίζετε.

“Nor did they ever hope,” he says, “that you would attain such power. For they were content if they could deceive maidservants or slaves, and through them wives, and men such as Cornelius and Sergius. If even one of these should appear among the notable men of that age—remember that these things occurred in the time of Tiberius or Claudius—then consider me a liar about them all” (Cyril of Alexandria, bk. VI, *Against Julian*, p. 206). But Caecilius does not hesitate to assert that Christians in his own day were still illiterate and uneducated men of the vilest populace.

Yet it is absurd, he says—or, if you prefer, a matter for indignation—that they should decide something concerning God without hesitation, while very many philosophers are still deliberating about him. Nor should you say that Caecilius gratuitously invented this claim about so many philosophers. For Cicero asserts not only that the opinions of these most learned men, as he calls them, concerning God were various and [Col. 0411B] widely divergent: “But,” he says, “those who declared that gods exist are found in such variety and disagreement that it would be troublesome to enumerate their opinions.” And a little later: “Surely so great a doubt among the most learned men concerning the greatest matter will compel even those who think that they possess some knowledge to entertain doubt” (Cicero, bk. II, *On the Nature of the Gods*, pp. 195, 197).

Octavius—or rather Minucius under Octavius’s name—dissolves this argument of Caecilius and the other pagans by two lines of reasoning and utterly overturns it. First, he grants Caecilius freely, and concedes, that all Christians were poor, illiterate, and unskilled, as Caecilius nevertheless falsely contended. But he demonstrates by multiple arguments that the argument [Col. 0411C] derived from this is utterly feeble and weak. For all human beings whatsoever, of every age, sex, and rank, are naturally capable of sense and reason, and wisdom is implanted in them not by chance but by nature.

Next, the pagan philosophers themselves, as well as other inventors of the arts, had at first been commoners, uneducated, and half-naked. Indeed, says Octavius, our own people, however poor, devised practical wisdom and afterward transmitted instruction to others. Therefore, intellect is neither given by resources and riches nor acquired by study and labor, but is engendered in a human being together with his very mind.

Finally, in debates and controversies what is sought is not so much the authority of the disputant as the weight and truth of his reasons. This indeed, to note it in passing, is asserted by Cicero in these words: “For in disputation it is not so much authority [variant: authorities] that should be sought as [Col. 0411D] the weight of reason” (Cicero, bk. I, *On the Nature of the Gods*, near the beginning, p. 197). Therefore, whoever disputes concerning God and divine matters persuades everyone the more effectively that he is defending the truth insofar as his speech is more unpolished and less adorned with the display of empty eloquence. But it is better to hear Octavius disputing against Caecilius in his own words:

> “Let all know,” he says, “that human beings, without distinction of age, sex, or rank, were created capable and fit for reason and perception, and that they did not obtain wisdom by fortune but possess it implanted by nature. Indeed, even the philosophers themselves, or any other [Col. 0412A] inventors of arts who became renowned in human memory, were regarded as commoners, uneducated, and half-naked before the ingenuity of their minds gained them a celebrated name. But the rich, bound to their possessions, have been accustomed to look up to gold rather than heaven. Our poor people both devised practical wisdom and transmitted learning to others. Hence it is apparent that intellect is not bestowed by wealth or procured by study, but is generated together with the very formation of the mind. There is therefore nothing for indignation or grief if anyone investigates, perceives, and declares something concerning divine matters, since it is not the authority of the disputant but the truth of the disputation itself that is required; and indeed, the more unskilled the speech, the more evident it is, since it is not embellished by the pomp and charm of eloquence, but is sustained, just as it is, by the rule of what is right.”

Cyril of Alexandria argues in precisely the same way [Col. 0412B] against Julian when he makes the same objection:

> “As for the fact that petty women believed, and servants and certain obscure persons were called, he uses this to reproach the Savior’s clemency. But is it not better to hold that a human being, insofar as he is born human, is in no way superior to another human being, but that one rational principle of being extends through all? For all have one entrance into the world and an equal departure, as it is written . . . All human beings indeed have one nature. And that nature is so constituted as to be capable of everything worthy of praise. For it is intelligent and able to distinguish better things from worse. Nothing, therefore, prevents a man of the lowest station from being wise, one deprived of money from cultivating justice, and one of servile [Col. 0412C] condition from appearing fair and honorable . . . For my part, I would say that a poor man, if he has adopted a right and honorable manner of life, is beyond comparison superior to a rich miser, and a wise slave to an insane master . . . Moreover, one may see that those among the Greeks who surpassed others most greatly in the praise of virtue were artisans at first and practiced common and menial trades received from their parents, yet not without admirable wisdom. I pass over many. I come to Socrates himself, the son of Sophroniscus, who was born of a mother who was a midwife and a father who was a sculptor; he became highly skilled in that art and did not consider it a disgrace to himself” (Cyril of Alexandria, bk. VI, *Against Julian*, p. 207).

Because it would be too long to reproduce Cyril’s Greek words here, you may read them in his book, where, after proving the point most clearly both by the authority and [Col. 0412D] the words of Porphyry, copied from the third book of the *History of Philosophers*, he confirms it by the testimony of Timaeus. To these may be added Laertius, who in the *Life of Socrates* cites Plato, who relates that Socrates was born to Sophroniscus, a stonecutter, whose craft he afterward practiced, and to Phaenarete, a midwife (Laertius, bk. II, §18). Valerius Maximus, Suidas, Timon—who will be cited by us in the following article—and others support him (Valerius Maximus, bk. III, ch. 4).

What, moreover, of the fact that Cyril in the same place, still with Porphyry as witness, demonstrates that Plato was first exercised for a long time in wrestling schools and athletic exertions, or, as some say, that he passed from the Isthmian and Pythian games, or other contests, [Col. 0413A] to philosophy (Cyril of Alexandria, bk. VI, *Against Julian*, p. 208)?

He adds, moreover, that Zalmoxis the Thracian had been Pythagoras’s slave, and that Pythagoras instructed many women in philosophical disciplines, just as Plato instructed Marcella. To this may further be added what we have discussed elsewhere concerning women philosophers, painters, and poets (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. II, ch. 18, art. 4, p. 1155; and ch. 24, art. 3, p. 25 ff.).

Finally, if by the designation “our own people” Minucius meant the Hebrews, who transmitted arts and disciplines to the pagans, this has already been proved by us from Justin Martyr and Clement of Alexandria in vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. II, dissertation 2, ch. 7, §2, p. 387. Likewise, bk. III, diss. 2, ch. 1, art. 1, [Col. 0413B] p. 834; ch. 15, art. 1 ff.; and from what was said concerning Thales, ch. 17, art. 1, p. 1121; concerning Pythagoras, ibid., ch. 17, art. 2; concerning Plato, ibid., art. 4; concerning the philosophers’ thefts and plunderings from our sacred Scripture, ch. 16, art. 2; and finally concerning the inventors of the arts and sciences, ibid., ch. 31, art. 1, p. 1187 ff.

Although this answer of Minucius, which utterly overturns Caecilius’s frivolous argument, should have fully satisfied both Caecilius himself and anyone else, however difficult to please, he nevertheless adds a second answer. For he plainly and utterly denies that the Christians of his time were such people as Caecilius so boldly asserted: poor and uneducated men of the lowest station of all. “For we do not,” he says, “necessarily belong [Col. 0413C] to the lowest class merely because we reject your offices and purple robes.” This, certainly, and no more, is what this exceedingly modest man says to deny in very few words the truth of Caecilius’s reckless assertion.

But what Minucius denies with such modesty, Origen, in answering Celsus, openly declares to be a gross insult and an impudent slander (Origen, bk. III, *Against Celsus*, p. 142 ff.). Nor is this surprising. For Tertullian, a little older than Minucius, left this account of himself and all the Christians of his age: “We are but of yesterday, and we have filled everything you possess—cities, islands, fortresses, municipalities, council chambers, the camps themselves, tribes, wards, palace, senate, forum. We leave you only your temples” [Col. 0413D] (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, ch. 37, p. 33). Yet surely among those who filled cities, the forum, camps, wards, the palace, the senate, and so many other places, very many, as we have already said, were neither persons of the lowest station, nor poor, nor uneducated and illiterate.

But why, someone will object, did Minucius pass over these and other matters to be cited by us below in such profound silence? Why, we too ask, did he not at once stop the mouth of Caecilius, who lied so shamelessly, by his own example alone? For a man could not be called one of the dregs of the lowest populace who, as we have already observed from the highly reliable witnesses Lactantius and Jerome, was a distinguished advocate at Rome. We shall therefore not find it difficult to believe that he thought he ought to employ so modest and restrained a response [Col. 0414A] so that by such gentleness and moderation he might more easily entice his opponent to renounce his errors and embrace the Christian religion.

Nothing, moreover, was more foolish than Caecilius’s insistence that Christians were a furtive, light-shunning nation, chattering in corners. For they proclaimed their teachings publicly; but the pagans blushed and were afraid to hear them, lest they should be unable to deny that they were true. More on this below.

Lastly, from what has been said it is still clearly demonstrated that, however various and divergent the opinions of the pagan philosophers concerning God were, however much they doubted concerning him, and even if, as you please, some denied that he existed, Christians—even if poor and [Col. 0414B] illiterate—could nevertheless dispute concerning him, determine something certain, and render a true judgment.

In vain, therefore, does Caecilius exhort them at the end of his speech to cease “probing the regions of heaven and the destinies and secrets of the world. For it is enough to look at what is before one’s feet, especially for the uneducated, uncultivated, and rude.” Cicero’s words show that this was drawn from Democritus, from whose spring Epicurus watered his little gardens with empty and false opinions: “Democritus, as a natural philosopher—a class of men than which none is more arrogant—talks nonsense not without wit: ‘No one looks at what lies before his feet; he scrutinizes the regions of heaven’” (Cicero, bk. II, *On Divination*, p. 282, line 30). For this argument of a proud man, intemperately abusing learning and leisure, has been utterly overturned by what has thus far been said.

## ARTICLE II  
### Examination of the Arguments by Which Caecilius Attempts to Prove That Christians and Others May Not Lawfully Dispute Concerning God

[Col. 0414C]

Caecilius proves by another line of reasoning that Christians should be barred from every disputation concerning God, one that is entirely common to them with the rest of humankind. For he objects that the weakness of human nature is such that no one is permitted to know or investigate what hangs in heaven above us or lies below in the bowels of the earth: “Let human mediocrity,” he says, “keep away from the investigation of divine things, since it is neither given to us to know nor permitted to investigate what hangs in heaven above us . . . or what lies beneath the earth.” For it is enough for each person [Col. 0414D] if, “according to the ancient oracle of the sage, we know ourselves.” He alludes to that most celebrated maxim, γνῶθι σεαυτόν, “Know yourself,” which we have noted elsewhere was ascribed by some to Thales, by others to Chilon, and also to the Pythia (vol. I, *Apparatus*, bk. III, diss. 2, ch. 14, art. 3, p. 1097; and ch. 19, art. 4, p. 1170).

Caecilius adds that if at any time, indulging in mad and foolish labor, we wander beyond the bounds of our lowly condition, this error must not be entangled with the empty and fearsome opinions of Christians. He therefore wishes no disputation concerning God and divine matters to be undertaken by Christians, and holds it sufficient for them to know themselves.

[Col. 0415A] He attempts to establish and confirm this, as it seemed to him, by the great authority and testimony of Socrates, who was declared by a divine oracle to be the wisest of men: “If there is a desire to philosophize,” he says, “let any one of you who is great enough imitate Socrates, the prince of wisdom, if he can. Whenever that man was questioned concerning heavenly matters, his familiar reply was: **WHAT IS ABOVE US IS NO CONCERN OF OURS**. He therefore deservedly obtained from the oracle its testimony to his singular prudence. He himself perceived why the oracle had placed him above all others: not because he had discovered everything, but because he had learned that he knew nothing. Thus the confession of one’s ignorance is the height of prudence.”
