From this single passage of Tertullian, however, we may infer that, if none or few of the morning combatants survived, [Col. 0362A] such things were performed even upon corpses. The former escorted souls; the latter, corpses. Thus, for sport and laughter, the man who played Mercury tested the dead with a red-hot iron rod: this was his cautery for rousing whatever remnants of their souls might perhaps be faint and sluggish. The man who played Pluto also proceeded with a staff, but one fitted with a hammer, so that he could more easily crush [Col. 0362B] anything of a hidden soul which might perhaps rebel in the corpses of those gladiators fresh from combat. There is a witty epigram by Nicarchus on the physician Zopyrus in Book II of the *Anthology*. It enumerates [Col. 0362C] several souls whom that physician sent down to gloomy Tartarus, and says that this is no wonder, since, equipped like Mercury with caduceus and winged sandals, he conducts to the underworld those whom he undertakes to treat:

> It is impious to carry living bodies in the Stygian boat,

Mercury was introduced as first testing with his cautery whether they were truly dead. Aldus has “with a cautery.” HAV.].

We have also seen Jove’s brother escorting gladiators’ corpses with a hammer [Note: [Col. 0362C] **Jove’s brother.** This passage is illustrated by *To the Nations* I.10, where it is worded thus: “Because Dis Pater, Jove’s brother, conducts the funerals of gladiators with a hammer; because Mercury, with little wings upon his bald head and a little flame upon his caduceus, tests with a cautery bodies already lifeless or pretending to be dead.” From this it is clear that the tip of the caduceus was heated and glowing and served this cruelty. It is also clear that *deducere*, “escort,” must be retained against Junius, who prefers *diducere*, “tear apart,” a reading Scaliger too approved. From this [Col. 0362D] one may understand that concluding line of a mime and the gesture of the actor who, pointing to the senate, pronounced the words reported by Suetonius in *Nero*, chapter 39: “Orcus leads your feet.” For although Pluto might perhaps tear apart those corpses by pounding them with his hammer-headed staff, he first took them by the feet and dragged them away. I believe this scene was represented because they imagined that nothing of us except faint shades and images,

> Pallid in wondrous forms,

passed down to Orcus.

Pluto, then, was introduced with a hammer so that he might pound those gleaming and well-fed bodies of gladiators—especially if any portion of the soul had been roused by Mercury’s cautery and some hidden spirit appeared—and, like goldsmiths working metals, draw them out until nothing remained but “the thread of a form,” to speak with Persius, which could readily enter Pluto’s kingdom. Who could continue to investigate every single one of these things? But these are theatrical amusements. > Nor shun the Memphite rites of the heifer of the Nile.

> She makes many women what she herself was to Jove.

That is, as the same poet says, III.635:

> When they attend upon the heifer of Pharos with rattles,

> [Col. 0363D] Excepting those whom she herself bids come?

Observe the gradation of the discourse: from temples to altars, from altars to the chambers of the temple-keepers, and thence to the sacred dress itself and the administration of the rite, while incense offered by ostensibly pure hands burns in the brazier. The story of Clodius is well known: he violated Caesar’s wife during the rites of the Good Goddess. Certainly those caught committing sacrilege always belong to your own people. Christians do not know the temples even by daylight; perhaps they too would plunder them, if they too adored them. What, then, do those worship who do not worship such things?

It is now surely open to understanding that those who do not belong to falsehood [Col. 0363B] are worshipers of truth, and that they no longer err in that wherein, by recognizing that they had erred, they have ceased to err. Grasp this first, and from it imbibe the entire order of our sacred teaching—but only after the false opinions have first been refuted.

## CHAPTER XVI

**ARGUMENT.—Now intending to show what God the Christians worship and how they worship him, he first prepares to overthrow the false opinions concerning that worship: namely, concerning an ass’s [Col. 0364A] head, the cross, the sun, and that fictitious two-formed divinity which bore the inscription ONOKOITIS. He disposes of all these charges with laughter and biting wit, and shows that they apply more closely to the accusers themselves. In this way he clears a path for himself toward the demonstration of our religion.**
