# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN AGAINST HERMOGENES

## Synopsis

[Col. 0195D] This Hermogenes was African by nationality and a painter by profession, entirely distinct from the man of whom the Apostle speaks together with Phygelus or Philetus in 2 Timothy 1. Having at first professed himself a Christian, [Col. 0196D] he bore witness to the cunning and deceitful disposition of his nation when, degenerating, he rushed into pagan philosophy and [Col. 0197A] followed the delusions of the Stoics. While still alive, Hermogenes taught that eternal matter existed. He relied upon this principle: God is good and supremely good, and wills to make things good and supremely good just as much as he knows how—indeed, he both wills and devises nothing that is not good and supremely good. . . . Yet evil things are found to have been made by him, certainly not by his choice and will; therefore they must undoubtedly have been made from matter, owing to the defect of some thing. Tertullian replied that Hermogenes introduces two Gods by introducing matter as God’s equal. God, however, must be one; but he cannot be unique if anything is made equal to him, and matter will be made equal to God when it is regarded as eternal. Likewise, God is not almighty if he needed matter, while matter supplied itself to a needy God—one inferior, powerless, and insufficiently capable of making from nothing whatever he wished. Furthermore, if matter is evil and eternal, evil is assuredly unavoidable and necessary; if matter can be changed into good, it is therefore not eternal. Moreover, God is here the author of evil: although he ought to have brought forth from matter either all things or only good things, he also brought forth evil things—either certainly willing evil things to exist, if he could have caused them not to exist, or else unwilling to make all things good, if he willed this but did not do it. LUMP.

## CHAPTER I

For the sake of brevity, we are accustomed to enter a preliminary objection against heretics on the ground of their lateness. For insofar as the rule of truth came first—a rule that also foretold future heresies [Note: [Col. 0197C] “Renounced,” Semler.]—so every later doctrine will be prejudged as heresy, since such doctrines are the things foretold as future by the more ancient rule of truth. He appears to know Christ the Lord differently, not as a different Christ [Note: [Col. 0197C] “He appears to know Christ the Lord, not as another, but otherwise; yet he makes him another,” Vatican codex. “He appears to know Christ the Lord, not as another, differently; yet another,” François, Junius.]; nevertheless, by knowing him differently he makes him another. Indeed, he takes away everything that constitutes God by refusing to admit that he made all things from nothing. For, turning from Christians to philosophers, from the Church to the Academy and the Stoa, he took from the Stoics the doctrine that matter must be placed alongside the Lord: matter that had itself always existed, neither born nor made, having absolutely [Col. 0198B] neither beginning nor end, and from which the Lord afterward made all things.

## CHAPTER II

This first shadow, entirely without light, that most wretched painter colored with the following arguments. He laid down beforehand that the Lord made all things either [Note: [Col. 0198C] Junius, incorrectly, reads “he says.”] from himself, or from nothing, or from something; so that, after showing that he could have made them neither from himself nor from nothing [Note: [Col. 0198D] “Nor from nothing,” etc. Pagan wisdom is utter folly; for Hermogenes had drawn from the Stoic school the doctrine that matter had existed eternally and that God therefore did not make all things from nothing. You will not marvel at this in a pagan philosopher, for, as is stated below, philosophers are the patriarchs of heretics; there he speaks of those who obstinately adhere to the tenets of the pagans. LE PR.], he might establish the remaining alternative: that God made them from something, and that this “something” was matter. He denies that God could have made anything from himself, because whatever the Lord made from himself would have been parts of himself. Yet the Lord, as indivisible, unchangeable, and always the same, could not be divided into parts. Moreover, if he had made anything from himself, that thing would have been something of himself. But both that which was made and that which made it [Col. 0198C] would have to be regarded as imperfect, because the former would be made from only a part, and the latter would make it with only a part. Or, if the whole made the whole, [Col. 0199A] he would have had to be at once whole and not whole: for he would have to be whole in order to make himself, and not whole in order to be made from himself. [Note: [Col. 0199C] “That, moreover,” Junius.] This, moreover, is exceedingly difficult. For if he existed, he would not be made, since he already existed; but if he did not exist, he would not make, since he would be nothing. He who always exists is not made but exists for ever and ever. Therefore he did not make from himself, since his constitution was not such [Note: [Col. 0199C] “Would be made,” Semler; Oberthür considers this reading probable, following Junius.] that he could make from himself.

Hermogenes similarly contends that God could not make from nothing, defining the Lord as good and supremely good, one who wills to make things good and supremely good just as much as he is good; indeed, he both wills and makes nothing that is not good and supremely good. Accordingly, all things ought to be good, and everything made by him ought to be good and supremely good in accordance with his own nature. Yet evil things are also found to have been made by him—certainly not by his choice [Col. 0199B] or will, because, if they were made by his choice and will, he would make nothing inconsistent with or unworthy of himself. What he did not make by his own choice must therefore be understood to have been made through the defect of some other thing, undoubtedly from matter.

## CHAPTER III

He adds another argument: God has always been God [Note: [Col. 0199C] “God has always been God and likewise has always been Lord; he was never not Lord,” Junius.] and also Lord; he was never not God. Yet he could in no way always have been regarded as Lord, just as he was always God, unless there had always existed in the past something of which he was always regarded as Lord. Matter, therefore, always existed for God to be its Lord.

I shall hasten at once to destroy this conjecture of his, which I have thought fit thus far to set out for the sake of those who do not understand, so that they may know that his other arguments can likewise both be understood and refuted. We say that the name “God” has always existed with and [Col. 0199C] within God himself, but not the name “Lord”; for the condition of each name is different. “God” [Note: [Col. 0199C] “But God,” Semler.] is the name of his substance, that is, of the divinity; “Lord,” however, is the name not of substance but of power. His substance has always existed together with its name, which is “God.” “Lord” came afterward, clearly as the designation of a thing that had been added [Note: [Col. 0199C] Junius correctly reads “with the designation of a thing that had occurred,” according to Latinus.]. For from the time when things [Col. 0200A] over which the Lord’s power could operate began to exist, from that time he both became and was called Lord through the accession of power. For God is also Father, and God is Judge; yet he is not therefore always Father and Judge simply because he is always God. For he could not be Father before the Son, nor Judge before an offense. There was, however, a time [Note: [Col. 0199D] “There was a time when,” etc. Everyone will readily recognize that an error must be guarded against in this passage, namely Arianism. For their expression ἦν ὅτε οὐκ ἦν, “there was a time when he was not,” is well known, and these words convey precisely the same meaning. LE PR.] when neither [Note: [Col. 0199C] “And,” Semler, Oberthür.] offense nor Son existed—the former to make the Lord [Note: [Col. 0199C] “God,” Latinus and others.] a Judge, and the latter to make the Lord a Father. So too he was not Lord before the things [Note: [Col. 0199D] “Previously,” Semler.] of which he would become Lord; he was only destined to be Lord at some future time: just as Father through the Son, and Judge through offense, so also Lord through the things he would make to serve him.

Do I seem to you to be engaging in clever argument, Hermogenes [Note: [Col. 0200C] “Do I seem to you to be quibbling, Hermogenes? Scripture vigorously—” or so Junius.]? Scripture vigorously supports us, distinguishing the two names for him and showing each at its proper time. For it immediately names him “God” [Note: [Col. 0200C] “Lord,” Semler.], which he always was: [Col. 0200B] “In the beginning God made heaven and earth.” Thereafter, for as long as he was making the things whose Lord he was to become, Scripture employs “God” alone: “And God said,” “and God made,” “and God saw”; nowhere yet does it say “Lord.” But when he had completed all things, and most particularly man himself, who was properly to understand the Lord [Note: [Col. 0200C] “The Lord, and was to understand,” Semler.], he is also designated “Lord” [Note: [Col. 0200C] “Was to be designated,” Semler, Oberthür.]. Then Scripture also added the name “Lord”: “And the Lord God took [Note: [Col. 0199D] “And God took.” What the author observes is indeed true: that God is not called “Lord” until he has completed all things, and especially man himself. Yet even before the Scripture cited here, immediately at the beginning of chapter 2, there occur: “For the Lord God had not caused it to rain,” and “the Lord God planted a paradise”; only then comes, “And the Lord God took the man.” PAM.] the man whom he had formed.” And “the Lord God commanded” [Note: [Col. 0200C] “Commanded,” Semler.]. From that point onward he is “Lord,” whereas previously he was only “God,” from the time when he possessed something whose Lord he was. For he was God to himself; but he was then God to created things when he was also their Lord.

Therefore, insofar as Hermogenes thinks that matter always existed because the Lord always existed, it will instead be established that nothing existed, because it is established that he was not always Lord. I too shall add something for the sake of those who do not [Col. 0200C] understand, of whom Hermogenes is [Note: [Col. 0199D] “Of whom Hermogenes is the finishing line.” In this very work he says that the finishing line is the end of everything. He means that Hermogenes’ foolishness is the end of all foolishness—that is, the highest foolishness, beyond which none exists. RIG.—“Of whom Hermogenes is the finishing line.” In the courses where races were publicly held in ancient times, the beginning and end were marked with lines. Hence whatever is last came proverbially to be called “the finishing line.” Therefore, when Tertullian here calls Hermogenes the finishing line of those who do not understand, take him to mean “the most ignorant of all.” Horace closes his letter to Quintius thus:

> Death is the final boundary-line of things.

At the same time he makes a tacit allusion to Hermogenes’ art, since as a painter he was accustomed to draw lines with a brush. Thus below, speaking of matter, he says: “If it is in a place, it is therefore within a place; if within a place, it is therefore bounded by the place within which it is; if it is bounded, it has a finishing line, which—as far as a painter properly understands—it recognizes as the end of everything whose finishing line it is,” etc. In the book *Against Praxeas*, he calls God the finishing line of the universe. RHEN.] the finishing line; indeed, I shall turn his own *epinoēmata*—afterthoughts [Note: [Col. 0200D] *Epinoēmata*. We have emended the text thus from Ursinus’ book. RIG.]—against him [Note: [Col. 0200C] “I shall turn this against him from his repentance,” Semler, [Col. 0200D] Oberthür.]. For since he denies that matter was born or made, I likewise find that the title “Lord” did not apply to God in relation to matter. Matter must necessarily have been free, since, by having no origin, it had no author; being what it was, it served no one [Col. 0201A] [Note: [Col. 0201C] “It had no thing which it was, serving no one,” Rhenanus, François, Paris; “it had no author of what it was,” Wouwerius, Venice; “it had no thing which it was, and therefore served no one,” Vatican codex; “it had no author of what it was and therefore served no one,” Junius.]. Accordingly, from the moment God exercised his power upon it by making things from matter, from that moment matter submitted to God as Lord; this demonstrates that he had not been Lord for as long as matter existed [Note: [Col. 0201C] “This,” Junius, Latinus; “existed,” Rigault, Venice, more correctly.].

From this point, then, I shall begin to reconsider the subject of matter: Hermogenes equates it with God as likewise unborn, likewise unmade, likewise eternal, set forth without beginning and without end [Note: [Col. 0201C] “Set forth,” Junius and the rest.]. For what other mark belongs to God than eternity? What other condition belongs to eternity than always to have existed and always to exist in the future, by virtue of having neither beginning nor end? If this belongs properly to God, then even if it is ascribed to another, it will no longer be proper to God but common to him and to that to which it is also ascribed. For though there are those who are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth, in name, yet there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things [Col. 0201B] (1 Cor. 8:5). All the more, among us, whatever belongs properly to God ought to belong to God alone; and, as I have said, it would no longer be proper to him if it belonged to another. But if God is one, what belongs to him must necessarily be unique, so that it belongs to one alone [Note: [Col. 0201D] “For, as I have said, it would no longer be proper to him if it belonged to another; but if God is one, it must necessarily be unique, so that it belongs to one alone,” Junius; “but if God, unique,” codex of Wouwerius.]. Or what will be unique and singular unless nothing is equal to it? What will be supreme unless it is above all things, before all things, and the source from which all things come? [Note: [Col. 0201D] “God alone is God by possessing these things.” God alone is master of these things; he alone possesses them. RIG.] God is God by possessing these things alone, and by possessing them alone he is one [Note: [Col. 0201D] “God alone, by possessing these things, is one,” Semler, Oberthür.]. If another possesses them, there will then be as many gods as there are possessors of divine attributes.

Thus Hermogenes introduces two gods. He introduces matter as equal to God [Note: [Col. 0202C] “He introduces matter when—,” Junius.]. God, however, must be one, because God is that which is supreme; but the supreme will not be supreme unless it is unique, and it cannot be unique if anything is made equal to it. Matter, however, will be made equal to God when [Col. 0201C] it is regarded as eternal [Note: [Col. 0202C] Junius conjectures “when it will be regarded as eternal,” perhaps for “when.”].

## CHAPTER V

“But God is God, and matter is matter”—as though diversity of names prevented comparison when identity of condition is claimed. Let their natures be different and their forms not the same, provided that [Note: [Col. 0202C] “Provided the status of its name,” Semler.] the principle of their condition is one. Is God unborn? Is matter not also unborn? Is God eternal? Is matter not also eternal? Both are without beginning, [Col. 0202A] both without end, both are also authors of the universe—both he who made it and that from which he made it. For matter too cannot but be reckoned an author of all things, since the universe is constituted from it.

How will he answer [Note: [Col. 0202C] The question mark is absent from the editions of Rhenanus and Semler, and more correctly.]? That matter is not immediately comparable to God if it possesses something belonging to God, because, since it does not possess the whole, it does not attain to a complete comparison? What more has he left to God, so that he may not appear to have given everything belonging to God to matter? “Or,” he says, “although matter is thus constituted, God’s authority [Note: [Col. 0202C] Rhenanus conjectured “his principle remains secure”; others, according to Junius, who approves it: “God’s authority and substance remain secure, by which he is regarded as the sole and first author and the Lord of all.”] and substance remain secure, inasmuch as he is regarded as the sole and first author and as the Lord of all.” But truth requires us so to defend one God that whatever belongs to him belongs to him alone. For it will belong to him if it belongs to him alone; and for this reason another god cannot be admitted, since no one is permitted to possess anything belonging to God.

“Then,” you say, “neither do we [Col. 0202B] possess anything belonging to God.” On the contrary, we possess it and shall possess it, but from him, not from ourselves. For we too shall be gods [Note: [Col. 0202D] Others read “gods” (*dii*).], if we have deserved to be those of whom he declared: “I said, You are gods”; and, “God stood in the assembly of the gods” (Ps. 81:1). But this is by his grace, not from our own property, because he alone is the one who makes gods. Matter [Note: [Col. 0202D] “Matter,” Semler.] makes its own that which it possesses in common with God. Or, if it received from God that which belongs to God—I mean the order of eternity—it can be believed both to possess something in common with God and yet not to be a god. But what sort of position is it when Hermogenes confesses that matter possesses something in common with God, yet wishes that which he does not deny matter possesses to belong to God alone?

## CHAPTER VI

He says that God’s position remains secure [Note: [Col. 0201D] “He says that God’s position remains secure, so that he is both alone,” etc. Tertullian reveals a self-contradictory or apparently contradictory sense—ἐναντιοφανές—in Hermogenes, who affirms that God is alone and first, although he does not shrink from saying that matter is coeval and coeternal with him. This is impious and drawn from the storehouse of pagan philosophy. Laertius taught me that Plato admitted two principles of things, God and matter. I marvel at this in him, though in other matters it does not seem unusual to me. LE PR.], so that he is alone, first, the author of all, the Lord of all, and comparable to no one—attributes that he immediately ascribes to matter as well [Col. 0202C] [Note: [Col. 0202D] “Comparable through those things which he is not. He ascribes them to matter also,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.]. “I indeed am God” [Note: [Col. 0202D] “He indeed is God,” in the same editions.], God will testify; and he has sometimes sworn by himself that there is no other like him (Deut. 32:39–40). But Hermogenes will make him a liar, for matter too will be like God: unmade, unborn, having neither beginning nor end.

God will say, “I am first” (Isa. 41, 44, 48) [Note: [Col. 0202D] “God will say: I am first.” This occurs three times in Isaiah, chapters 41, 44, and 48, in these words: “I am first and last.” But the statement “I stretched out heaven alone,” according to the Septuagint, is found in Isaiah 44, where the edition translated from the Hebrew has “stretching out,” preceded by “I, the Lord, completing” or “making all things.” The author also alludes to this when he makes God the author of all. PAM.]. But how is he first when matter is coeval with him? Among those who are coeval and contemporary there is no order—or else [Col. 0203A] matter too is first.

[Note: [Col. 0203D] “‘I stretched out,’ he says, ‘heaven alone.’” Thus Isaiah 44: Ἐξέτεινα τὸν οὐρανὸν μόνος, “I stretched out heaven alone.” And Psalm 104: ὁ ἐκτείνων τὸν οὐρανὸν ὡσεὶ δέρριν, “who stretches out heaven like a hide.” These words are also cited by pagan writers. Thus Trypho to Critias: Εἰ οὐρανὸν ὡς δέρριν ἐξήπλωσε, γῆν δὲ ἐφ’ ὕδατος ἔπηξεν, ἀστέρας ἐμόρφωσεν, “If he spread out heaven like a hide, fixed the earth upon water, and fashioned the stars.”]

“I stretched out,” he says, “heaven alone” (Isa. 44). But he did not do so alone, since he stretched it out together with that from which he stretched it. When Hermogenes asserts that matter existed without prejudice to God’s condition [Note: [Col. 0203D] “That God existed without prejudice to matter’s condition,” Rhenanus, Semler; the codex of Wouwerius has “that God existed without prejudice to matter’s condition,” with Junius’ approval.], see whether we may not reply to him [Note: [Col. 0203D] “See whether he is not mocked by us,” Rhenanus, Semler, Oberthür.] that God likewise existed without prejudice to matter’s condition, although the condition of both was common. It will therefore remain secure for matter too that it existed, though with God, just as God too was alone, though with matter. Matter too was first with God, just as God was first with it. Matter too was incomparable with God, just as God was incomparable with it; and it was an author with God and a mistress with God. Thus, by allowing matter to possess something but supposedly not everything, Hermogenes has left God nothing that he has not also conferred upon matter, so that matter is not compared with God, but God rather with matter.

Indeed, since the attributes [Col. 0203B] that we claim as proper to God [Note: [Col. 0203D] “Since the things proper to God,” in the same editions.]—always to have existed, to be without beginning and without end, to have been first and alone, and to be the author of all—also belong to matter, I ask: what different and alien attribute, unlike God’s and thus private to itself, did matter possess, by which it is not compared with God? Since all God’s proper attributes are enumerated in it, these sufficiently prejudge the rest of the comparison.

## CHAPTER VII

If Hermogenes contends that matter is lesser and lower than God and therefore incomparable with him as the greater and higher, I enter the preliminary objection that whatever is eternal and unborn admits no diminution or humiliation. For this is precisely what makes God as great as he is: inferior and subordinate to none, indeed greater and higher than all. For just as other things, which are born or [Col. 0203C] come to an end and therefore are not eternal, once exposed to an end and also admitting a beginning, admit what God does not—by which I mean diminution for the present and subjection—because they were born and made; so God does not admit them precisely because he was neither born at all nor made. But the condition of matter [Note: [Col. 0203D] “And of matter,” Rhenanus, etc.] is of this same kind.

Therefore, since there are two eternal things, unborn and unmade—God and matter [Note: [Col. 0203D] “God and matter” in the dative, Rhenanus and the same other editors, as usual.]—their condition is common for the same reason: they equally possess that which admits neither diminution nor subjection, namely eternity. We say that neither is lesser or greater than the other, neither lower nor higher than the other, but that both stand equally great, equally exalted, [Col. 0204A] and equally possessed of the solid and perfect blessedness that is reckoned as eternity.

For we shall not approach the opinions of the pagans, who, if ever they are compelled to confess God, nevertheless wish there to be other gods beneath him. Divinity, however, admits no degree, since it is unique. If it is also present in matter, because matter is likewise unborn, unmade, and eternal, divinity will be present in both, since nowhere can it be less than itself. How, then, will Hermogenes dare to distinguish matter from God and thus subject it to God—eternal to eternal, unborn to unborn, author to author? Matter dares to say: “I too am first”; “I am before all things”; “I am that from which all things come. We were equals; we existed together; both of us are without beginning and without end; both without author and without God. What God subjects me [Note: [Col. 0204D] Perhaps “to God,” Junius.] to one who is my contemporary and coeternal? If he is called ‘God,’ I too have my own name. Either I [Col. 0204B] am God, or he is matter [Note: [Col. 0204D] “And he is matter,” Junius.], because we are both what each of us is.” Do you therefore think that you have not compared matter with God merely because you supposedly subject it to him?

## CHAPTER VIII

On the contrary, Hermogenes even places matter above God and subjects God rather to matter when he maintains that God made all things from matter. For if God employed it for the works of the world, matter is now found to be superior, since it supplied him with the means of working; and God appears subject to matter, whose substance he needed. For everyone needs that [Note: [Col. 0203D] “That from whose he uses”: an elliptical expression, with “things” or something similar understood. He also used this form of speech in earlier books. In *Against Marcion*, Book V: “If it is understood in the Creator’s.” In the same book: “And it can be opposed from our party’s.” Again, shortly afterward there follows: “He is not lesser than that from whose he uses.” It is an imitation of the Greeks. RHEN.] whose resources he uses; everyone is subjected to that which he needs in order to be able to use it. Likewise, everyone who uses what belongs to another is lesser than the one whose resources he uses; and everyone who permits another to use what belongs to himself is, in this respect, superior to the one whom he permits to use it.

Accordingly, matter itself had no need of God, but [Col. 0204C] offered itself to the needy God [Note: [Col. 0204D] “But it had needed to offer itself to God,” Latinus; “but it offered itself to the needy God,” Junius. “But it offered itself to the needy God, being rich”—codex of Wouwerius; perhaps “but it, being rich, offered itself to the needy God.”], rich, wealthy, and generous as it was, to one who was, I suppose, inferior, powerless, and insufficiently capable of making from nothing whatever he wished. It truly conferred a great benefit upon God, enabling him today to possess that through which he might be known as God and called almighty—except that he is now not almighty, if he also lacks this power: to bring forth all things from nothing.

Matter certainly also rendered some service to itself, enabling it to be recognized together with God as coequal with God, indeed even as his helper. Yet only Hermogenes recognized it, along with [Note: [Col. 0204D] “The philosophers, patriarchs of the heretics.” The author treated this expressly above in *On the Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 7. PAM.—“Patriarchs of the heretics.” In his letter to Ctesiphon, Saint Jerome cites this passage of Tertullian. The same writer, while explaining the words in the prophet Nahum, “it leapt forth like a locust,” says: “For all their doctrines”—he is speaking of heretics—“since they are cold and cannot fly, find a seat and resting place among the thorn-brakes of Aristotle and Chrysippus.” RHEN.] the philosophers, the patriarchs of the heretics. For until now it escaped the notice of the prophets and Apostles—and, I suppose, of Christ as well [Note: [Col. 0204D] “I suppose, of Christ as well,” François, Paris, Rigault; but Junius reads “the prophets, Apostles, and Christ.”].

## CHAPTER IX

[Col. 0205A]

Hermogenes cannot say that God used matter as its Lord for the works of the world, since a Lord [Note: [Col. 0205C] “God,” Semler, Oberthür.] could not exist over a substance coequal with himself. Perhaps, then, he used it by sufferance. [Note: [Col. 0205D] “And therefore by sufferance, not by ownership.” To take another’s property by sufferance, ownership, or force are expressions used by jurists. Not only this passage shows that Tertullian possessed very extensive knowledge of civil law. RHEN.] He used it by sufferance, therefore, and not by ownership. Since it was evil [Note: [Col. 0205C] “Was not,” Semler, Oberthür. “Not” is absent from François, Paris, and Rigault, more correctly.], he nevertheless endured using evil matter, evidently under the constraint of his own limited power, whereby he was incapable of employing nothing as his material. He did not use it by virtue of a power that, had he possessed it as God over matter—which he knew to be evil—would have caused him first to convert it into good [Note: [Col. 0205C] “In the good,” Semler, Oberthür.] as Lord and as good, so that he might then use good matter, not evil. But because he was indeed good, yet was not Lord, he used matter as he found it. He thus revealed that his necessity yielded to the condition of matter, which he would have corrected if he had been its Lord.

This is how Hermogenes must be answered [Col. 0205B] when he argues from dominion that God used matter, though it was a thing not his own, because it had not been made by him. Evil, therefore, now comes from him who, if not the author of evil because he did not make it, was certainly its permitter because he ruled it. But if matter did not belong to him either, so that its evil was not God’s evil [Note: [Col. 0205C] “By which the evil is not God’s,” Semler, Oberthür.], then he used another’s property: either he used it by sufferance, because he needed it, or else wrongfully, because he was stronger than it. For in these three ways the property of others is taken: by right, by favor, or by assault—that is, by ownership, sufferance, or force. Since ownership is unavailable [Note: [Col. 0205C] Rhenanus separates the text here; Semler does not approve.], let Hermogenes choose which befits God: that he made all things from matter [Note: [Col. 0205D] “By sufferance or by force.” Something is said to be done by sufferance when it is extorted by entreaties; for sometimes that which would otherwise have been refused is granted to importunity and troublesome persistence. LE PR.] by sufferance or by force [Note: [Col. 0205C] “Had made,” François.]. Would God, then, not have judged it better to make absolutely nothing than to make by sufferance or force—and that from evil?
