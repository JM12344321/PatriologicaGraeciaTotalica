## CHAPTER VIII

Let the heretic now cease borrowing poison from the Jew, as an asp does, so they say, from a viper; [Note: [Col. 0331D] *To borrow* (*mutuare*), others.] let him henceforth vomit out the venom of his own ingenuity by maintaining that Christ was a phantom.

Except that even this doctrine will have other authors: in a certain sense premature and abortive Marcionites, [Note: [Col. 0331D] “Premature and abortive,” etc. Other heretics, namely, preceded the Marcionites; they were, as it were, the abortive offspring and premature fruits of the heresy. In the book *On the Pallium*, the city of Utica is similarly called “prematurely Roman,” since it had adopted Roman dress before its time, before it had obtained the legal status of a colony. LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 0331D] *Abortive* (*aborsivos*), others.] whom the apostle John (1 John 4:2) pronounced antichrists because they denied that Christ had come in the flesh. Yet they did not do so in order to establish the claim of another god—for they would have been censured on that account [Note: [Col. 0332D] *Just* (*justo*), Par.] as well—but because they had rashly deemed it incredible that God should be flesh.

All the more, therefore, did Marcion the antichrist seize upon this presumption for himself, being, of course, better suited to rejecting Christ’s bodily substance, since he had represented Christ’s own god as neither the creator of the flesh [Col. 0331D] nor its raiser from the dead: a god supremely good, naturally, and in this respect [Note: [Col. 0332D] *And* added by Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] utterly unlike the falsehoods and deceptions of the Creator. Therefore, lest his Christ lie or deceive, [Col. 0332A] and perhaps thereby be assigned to the Creator, he was not what he appeared to be, and misrepresented what he was: flesh, yet not flesh; man, yet not man. By the same reasoning Christ was God, yet not God. Why, indeed, should he not have borne a phantom of God as well?

Am I to believe him concerning his inward substance when I have been deceived concerning his outward one? How will he be regarded as truthful in what is hidden when he has been found so deceptive in what is manifest? And how, by mingling the truth of the spirit with the deception of the flesh within his own person, does he unite what the Apostle denies can have fellowship (2 Cor. 6:14): light—that is, truth—and deception—that is, darkness?

Now, once Christ’s flesh is discovered to be a lie, it follows that everything performed through Christ’s flesh was performed as a lie: his encounters, contacts, companionship, and even his miracles. For if, by touching someone or by being touched by someone, he delivered that person [Col. 0332B] from an affliction, what was done bodily cannot be believed to have been done truly without the reality of his body itself. Nothing solid could be accomplished by what is insubstantial, nothing full by what is empty. An apparent condition, an apparent action; an imaginary worker, imaginary works.

Thus even the sufferings of his Christ will not deserve belief, for one who has not truly suffered has suffered nothing. A phantom, however, could not truly suffer. The whole work of God has therefore been overturned. The whole weight and fruit of the Christian name is overturned as well: the death of Christ is denied, though the Apostle insists upon it so emphatically—upon its truth, of course—establishing it as the supreme foundation of the gospel, of our salvation, and of his own preaching: “For I delivered to you first of all,” he says (1 Cor. 15:3–4), “that Christ died for our sins, and that he was buried, [Col. 0332C] and that he rose [Note: [Col. 0332D] *Rose* (*resurrexit*), Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] on the third day.”

Furthermore, if his flesh is denied, how is his death affirmed, when death is the peculiar suffering of flesh as it returns [Note: [Col. 0332D] *Returns* (*revertentis*), Lat.] through death to the earth from which it was taken, according to the law of its creator? But if death is denied by denying the flesh, neither will his resurrection be established. For by the same reasoning by which he did not die, he also did not rise, since he did not possess the substance of flesh, to which both death and resurrection belong (1 Cor. 15:13).

Consequently, if Christ’s resurrection is weakened, our own also is overthrown. For the resurrection for whose sake Christ came will have no force if Christ did not rise. [Note: [Col. 0332D] *Will have force* (*valebit*), Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] For just as those who said that there was no resurrection of the dead are refuted by the Apostle on the basis of Christ’s resurrection, so, if Christ’s resurrection does not stand, the resurrection of the dead [Col. 0332D] is also abolished. Thus [Note: [Col. 0332D] *And* added by Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] our faith is empty, the preaching of the apostles is empty, and they are even found to be false witnesses of God, because they testified that [Col. 0333A] he had raised Christ when he did not raise him; and we are still in our sins, while those who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished—though, to be sure, they may rise again as phantoms, just as Christ did.

## CHAPTER IX

In this question you may suppose that the Creator’s angels should be set against us as an objection, on the ground that they too acted before Abraham and Lot in a phantom—namely, an apparent flesh—and nevertheless truly encountered them, ate, and carried out what they had been commanded to do.

First, you will not be admitted to the examples of the God whom you demolish. For the better and more perfect the god whom you introduce, the less appropriate are the examples of the Creator to him, since unless he is wholly different, he will not be better and more perfect at all.

Next, understand that not even this will be conceded to you: that [Col. 0333B] the angels’ flesh was apparent rather than of true and solid human substance. For if it was not difficult for him to display true sensations and actions in apparent flesh, it was much easier for him to give a true substance of flesh to true sensations and actions, especially because he is its own creator and craftsman.

Your god, however, since he produced no flesh whatsoever, may perhaps deservedly have introduced a phantom of that whose reality he had been unable to produce. My God, however, who fashioned flesh from clay in this condition—not yet from marital seed, and yet flesh nonetheless—could equally have constructed flesh for angels from any material whatever, since he also built the world out of nothing into so many bodies of such diverse kinds, and did so by his word.

And if your god at some time promises the true substance of angels to human beings—[Col. 0333C] “For they will be,” he says (Luke 20:36), “like the angels”—why should my God not also have fitted the angels with the true substance of human beings, taken from whatever source? [Note: [Col. 0333D] The second *from whatever source* omitted by Wouw., Pam., Par., Fran.] Since you will not answer me where the angelic substance in your system is taken from, it is enough for me to affirm what is fitting for God: namely, the reality of the thing that he presented to three witnesses, the senses—sight, touch, and hearing. It is more difficult for God to lie than to produce the reality of flesh from whatever source, [Note: [Col. 0333D] *From whatever source* only once in Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] even though that flesh was not born.

Moreover, to other heretics who maintain [Note: [Col. 0333D] “Moreover, to other heretics who maintain.” He is treating of Apelles, who, as is indicated in *On the Flesh of Christ*, ch. 6, appears to have asserted that not only Christ’s flesh but also that of the angels was made from the stars. PAM.] that the flesh of the angels, if it had truly been human, would have had to be born from flesh, we answer on a definite principle that it was both truly human and unborn: truly human because of the truthfulness of God, who is a stranger to falsehood and deception, [Col. 0334A] and because the angels could not have been treated in a human manner by human beings unless they possessed human substance; yet unborn because Christ alone was to be born into flesh from flesh, so that by his birth [Note: [Col. 0333D] *His* omitted by Par.] he might reform our birth, and thus also dissolve our death by his own death, rising again in the flesh in which he was born, so that in it he might also be capable of dying.

For this reason he himself, together with the angels, then appeared before Abraham in the reality of flesh, but flesh not yet born, because it was not yet destined to die; yet [Note: [Col. 0333D] *And* omitted by Par.] it was already learning to dwell among human beings. Still more, the angels, who were not appointed to die for us, ought not to have acquired their brief tenure of flesh through birth, since they were not going to put it off through death. Rather, it was taken from whatever source [Note: [Col. 0334D] *From whatever source* only once in Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] and relinquished in some manner altogether; nevertheless, they did not falsely represent it.

[Col. 0334B] If the Creator “makes his angels spirits and his ministers a blazing fire” (Ps. 103:4)—as truly spirits as fire—the same Creator also truly made them flesh. Let us now therefore recall, and declare to the heretics, that the promise of one day transforming human beings into angels belongs to the God who once formed angels into human beings.

## CHAPTER X

Therefore, since you have not been admitted to share in the Creator’s examples, because they are foreign to your god and have their own reasons, I should like you in turn to disclose the design of your god [Note: [Col. 0333D] “I should like you in turn to disclose the design of your god.” The sense itself compelled me to alter the reading here and substitute *of your god* (*tui*), so as to yield “his own Christ,” etc.; the old copies agree in their error. RHEN.] by which he presented his Christ without the reality of flesh.

If he despised flesh as earthly and, as you say, stuffed with filth, [Note: [Col. 0334D] *Stuffed* (*infarsam*), Jun.] why did he not similarly despise its likeness? For no image of a thing deserving contempt deserves respect: the likeness follows the condition of the thing. But how could he have dwelt [Col. 0334C] among human beings except through an image of human substance? Why, then, did he not rather do so through its reality, so that his dwelling among them might be real, if he had to dwell among them? How much more worthily would necessity have administered faith than a trick! [Note: [Col. 0333D] “Than a trick.” *Stropha* signifies deceit, cunning, and imposture; Greek στροφή (*strophē*). RHEN.]

You establish a sufficiently wretched god by the very fact that he could not present his Christ except in the likeness of an unworthy thing—and a foreign thing at that. For it will be appropriate to use certain unworthy things if [Note: [Col. 0334D] *If* omitted by Pam., Par., Fran.; *just as*, Rhen.] he uses ours, [Note: [Col. 0333D] “For it will sometimes be appropriate to use unworthy things if he uses ours.” So the Pithou manuscript reads. These words, however, seem to be spoken in Marcion’s person, from “You establish a sufficiently wretched god” as far as “seemed to have needed both an unworthy and a foreign thing.” “If God uses our members,” he says, “so that he can dwell among us, there are many things in our body unworthy of God, which it will nevertheless be appropriate for God to use if he wishes to use ours. But even if ours were worthy of God, it is nevertheless unfitting for God to use things belonging to another.” The preceding words, “But how could he have dwelt among human beings except through an image of human substance?” are spoken in Marcion’s person as well. Similar instances occur throughout Septimius’ disputations, and unless they are skillfully distinguished they throw the whole argument into confusion. RHEN.] just as it will be unfitting to use things belonging to another, even if they are worthy. Why did he not come in some other, more worthy substance, and above all in his own, lest he appear to have needed something both unworthy and foreign?

If my Creator encountered man through a bush and fire, and later through a cloud and a fiery mass, [Col. 0335A] and used the bodies of the elements in manifestations of himself, these examples sufficiently demonstrate the divine power: God did not need the provision either of false or even of true flesh.

Moreover, if we look to the precise point, no substance is worthy for God to put on. Whatever he puts on, he himself makes worthy—provided, however, that there is no falsehood. What sort of reasoning, then, is it that he should have regarded the reality of flesh as more disgraceful than a lie about flesh? On the contrary, he honored flesh by fashioning its likeness. How great a thing flesh must now be, when a phantom of it was necessary to the superior god!

## CHAPTER XI

Marcion adopted all these deceptions concerning Christ’s apparent corporeality [Note: [Col. 0335D] “Apparent corporeality in Christ.” Boethius, *On Faith*: “Even today there are those who deny that he bore our corporeality.” RIG.] with this purpose: that Christ’s birth [Col. 0335B] should not also be defended by the testimony of his human substance, and that Christ should not thereby be vindicated for the Creator, since he had been proclaimed as capable of birth and therefore as fleshly.

Here too the man from Pontus is utterly foolish, [Note: [Col. 0335D] *This* (*hoc*), Jun.] as though it would not more readily be believed that flesh in God was unborn than that it was false—especially since the Creator’s angels, who dwelt in true yet unborn flesh, had prepared the way for this belief.

Indeed, that Philumene more successfully persuaded Apelles and the other deserters from Marcion that Christ had carried flesh about with him in very truth, though flesh of no birth, since he had borrowed it from the elements. But if Marcion was afraid that belief in the flesh would also induce belief in Christ’s birth, then without doubt one who appeared to be a man would certainly be believed to have been born. For a certain woman had even cried out (Luke 11:27), “Blessed is the womb that bore you, and the breasts that you sucked.” And [Col. 0335C] how were his mother and brothers said to be standing outside (Luke 8)? We shall examine these passages at their proper time.

Certainly, since he also proclaimed himself the Son of Man, he was confessing that he had been born. For the present I defer all these matters to the examination of the Gospel. Nevertheless, given what I established above—that one who appeared to be a man would necessarily be believed to have been born—Marcion vainly thought that belief in his birth could be erased by the device of imaginary flesh. For what advantage was gained by its not being real, if it was taken as real—both the flesh and the birth?

Or if you say, “Let human opinion see to itself,” you now honor your god with the title of deceiver, if he knew himself to be something other than what he had caused human beings to suppose. At that point you might also have assigned him an apparent birth, [Note: [Col. 0335D] “Assigned him an apparent birth.” As though he had truly been born, although in actual fact he had not. For that is what the word *apparent* means here, just as he had recently spoken of “apparent corporeality in Christ.” LE PR.] lest you also have stumbled into [Col. 0336A] this question. For even poor women sometimes imagine themselves pregnant when they are swollen either by a discharge of blood [Note: [Col. 0335D] “By a discharge of blood.” Thus in *On the Veiling of Virgins*: “Modesty is clothed everywhere, and the menses pay their dues.” RIG.] or by some illness.

And certainly he ought to have run through the entire drama of the phantom, [Note: [Col. 0335D] “He ought to have run through the drama of the phantom.” Thus he says “the drama of the phantom,” just as in *On Heresies* he says “the drama of error,” meaning what they call an act. The sense is: Marcion’s Christ ought to have served the drama completely and performed the story he had undertaken; if he bore apparent flesh, he also ought to have had an apparent birth, for in that way he would have run through the drama of the phantom. RHEN.] so that, after playing the role of that substance itself, he would not have omitted from the performance the origin of the flesh. [Note: [Col. 0335D] *Desultasset*, Seml.; *dealtasset*, Rhen.] Plainly, you refused the lie of a birth, for you produced the flesh itself as real. A real birth, naturally, would be most disgraceful for God.

Come now, make your concluding speech [Note: [Col. 0336D] “Come now, make your concluding speech.” You have this passage reproduced in almost the same words in *On the Flesh of Christ*, not very far from the beginning. RHEN.] against those most holy and venerable works of nature. Rail against the totality of what you are. Destroy the origin of flesh and soul. Call the womb a sewer, though it is the workshop for bringing forth so great a living creature—that is, man. Persecute the unclean and shameful torments of childbirth, and thereafter the filthy, anxious, and ridiculous outcomes of infancy itself. [Note: [Col. 0336D] “Ridiculous outcomes.” I once thought that *slippery* (*lubricos*) should be read, but the author is referring to what he says in the book just cited when speaking of a newborn infant: “he is mocked with caresses”; and shortly afterward, “reared amid mockeries,” etc. RHEN.]

Yet once you have destroyed [Col. 0336B] all these things in order to affirm them unworthy [Note: [Col. 0336D] *Worthy*, Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] of God, birth will not be more unworthy than death, infancy than the cross, nature than punishment, or condemnation than flesh. If Christ truly suffered these things, it was less for him to be born. [Note: [Col. 0336D] *Would have been* (*fuisset*), Seml., Pam.] If he suffered them falsely, as a phantom, he could also have been born falsely.

These are Marcion’s principal arguments by which he creates another Christ. I believe we have sufficiently demonstrated that they do not stand at all, since we show that the reality, rather than the falsehood, of the condition in which he presented his Christ is certainly more fitting for God. If it was real, it was flesh; if it was flesh, it was born. For the things this heresy attacks are confirmed when the things by which it attacks them are destroyed.

Accordingly, if he is held to be fleshly because he was born, and born because he was fleshly—because he was not a phantom—he must be recognized as the very one whom the Creator’s prophets proclaimed would come in the flesh and by [Col. 0336C] birth: namely, the Creator’s Christ.

## CHAPTER XII

Appeal now, as you are accustomed to do, to this comparison with Isaiah’s Christ, contending that it agrees with him in no respect. First, you say, Isaiah’s Christ (Isa. 7:14) was to be called Emmanuel; next, he was to take the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against the king of the Assyrians. But the one who came was neither revealed under such a name nor performed any military exploit.

I, however, will admonish you to examine the connection between the two passages as well. For the interpretation of Emmanuel—“God with us”—is also appended, so that you may consider not only the sound of the name but also [Col. 0337A] its meaning. For the Hebrew sound, which is “Emmanuel,” belongs to its own nation. Its meaning, however, which is “God with us,” is made common to all through translation.

Ask, therefore, whether the expression “God with us”—which is Emmanuel—has been applied to Christ from the time that Christ shone forth. And I think you will not deny it, since you yourself also say that he is called “God-with-us,” that is, Emmanuel.

Or if you are so foolish that, because among your people he is called “God-with-us” rather than “Emmanuel,” you therefore refuse to admit that the one whose proper name was to be Emmanuel has come—as though “Emmanuel” were not also “God with us”—you will find that Hebrew Christians, and indeed even Marcionites, say “Emmanuel” when they wish to say “God-with-us.” Likewise, whatever sound any nation uses to say “God-with-us,” it will pronounce “Emmanuel,” preserving the meaning while dispensing with the sound.

[Col. 0337B] But if Emmanuel is “God-with-us,” and Christ is God with us, who is also in us—“For as many of you as have been baptized into Christ [Note: [Col. 0337C] *Christ* in the dative, Par., Fran.] have put on Christ”—then Christ is as properly indicated by the meaning of the name, which is “God-with-us,” as by the sound of the name, which is “Emmanuel.” And thus it is established that the one proclaimed as Emmanuel has already come, because that which Emmanuel signifies has come: namely, God-with-us.

## CHAPTER XIII

You are likewise led astray by the sound of names when you understand “the might of Damascus,” “the spoils of Samaria,” and “the king of the Assyrians” as though they foretold that the Creator’s Christ would be a warrior, failing to notice what Scripture places first (Isa. 8:4): [Col. 0337C] [Note: [Col. 0337C] *Promises* (*promittat*), Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] “For before he knows [Note: [Col. 0337C] “The child” added by Pam., Rig., Fran., Venet.; it was not in the manuscripts.] how to call father and mother, [Col. 0337C] [Note: [Col. 0337C] “For before the child knows how to call father,” etc. Here too the word *child* has been supplied, since all editions of Isaiah 8 contain it. PAM.] he will take the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against the king of the Assyrians.”

You must first examine the indication of his age and ask whether it could present Christ even as a man, much less as a commander. An infant, of course, was going to summon troops to arms with his wailing and give the signals of war not with a trumpet but with a rattle; he was going to direct the enemy not from horseback, from a chariot, or from a wall, but from the neck or back of his nurse or bearer, [Col. 0338A] and so subdue Damascus and Samaria in place of her breasts.

It may be otherwise among the people of Pontus, where the infants of a barbarian [Note: [Col. 0337C] *Barbarous country* (*barbariae*), Venet., Pam., Rig.] nation burst into battle, first anointed, I suppose, in the sun, [Note: [Col. 0337C] *Throne* (*solium*), Jun.] [Note: [Col. 0337D] “First anointed, I suppose, in the sun.” He alludes to ἡλίωσις (*hēliōsis*), or exposure to the sun, whereby they were warmed after being anointed, so that their limbs might be dried and strengthened. Persius, Satire 4:

“Or if you idle about anointed and fix the sun upon your skin.” RIG.]

then armed with swaddling clothes and paid their stipend in butter—infants who know how to wield a lance before they know how to handle a platter. [Note: [Col. 0337D] “Who know how to wield a lance before they know how to handle a platter.” Since in ch. 1 of Book I *Against Marcion* the verb *lancinatur* occurs in the sense “is pricked,” at first glance *lancinare* seems to have the same meaning here, just as *lanceare* means “to pierce with lances.” But we think *lancire* is better understood here, because “paid their stipend in butter” precedes it, in the sense of “putting their hands to platters” (*lances*). Let the reader choose whichever he wishes. Meanwhile, this conjecture is supported by what follows, “to soldier before living,” and the Author is fond of expressions of this kind. PAM.]

But if nature nowhere permits this—[Note: [Col. 0337D] “If nature nowhere permits this, it follows that the declaration should be regarded as figurative.” He gave precisely this warning in the same words in the book *Against the Jews*. Augustine speaks similarly in Book XI of *The Literal Meaning of Genesis*: “If in the words of God, or of any person taken up into the prophetic office, something is said that cannot be understood literally without absurdity, it must without doubt be taken figuratively for the sake of some signification.” Similar statements occur in Book III of *On Christian Doctrine*, ch. 15. RIG.] to serve as a soldier before beginning to live, or to take the might of Damascus before knowing the names “father” and “mother”—it follows that the declaration should be regarded as figurative.

“But,” he says, “nature does not permit a virgin to give birth either, and yet the prophet is believed.” [Note: [Col. 0338C] *The prophecy*, Fran.] And rightly so. For he prepared belief in the incredible event by stating the reason: it would occur as a sign. “Therefore,” he says (Isa. 7:14), “the Lord will give you a sign: behold, a virgin will conceive in her womb [Col. 0338B] and bear a son.” But a sign from God, unless it were some extraordinary marvel, would not have been worthy of so much notice. [Note: [Col. 0338D] “But a sign from God, unless it were some extraordinary marvel, would not have been worthy of so much notice.” Perhaps the truer reading is “would not have been a sign.” For thus he writes in the book *Against the Jews*: “But a sign from God, unless it were some extraordinary marvel, would not appear to be a sign.” Procopius on Isaiah: τὸ γὰρ σημεῖον, τερατίου τινὸς, καὶ παρηλλαγμένου τῆς τῶν ἀνθρώπων συνηθείας ἐστὶν ἐπίδειξις: “For a sign is evidence of something marvelous and exceeding customary human experience.” RIG. and EDD.] [Note: [Col. 0338C] *Now a sign*, Lat.]

Finally, even the Jews, whenever they dare to lie in order to cast us down, alleging that Scripture says not that a virgin but that a young woman would conceive [Note: [Col. 0338D] “But that a young woman would conceive.” The Jews’ fable or quibble—that the text should read “young woman,” not “virgin”—has now been exploded. See Blessed Jerome, Book I *Against Jovinian*. LE PR.] and give birth, are refuted on this very ground: an everyday occurrence—namely, the pregnancy and delivery of a young woman—could not appear to be any sign.

Therefore a virgin and mother, appointed as a sign, is rightly believed; [Note: [Col. 0338C] *Would be believed*, Par.] but not, in the same manner, an infant warrior. For here no rationale of a sign is involved. Once the sign has been assigned to the new birth, however, a different sequence is then proclaimed after the sign concerning the child: he is to eat honey and butter. This is certainly not given as a sign that he will not consent to wickedness, for this too belongs to infancy; [Note: [Col. 0338C] *To infancy* (*infantiae*), Wouw.] rather, it concerns his taking the might of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria against [Col. 0338C] the king of the Assyrians.

Preserve the measure of his age and seek the meaning of the proclamation. Better still, restore to the Gospel of truth what you later removed from it, and the prophecy is understood precisely as its fulfillment is reported.

For [Note: [Col. 0338C] *But* (*autem*), Seml.] let those eastern Magi remain, bestowing gold and incense upon Christ when he was a newborn infant, and the child has taken the might of Damascus without battle or arms. [Col. 0339A] For apart from the fact that everyone knows that the might of the East—that is, its power and resources—has customarily abounded in gold and perfumes, the Creator [Note: [Col. 0339C] *Of the Creator* (*Creatoris*), Fran.] certainly identifies the might of other nations too with gold, as through Zechariah (Zech. 14:14): “And Judah will stretch out [Note: [Col. 0339C] *Display* (*praetendet*), Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] at Jerusalem and gather all the wealth of the surrounding peoples, gold and silver.”

David also speaks of that gift of gold: “And there will be given to him from the gold of Arabia”; and again: “The kings of Arabia and Saba will bring [Note: [Col. 0339C] *Offer*, Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] gifts to him.” [Note: [Col. 0339D] “They will bring gifts to him.” The Vulgate reads better there, “they will lead forward,” for the Greek is προσάξουσιν (*prosaxousin*). LE PR.]

For the East generally had Magi as kings, [Note: [Col. 0339C] *Kings* absent from Par.] [Note: [Col. 0339D] “For the East generally had Magi as kings.” By “kings” he means the rulers of some city or region, such as the king in Phaedrus’ fable before whom a physician who had formerly been a cobbler was mocked; many such kings likewise occur in the sacred writings. Septimius says that kings of this kind in various cities throughout the East were generally Magi, that is, men skilled in knowledge of the stars and heavenly bodies. Moreover, Porphyry teaches in his book περὶ ἀποχῆς (*On Abstinence*) that *Magi* is a Persian term, signifying τοὺς περὶ τὸ θεῖον σοφοὺς, καὶ τούτου θεράποντας, μέγα καὶ σεβάσμιον γένος, “those who are wise about the divine and serve it, a great and venerable race.” RIG.] and Damascus was formerly reckoned part of Arabia, before it had been transferred [Note: [Col. 0339D] *Was* (*erat*), Rhen., Seml., Oberth.] to Syrophoenicia when the Syrian territories were divided. Christ then took its might by receiving its insignia, namely, gold and perfumes.

The spoils of Samaria, however, were [Col. 0339B] the Magi themselves. After they recognized him, honored him with gifts, and worshiped him on bended knee as God and king, under the testimony of the star that indicated and guided them, they became the spoils of Samaria—that is, of idolatry—by believing in Christ. For he designated idolatry by the name of Samaria, since Samaria had become infamous through the idolatry by which it had then revolted from God under King Jeroboam.

Nor is it anything new for the Creator to use names figuratively, transferring them on the basis of comparable crimes. For he calls the rulers of the Jews “rulers of Sodom,” and the people themselves “the people of Gomorrah.” The same God says elsewhere (Ezek. 16:3), “Your father was an Amorite and your mother a Hittite,” because of their similar impiety, though he had once even called them his own children: “I have begotten children and exalted them.”

Thus too [Col. 0339C] “Egypt” is sometimes understood in his writings as the whole world, under an indictment of superstition and accursedness. Thus also “Babylon,” in our John, is a figure of the city of Rome, [Note: [Col. 0339D] “Thus also Babylon, in our John, is a figure of the city of Rome.” It is a common slander that Rome is called Babylon. For that distinguished man and others understand this of ancient Rome, which can never be sufficiently condemned for its incredible worship of idols. Since the religion of Christ was established, what St. Prosper says in his poem *On the Ungrateful* ought to be said of Rome:

“What she does not possess by arms,  
she holds by religion.” LE PR.]

inasmuch as Rome is similarly great, proud in its dominion, and a conqueror of God’s saints.

By this usage, therefore, he also represented the Magi as despoiled under the designation “Samaritans,” because they had possessed idolatry in common with the Samaritans, as we have said.

As for “against the king of the Assyrians,” understand it as “against Herod,” [Col. 0340A] whom the Magi certainly opposed at that time by not reporting back to him concerning Christ, whom he was seeking to destroy.
