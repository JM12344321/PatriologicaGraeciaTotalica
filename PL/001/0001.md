## Preface

### Preface to Tertullian

[Col. 0009A] As we undertake, with the favor of God and of the blessed Virgin Mother of God, to publish anew all the Fathers and ecclesiastical writers of the Latin tongue, we asked—after weighing chronology, subject matter, and works—which man should march first as standard-bearer before so great a host of witnesses to the Christian cause. A man of better reputation and a name of holier omen could indeed readily have been found among the earliest cradles of the Church; yet no one, apart from our QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN, was more outstanding in intellectual power and abundance of learning.

His genius must indeed be praised, but his heresy condemned; and the last [Col. 0009B] and greater part of so great a man’s long life, spent in foul errors, must certainly be lamented. Yet since the other and better part was vigorously devoted to battles for the faith, let him at least, in view of chronology and the right of seniority, be granted first place. Let the Western Church deservedly claim him as her first champion. Protected, therefore, by the shadow and patronage of his great name, may this new and complete series of all the testimonies by which Catholic Tradition grew arise and flourish. It is to be assembled from every work, great and small, produced and still surviving throughout the Christian world for the defense of the faith, from the apostolic age to the last of the Fathers. It will republish everything already printed; supply the place of old books that disappear day by day or are rarely found; answer the earnest and longstanding desire of many; fulfill what was announced long ago; and begin late so as to begin more prudently. For the undertaking is arduous and immense; yet by tireless effort and continuous labor, if only good and favoring [Col. 0009C] God assists us, it is to be carried through to its final end.

As we make such a beginning, may it be happy and auspicious for us to reread what is impressed upon the very first steps, and to learn from Tertullian’s miserable fall: no support from even the most fertile genius has ever been necessary for planting or preserving the faith, nor must this pillar and foundation of truth be propped up by the frail and perishable hand of men in order that the Church’s foundation may stand firm and unshaken [Note: [Col. 0009D] 1 Tim. 3:15]. For no one can lay another foundation besides that which has been laid, which is CHRIST JESUS [Note: [Col. 0009D] 1 Cor. 3:11]. God’s firm foundation stands, bearing this seal: “The Lord knows those who are his,” and, “Let everyone [Col. 0009D] who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity” [Note: [Col. 0009D] 2 Tim. 2:19].

Meanwhile, the character of this first witness who meets us is so varied and equivocal that something must be said by way of preface about his proper [Col. 0010A] use and true authority. Others will each contribute what must be discussed about him in the course of this work:

First PAMELIUS will construct the Septimian annals through twenty-four consular years.

Then Johann August NOESSELT and Dom Gottfried LUMPER will expressly discuss the occasion, date, and authority of each book, how many there are, and when each was written, whether before or after its author embraced Montanism.

Next Tertullian’s Paradoxes, together with their antidote, will be reviewed through the renewed labor of Pamelius.

Along with these will follow Beatus RHENANUS and ANDREAS [Col. 0010B] HOYUS of Bruges, explaining the proverbial expressions Tertullian uses most frequently, from which no small light is cast over the whole work.

Then, in a very rare and deeply learned dissertation on the apologetic books, Dom NICOLAS LE NOURRY will explain all the difficult points of Septimius’s doctrine at greater length and fullness.

Meanwhile there is appended a brief and lucid investigation by LAURENCE MOSHEIM on the date of the *Apology* and the beginning of the Severan persecution.

There follows the complete text of Tertullian, illustrated with variant readings from the Rhenanus, Pamelius, Franeker, Rigault, Paris, Venice, Halle, and Würzburg editions; corrected also by the emendations of Junius, Latinius, Wouwerius, Salmasius, Ciacconius, Scaliger, Semler, Ritter, and Leopold; and furnished with the extensive commentaries of Rhenanus, [Col. 0010C] Pamelius, Pancirolus, La Cerda, Albaspinaeus, Havercamp, Muratori, Dom Corbinian Thomas, and various others.

When the labors of so many and such men have been completed, there will be added, as a crowning abundance, a very full index of Tertullian’s Latin usage, arranged chiefly through the work of Gottfried Schutz.

Everyone may therefore see how small a province remains for the new editors and how nearly nothing must be added from their own store, except perhaps the matters we resolved above to undertake: first, what must be said about Tertullian’s use and authority—though even here we are not wholly left to ourselves, since an excellent shorter [Col. 0010D] essay by Dom Corbinian Thomas on the same subject is available; then, what principal labors have been devoted to Tertullian in the republic of letters down to our own time, which we shall in turn receive as carefully investigated by the very learned Ceillier, Lumper, Schram, and Schoennemann; and finally, as a crown, it remains to indicate more fully in this introduction by what ordering of subjects, sequence of books, addition of dissertations, variety of collated readings, abundance and selection of commentaries, and culmination of appendices and indexes this new edition is to be distinguished from others.

[Col. 0011A]

## Article I. On the Authority of Tertullian

Authority in one who possesses the power to teach others is rightly derived from two sources: esteem for his knowledge and confidence in his truthfulness.

Supreme authority belongs to God alone, since he alone possesses supreme truth in knowing and supreme truthfulness in speaking.

[Col. 0011B] Every other authority becomes greater in proportion as it approaches that authority more nearly and participates in it more perfectly.

Hence for us Catholics there is no greater authority under the sun—indeed none greater in heaven or on earth, and none other than that of God himself—than the teaching power of the Church. It rests, is guided, and is confirmed by the word of the power of God [Note: [Col. 0011D] Heb. 1:3], in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge [Note: [Col. 0011D] Heb. 1:3], and by the infallible assistance of the Holy Spirit, which will never fail even to the consummation of the age.

Blessed Vincent of Lérins teaches that all Catholics who strive to prove themselves legitimate children of the Church must cling to, fasten themselves upon, and die in the faith of the holy Fathers [Note: [Col. 0011D] Col. 2:3]. With the consent of the whole Tradition, he lays down this rule for assenting [Col. 0011C] to the Fathers: whatever you know to have been held, written, and taught openly, frequently, and perseveringly, not by one or two alone but by all alike with one and the same agreement, understand that this must also be believed by you without any doubt.

To Fathers taken individually no authority belongs except private authority, to be discerned from various signs, some peculiar to each and others drawn from without.

In the men themselves we must consider both what belongs to a rightly formed intellect and what belongs to a sound will: on one side, the force of their genius, abundance of learning, and manner and customary usage of speech, from which it may safely be believed that they are not deceived; on the other, uprightness of morals and holiness of life, by which every [Col. 0011D] suspicion that they deceive or wish to deceive is removed.

To these, as it were, intrinsic signs of authority are added the various circumstances of times, places, ranks, and all else amid which authors wrote, and especially the judgment of such great weight by which the Church approves and evaluates certain writers through a title tacit or solemn, special or most special.

Yet ecclesiastical writers who have not attained the highest and legitimate peaks of holiness, or have not trodden the straight path of faith with an unwavering foot, should not all be cast aside or rejected. Since they remained at least for a time in the bosom of orthodox faith, it is neither fair nor lawful to deny them all credit. Indeed, wherever they agree with Catholics, confidence in them is all the greater, since no sane person will think that collusion and a friendly conspiracy occurred on both sides.

[Col. 0012A] With these Catholic criteria of patristic study established, we must now come to our subject. QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN must now be weighed with an even and steady balance: deeply skilled in so many ancient disciplines; powerful in philosophical, historical, legal, oratorical, and poetic learning; renowned before accepting the faith for his distinction at the bar, and more renowned after receiving it, as defender [Col. 0012B] and advocate of the entire Christian cause before the emperors in the forum of the Roman world; herald of the second Christian era, nearest that of the Apostles; first witness and champion of the Latin Church, indeed the one man whose defenses have come down to us; distinguished first-fruits of the African faith; pupil of apostolic men; himself teacher of martyrs, bishops, and doctors; advanced to holy orders at Rome and enrolled among the presbyters at Carthage; thoroughly versed in mysteries; conqueror of all the heretics of his age, indeed a notable overthrower of Montanus; and yet—would that it were otherwise!—a Montanist, a heretic, and the dead head of a heresy, at last no longer a man of the Church!…

Since he underwent so many changes, our estimate of him must be manifold, becoming different according to the different character which he, so unlike himself, assumes. Above all, in him as in the other doctors of the Church, it is of some value to distinguish secular learning from sacred equipment, the private writer from the Catholic or Montanist author, and a merely literary or historical matter from a dogmatic cause.

[Col. 0012C] But what reason would there be to linger long over Septimius’s secular reputation in this preface and threshold to a purely patristic work? I shall therefore state briefly what sort of rhetorician, grammarian, dialectician, or cultivator of literary form he was, and what sort of philosopher, philologist, investigator of antiquity, master of history, jurist, and man practiced in every kind of knowledge.

[Note: [Col. 0011D] Vincent of Lérins, *Commonitorium* II, 20.]

What judgment should be made of his manner of speaking, discussing, and arguing you will find a little below, elegantly and expressly explained by the very learned Dom Lumper [Note: [Col. 0012D] See below, vol. I, cols. 89–96]. It is useful to add only a few words, lest a highly fastidious [Col. 0012D] reader and devotee of more polished learning stumble and stick upon the rough places of Tertullian’s Latin. Tertullian, almost the first of all to transmit dogmas and mysteries hitherto wholly unknown to Latin speech, could—and indeed had to—introduce new formulas and unheard words, or at least words used in another sense. That ecclesiastical Latin was being born and was almost wailing in its cradle. From the beginning it had its own modes and its own laws, whether in prose speech or rhythmic measure, especially suited to treating heavenly mysteries; it established and preserved them. No small part of Tertullian’s praise is that he contributed wonderfully to forming and spreading them through men’s mouths. [Col. 0013A] For all who came after him, and especially the many produced by the African Church—most fertile in illustrious men in those days—marveled at his genius and imitated even his difficult and harsh language. Led by Cyprian, an assiduous admirer of the man he called his most deserving master, they eagerly fashioned themselves and their speech after Tertullian’s diction.

It will not be irrelevant to observe in passing that Tertullian’s genius suffered a certain decline after his departure from the true faith. In books apparently written in his last period amid the dregs of Montanism, he either becomes harsher and more uneven, swept away by an uncontrollable itch to speak and a restless combativeness, or deliberately wraps himself in obscurer meaning and speaks only through tangled riddles. These faults may be almost touched with the finger especially in *On the Pallium* and *Against the Valentinians*.

[Col. 0013B] Perhaps the same change for the worse may be found when, forgetting the harshness of his unadorned and ill-composed diction, you look deeply into the subject and the inward substance of his thought. The majesty and grand eloquence of the *Apology*, the *Prescription*, and the books *On Prayer*, *On Baptism*, and *On Repentance*, sometimes restrained by extremely sharp and Laconic brevity, declines into shapeless and diffuse eloquence that tosses out whatever comes to hand, even tasteless things, and does not disdain buffoonery or rather impudent jokes.

But since these points, as I have said, belong more to secular criticism than to Tertullian’s dogmatic authority, we now set our sails toward that subject, unwilling to be carried elsewhere.

[Col. 0013C] By sacred learning, says Walch [Note: [Col. 0013D] *Bibliotheca Patristica*, new ed., Jena, 1834, ch. 7, §46], we mean that which treats matters disclosed not by nature and knowable by human faculty, but divinely manifested and contained in the most holy code (to which venerable Catholic Tradition is also added). Knowledge of these matters consists in various disciplines: exegetical, dogmatic, polemical, moral, symbolic, catechetical, and homiletic theology, together with ecclesiastical history.

Since Tertullian is commended under almost all these titles, from them must be drawn the principal signs of authority that are proper and peculiar to him.

To begin with the Word of God, Tertullian makes a by no means contemptible contribution to its interpretation and to exegetical science. Whether Catholic [Col. 0013D] or Montanist, he always taught that the Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments were inspired [Note: [Col. 0013D] Cf. *On the Dress of Women* I.3, vol. I, col. 1307; *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 22, vol. II, col. 823; *Against Marcion* III.6, col. 325; V.7, col. 485; *On the Soul* 2, col. 648]. He adores the plenitude of Scripture no less than its divinity [Note: [Col. 0013D] *Against Hermogenes* 22, vol. II, col. 217]. In adoring it he does not remove tradition; rather, he openly declares that interpretation of Scripture must be referred to tradition—that is, to the Apostles and disciples of the Lord [Note: [Col. 0013D] *Scorpiace* 12, vol. II, col. 146]. Although he nowhere formally discussed the canon [Col. 0014A] of Sacred Scripture, it can nevertheless be gathered from various passages of his works that books cast out of the canon in our age by pseudo-critics were numbered by him among the sacred and divinely inspired books [Note: [Col. 0014D] Cf. Gottfried Lumper, *Historia theologico-critica de vita, scriptis atque doctrina SS. Patrum*, vol. VII, ch. 6, art. 1, p. 242 ff.].

Yet Tertullian’s authority as an exegete is utterly overturned if his latest editor and most insolent detractor, J. Salomo Semler, is to be believed. With brazen front Semler asserted that this writer never used with his own eyes and hands any Greek codex of the Gospels, Epistles, or other sacred books, and never wished to use Greek codices because they were corrupted; and that this same man, willingly ignorant of Greek codices, corrupted the Latin books and enlarged them with interpolated glosses and opinions still [Col. 0014B] surviving, of which Tertullian himself was the first and sole certain author [Note: [Col. 0014D] Dissertation on the varied and uncertain character of these books, reprinted with the works of Tertullian, Halle-Magdeburg, 1828, vol. V, pp. 176–313]. But, I ask, what credit belongs to an itch for innovation so insolent that the same man dares declare that all Latin writers in these first centuries never consulted Greek books, and all conspired to insert deliberately into their own books, against the witness of the Greek codices, new glosses—while the first beginnings and recent rudiments of that Latin translation arose in Tertullian’s time, if not from Tertullian himself? But why recount these things, when in this same dissertation Tertullian’s detractor reached such audacity that, after dispersing the darkness of so long a time—as that exceedingly novel and perverse man puts it—he discovered an association, unheard of in all preceding antiquity, of conspirators dwelling at Rome, both Greek [Col. 0014C] and Latin, from whose workshop there secretly issued many works of Tertullian, some of Clement of Alexandria, and all of Irenaeus, entirely counterfeit and spurious? When first published, these claims were deservedly regarded as monstrosities and attacked and routed by the most learned men of Germany itself [Note: [Col. 0014D] Cf. Johann Daniel Schumann, *Observationes in veteres Ecclesiae scriptores*, specimens III and IV, on interpolations wrongly imputed to Tertullian’s New Testament codex, Hanover, 1776–1777, quarto; C. W. F. Walch, on the authenticity of Irenaeus’s books against J. Sal. Semler, in *Novi Commentarii Societatis Regiae Gottingensis*, vol. V, part II, p. 3]. To expose them is enough to make them rejected with ridicule [Note: [Col. 0014D] Cf. the points briefly touched below, vol. I, cols. 55–56 n.; vol. II, cols. 548–49, 551–52, 554, 538, 565, 594].

The dogmatic authority of Tertullian the Catholic, however, rises high; even that of Tertullian the Montanist must sometimes not be despised. Our author embraces in a remarkable way the three methods used by the Fathers in expounding the faith: the symbolic, in which the weightiest linked dogmas of faith are recited continuously in a few words; the polemical, adapted to refuting Gentiles, Jews, or heretics; and the discursive or catechetical, which for the use of Christian believers [Col. 0014D] explains now one point of doctrine and now another.

[Col. 0015A] For he has three formulas of faith: one, the shorter, in *On the Veiling of Virgins* [Note: [Col. 0015D] Ch. 1, vol. II, col. 888]; another, longer, in the *Prescription against Heretics* [Note: [Col. 0015D] Ch. 13, vol. II, col. 26]; and a third and later one in *Against Praxeas* [Note: [Col. 0015D] Ch. 2, vol. II, col. 156].

He was the first among the Latins to attack every enemy of the newborn Church with polemical and apologetic writings. Against the nations he wrote the seven books we have entitled apologetic: the two books *To the Nations* and the great *Apology*, the latter addressed to governors and the former to the people; likewise *On the Testimony of the Soul*, a fuller treatment of chapter 17 of the *Apology*; and the book *To Scapula*, in which he complains of unjust and cruel persecution and extols the virtues of Christians. To these are added *To the Martyrs*, an apology on the glory of martyrdom; *On the Shows*, an exhortation to Christians to avoid [Col. 0015B] the shows of the heathen; and *On Idolatry*, addressed to all worshipers either of Christ or of the gods, on the great gravity of that crime.

Following the footsteps of Justin the philosopher, Irenaeus the martyr, and others whose names he himself displays, he demonstrates to the Jews that the Messiah foretold by the prophets has come [Note: [Col. 0015D] Cf. G. H. Scharffenberg, academic commentary on Justin, Tertullian, and Cyprian against the Jews, Lund-Gothenburg, 1820, quarto]. He slays all heretics generally in the *Prescription*, not even excepting Montanus, and then pierces them individually, as if in single combat. Thus he assails, conquers, and crushes the disordered troop of Gnostics ten times [Note: [Col. 0015D] In *Scorpiace*, *Against the Gnostics*, *Against Hermogenes*, the five books *Against Marcion*, *Against the Valentinians*, *On the Flesh of Christ*, and *On the Resurrection of the Flesh]; and he apparently did not quit this battle line, like a discharged veteran soldier, until the enemy had been scattered and put to flight. He also opposed Praxeas, who asserted one God but not a triune God, and Montanus, to whom he miserably defected, [Col. 0015C] in the elegant and charming little book *On Repentance*. Finally, he explodes the sophists’ tasteless philosophizing *On the Soul*. With this tireless labor and ceaseless contest Tertullian fought; and when, advanced in age and stricken by an incurable wound, he deserted to Montanus’s side, far from relaxing his assault, he armed himself more fiercely for battle and seemed to say to himself: “Woe to me if I do not fight!”

Again, he was the first to offer Latin ears a distinct treatise on a particular dogmatic topic, composed in lucid speech. Indeed, he excelled so greatly in treating these dogmas separately that I dare say that, through the boundless passage of years, nothing has yet appeared that can deservedly be thought to surpass Tertullian’s books *On Prayer*, *On Baptism*, *On Repentance*, *On Patience*, and the other works [Col. 0015D] we judged should be styled dogmatic.

Meanwhile I shall not deny that even Tertullian’s best writings suffer here and there from many errors and are disfigured by much poisonous corruption. I would have you see these treated to some extent with the best and most wholesome antidote, reviewed again through the labor of the old editors and placed at the front of this volume.

[Col. 0016A] Another praise of Tertullian remains more intact and cannot be sufficiently celebrated: his moral and hortatory excellence, to be gathered from his many books concerning Christian conduct. These form two series, the first written before the author embraced Montanism, the second after he was already a Montanist. In both, the mirror of Christian life is vividly expressed: in its perennial fountain, private and public prayer [Note: [Col. 0016D] *On Prayer*]; in its most abundant streams, namely the sacraments [Note: [Col. 0016D] *On Baptism*, *On Repentance*, *On the Eucharist*, *On the Soldier’s Crown*, *On Marriage*, *To His Wife*]; in its ordinary acts [Note: [Col. 0016D] *On Patience*, *On the Dress of Women*] or its most excellent ones [Note: [Col. 0016D] *On the Veiling of Virgins*]; in the liturgical rites that give it outward form [Note: [Col. 0016D] *On Prayer*]; in avoiding the enticements of the world or flesh, where the Christian soul is endangered [Note: [Col. 0016D] *On the Shows*]; in undertaking works by which [Col. 0016B] it revives [Note: [Col. 0016D] *On Repentance*, *On Fasting*]; or in entering the bloody arena of martyrdom by which it is glorified [Note: [Col. 0016D] *To the Martyrs*, *Scorpiace*]. In treating these matters, the more rigid harshness of the Montanist is certainly carried beyond legitimate bounds rather often, and provision has carefully been made against the error hidden in our books. Besides, one must frequently note the impulse of a fervent genius that urges men toward great things and exceeds the common measure of mankind.

You have, then, TERTULLIAN commended solely from his own writings and his peculiar resources. He is further commended by the ancient times in which he wrote: the first among the Africans, the first among the Latins after the Roman Pontiffs who is at least known and preserved. They were blessed times of teachers and disciples, when in the new seed of the Christians the blood [Col. 0016C] of Christ and the martyrs still ran warmer, when the Apostles’ voice still sounded and their faces were still represented, when their authentic letters were publicly read [Note: [Col. 0016D] *Prescription* 36, vol. II, col. 49]. He is commended also by his native city and the Church of which he was the first priest known to us: Carthage, Rome’s rival as conqueror, yet conquered by faith; won by Peter’s disciples as Rome was by Peter; new, if you will, but already the happy and exultant mother of her infants. Its noble offspring had only lately been announced to the world: the Scillitan Martyrs and the company of Perpetua, Felicity, and their companions, whose contemporary our Septimius was—indeed, as some think, their witness and panegyrist [Note: [Col. 0016D] Cf. J. A. Noesselt’s dissertation below, vol. I, col. 85 ff.]. This Church long flourished in the holiness and learning of men who followed Tertullian in unbroken succession, as though our author had roused their talents, trained their hands for battle, marshaled their ranks [Col. 0016D] for the field, and bestowed and fashioned for them a new idiom, like a military watchword, afterward to be spread throughout the whole Christian West. He is commended by the very holy men with whom he lived, conversed, or was joined by circumstance [Col. 0017A] or shared studies: Irenaeus, whom he mentions with reverence; Gaius and Hippolytus, disciples of that same Irenaeus; his contemporary Clement of Alexandria; Origen, whom he perhaps met at Rome; and finally the supreme bishops of bishops, who instilled in him the most uncorrupted doctrine, the whole of which the Apostles poured out at Rome with their blood [Note: [Col. 0017D] *Prescription* 36, vol. II, col. 49]. He is commended by the hundreds of authors of every kind whom he kept before his eyes—sacred and profane, historians, philosophers, poets, jurists, and masters of every discipline—whose volumes he handled by night and by day, and whose testimonies he set at the head of all his tablets. He is commended by posterity, both those nearest to him and the most recent: his fellow Africans, led by St. Cyprian, who, when asking for Septimius’s [Col. 0017B] books, used to say, “Give me the master” [Note: [Col. 0017D] St. Jerome, *On Ecclesiastical Writers*]; and our own countrymen, heralded by the illustrious Bossuet of Meaux, who whenever he summons Tertullian seems to burst forth with these or like words: “A great man, a weighty man, powerful in authority and force.”

Great, then, is the name of QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN. But how much greater it would have been had that Lucifer of the African Church, who rose in the morning, not fallen like lightning from heaven; had he who wounded nations not crashed to earth; had he not said in his heart, “I will ascend above the height of the clouds” [Note: [Col. 0017D] Isa. 14:12, 14]! For like Satan, cast down by the lightning of pride, he fell; and once fallen, whether he rose again no one even dares ask. Solomon is questioned; Origen is debated; Tertullian is passed over in silence. Yet he wasted away in prolonged old age, and he lacked no long interval for repentance [Col. 0017C]. His last writings all too strongly persuade and convict us that he remained miserably obstinate and, as if maddened by the single blow that felled him, continued for almost forty years—until nearly his eightieth year—fierce in hostile anger against the Church and threatening missiles even with a failing hand.

Who, then, would not fear and shudder at God’s unsearchable judgments? Who would have thought that so auspicious a beginning for TERTULLIAN would end thus? Once the *Apology* had been issued at the beginning of the century and the other addresses for the Christian cause delivered; once confessors had thereby been strengthened or martyrs raised to the laurel; once the heat of persecutions had cooled and the Church breathed a little; and once she had at last been settled in peace for almost forty years, a new order of affairs and a more pleasant face of the times were dawning. With eager ears, both Gentiles and Christians attended [Col. 0017D] those who preached peace and good things. What Irenaeus was to do in Gaul, Clement in Egypt, and Hippolytus in Latium, Tertullian seemed about to do in Africa and throughout the West: so that, the common people having been won, nobles and wise men too might be won, and the Church built from bottom to top and the Christian world established. This, unless I am mistaken, was the splendid role for which Tertullian was destined. What more? He fell, dragged down to hell, into the depth of the pit [Note: [Col. 0017D] Isa. 14:15]. This, then, was contained in God’s unsearchable [Col. 0018A] decree: that this new Church, growing through so many wounds, after at last triumphing over Jews, pagans, and Gnostics, should strike against this stumbling-stone; that when she deserved to be refreshed by the aid of so great a spirit, she should lose him through his fall, endure him as an enemy, and mourn him as he resisted and insulted his mother’s bosom for forty years. We should no longer ask, as many have wished, whether he benefited the Church more than he harmed her, but rather whether or not he benefited himself unto life. He is not a man of the Church, though returned from so many labors and battles! Yet would that we might trust more confidently in the pious prayers scattered repeatedly through his works and fulfill his wish when he said to catechumens: “Therefore, blessed ones whom the grace of God awaits, when you ascend from that most holy bath of new birth and for the first time spread out your [Col. 0018B] hands before your Mother with your brethren, ask of the Father, ask of the Lord, special riches of grace and distributions of charisms,” adding, “‘Ask and you shall receive,’ he says. For you have sought and found; you have knocked, and it has been opened to you. I ask only that, when you pray, you remember also TERTULLIAN the sinner” [Note: [Col. 0018C] *On Baptism* 20, final words. Judgments concerning the greatest men often vary so much that some raise them to heaven while others thrust them to the bottom. So it has generally happened to our Tertullian: as he has not lacked detractors, so he has found many champions and exceedingly generous admirers. Some have thought all his surviving works written during his Montanism, while others deny that he fell into the criminal communion of Montanus. There have even been those who celebrate Tertullian as a martyr, said to have suffered under Decius. On these and other matters concerning Tertullian’s history and authority, cf. *Magdeburg Centuries*, cent. III, ch. 12; Joachim Zentgrav, *Exercise on Tertullian’s Fall to the Montanists*, Strasbourg, 1704, quarto; Timann Gessell, *Sacred and Ecclesiastical History of the New Testament*, p. 299; Pierre Allix, *Dissertation on the Life and Writings of Tertullian*, Paris, 1680, octavo; De la Motte, *History of Tertullian and Origen*, Lyon, 1701, octavo; Nicolas Le [Col. 0018D] Nourry, dissertation on Tertullian’s *Apology*, books *To the Nations*, and *To Scapula*, below, vol. I, col. 705 ff.; Johann W. Hopfmann, dissertation arguing that all surviving works of Q. S. F. Tertullian appear to have been written during Montanism, Wittenberg, 1738, quarto; J. A. Noesselt, Dissertation III on the true date and teaching of the surviving writings of Q. Septimius Florens Tertullian, Halle, 1757; the same author’s three commentaries concerning ecclesiastical history, Halle, 1817; Giuseppe Antonio Cantova, two dissertations on Septimius and St. Epiphanius, Milan, 1783, octavo; H. ## Article II. On the Use of Tertullian

We think that a still sharper judgment is altogether required for the proper use of this most ancient Father than was just required for discerning TERTULLIAN’s true authority. In his writings, therefore, one must again consider both the author’s condition, now Catholic and now Montanist, and the books’ doctrine or historical information, each either [Col. 0018C] sacred or profane. His sacred doctrine concerns dogmas [Col. 0019A] of faith, while his historical information concerns the practices then customary in the Church, that is, matters of discipline.

Lest these preliminary warnings grow beyond measure, it is not our task to traverse each item separately or enumerate exactly everything he said rightly or rashly concerning sacred exegesis, dogmatic or moral theology, the Church’s rites and customs, or the history of times and opinions.

Only some principal heads of Tertullian’s doctrine and his gravest errors must be indicated in summary, and the more notable passages of his works chiefly useful for exegetical, dogmatic, moral, liturgical, and ascetical purposes must be pointed out. Thus anyone devoted to patristic study may more eagerly equip himself to examine them fully, and a safer and easier road may especially be opened to younger students of theology and [Col. 0019B] patrology.

Before all else TERTULLIAN proclaims in a full voice that the whole edifice of Christian faith rests upon the firmest historical truth and the venerable authority of tradition. These are the inescapable facts upon which the whole Christian reality hangs, as by a chain of bronze and gold: God the Father sends his Son into this world to build the Christian Church. Jesus Christ, for the same end, sends his Apostles and teaches them all the truths that supply this building with its foundation and growth. The Apostles hand on the doctrine received from the Lord to all the churches to which they are sent. Those churches hold that apostolic doctrine most firmly and communicate it to others that saw none of the Apostles [Col. 0019C]. All those churches, united with one another, constitute one and the same body in the unity of the same doctrine and the same faith. Who, then, will desert this Church, the immovable pillar of truth, to embrace sects separated from her?
