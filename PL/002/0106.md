Therefore, those who received another Paraclete in the apostles and through the apostles—a Paraclete whom they did not recognize more fully even in the Prophets—now no longer possess him even in the apostles. Come now, let them show, at least from the apostolic record, that stains with which the flesh is bespattered after baptism can be washed away by repentance.

> **Note [Col. 1001C].** *Propius* in Rhenanus, Oberthür, and Leopold.

> **Note [Col. 1001D].** “Or let them teach it from the apostolic record.” It does not follow: “From the Acts of the Apostles—which he calls the apostolic record—it is not taught that stains with which the flesh is bespattered after baptism are washed away by repentance; therefore sins committed after baptism are not erased by repentance,” since this is sufficiently established by other Scriptures. —PAM.

> **Note [Col. 1001D].** “The flesh bespattered after baptism.” Here he has used *respersae* for “sprinkled and stained anew,” namely with fresh filth after the washing. —RIG.

In the apostles too we recognize the form of the old law in its demonstration of how grave adultery is, lest perhaps it be regarded as less serious under the newness of the disciplines than under the old. When the Gospel first thundered forth and shook the old order, so that the question whether the law was to be retained was debated, the apostles issued this first rule, on the authority of the Holy Spirit, to those who had already begun to be chosen from among the nations:

> “It has seemed good,” they say, “to the Holy Spirit and to us to impose no further burden upon you than those things from which it is necessary to abstain: from sacrifices, from fornication, and from blood. By guarding yourselves from these things you do well” (Acts 15:28 ff.), the Holy Spirit bearing you along.

> **Note [Col. 1001D].** “From sacrifices, from fornication, and from blood.” Irenaeus preserves the same order: “They must be instructed to abstain from the vanities of idols, and from fornication, and from blood, and not to do to others whatever they do not wish to be done to themselves.” A little later, in place of “the vanities of idols,” he says “things sacrificed to idols.” These two words, *eidōlothyton kai haima* (εἰδωλόθυτον καὶ αἷμα), “an idol-sacrifice and blood,” received various interpretations and even disturbed the ancient manuscripts of Acts through ambiguity of readings. But what is read here and in Irenaeus is perfectly clear concerning these three principal defilements (*alisgēmata*, ἀλισγήματα): those “of idols, fornication, and blood” (*eidōlōn, porneias, haimatos*, εἰδώλων, πορνείας, αἵματος). —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1001C].** “Forbidding” (*vetante*) in Wouwer’s manuscript.

> **Note [Col. 1002D].** It is sufficient that here too the place belonging to adultery and fornication has been preserved. But Luke himself did not preserve it. For in Acts 15, after first saying, “of idols and of fornication and of blood” (*tōn eidōlōn kai tēs porneias kai tou haimatos*, τῶν εἰδώλων καὶ τῆς πορνείας καὶ τοῦ αἵματος), he afterward prescribed them as “things sacrificed to idols, and blood, and fornication” (*eidōlothytōn kai haimatos kai porneias*, εἰδωλοθύτων καὶ αἵματος καὶ πορνείας). Accordingly, Septimius’s argument from the order or position of the words is quite frivolous, as is the vain conjecture of those who think that the words should be connected thus: “For the Holy Spirit it is sufficient that here too the place belonging to adultery and fornication has been preserved.” Irenaeus contradicts this, nor did it ever occur to our Septimius. —RIG.

It is sufficient that here too the place of honor belonging to adultery and fornication has been preserved between idolatry and murder; for we shall understand the prohibition of blood to refer much more especially to human blood.

> **Note [Col. 1002C].** “The Holy Spirit: it is sufficient,” Junius.

What sort of crimes, then, do the apostles intend these to be, which alone they select from the former law for observance, and which alone they prescribe as things from which it is absolutely necessary to abstain? Not that they permit the others, but because they set forth these alone as plainly incapable of remission, while, for the sake of the Gentiles, they made the remaining burdens of the law remissible. Why, then, do they free our neck from so great a yoke, unless they mean always to impose upon them these concise summaries of discipline?

> **Note [Col. 1002C].** “Upon him” (*illi*) in Rigault and Venice.

Why do they loosen so many bonds, unless they mean to bind us perpetually to the more indispensable ones? They released us from many things so that we might be bound to observe the more perilous. The matter was settled by compensation: we gained many concessions so that we might render certain obligations. But a compensation is not revocable—

> **Note [Col. 1002D].** “And it will be revoked by the same things.” Something is missing which cannot be restored except with the aid of a manuscript. Or certainly one should write: “Unless, finally, it is revoked by repetition—repetition, that is, of adultery, bloodshed, and idolatry.” —RIG.

—and it will be revoked by those same things: namely, by a repetition of adultery, bloodshed, and idolatry. For the whole law will then be resumed if the condition of pardon is dissolved. But the Holy Spirit did not make a trivial covenant with us; indeed, he made it voluntarily and is therefore all the more to be honored. No one dissolves his compact except an ungrateful person. He will neither take back what he released nor release what he retained. The settled state of the New Testament is forever unchangeable, and surely the recital of that decree and that decision will last until the end of the age. He has sufficiently denied pardon for the things whose observance he chose; he claimed for punishment what he did not similarly concede. Hence it is that peace is restored by the churches neither to idolatry nor to bloodshed. We may not, I think, believe that the apostles fell away from this determination of their own; or, if some can believe it, they will have to prove it.

## CHAPTER XIII

[Col. 1003A]

We are, to be sure, well acquainted with their suspicions here too. For they actually suppose that the apostle Paul, in the Second Epistle to the Corinthians (2 Cor. 2:5–11), granted pardon to the same fornicator whom in the First Epistle he pronounced must be delivered to Satan for the destruction of the flesh (1 Cor. 5:3 ff.), the impious man who had inherited his father’s marriage-bed;

> **Note [Col. 1003D].** “Granted pardon to the same fornicator,” etc. You will observe, of course, that in 1 Corinthians 5 the man who had polluted himself with incest is called a fornicator. Hence Tertullian afterward calls him a defiler of his father’s bed, and thus uses “fornication” and “incest” indiscriminately. —LE PR.

and they suppose that he afterward reversed his very pen, writing:

> “But if anyone has caused sorrow, he has not caused sorrow to me, but in part—lest I burden all of you. For such a person this rebuke inflicted by many is sufficient; so that, on the contrary, you ought rather to forgive and encourage him, lest such a person perhaps be swallowed up by more abundant sorrow. Therefore I ask you to confirm your love toward him. For this purpose I also wrote, that I might know the proof of you, whether you obey me in everything. But if you forgive anyone, I also forgive him. For if I have forgiven anything, I have forgiven it in the person of Christ, lest we be defrauded by Satan, since we are not ignorant of his devices” (2 Cor. 2:5–11).

> **Note [Col. 1003D].** *Quasive* in Rhenanus and Oberthür; *quasi vel* in Leopold.

What here is understood concerning the fornicator, the defiler of his father’s bed, the Christian who surpassed the shamelessness of the Gentiles, when surely Paul would have absolved by a specific pardon the man whom he had condemned in a specific wrath?

> **Note [Col. 1003D].** “The shameless Gentile,” Paris.

He shows mercy more obscurely than he expresses indignation; he is clearer in severity than in leniency. Yet anger more readily takes an indirect course than indulgence does; sorrowful matters are kept waiting more often than joyful ones. It was, of course, a matter of some slight indulgence! If it were being considered now, when even the greatest concessions are not usually granted without public declaration, still less would it be granted without any indication.

You, at any rate, when bringing the adulterer’s repentance into the church in order to entreat the brotherhood, prostrate him in the midst, before the widows and the presbyters, clothed in sackcloth and covered with ashes, arranged as a spectacle of disgrace and horror, clutching at everyone’s garments, licking everyone’s footprints, clasping everyone’s knees.

> **Note [Col. 1003D].** “Clothed in sackcloth and covered with ashes.” In the penitents’ squalor of sackcloth and ashes. —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1003D].** “Prostrating,” in Pamelius, Oberthür, and Semler.

And for the sake of that man’s final fate, with every enticement to mercy that you can muster, you deliver your public address, good shepherd and blessed pope; and in the parable of the sheep you seek your goats. You make sure that your sheep does not again leap from the flock—as though from then onward something could be permitted that was not permitted even once—and you fill the rest with fear at the very moment when you grant indulgence.

> **Note [Col. 1003D].** “The blessed pope delivers a public address.” In this very ancient author the title *pope* occurs, and so does “blessed,” as in chapter 30 of *On Prescription*, where he calls Eleutherius blessed; he was also Roman pontiff and martyr. —LE PR.

> **Note [Col. 1004D].** La Cerda deletes “your sheep.”

Yet would the apostle have so carelessly forgiven criminal lust burdened with the further guilt of incest that he did not require from the man even that garb appointed for repentance which you ought to have learned from the apostle himself, nor threaten anything concerning the future, nor address him at all concerning his subsequent conduct?

> **Note [Col. 1003D].** “The garb appointed for repentance.” That is, appointed by the consent of the nations, dedicated, and regarded as most appropriate to the grief of penitents or defendants; or else the garb assigned and bequeathed to the penitent, as though by a sentence expressed in these words: “Whoever, being an adulterer or fornicator, asks that the Church’s peace be restored to him, let him be sentenced, in mourning and squalor, in sackcloth and ashes, to clasp the knees of the brethren.” Otherwise, the reading *legitimum* (“legitimate”), which I see proposed, would not displease me. —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1004D].** “Legitimate” (*legitimum*) in Leopold, from Wouwer’s manuscript.

Indeed, he even begs them of his own accord to confirm their love toward him, as one offering satisfaction rather than granting forgiveness. And yet I hear of love, not communion. So too he writes to the Thessalonians:

> “But if anyone does not obey our word through this letter, mark that man and do not associate with him, that he may be ashamed; do not regard him as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother” (2 Thess. 3:14).

> **Note [Col. 1004D].** “Beyond” (*ultra*) in Oberthür and Semler.

Thus he could have said that only love, and not communion, was granted even to the fornicator. But not even love was granted to the incestuous man, whom he had commanded, of course, to be removed from their midst—and therefore all the more from their affections.

But was Paul afraid that they might be defrauded by Satan through the loss of the very person whom he himself had cast out to Satan? Or that the man whom he had condemned to the destruction of the flesh might be swallowed up by an abundance of grief? At this point they interpret the destruction of the flesh as the work of repentance: the flesh is said to make satisfaction to God by being worn away with fasts, filth, every form of neglect, and deliberate ill-treatment. From this they argue that the fornicator—or rather, that incestuous man—was delivered by the apostle to Satan not for ruin but for correction, as one who would afterward obtain pardon because of the destruction, that is, the affliction, of his flesh, and therefore did obtain it.

Certainly, the same apostle delivered Hymenaeus and Alexander to Satan so that they might be corrected and cease blaspheming, as he writes to his Timothy (1 Tim. 1:20). But he also says that there was given to him a stake, an angel of Satan, to buffet him lest he exalt himself (2 Cor. 12:7).

> **Note [Col. 1004D].** “But he also says that there was given to him a stake, an angel of Satan.” Someone may wonder, not without reason, that Tertullian, after rendering Paul’s *skolops* (σκόλοψ), “thorn,” in Latin as *sudis*, “stake,” immediately inclines toward the opinion of those who suppose that these words signify pain in the ear or the head. —RIG.

If they also bring this forward so that we may understand those delivered by him to Satan as delivered for correction rather than for destruction, what similarity is there between blasphemy and incest, on the one hand, and, on the other, a soul untouched by these things—a soul exalted by nothing other than the highest holiness and complete innocence—which in the apostle was perhaps restrained by blows, through pain in the ear or the head, as they say? Incest and blasphemy, however, deserved to deliver the whole persons themselves into the possession of Satan, not merely to his angel.

There is also a difference in this very point—and indeed it is highly pertinent to this issue—that we read that those men were delivered by the apostle to Satan, whereas an angel of Satan was given to the apostle. Finally, when Paul entreats the Lord, what does he hear?

> “Let my grace be sufficient for you, for power is perfected in weakness” (2 Cor. 12:9).

Those delivered to Satan cannot hear this.

But if the crime of Hymenaeus and Alexander—namely blasphemy—is unforgivable both in this age and in the age to come, the apostle surely would not, contrary to the Lord’s determination, have delivered to Satan under hope of pardon men who had already sunk from faith into blasphemy.

> **Note [Col. 1005D].** “Contrary to the Lord’s determination.” That is, contrary to the Lord’s sentence and decision. For in Septimius, *determinare* means to judge, pronounce, or define by judgment. —RIG.

Hence he also pronounced them shipwrecked concerning the faith, as no longer possessing the consolation of the Church’s ship; for pardon is denied to those who have crashed from faith into blasphemy. Gentiles and heretics, however, emerge from blasphemy every day.

But even if he said, “I delivered them to Satan so that they might receive the discipline of not blaspheming,” he was speaking about the others, who, through those men’s delivery to Satan—that is, their expulsion from the Church—were to be taught that they must not blaspheme. In the same way, therefore, he delivered the incestuous fornicator to Satan not for correction but for destruction, since by sinning worse than the Gentiles he had already passed over to Satan, so that the others might learn not to fornicate.

Finally, he says “for the destruction of the flesh,” not “for its torment.” He condemns the very substance through which the man had fallen away, which had already perished from that point onward through the loss of baptism:

> **Note [Col. 1005D].** “Through the loss of baptism.” A person is said to have lost baptism when, after the washing, he has fallen into the crime of idolatry, adultery, or murder. Conversely, in canon 38 of the Council of Elvira, one who after receiving baptism has been guilty of none of these crimes is said to “have his washing intact.” —RIG.

> “So that the spirit,” he says, “may be saved in the day of the Lord” (1 Cor. 5:5).

Here too it must be asked whether the spirit of the man himself will be saved. Will a spirit polluted by so great a crime, then, be saved because the flesh has been destroyed, so that it may be saved in punishment? The opposing interpretation, therefore, will assign punishment without the flesh, if we thereby lose the resurrection of the flesh.

> **Note [Col. 1005D].** “The opposing interpretation, therefore, will assign punishment without the flesh; thus we lose the resurrection of the flesh.” There were those who said that Paul’s words, “To deliver him to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord,” were to be understood as follows: the flesh of that criminal whom Paul delivered to Satan perished after being tortured by various torments, so that, having thus been punished in the flesh, his spirit might be saved in the day of the Lord. This interpretation, however, was contrary to that of Septimius, who did not concede that even in this way the spirit would be saved. He therefore says that the opinion opposed to his own assigns to that criminal the punishment of being without flesh—that is, that his flesh would not rise. From this there follows the utterly absurd consequence that there would be no resurrection of that flesh. “Thus,” he says, “we lose the resurrection of the flesh.” I see this reading produced from Ursinus’s manuscripts. Hence it becomes likely that the preceding words too—which otherwise I would certainly suspect had been incorrectly transmitted to us—stand in the same manuscripts just as they are printed here. —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1005C].** “Interpretation.” Thus Leopold, from Wouwer’s manuscript.

It remains, therefore, that by “spirit” he meant the spirit that belongs to the Church, which must be kept safe—that is, intact—in the day of the Lord from the contagion of impurities by the expulsion of the incestuous fornicator. For he adds:

> “Do you not know that a little leaven makes the whole lump lose its savor?” (1 Cor. 5:6).

And yet incestuous fornication was not a little but a great leaven.

## CHAPTER XIV

Having thus examined these intervening matters, I return to the Second Epistle to the Corinthians, in order to prove that this statement of the apostle too—“For such a man this rebuke inflicted by many is sufficient” (2 Cor. 2:6)—does not apply to the person of the fornicator. For if he had pronounced that the man must be delivered to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, he had surely condemned rather than rebuked him. It was therefore another person for whom he wished rebuke to suffice, since the fornicator had received from his sentence not rebuke but condemnation.

I submit this very matter to you for consideration: whether there were not others in the First Epistle who caused the apostle sorrow by behaving disorderly, and who were in turn made sorrowful by him when they received rebuke according to the meaning of the Second Epistle—some one of whom could have obtained pardon in that epistle.

Let us observe, moreover, that the whole First Epistle was written, so to speak, not with ink but with gall: swelling, indignant, disdainful, threatening, reproachful, and directed in each particular matter against certain persons as though they were the contractors responsible for those offenses.

> **Note [Col. 1006D].** “And in each particular matter.” That is, in connection with specific crimes. —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1006D].** “Against certain persons as though they were the contractors responsible for them.” He calls conspicuous criminals “contractors,” as though they had brazenly purchased the right to commit crimes, with Satan—the great wholesaler and dealer in every form of wickedness—awarding the contract. —RIG.

For schisms, rivalries, dissensions, presumptions, arrogance, and contentions had demanded such treatment: that they should be burdened with reproach, beaten down by correction, have their pride filed away, and be frightened by severity.

And what a goad to humility is reproach!

> **Note [Col. 1006D].** “And what a goad to humility is reproach?” What a goad is humility humbled into reproach? That is, so humbled and abased that it makes his adversaries objects of reproach. —RIG.

> “I thank God that I baptized none of you except Crispus and Gaius, lest anyone say that I baptized in my own name” (1 Cor. 1:14–15).

> “For I determined to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ, and him crucified” (1 Cor. 2:2).

> “And I think God has selected us apostles as the last of all, like men condemned to fight beasts, because we have become a spectacle to this world, to angels, and to human beings; and we have become the refuse of this world, the offscouring of all” (1 Cor. 4:9, 13).

> **Note [Col. 1006D].** “Like men condemned to fight beasts.” *Bestiarii* are slaves condemned to the beasts. Gloss: *bestiarius*, *thēriomachos* (θηριομάχος), “beast-fighter.” He uses the same word in chapter 9 of the *Apology*. —LE PR.

And:

> “Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? Have I not seen Christ Jesus our Lord?” (1 Cor. 9:1).

Against what sort of haughtiness was he compelled to declare:

> “But to me it is a very small thing that I should be examined by you or by a human day of judgment; for I am not conscious of anything against myself” (1 Cor. 4:3–4);

> **Note [Col. 1006C].** “See from what sort,” La Cerda.

and:

> “No one shall make my glory empty” (1 Cor. 9:15).

> “Do you not know that we shall judge angels?” (1 Cor. 6:3).

But how open is the freedom of his rebuke, how exposed the edge of his spiritual sword!

> “Already you are enriched; already you are satisfied; already you reign” (1 Cor. 4:8).

And:

> “If anyone thinks that he knows, he does not yet know how he ought to know” (1 Cor. 8:2).

Is he not then striking someone in the face as well?

> “For who,” he says, “distinguishes you? And what do you have that you did not receive? But if you received it, why do you boast as though you had not received it?” (1 Cor. 4:7).

Does he not also strike those others directly in the mouth?

> “But some, in their conscience, still eat it as something sacrificed to an idol. And by thus sinning, striking the weak consciences of the brethren, they will sin against Christ.”

Then he even names names:

> “Or do we not have authority to eat and drink, and to take women about with us, as do the other apostles, the Lord’s brothers, and Cephas?” (1 Cor. 9:4–5).

And:

> “If others share in this authority over you, do not we all the more?” (1 Cor. 9:12).

Likewise he pierces particular individuals with his pen:

> “Therefore let him who thinks that he stands take care lest he fall” (1 Cor. 10:12);

and:

> “If anyone appears contentious, we have no such custom, nor does the Church of the Lord” (1 Cor. 11:16).

With such a concluding clause woven out of a curse—

> “If anyone does not love the Lord Jesus, let him be anathema. *Maranatha* (‘Our Lord, come!’)” (1 Cor. 16:22)—

he surely struck someone.

But I shall take my stand especially where the apostle burns most fiercely, where the fornicator himself also caused trouble for others:

> “As though I were not coming to you, some have become puffed up. But I shall come to you soon, if the Lord permits, and I shall learn not the speech of those who are puffed up, but their power. For the kingdom of God is not in speech but in power. And what do you wish? Shall I come to you with a rod, or in a spirit of gentleness?” (1 Cor. 4:18–21).

For what lay beneath this?

> “Fornication is actually reported among you, and such fornication as is not found even among the Gentiles: that someone has his father’s wife. And you are puffed up and have not rather mourned, so that the man who committed such a deed might be removed from your midst!” (1 Cor. 5:1–2).

For whom were they to mourn? Surely for one dead. To whom were they to mourn? Surely to the Lord. In what manner was he to be removed from their midst? Surely not by being placed outside the Church; for they would not ask from God something that lay within the presiding officer’s duty. Rather, through death—this death being both common to all and proper to the flesh itself—his very flesh, which was already a corpse, already a sheath wasting away in irrecoverable impurity, ought to be removed more fully from the Church.

> **Note [Col. 1007D].** “How he might be removed,” Pamelius.—“To the Lord, that in what manner,” La Cerda and Leopold.

> **Note [Col. 1007D].** “Placed outside the Church.” This means nothing other than excommunication, or “divine censure,” as the same author says in chapter 39 of the *Apology*: that is, when certain persons, because of their crimes, are wholly forbidden the communion of the brethren. Concerning this censure, in Act III of the Council of Chalcedon, in the council’s letter to Dioscorus, it is written: *keleuei hē theosebeia hymōn tois epitimiois tois ekklēsiastikois chrēsōmetha kat’ autou* (κελεύει ἡ θεοσέβεια ὑμῶν τοῖς ἐπιτιμίοις τοῖς ἐκκλησιαστικοῖς χρησώμεθα κατ’ αὐτοῦ), “Your piety demands that we employ an ecclesiastical penalty against him.” —LE PR.

> **Note [Col. 1007D].** “Of his own flesh, which was already a corpse, already a sheath wasting away in irrecoverable impurity.” He describes the body as though it were the sheath of a most worthless soul, thoroughly polluted by the foul filth of crimes. —RIG.—“Wasting away in irrecoverable impurity.” He calls that impurity irrecoverable from which nothing can be recovered: impurity, namely, curable by no art. Thus below, where he speaks of the curable impurity of leprosy, he uses the terms “recovery” and “restoration.” —RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1007D].** Others read “a captive” (*captivum*).—La Cerda reads “that which was corruptible.”

And therefore, in the manner in which he could meanwhile be removed, Paul judged that such a person must be delivered to Satan for the destruction of the flesh. For the curse followed the flesh that was being cast out to the devil, so that it might be discharged from service under the sacrament of blessing, never to return to the camp of the Church.

We therefore see in this passage the apostle’s severity divided between a certain puffed-up man and a certain incestuous man, armed against the one with a rod and against the other with a sentence: with the rod that he threatened, and the sentence that he executed; the former still flashing, the latter immediately striking like lightning; by the one he rebuked, by the other he condemned.

> **Note [Col. 1007D].** “Which” (*qua*) in Oberthür and Semler.

And it is certain that from that time the man merely rebuked trembled beneath the threat of the rod, whereas the condemned man perished beneath the immediate infliction of punishment: the former, fearing the blow, immediately withdrew; the latter paid the penalty.

When the letters of the same apostle to the Corinthians mention pardon a second time, pardon is indeed granted—but it is uncertain to whom, because neither the person nor the cause is specified.

> **Note [Col. 1008D].** “Let it be” (*sit*) in Rigault and Leopold.

I shall compare the matter with the relevant meanings. If the incestuous man is put forward, the puffed-up man will also be found there. Certainly the account of the matter is sufficient: the puffed-up man was rebuked, whereas the incestuous man was condemned. The puffed-up man is forgiven, but as one rebuked; the incestuous man does not appear to have been forgiven, since he was condemned.

> **Note [Col. 1008D].** “The reasoning of the matter,” Pamelius; “relation,” Gelenius.

If pardon was granted to the man whose being swallowed up by excessive sorrow was feared, it was the man merely rebuked who was still in danger of being swallowed up, failing beneath the threat and grieving beneath the rebuke. But the condemned man, condemned by both guilt and sentence, was already reckoned as swallowed up; he was no longer to grieve, but to suffer what he could have grieved over before suffering it.

> **Note [Col. 1008D].** “By grief,” Gelenius.

If he was forgiven so that “we might not be defrauded by Satan,” the loss being guarded against was surely in something that had not yet perished. No precaution is taken concerning what is already past, but concerning what is still safe. The condemned man, however—delivered, indeed, into Satan’s possession—had already then perished from the Church when he committed such a deed, much more when he was also cast out by the Church herself. How could Paul fear suffering the loss of one whom he had already lost after he had been seized away, and whom, once condemned, he could no longer possess?

Finally, what will it be fitting for a judge to pardon: that which he has settled by final pronouncement, or that which he has left pending by an interlocutory ruling? And this is especially true of a judge who is not accustomed to rebuild what he has destroyed, lest he be regarded as a transgressor.

Come now: even if the First Epistle had not saddened so many persons; if it had rebuked no one and terrified no one; if it had struck only the incestuous man; if it had cast no one else into fear on account of his case and had not thrown the puffed-up man into consternation—would you not more reasonably suspect, and more faithfully argue, that there was at that time some entirely different person among the Corinthians involved in the same affair, so that, having been rebuked, terrified, and now wounded by grief, he afterward received pardon because the limited measure of his offense permitted it, rather than interpret that pardon as referring to the incestuous fornicator?

You ought to have read this—even if not in the epistle itself, at least in the apostle’s own character—impressed more clearly by his sense of shame than by his pen.

> **Note [Col. 1008D].** “In the apostle’s own character.” He has used *secta* for “conduct.” Thus Justinian, near the end of the title *On Legacies*, where he treats disgraceful legacies, says: “The character of my times does not permit testamentary dispositions of this kind to stand.” Thus by “the apostle’s character” he means what he shortly afterward calls the apostle’s nature and purpose, the form and rule of his teachings. So above: “from the pattern of Peter.” —RIG.

Otherwise you would stain Paul, the apostle of Christ, teacher of the nations in faith and truth, vessel of election, founder of churches, censor of disciplines, with such fickleness that either he rashly condemned one whom he was soon to absolve, or rashly absolved one whom he had not rashly condemned—even if it had been merely for the fornication of ordinary unchastity, much more for incestuous marriage, impious lust, and parricidal desire. He does not even compare that desire with the nations, lest it be attributed to customary practice; he had judged it while absent, lest the defendant gain an interval of delay; and he had condemned it after invoking even the power of the Lord, lest it appear to be a human sentence.

He therefore made sport of his own spirit, of the angel of the Church, and of the power of the Lord if he rescinded what he had pronounced in accordance with their counsel.

> **Note [Col. 1009D].** “Of the angel of the Church.” As though an angel presided over the congregations of the Church—unless one prefers to refer it to the bishop of Corinth, using the same phrase in which the blessed apostle John writes to the angels of the seven churches in Revelation 2 and 3. —PAM.

## CHAPTER XV

[Col. 1009B]

Even if you take the following passages of that epistle as extending the apostle’s denunciation, they too will not be reconcilable with the erasure of the incest, lest here also the apostle be put to shame by the inconsistency of his later sentiments.

For what sense would it make that, immediately after granting an incestuous fornicator the right of return to ecclesiastical peace, he should at once press upon them the rejection of impurities, the cutting away of stains, and the encouragement of holiness, as though he had decreed nothing contrary to these principles shortly before?

Consider, finally, whether it belongs to the same man to say:

> “Therefore, having this ministry according as we have obtained mercy, we do not lose heart, but renounce the hidden things of disgrace” (2 Cor. 4:1–2),

when he had revoked the condemnation of a deed that was not merely disgraceful but openly criminal;

> **Note [Col. 1009D].** “Had condemned beforehand,” La Cerda.

> **Note [Col. 1010D].** “Had revoked the condemnation” (*dedamnaverit*): had rescinded the sentence of condemnation. Below he will say “had restored,” that is, had reincorporated the man into the Church. —RIG.

whether it belongs to the same man to excuse any act of unchastity when, among the titles of his labors, after distress and oppression, after fasts and vigils, he also proclaimed chastity (2 Cor. 6:5 ff.);

> **Note [Col. 1009D].** The word “is” (*sit*) is absent from the Paris edition.

whether it belongs to the same man to receive every reprobate into communion when he writes:

> “For what fellowship has righteousness with iniquity? What communion has light with darkness? What concord has Christ with Belial? Or what portion has the faithful with the faithless? Or what agreement has the temple of God with idols?” (2 Cor. 6:14–16).

Will he not consistently have to hear: “And how do you separate the things that you joined above by restoring the incestuous man?” For when that man is reincorporated into the Church, righteousness is joined in fellowship with iniquity, darkness communes with light, Belial accords with Christ, and the faithless shares the sacraments with the faithful. And let the idols look to themselves: the very defiler of God’s temple enters God’s temple.

For here too he says:

> “For you are the temple of the living God; for he says, ‘I shall dwell in you and walk among you, and I shall be their God, and they shall be my people. Therefore depart from their midst, be separate, and do not touch what is unclean’” (2 Cor. 6:16–17).

You unfold this passage too, O Apostle, at the very moment when you yourself extend your hand to so great a whirlpool of impurities; and you add still further:

> “Therefore, beloved, having this promise, let us cleanse ourselves from every defilement of flesh and spirit, perfecting chastity in the fear of God” (2 Cor. 7:1).

> **Note [Col. 1010D].** “Of faith” in Gelenius.

I ask you: had the man who drives such principles into our minds recalled some fornicator into the Church? Or does he write them precisely so that he may not now appear to you to have recalled one?

These passages ought both to prescribe the interpretation of what precedes and to prejudge what follows. For near the end of the epistle he says:

> “Lest, when I come again, my God humble me, and I mourn many of those who sinned before and did not repent of the impurity, fornication, and vileness that they committed” (2 Cor. 12:21).

He surely does not determine that those whom he was going to find in the Church should be received if they had entered upon repentance, but that they should be shunned and, without doubt, expelled, so that they might forfeit their repentance.

> **Note [Col. 1010D].** “So that they might forfeit their repentance.” Without any hope that the Church would ever be restored to them: such as those tears, fasting from peace, of which he spoke above, which would bring back from the Church’s bosom nothing more than the public disclosure of disgrace. —RIG.

Besides, it is not appropriate to claim that he revealed anything here about communion, since above he had denied communion between light and darkness and fellowship between righteousness and iniquity.

But all these people are ignorant of the apostle when they understand anything contrary to the nature and purpose of the man himself, contrary to the form and rule of his teaching: a teacher, even by his own example, of all holiness; an execrator and purifier of all impurity; and everywhere such a man that one would sooner presume him to have restored the Church to an incestuous man than to some offender whose guilt was more ordinarily human.

> **Note [Col. 1010D].** “To some offender whose guilt was more ordinarily human.” That is, whose sin might appear to have been committed through human frailty. —RIG.
