This was the reading in the editions published up to now. Hence someone might say that this argument of Tertullian against second marriages, drawn from the examples of women “who, under no pressure arising from beauty or youth, prefer holiness to husbands,” is invalid. For what wonder is it if ugly women and those already old persevere in widowhood? They are chaste because no one has asked them; indeed, it would be an extraordinary example if any woman afflicted by this misfortune were to find another husband.

But the Author’s own hand, most attentive to the precision of his style, wrote otherwise. The ancient manuscripts have: “Against these counsels of the flesh, set the examples of our sisters whose names are known to the Lord, who allow no occasion of beauty or youth, after sending their husbands on before them, to take precedence over holiness.” He uses the examples of women whose husbands have already been received into heaven. For by “those sent on before,” he means husbands who went before their wives to God and died before them. Such women, I say, although beautiful and still flourishing in youth after their husbands have been received into heaven, nevertheless do not put forward beauty or youth as an excuse, nor do they allow themselves to be flattered by them; instead, they preserve the integrity of their flesh in honor and remembrance of their one marriage.

See how one who has done good to a widow is regarded as an equal; see how highly the widow herself is valued, since her champion has reasoned with the Lord [Note: [Col. 1288C] “Her champion with the Lord.” An *assertor* is an avenger who claims someone for freedom; here it means a patron who undertakes to protect a person. RHEN.] [Note: [Col. 1287B] “Will reason,” Franequer, Parisin.; “will reason with the Lord,” Rigalt.]!

I do not think so much has been granted to virgins, although in them unimpaired integrity and complete holiness will see the face of God from close at hand. Yet the widow possesses something more laborious, because it is easy not to seek what you do not know and to reject what you have never desired. More glorious is the continence that feels its own right and knows what it has experienced. A virgin may be regarded as happier, but a widow as more laborious: the former because she has always possessed what is good, the latter because she has discovered what is good for herself. In the one, grace is crowned; in the other [Col. 1288A], virtue.

For some things belong to divine generosity, others to our own exertion. Those bestowed by the Lord are governed by his grace; those sought by a human being are accomplished through diligence. Strive, therefore, for the virtue of continence—and modesty [Note: [Col. 1288B] Rig. omits “modesty”]—which provides for chastity; for diligence, which has no dealings with trifles; and for frugality, which despises the world [Note: [Col. 1288B] “Mirror,” Rig.].

Pursue companionship [Note: [Col. 1288B] “Companions and conversations,” Rig.] and associations worthy of God, remembering that verse sanctified by the Apostle:

> Bad company corrupts good morals.

## CHAPTER IX

Talkative, idle, wine-bibbing, and inquisitive companions are especially harmful to a resolution of widowhood. Through talkativeness, words hostile to modesty creep in; through idleness, they lead one away from seriousness [Note: [Col. 1288B] “They lead away from truth,” Fran.; “they introduce complacency,” Jun., Paris.]; through drunkenness, they insinuate every kind of evil; through inquisitiveness, they convey rivalry in lust.

No woman of this kind knows how to speak [Note: [Col. 1288C] “Was unwilling,” Rig., incorrectly.] of the good of having one husband. For their god, as the Apostle says, is their belly (Phil. 3:19), and likewise those parts that are near the belly.

These things I now commend to you, dearest fellow servant. They have indeed been treated more fully after the Apostle, but they will also serve as consolation to you, because, if events turn out so, you will keep my memory present in them.

# BOOK II

[Col. 1287]

## [SUMMARY]

[Col. 1287C] **SUMMARY.—**This book has nearly the same subject as the preceding one, except that the Author, mindful of human frailty, exhorts his wife that, if she should ever contemplate a second marriage, she should abstain from marriage with Gentiles, as something unlawful, resembling fornication, harmful to faith, inclining toward unbelief, and alien to the Church’s sacrament of marriage, which is magnificently described. EDD.

[Col. 1287D] The first book, therefore, teaches:

I. That marriage, instituted by God, is one, chapters 1–2.  
II. That abstinence is better than marriage, chapter 3.  
III. Examples of continent widows, chapter 4.  
IV. That it is not expedient to enter a second marriage for the sake of having children, chapter 5.  
V. Examples of persons abstaining from marriage, chapter 6.  
VI. That second marriages are less pleasing to God, chapter 7.  
VII. To these he adds, as a conclusion, a eulogy of widowhood.

[Col. 1288D] In the second book it is established:

I. That marriage with a Gentile is forbidden, chapters 1–2.  
II. That it is unclean, like fornication, chapter 3.  
III. That it is an impediment to the duties of true religion, chapter 4.  
IV. That it encourages Gentile rites, chapter 6.  
V. But that in the Church marriage is a great and holy sacrament, which the Church brings about, the offering confirms, the blessing seals, the angels announce, and the Father ratifies, chapter 8. EDD., from D. SCHRAM.
