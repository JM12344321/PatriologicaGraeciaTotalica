Eusebius also presents to us a fragment of a letter from Dionysius, bishop of the Corinthians, to the Romans, in which he praises their custom—originating from the very beginning of the Christian religion and enlarged by Soter, their bishop—whereby they relieved, through various kinds of benefactions, the poverty of Christians, including those living in other cities and those condemned to the mines (Euseb., *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, [Col. 0954A] chap. 33, p. 145). Indeed, Cyprian also wrote these words to the presbyters and deacons:

> As regards providing funds either for those who, having confessed God with a glorious voice, have been placed in prison, or for those who suffer as poor and needy yet nevertheless persevere in the Lord, I ask that nothing be lacking, since the entire sum that has been collected has been distributed there among the clergy for such contingencies, so that several of them might have the means to minister to the necessities and afflictions of individuals.  
> (Cyprian, epist. 5)

He also established in the following terms how the needs of the indigent were to be relieved by this contribution and these alms:

> But if such a person pleads destitution and the necessity of poverty, his need too can be relieved among the others who are supported by the provisions of the Church, provided, however, that he is content with more frugal but innocent [Col. 0954B] food.  
> (Idem, epist. 2, to Eucratius)

In this manner, then, and for this reason, the money offered in the synaxes and deposited with the clergy was distributed to the needy persons who have been named.

For this reason Tertullian extols so highly this charity of the Christians, through which they held everything in common except their wives, just as he inveighs against the pagans who, as we showed above, divided everything through avarice and insatiable greed (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 471). Nor is it surprising, he says, if such great charity is *conviolatur*—“is violated”—by men so avaricious and unimbued with the Christian religion, as the word is written in our older manuscripts and in the old editions; in the fourth, however, it is simply *violatur*, “is violated,” though always with the same sense. Yet Rigaltius, contrary to the evidence of all the manuscripts, printed *convivatur*, [Col. 0954C] that is, “Christian charity holds a banquet.” But this correction is supported by the authority of no manuscript. Moreover, Tertullian is speaking there of fraternal charity, so violated by the pagans that they branded the Christians’ little suppers, by which it was especially nourished, as extravagant. But these must now be discussed.

## ARTICLE III. On the supper given in the synaxes of the Christians and the prayers preceding it; and, against the shameless slanders of the pagans, a demonstration of how frugal, sober, and modest it was

The pagans knew very well that the Christians dined in their synaxes, but they certainly did not know with what great modesty, frugality, and piety this supper was furnished. Therefore, always inflamed with [Col. 0954D] their customary unbridled fury against those same Christians, “Besides branding our little suppers as criminal,” says Tertullian, “they censured them as extravagant” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 473). Thus this accusation had two counts. The first was that those suppers were infamous for crimes—namely, the murder of an infant, the dreadful eating of its flesh, and the most shameful incest. But our Author utterly overthrows this wholly false accusation, as we shall see below. The second charge, that of extravagance, he demolishes by a plain and entirely truthful account of that supper. Please do not find it burdensome, then, to hear him relate it and to examine his words carefully with us. He speaks as follows:

> Our supper reveals the rationale [Col. 0955A] of its name: it is called by what among the Greeks means love.

In the old editions the reading is: “It is called [non-Latin text omitted], which among the Greeks means love” (Tert., *Apol.*, chap. 39, p. 473). Did the editors of those editions, then, add this Greek word, which scribes ignorant of Greek had not dared to write? Yet in another book it is found written in Latin, where Tertullian himself writes the following about martyrs sent to prison:

> The flesh does not lose what is its due, through the care of the Church and the *agape* of the brethren.  
> (Idem, *To the Martyrs*, chap. 2)

There, however, that noun is taken in its proper signification, for charity and love. Yet in his *Apologeticum*, as also in Origen (Origen, *Against Celsus*, book I), cited by us elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 715), the Christians’ supper is called by this name, because in it their mutual love [Col. 0955B] and charity were especially conspicuous.

That supper, therefore, given as a pledge of this love, could certainly not have been wanton or extravagant. Yet even if it had contained something lavish and costly, it should not therefore have been reproached by the pagans. For however costly it might have been, the needy were assisted by it. But, as our Author most aptly observes:

> It is a gain for piety to incur expense.  
> (Tertullian, *Apol.*, chap. 39, p. 473)

So far, however, were the Christians from incurring very great expense upon this supper that it was provided with the utmost frugality and modesty. Indeed, it admitted nothing intemperate or immoderate, or, as Tertullian says, “nothing base, nothing immodest” (Ibid., p. 477). Now the word *vile* is sometimes taken to mean sordid and abject, and sometimes also abundant; in this latter sense [Col. 0955C] Virgil said, “and the plentiful kidney bean” (Virgil, *Georgics* I, v. 257), that is, as Servius observes, abundant. For what is abundant is cheap, as in: “or loads [the table] with cheap fruits.” But if our Author took the word in this latter signification, his meaning will be that there was nothing superfluous or excessive in the Christians’ supper. It seems easier to us, however, to accept that the adjective *vile* is employed in the former sense, for sordid and abject. For he contrasts the Christians’ supper with the banquets of the pagans, into which infamous parasites, the basest of all men, were admitted, as he puts it, “to be fattened upon insults.” Nor was any immodesty seen in it; and it differed completely from the feasts of the pagans and the Bacchanals mentioned by him in the same place, matters that we have discussed elsewhere and must discuss again below.

[Col. 0955D] Moreover, “No one reclines at the Christians’ sacred table,” says Tertullian, “before a prayer to God is first tasted” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 477). He had already said earlier that they assembled in order to pray to and entreat God, as we observed. Here, then, he speaks of the prayers that they were accustomed to place before the supper. From Justin Martyr, however, we learn that they were poured forth after the reading of sacred Scripture and the presiding minister’s exhortation, but before the bread, wine, and water were offered.

Tertullian continues: “As much is eaten as the hungry can take” (Ibid.); this is indeed the reading of our manuscripts and of the old printed editions. It displeased Rigaltius, however, and on his own authority he wished *capiunt*, “can take,” to be written *cupiunt*, “desire,” [Col. 0956A] doubtless because he thought the meaning there was that no one was compelled to eat or drink more or less. But what if our Author’s meaning is that the hungry then ate not beyond due measure, as he himself says later (Ibid., chap. 42, p. 491), or improperly, but only as much as they could take to allay hunger moderately, so that they were led to take food and drink by necessity, not by pleasure? For he immediately added: “As much is drunk as is useful to the chaste” (Ibid., chap. 39, p. 477), that is, with such sobriety that chastity suffers no injury from it.

With this temperance in food and drink, therefore, “they are so satisfied,” he says (Ibid.), that is, they use them so temperately, “as those who remember that even during the night they must worship God.” Elsewhere we have made it manifest to everyone [Col. 0956B] (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation 1, chap. 6, p. 712 ff.) that it was then an established custom and practice for Christians to rise during the night to pray to God. Therefore he wrote to his wife:

> Will you escape notice . . . when you rise even during the night to pray?  
> (Tertullian, *To His Wife*, book II, chap. 5)

Yet in that passage of the *Apologeticum* the discussion does not concern every night, but only those on which the feast days of the sacred synaxes were celebrated. Finally, during the supper, he says, the Christians “converse as those who know that God hears” (Idem, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 477) whatever they discussed among themselves concerning divine matters.

He adds further that hymns were afterward sung to God, and from this, he says, “it is proved how he who sang had drunk” (Ibid.), that is, with what sobriety [Col. 0956C] and temperance he had drunk. Who, from this entirely candid description of the Christian supper, would not understand how moderate, frugal, and devout it was?

## ARTICLE IV. On what was done after supper in the synaxes of the Christians; on water for the hands; on the lighting of lamps and the hour of the synaxes; on songs and hymns; on thanksgiving and the Christians’ departure

After the supper had been given and concluded in the manner we have explained, Tertullian relates three further things done by the Christians in these words:

> After water for the hands and the lamps, each person, according as he is able, is invited into the midst to sing to God either from the holy Scriptures or from his own composition.  
> (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 477)

First, therefore, [Col. 0956D] he noted the water for the hands, which was of course provided for washing them. This custom had become prevalent not only among Christians but also among pagans. Athenaeus relates that before and after supper the Greeks provided water for washing the hands, and that the first was called by the ancients [non-Latin text omitted], the second [non-Latin text omitted] (Athenaeus, *Deipnosophists*, book XI, chap. 18, p. 408; idem, book IV, chap. 1, p. 128, and chap. 2, p. 129). He confirms this with various testimonies from writers. Earlier, moreover, he published a fragment of a letter of Hippolochus, who had written that the guests, when filled with food, washed their hands. Lampridius likewise recorded that courses were served by Heliogabalus to parasites in such a way that they drank with each one and washed their hands as though they had eaten (Lampridius, *Life of Heliogabalus*, [Col. 0957A] p. 343). Thus the custom of washing the hands after supper, being decorous, was also observed by Christians. See Theodoret and others (Theodoret, *Ecclesiastical History*, book I, chap. 18).

The two words of Tertullian that follow—“and the lamps”—will doubtless seem more difficult of explanation to many. We are certainly aware that some have noted that Wouwer’s manuscript reads “to the lamps”; but that manuscript alone should not be preferred to all the others. Our Author undoubtedly wished there to notify us that lamps were then lit, because the Christians assembled toward evening and ate supper after the day had already ended and after all the things we have related had been done. Thus these lamps were lit out of necessity and to dispel the darkness of night, as he indicates elsewhere (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, [Col. 0957B] chap. 7, p. 306). But we have already made some observations about them elsewhere and shall make further observations later (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 25, art. 2).

Someone, however, will doubtless object to us: If these Christian synaxes were convened toward evening and they began their supper at the commencement of night, why does Tertullian himself elsewhere call these assemblies “before dawn”? Why did he say in another book:

> We receive the sacrament of the Eucharist even at assemblies before dawn.  
> (Tertullian, *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chap. 3)

He testifies that they were called by no other name by Pliny the Younger in his letter to the emperor Trajan. For in it, as Tertullian himself reported, Pliny had written that he had discovered nothing in the rites of those Christians except “assemblies before dawn for singing to Christ and to God” (Idem, *Apologeticum*, [Col. 0957C] chap. 2, p. 273). Indeed, these assemblies are again called “nocturnal convocations” by Tertullian:

> Who will willingly endure his wife being taken from his side for nocturnal convocations, if occasion should require it?

And a little later:

> Will the husband not know what you taste in secret before all food, and indeed in the morning hours? And if he knows it to be bread, he will not believe it is the bread that it is said to be.  
> (Idem, *To His Wife*, book II, chap. 4)

In a similar manner Cyprian also wrote these words to Caecilius:

> Unless someone fears this in the morning celebrations, lest through the taste of wine he smell of the blood of Christ.  
> (Cyprian, epist. 63, to Caecilius)

In several other manuscripts, however, the passage reads as follows:

> Someone fears this, lest he offer wine, lest through the taste of the wine, with its fragrance spreading, the smell of wine should be detected by an unbeliever’s awareness in the morning hours, [Col. 0957D] and he should be recognized as a Christian, while we commemorate the blood of Christ in the offering of wine.

Some suppose that the knot of this difficulty can be untied by distinguishing two kinds of synaxes. One, they say, was that in which the Eucharist was received by fasting Christians very early in the morning. The other, they think, was the one under discussion, which they called the *agape* and which was ordinarily begun toward evening. But Socrates and Sozomen appear to tell against this answer, for they assert that Christians did not everywhere observe the same custom in holding their assemblies. They relate that in some Churches synaxes were held on the Sabbath, in others on Sunday; likewise, in some the sacred mysteries were received [Col. 0958A] toward evening by persons who had eaten, but in others before any food (Socrates, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chap. 2, p. 287; Sozomen, *Ecclesiastical History*, book VII, chap. 17, p. 735). If someone says that they are speaking of their own time rather than of the most ancient period, including the time of our Tertullian, he will neither prove this easily nor accomplish much by it. For Tertullian, no less than Justin Martyr, as is plain from what we noted above, argues against pagans who cried out, as falsely as audaciously, that utterly horrendous crimes were perpetrated in the synaxes of Christians. Does he not, then, speak plainly of all the synaxes, in which he briefly and truthfully sets forth what was done there? It is also certain that at certain solemnities, such as the feast of Easter, Christians kept vigil throughout the entire night. For Tertullian wrote to his wife:

> Who will calmly endure her spending the night away at the solemn observances [Col. 0958B] of Easter?  
> (Tertullian, *To His Wife*, book II, chap. 4)

But in the *Apologeticum* he discusses not only these but also other synaxes, which Christians were accustomed to prolong far into the night. There is therefore nothing to prevent their being called assemblies before dawn and nocturnal convocations.

Thirdly, Tertullian relates that after that Christian supper, “each person, according as he is able, is invited into the midst to sing to God either from the holy Scriptures or from his own composition” (Idem, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 477). Thus, after the table was removed, each person was invited to sing, in the midst of the whole gathering and circle, some hymn either taken from sacred Scripture or composed by his own talent in honor of God. Cyprian exhorts Donatus to join in singing psalms one day in the following [Col. 0958C] words:

> And since there is now holiday repose and the time is free, let us gladly spend what remains of this day, now that the sun is declining toward evening; nor let even the hour of the banquet be without heavenly grace. Let the sober banquet resound with psalms; and since your memory is retentive and your voice melodious, undertake this customary office. You will feed your dearest friends the more if we have spiritual hearing; let religious sweetness delight their ears.  
> (Cyprian, *To Donatus*, at the end)

If anyone objects that Cyprian is not speaking there about sacred synaxes, what will he achieve by that? For if psalms were sung at ordinary suppers, how much more ought they to have been sung in sacred synaxes? And certainly Pliny, cited by Tertullian himself (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 467), had written to Trajan, as we saw earlier, that he had detected no other [Col. 0958D] offense in the Christians’ assemblies before dawn except that they sang a hymn to Christ as to God. We have discussed these hymns elsewhere (*Apparatus*, vol. I, pp. 413, 656, and 658).

Moreover, the end of this banquet was such as its beginning had been. “For likewise,” says Tertullian, “prayer brings the banquet to an end.” Thus, when the singing of psalms, songs, or hymns was finished, the Christians again poured forth prayers to give thanks to God. “Finally, they depart from there”—the words are still Tertullian’s—“with the same care for modesty and chastity, as persons who have partaken not so much of a supper as of discipline” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 477), that is, a rule for living piously and holily, and the divine and spiritual teaching upon which they had been fed. [Col. 0959A] But we have dealt elsewhere with this modest, religious, and chaste departure of the Christians from the church (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 713).

## ARTICLE V. How the things said about the synaxes of the Christians may be confirmed from the assemblies of the Therapeutae

Philo the Jew, and Eusebius after him, recorded many things concerning the assemblies of the Therapeutae similar to what we have thus far seen done in the synaxes of the Christians. Since these accounts can shed mutual light upon one another, we think it will be unwelcome to no one, and perhaps not wholly useless to many, if by a brief account we set before everyone’s eyes what [Col. 0959B] was done by those Therapeutae. For whether they observed the Mosaic law, as Philo thinks, or the Christian law, as Eusebius believed, or both together, as certain more recent scholars have held, their rites can certainly give clear confirmation to what Tertullian reported concerning the Christians.

Philo the Jew relates that for these Therapeutae, whom Eusebius considers disciples of Mark the Evangelist, the seventh day of every week was festive and sacred, but especially the one that recurred after seven weeks (Philo, *On the Contemplative Life*, pp. 893, 899 ff.; Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, chap. 16 ff., p. 53 ff.). On that day they all, both men and women, assembled at a signal in one place, entered upon a banquet, and, raising [Col. 0959C] their eyes and hands to heaven, prayed that it might be pleasing to God. When the prayers were completed, they reclined according to each person’s rank, yet in such a way that the men occupied the right side and the women—nearly all of whom were virgins by their own choice—the left. Nothing was served at this banquet except bread for food, salt as relish, and, for those of more delicate taste, hyssop as seasoning; while everyone had water alone to drink, either cold or, for the elderly, warm, since they regarded wine as poison. Throughout the entire banquet there was the profoundest silence on the part of all, while only one person either proposed some question from the sacred Scriptures or resolved one that had been proposed.

Afterward the person presiding over the assembly led a hymn composed by himself or taken from the sacred prophets; each person followed him in his proper and fitting order, while the others [Col. 0959D] remained silent, raising their voices without distinction of sex only at the final refrain of the hymn. Then, when the table had been removed, they all arose and, led by the cantor, sang hymns in honor of God, now with one voice, now antiphonally, moving their steps sometimes forward, sometimes backward, or to the right or left, until finally they all formed a single chorus together. At last, after they had passed the night in this manner, they turned their faces and their entire bodies toward the east, prayed there for a happy and auspicious day, and each returned home.

It is no part of our present undertaking to determine whether these Therapeutae were Jews or Christians as well as Jews, or to settle the considerable dispute [Col. 0960A] that has arisen among the learned on this subject and that does not yet seem to us to have been resolved. For our purpose it is sufficient to note that Eusebius acknowledges that the things we have seen described by Philo were observed by Christians: [non-Latin text omitted]. “And especially the all-night vigils of the great solemnity, the pious exercises performed in them, and the hymns customarily recited by us” (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, chap. 17, p. 57).

Nor indeed should anyone object that Eusebius erred in taking these Therapeutae, who were actually Jews and professed the Jewish law, for Christians. For even if he did suffer some human error there, no one can deny that the sacred rites of the Christians observed in the Churches in his own and even the preceding age were known to him. Yet he plainly affirms that there were many [Col. 0960B] similar sacred exercises, rites, and vigils in the assemblies of the Christians and of the Therapeutae.

Moreover, from what has thus far been discussed concerning the supper and synaxes of those same Christians, the utterly shameless slander of the pagans—whereby they falsely asserted that these were both extravagant and infamous for horrendous crimes—ought to be entirely manifest to everyone. For so enormous and shameless a lie is utterly overthrown by Tertullian’s simple and truthful description of these synaxes, a description fully confirmed by others.

Furthermore, “since the good meet in these synaxes,” says our Author, “since the pious and the chaste assemble, it must be called not a faction but a *curia*” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 39, p. 477). It was not, indeed, an unlawful and seditious faction, such as that of the pagans was accustomed to be, as we shall say below; [Col. 0960C] rather, it was a *curia* in which, as Varro and Festus say, public care for sacred affairs was exercised (Varro, *On the Latin Language*, book V, § 6; Festus, s.v. *Curia*), and which, to use Cicero’s words, was “the avenger of rashness and the regulator of duty” (Cicero, *For Flaccus*, p. 367, line 28). There, if anyone had previously sinned in any respect, he was chastised according to his deserts; and the presidents diligently taught and prudently regulated the pious duties of every individual. What, then, could be more absurd and false than to accuse those sacred congregations of being an unlawful faction, when Christians, as Tertullian rightly asserts, harmed no one and saddened no one except unto salutary repentance, as the Apostle says, if any had perhaps fallen through human frailty or a perverse disposition into some grave crime (Tertullian, loc. cit.)? [Col. 0960D] But certainly very few such persons were found. “For all the rest of us,” Tertullian further says, “when gathered in such holy synaxes, are the same as when dispersed; all together the same as each individually” (Ibid.): namely, upright, good, pious, and chaste, as we have thus far demonstrated more fully and as will be confirmed in the following article.

## ARTICLE VI. The argument of the pagans against the pious and chaste conduct of the Christians is refuted, an argument drawn from the fact that some Christians had fallen away from the discipline of morals, from doctrine, and from the Christian religion itself

Against what had been argued concerning the most holy synaxes and conduct of the Christians—matters that could not have been unknown to anyone—the pagans [Col. 0961A] audaciously rose up and objected to us, as Tertullian puts it, “that certain of our people depart from the rule of discipline” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 46, p. 513). Christians could depart from it in three ways: first, through certain crimes and corrupt conduct; second, through errors and heresy; third, through shameful desertion of the entire Christian religion itself and return to the impious worship of the gods.

As concerns the first, since the pagans’ grossly unjust detraction had been more than sufficiently refuted by what we have thus far related, Tertullian judged that nothing further or more specific needed to be said in the *Apologeticum*. In his book *To the Nations*, however, he examines and refutes that charge:

> But among you we are said to be the worst and most disgraceful in avarice, luxury, [Col. 0961B] and wickedness.  
> (Idem, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 5, p. 565)

Thus indeed they were accustomed to slander the Christians. But what does Tertullian reply?

> We shall not deny that some are.

Thus he admits and does not at all conceal that there were some Christians stained by certain crimes. But what follows from that against the rest, and much more against the Christian religion? Absolutely nothing, he says; indeed, this very fact does much to establish both its truth and its holiness. “For even this suffices,” he says, “as testimony to our name and to the perfect religion, if not all, if not the majority” are such (Ibid.). He gives the following fine and true reason:

> It is necessary that upon a body, however sound or pure you wish it to be, some mole (*aliqui*, for *aliquis*) should sprout, or a little wart arise, or a freckle blemish it.  
> (Ibid.)

[Col. 0961C] He indeed wrote *effruticet*, “should sprout,” just as in the book *On the Soul* he says that a human being is formed from two elements, “so that the two together may sprout forth as a human being from each substance” (Idem, *On the Soul*, chap. 27), that is, arise and grow. But this small blemish makes it more evident that the whole body is beautiful, healthy, and perfect. Thus certain—but very few—Christians, like a few moles upon their entire body and community, demonstrated more clearly the supreme perfection of the Christian religion.

Moreover, those Christians who strayed from the right rule were accustomed, according to the gravity of their offenses, to be admonished, rebuked, and chastised in the public synaxes, as we saw earlier (preceding article), and sometimes to be cut off and cast out like putrid members by the sword of excommunication? For this reason our Author wrote elsewhere to Scapula:

> We do not deny a deposit entrusted to us; we violate no one’s marriage; [Col. 0961D] we treat orphans dutifully; we relieve the needy; we return evil for evil to no one. Let those who falsely claim our sect look to themselves—those whom we ourselves reject.  
> (Tertullian, *To Scapula*, chap. 4)

He means those who, because of their crimes, had been cast out of the sacred assemblies of the Christians whose name they falsely claimed.

Others vaunted themselves as professing the Christian religion—namely, heretics—who, from the one way, as he says, of doctrine and truth, split off many crooked and inextricable paths and drove themselves and others headlong into the abyss of errors and heresies. But these, he says, arose “from the seed of the philosophers,” from whose “talents,” as he said elsewhere, “every heresy is animated,” [Col. 0962A] and elsewhere again, “heresies are suborned” (Idem, *Apologeticum*, chap. 47, p. 519; idem, *Against Marcion*, book I, chap. 13; *On Prescription*, chap. 7; idem, *Against Hermogenes*, chap. 8). For this reason philosophers are called by him more than once, and later by Jerome, “the patriarchs of the heretics” (Jerome, epist. 43 to Ctesiphon). And just as the philosophers distorted sacred Scripture of the Old Testament, as we observed above, so the heretics adulterated “this new-fledged equipment of ours”—that is, the books of the New Testament—“with their opinions toward philosophical propositions” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 47, p. 519); that is, they attempted to accommodate the doctrine handed down in the sacred books of the New Law to philosophical opinions. But by perversely mixing falsehoods with truths, [Col. 0962B] they departed from truth itself and adulterated the true teachings.

“But readily and plainly,” he continues, “we enter a prescription against our adulterators,” or, in other words, oppose an exception to these heretics who corrupt sacred Scripture, and refute them even by this argument alone: “that there is one rule of truth, which comes from Christ through his companions, to whom the various commentators will be proved considerably posterior” (Ibid., p. 519). He therefore defines most plainly that there is one sole rule of true religion, which was delivered to us by Christ and transmitted through his companions, the Apostles and first disciples, from whom we received it both in writing and orally. Whatever, therefore, the heretics devised that differed from this norm of tradition proceeded neither from Christ nor from his first disciples; it is manifestly false. For they will be proved later than these.

[Col. 0962C] But by whom, I ask you, will they be proved so? By Tertullian himself? If that is the case, in these words he promised his book *On the Prescriptions of Heretics*, where he actually proved it. But it is by no means agreed among scholars whether this book was published before or after the *Apologeticum*. Could he not, therefore, have said “they will be proved,” because it was exceedingly easy for anyone to prove it?

Moreover, he openly affirms that heretics, even before a public examination of their false opinions, are convicted of error by the very fact that those opinions are new and were handed down neither by Christ nor by his disciples, but were falsely contrived by the heretics themselves. Therefore elsewhere he promises to explain these matters more fully in his book *On the Prescriptions of Heretics*, as he states more clearly in these words:

> Another little book will maintain this position against the heretics, who are to be convicted [Col. 0962D] even without a reconsideration of their doctrines, because they are such on the grounds of a prescription of novelty.  
> (Idem, *Against Marcion*, book I, chap. 1)

The pagans, therefore, could draw absolutely no conclusion from the corruption of the heretics against the truth and excellence of the Christian religion. For, having been wholly cut off from the Catholic Church, they were Christians no longer except in name—indeed, not even in name. I would have you hear Tertullian himself speaking:

> If they are heretics, they cannot be Christians, since they do not derive from Christ what, in accordance with their own choice, they have followed, thereby assuming the names of heretics.  
> (Idem, *On the Prescription of Heretics*, chap. 37)

For this reason the Christian emperors [Col. 0963A] wished them to be “called by the name of their own author, whose crime they imitated in deserting God” (*Theodosian Code*, title *On Heretics*, law 65, p. 190, vol. VI), or, as Constantine the Great said in Socrates, “that they should also be called by the name of those whose conduct they have followed” (Socrates, book I, chap. 9, p. 32). If you ask the reason, receive it from Cyprian:

> Just as the devil is not Christ, although he deceives in his name, so neither can that person be regarded as a Christian who does not remain in the truth of the Gospel and of its faith.  
> (Cyprian, *On the Unity of the Church*)

You see how strong against heretics of every kind is this argument drawn from prescriptions, or from the ancient tradition of Christ’s disciples. For Tertullian plainly demonstrates that the pagans could establish absolutely nothing against the perfection of the Christian [Col. 0963B] religion from the most wicked errors of those heretics, since they had so corrupted Christ’s true doctrine that they were considered unworthy even of the name of Christians.

Finally there was a third class of Christians, deserters and apostates from their faith, who had returned to the impious worship of the gods like dogs to their vomit. But the pagans vainly supposed that anything could be inferred from the guilt of these apostates against the Christian religion. For those who had violated the faith they had pledged to Christ were no longer Christians and could not be called by that name. Hence Tertullian addresses the pagans in these words:

> Such persons neither assemble nor share with us, having become yours again after their offenses, since we do not even mingle with those whom [Col. 0963C] your violence and savagery have driven to denial.  
> (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 5)

How true this is everyone will readily understand from the things that Cyprian and our other ancient writers repeatedly recorded concerning the lapsed, who had renounced the Christian faith under the threats and tortures of tyrants. But a more suitable occasion for discussing them will be given to us. Tertullian therefore rightly concludes, once again addressing the pagans:

> Without cause you call those men Christians whom Christians themselves disown—Christians who do not know how to deny themselves, that is, the faith of Christ.  
> (Ibid.)

For true Christians, assailed by the most dreadful torments of every kind, steadfastly maintained the truth of the Christian religion even unto the cruelest death and obtained the crown of martyrdom. But what our Author [Col. 0963D] has handed down concerning these most holy martyrs must now be explained.

# CHAPTER XVI. On Christian Martyrs

## ARTICLE I. By what and how various and horrendous tortures Christians were tormented and slaughtered by the pagans; and why they were called *Sarmenticii* and *Semaxii*

No virtue of the Christians doubtless shone more brightly than their utterly unconquerable patience, constancy, and courage in enduring punishments in defense of their religion. For the greater the pagans’ hatred of them because they despised and undermined pagan religions, the more cruel [Col. 0964A] were the afflictions and torments—hitherto devised by no one—with which they mangled and killed them. Tertullian enumerates many of their punishments, which we must not pass over without some observation. First, then, he says:

> Every day we are besieged, every day betrayed; most often we are overwhelmed in our very assemblies and congregations.  
> (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 7, p. 307)

Thus not a few but very many were overwhelmed, not rarely but very frequently, in their assemblies and elsewhere. Nor is this surprising. For the pagans, having cast aside all humanity, raged against them, partly in obedience to their own dispositions and partly to the laws. Indeed, Tertullian further says:

> How often, bypassing you—that is, the governors—the hostile mob attacks us on its own authority with stones and fires!  
> (Ibid., chap. 37, p. 461)

[Col. 0964B] That is, without awaiting any judicial sentence, the pagan rabble, in a blind assault, buried Christians beneath blows from stones or burned them alive.
