We must therefore say that our Minucius, Tertullian, and others who speak in the same manner concede the point in one sense and deny it in another. For since they were disputing against the pagans, they therefore denied that Christians [Col. 0526D] possessed temples after the manner of the Gentiles, in which they worshipped fictitious gods and believed them to dwell, or altars and sacrificial tables for slaughtering and offering victims to them; but they did possess sacred buildings and altars in which they worshipped the true God according to the Christian rite and offered an unbloody sacrifice. Nothing, therefore, could be more absurd than to claim, as Dallaeus prattled, that the quoted words of Minucius excluded the altars of “Roman men”—the spiteful name by which that heretical man designates Catholics—because he and his associates had decided, on their own authority, to reject the Eucharistic sacrifice. For we have already shown, by the entirely certain testimony of the ancient Fathers, that in Minucius’s time altars were set up in Christian temples or churches, and that the sacrifice of the holy Eucharist [Col. 0527A] was offered. Augustine later asserts this most explicitly (Augustine, *City of God*, book VIII, ch. 27, p. 217; and book X, ch. 20, p. 255).

Moreover, since Minucius’s and Tertullian’s words concerning temples, altars, and images are the same, they must certainly be explained in the same way. They therefore deny that Christians possessed any images of the kind that the pagans had constructed of their gods. That this was Minucius’s meaning is gathered not only from what has been said thus far, but also from the entire dispute between him and Caecilius. Caecilius, indeed, objected that Christians, unlike the Gentiles, possessed neither temples nor images of false gods. Minucius acknowledges that this is certainly the case. But just as he does not deny that they possessed temples other than those of fictitious gods, [Col. 0527B] so too he does not deny that they possessed images other than those which the pagans had made of those gods.

Nor should you insist that Minucius excludes absolutely all statues and images, since he replies: “What image shall I fashion for God, when, if you judge rightly, man himself is God’s image?” For he had spoken similarly about temples, as we have noted: “What temple shall I build for him, when this whole world, fashioned by his work, cannot contain him?” Yet Dallaeus and other men of the same sect are compelled to confess that these latter words do not exclude actual Christian temples. They must therefore confess likewise that the former words of the same Minucius exclude only the statues and images of false gods, which Christians could not tolerate without idolatry. [Col. 0527C] We have discussed these Christian images and statues, and their use, in our preceding dissertations; and you may read much that has been handed down by Petavius, Thomassin, and many others (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 747 ff.; Petavius, vol. V, *Dogmatic Theology*, book XV, ch. 11; and Thomassin, book XII, *Dogmatic Theology on the Incarnation*, ch. 7 ff.). What our author held concerning the worship and honor shown by Christians to Christ’s cross must be investigated in the fourth article below.

## ARTICLE III. How Octavius refutes Caecilius’s charge that Christians worship a man fastened to a cross for a crime; the supreme divinity of Christ; how clearly the same Octavius shows that a criminal man was honored with divine worship by the Egyptians, and kings and emperors by the Romans, and that oaths were sworn by their guardian spirits; and whether Christians were permitted to swear by their welfare

[Col. 0527D]

Caecilius further accuses Christians of a twofold crime: “Those,” he says, “who tell—or rather, one tells—of a man punished for a crime by the supreme penalty, and of the deadly wood of the cross, as objects of their rites, assign altars appropriate to abandoned and criminal men, so that they may worship what they deserve.” You hear, surely, how the Gentiles condemned Christians for two further forms of impious worship, as they regarded them: first, because they worshipped a man subjected to the punishment of the cross for his crimes; and second, because [Col. 0528A] they worshipped and venerated the cross, the deadly instrument of that punishment.

But Minucius shows separately that both charges are false. As regards the former, he openly and steadfastly asserts that the Gentiles are completely deceived and have gone utterly astray in persuading themselves that an earthly man, fastened to the cross for his own crimes, could have been deemed God by Christians, or could have deserved to be so deemed, and on that account be accorded divine honor by them. For those same Christians were fully convinced that those who place their hope in a mortal man are utterly pitiable, since when that man dies, all the help they could expect from him comes to an end.

But Minucius himself must be heard: “In ascribing to our religion a guilty man and his cross, [Col. 0528B] you stray far from the neighborhood of truth when you suppose either that a guilty man deserved to be believed God, or that an earthly man could be. Truly pitiable is the man whose entire hope rests upon a mortal man; for all his assistance ends when that man has died.” He therefore categorically denies that Christians worship a guilty man deservedly punished with death.

What, then? Could Minucius have affirmed so certainly that Christians denied Christ the Savior absolutely all worship and veneration, and placed no hope whatever in him? Surely no human being has ever believed that. Since it is certain and established that Christians both worshipped Christ and placed their hope in him who had died for them, what can you infer from this except that Minucius took it as fixed and certain that [Col. 0528C] Christ was indeed worshipped by his followers—not as a man, from whom nothing more could be hoped after his death, but as God, in whom, as the true and immortal God, they always placed their greatest hope? And may it not rightly be inferred from this, to observe the point in passing, that Minucius was thoroughly persuaded that Christ is the true and supreme God, and of the same substance as his eternal Father? For, as we showed above, he taught with the greatest constancy that there is only one God. Therefore, when he acknowledged Christ as God, he certainly confessed him to be the one and same God with his Father.

Petavius therefore attempts to prove from these same words of Minucius, though by another line of reasoning, that Minucius acknowledged the supreme divinity of Christ (Petavius, vol. II, *Dogmatic Theology*, [Col. 0528D] preface, ch. 5, § 3). The learned man judges that Minucius confesses Christ to be true God and, under that name, to be worshipped by Christians, just as the Gentiles venerated their gods. But, with the most learned man’s permission, we may say that it must be explained somewhat more clearly how this can be inferred from the few words of Minucius that we have quoted in full. His argument, however, appears to be this: “You err, pagans, when you suppose that we believe a merely earthly and guilty man to be God. For we believe Christ to have been not only an innocent man, but also true God, upon whom our hope most firmly rests.”

For when Christians were interrogated by pagan judges, undeterred even by fear of the impending punishments, [Col. 0529A] they steadfastly replied, as Pliny wrote to the emperor Trajan, that they “sang a hymn together to Christ as to a god”—that is, to Christ, whom they asserted to be true God, and such as, in Petavius’s view, the pagans believed their own gods to be (Pliny, *Letters* 101).

Minucius then turns the pagans’ argument back upon them and demonstrates that they themselves actually rendered to a mere man the very religious worship which they falsely reproached Christians for rendering. For the Egyptians, he says, consulted one man selected from among them all, “propitiated him,” and slaughtered victims to him, whom they declared to be God. How true this is Porphyry will teach you, who wrote the following to Anebo, as Eusebius reports: [Col. 0529B] “They also worship a man near the village called Anabis, where sacrifice is offered to him and the sacred victims placed upon the altars are burned”—Ἂνθρωπον σέβουσι, κατ’ Ἄναβιν κώμην, ἐν ᾗ καὶ τούτῳ θύεται, καὶ ἐπὶ τῶν βωμῶν τὰ ἱερὰ ἐκκαίεται. “He himself, however, later eats only what has been prepared and set before him from his private store, as for a private man” (Eusebius, *Preparation of the Gospel*, book III, ch. 3, p. 94). Eusebius repeats the same account elsewhere without citing an author, as does Theodoret later; Theodoret does indeed cite Porphyry’s *Letter*, but gives its sense rather than its words (Theodoret, vol. IV, Sermon 3, *On Angels and Demons*, p. 516).

By yet another argument Minucius convicts the pagans, and especially the Romans, of the same impiety. For they not only shamefully flattered their own rulers and kings as [Col. 0529C] gods, but also invoked their divinity, supplicated their images, implored their guardian spirit, and found it safer to swear by Jupiter’s genius than by that of their king.

Concerning that criminal flattery by which the Romans enrolled their dead emperors among the gods, we read the following in Justin Martyr: Καὶ τί γὰρ ἀποθνήσκοντας παρ’ ὑμῖν αὐτοκράτορας ἀπαθανατίζεσθαι ἀξιοῦντες, καὶ ὁμνύντα τινὰ προάγετε ἑωρακέναι ἐκ τῆς πυρᾶς ἀνερχόμενον εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν τὸν κατακαέντα Καίσαρα. “For why is it that you always deem emperors who die among you worthy of being made immortal, and produce someone who confirms under oath that he saw the Caesar who was burned ascend from the funeral pyre into heaven?” And later: “And when emperors among you die, you consecrate images of them in [Col. 0529D] this fashion and call them gods in the inscriptions” (Justin Martyr, *First Apology*, pp. 67–90). But this impious apotheosis of Roman emperors can be unknown to no one who has even tasted their history with the tips of his lips. Yet the utterly detestable flattery of the pagans did not stop there: they erected temples and altars and appointed priests for these same emperors while they were still alive, as they did for Julius Caesar, Augustus Octavian, and others (Suetonius, *Life of Julius Caesar*, § 76; *Life of Augustus*, § 52; Cornelius Tacitus, *Annals*, book III, around the middle).

They held their own emperors—whether still living or, after death, proclaimed gods by them—in greater awe than the more ancient gods, consecrated long before by their [Col. 0530A] ancestors. Why, then? Because, Octavius says, it was “safer for them to swear falsely by Jupiter’s genius than by the king’s.” Here he undoubtedly followed Tertullian, who went before him and whose very words he seems to have read: “Among you Gentiles, perjury is committed more readily by all the gods than by the one genius of Caesar” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 28).

It is more certain than certainty itself that from the time of the emperor Augustus the custom prevailed of swearing by the emperor’s name or genius. For Horace, addressing Augustus himself, sang:

> While you are still with us, we lavish timely honors upon you,  
> And set up altars on which men swear by your name.  
> (Horace, *Letters*, book II, epistle 1, vv. 15–16.)

You hear clearly both that altars were erected, as we just said, to Augustus [Col. 0530B] while he was still alive, and that pagans swore by his name—that is, by his genius. After he had died, Claudius Caesar, according to Suetonius, “established no oath for himself more sacred or more frequent than that sworn by Augustus” (Suetonius, *Life of Claudius*, § 11), that is, by his genius and name. If you still desire proof of how sacred and religious this oath was, you will learn it from these words of Tacitus: “Aubrius was charged with the crime of having violated the name of Augustus by perjury. When this became known to Tiberius, he wrote to the consuls . . . . . . . . . that the oath must be regarded just as if he had deceived Jupiter” (Tacitus, *Annals*, book I, near the end).

Moreover, the Gentiles were accustomed to swear not only by Augustus’s genius but also by those of other emperors. [Col. 0530C] Apuleius is for us a most substantial witness to this most perverse custom. For when magistrates were searching for a certain gardener and asking where he was, “the soldiers,” he says, “insisted, swearing by the genius of the emperor, that he was neither there nor hiding anywhere” (Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, book IX, near the end). Caligula, moreover, according to Suetonius, subjected many men to severe punishments “because they had never sworn by his genius” (Suetonius, book IV, § 27). Finally, to omit further examples, Tertullian relates that the rebels who supported the parties of Cassius and Albinus swore falsely by the genii of the emperors (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 35; and *To Scapula*, ch. 2, p. 85).

But Christians detested that impious oath. Hence Polycarp, as [Col. 0530D] Eusebius relates, could never be compelled by the tyrant to swear by Caesar’s genius (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book IV, ch. 15, p. 131 ff.). Other Christians, as the same Tertullian testifies, were therefore proclaimed irreligious and enemies of the people because they refused to propitiate the images of the Caesars or to swear by their genii. But something further must be said about Tertullian in its proper place (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 32, p. 31; and *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 17, p. 61).

Some may perhaps ask us why Minucius so confidently declares that the pagans swore “more safely,” or, as Tertullian says, “more readily,” by Jupiter’s genius than by the emperor’s (*Apology*, ch. 28). We shall therefore give them two reasons. [Col. 0531A] The first, which Tertullian suggests to us, is that the Gentiles feared their gods less than the present power of the emperors. The second is that, according to Zosimus, forgiveness for an oath made to God was left to divine kindness (Zosimus, *History*, book V, at the end). But anyone who swore falsely by the emperor’s genius, even in a relatively trivial matter—for example, a financial one—was, as Ulpian noted, ordered to be beaten with clubs (Ulpian, book II, title 2, law 13, § *Si quis*). Anyone who desires more should consult Brissonius, Hansenius, Trelierus, and others who wrote on the oaths of the ancients, and Taffinus, who wrote on the worship of the genius and the custom of swearing by it (Brissonius, *On Formulae*, book VIII, p. 809; Hansenius, *On the Oaths of the Ancients*, ch. 16; Taffinus, *On the Secular Year*, etc., ch. 13).

## ARTICLE IV. In what sense Minucius said that crosses were neither worshipped nor desired by Christians

[Col. 0531B]

Octavius also responds in two ways to Caecilius’s second reproach concerning the worship of the cross. His first answer is contained in these words and no more: “We neither worship nor desire crosses.” But the briefer it is, the more obscure it will seem to many. Is it really so, you will say, that Christians worshipped neither the very cross of Christ nor any image whatever of it? Is not Christ’s cross called by Cyril of Jerusalem τὸ σωτήριον τοῦ σταυροῦ ξύλον, “the saving wood of the cross”; and by Eusebius σωτήριον σημεῖον, “the saving sign,” σωτήριον καὶ ζωοποιόν, “saving and life-giving,” [Col. 0531C] and also σωτήριον τροπαῖον, “the saving trophy”—the safeguard of the city of Rome, the empire, and the whole world, whose divine power the pagan emperor Licinius acknowledged? (Cyril of Jerusalem, *Letter to the Emperor Constantius*, p. 247.) Who, moreover, doubts that such a sign of the cross was honored by Christians with sincere veneration? Yet if anyone resists, let him hear Eusebius, who extols Constantine the Great with the highest praises because he honored that triumphant sign—τὸ νικοποιὸν ἐτίμα σημεῖον, “he honored the victory-making sign”—in which he had repeatedly experienced the power of divinity and the marvelous effects recorded there by Eusebius himself (Eusebius, *Life of Constantine*, book I, ch. 31, p. 423; book II, ch. 16, p. 451; book IV, ch. 21, p. 535; and *Oration in Praise of Constantine*, pp. 627–628). Ambrose, moreover, declares without hesitation that the cross of Christ was placed by Helena, the same Constantine’s mother, upon the head of kings, “so that it might be adored in kings” (Ambrose, *Oration on the Death of Theodosius*, § 46, p. 1211). Jerome says that Paula, “prostrate before the cross, worshipped as though she saw the Lord hanging upon it” (Jerome, Letter 86, or *Epitaph of Paula*, p. 673).

Why say more? Julian the Apostate, like Caecilius, reproached Christians because, while mocking and despising the shield of Jupiter and Mars said to have fallen from heaven, they venerated the sign of the cross. Cyril of Alexandria replies to him that τὸ σωτήριον ξύλον, “the saving wood,” τοῦ τιμίου σταυροῦ, “of the honorable cross,” revives the memory of all the benefits conferred upon us by Christ’s death [Col. 0532A] (Cyril of Alexandria, *Against Julian*, book VI, p. 194 ff.). Who can doubt that it is far more equitable and just to honor this saving sign than the images painted upon walls of the dreadful and most shameful crimes committed by the gods?

But if this is so, how could Minucius say: “We neither worship nor desire crosses”? Some have wrongly read *oramus*, “we pray to,” here. Others, who did not sufficiently grasp our author’s meaning, thought it should be emended and wished the actual reading to be *horremus*, “we shudder at them.” But all this is contrary to the authority of the manuscript codex and yields no better sense.

What, then, you will say, did Minucius mean by the words “We neither worship nor desire crosses,” or how can they be explained? It appears certain [Col. 0532B] that they cannot be understood of the actual beam and wood of Christ’s cross itself, of which Ambrose and Jerome, already cited by us, speak. For in his time that true cross of the Lord had not yet been discovered by Helena, the mother of Constantine the Great.

Accordingly, he is undoubtedly speaking either of the sign and image of this sacred cross, or of the crosses of other criminal men. That his words can be taken in this second sense may be inferred, not unreasonably, from the fact that, just as “the pagans,” according to Origen, on “reading” the history of Christ, supposed that Christians showed some veneration to everyone put upon a cross, so they also falsely believed that Christians revered crosses of every kind (Origen, *Against Celsus*, book II, p. 87). [Col. 0532C] Both Caecilius’s reproach and Minucius’s response appear to favor this explanation. For neither of them spoke of one particular cross, but of crosses in general. Minucius could therefore say with complete correctness that Christians neither worshipped nor desired the crosses of men condemned to death for their crimes.

But if anyone thinks that these words must be explained as referring to images of Christ’s true cross, he will undoubtedly suppose this to be Minucius’s intention and meaning: “We do not worship crosses, whether made of wood or of any other material, because we do not honor the wood itself or any similar material; rather, when we see the cross, we direct all our worship to Christ himself, who hung upon the cross.” Nor will this explanation seem excessively forced to some. For Ambrose, who, [Col. 0532D] as we observed, said that the true cross of Christ was adored in kings, had written a little earlier of that very cross: Helena “found the inscription” of the cross; “she adored the King, certainly not the wood, for that is pagan error and the vanity of the impious; rather, she adored him who hung upon the wood and was named in the inscription” (Ambrose, *Oration on the Death of Theodosius*, § 46, p. 1211).

But if that is our author’s meaning, by what right did he add, “nor do we desire them”? For what could be more desirable to Christians than to see an image of Christ’s cross, or to undergo the punishment of the cross for Christ? It may be answered that Minucius’s word “worship” refers to Caecilius’s earlier phrase “to be adored,” and “desire” to the words “crosses to be undergone,” so that the sense is: “We do not desire or seek crosses; [Col. 0533A] but if we are seized by tyrants and compelled to undergo them, we suffer with a willing spirit and desire to end our life by the punishment of the cross.” Tertullian speaks in the same sense: “Certainly we desire to suffer, but in the same way that no one willingly suffers war, since it is necessary both to fear and to incur danger; nevertheless, he fights with all his strength, and the man who complained of the battle rejoices when he conquers in it” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 50). But we cannot conceal that this second interpretation does some violence to Minucius’s words. For one and the same word will signify at once the mere sign of the cross and an actual cross. Consider, therefore, why the other interpretation should be preferred to this one.

Yet whatever judgment may be made concerning the meaning of these words of Minucius, it certainly cannot be denied that Christians [Col. 0533B] in his time rendered some honor to the sign of the Lord’s cross. For they placed it very frequently upon their foreheads; and Jerome exhorts the virgin Eustochium: “At every action, at every step, let your hand trace the Lord’s cross”; and another virgin: “Frequently fortify your forehead with the seal of the cross, lest the destroyer of Egypt find a place in you, but so that the firstborn who perish among the Egyptians may be preserved in your mind” (Jerome, Letter 18 to Eustochium, p. 46; and Letter 97 to Demetrias, p. 790). In our following dissertations we shall demonstrate that this Christian custom was far older, and we shall also note later, in another dissertation, that demons were very frequently driven away by Christians through this same sign of the cross ([Col. 0533C] *Dissertation on the Book of Lucius Caecilius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors*, ch. 5, arts. 2–3). What man of sound mind, therefore, will deny that Christians paid honor to the sign of Christ’s cross, with which they marked themselves very frequently, almost every hour, and by whose power they marveled that so many and such great miracles were wrought? Away, then, with Gronovius’s foolish comment on this passage of Minucius, in which he absurdly asserts that no honor should be paid to Christ’s cross. This matter must be treated again later; meanwhile you may consult Petavius, Thomassin, Gretser, and other theologians, whether scholastic theologians or controversialists (Petavius, vol. V, *Dogmatic Theology*, book XV, ch. 8; Thomassin, book XII, *Dogmatic Theology on the Incarnation*, ch. 8; *Theology on the Master of the Sentences*, book III, diss. 9; and *On St. Thomas*, part III, question 25).

## ARTICLE V. How Minucius turns back upon the pagans the weapon of those who charged Christians with worshipping the cross, since they honored crosses in the images of their gods, their *cantabra*, standards, and trophies; and how the sign of the cross is represented in ships’ swelling sails, in the yoke, and in a man praying with outstretched arms

[Col. 0533D]

Minucius was not satisfied merely to refute the pagans who reproached Christians for worshipping the cross; he also turns back upon them the very weapon they fashioned from that accusation: “You certainly consecrate wooden gods; perhaps you worship wooden crosses as parts of your gods. For your standards themselves, your *cantabra*”—some wrongly wish *labara* to be read [Col. 0534A]—“and your military banners: what else are they but gilded and decorated crosses? Your victorious trophies imitate not only the appearance of a simple cross, but even that of a man fastened to one.” Our author seems to have drawn this from Tertullian, who refutes in precisely the same way that empty pagan argument concerning the worship rendered by Christians to the cross: “He who considers us,” he says, “devotees of the cross will be our fellow-worshipper when he propitiates some piece of wood. Never mind its outward dress, so long as the material has the same quality; never mind its form, so long as it is the very body of a god. And yet how greatly are Attic Pallas and Pharian Ceres distinguished from the beam of a cross, when they stand without an image as a rough stake and shapeless piece of wood? Every piece of timber fixed upright is part of a cross; we, if anything, worship a whole [Col. 0534B] and complete god. We have said that the origin of your gods is fashioned by the modelers upon a cross. But you also adore Victories, since in trophies crosses are the inner framework of the trophies. The whole religion of the camp venerates the standards, swears by the standards, and sets the standards above all the gods. All those prominent arrangements of images are necklaces of crosses; those hanging cloths of the standards and *cantabra* are robes of crosses. I commend your diligence: you did not wish to consecrate naked and undecorated crosses” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 16, p. 17). The same points, repeated in different words in another book, may be read by everyone (ibid., *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 12, p. 58).

Why Minucius said that wooden crosses were worshipped by pagans as parts of their gods you will understand from these words of the same Tertullian: “The image of any god,” [Col. 0534C] he says, “is first molded in clay built up around a cross and stake. Upon a gibbet”—that is, a cross—“the body of your god is dedicated” (ibid., *Apology*, ch. 12, p. 14; *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 12, p. 58 ff.). He explains this more plainly and clearly in another book; but because his discussion there is rather long, we thought it better to refer you to the book itself. Yet it should not be passed over without observation that Minucius added the adverb “perhaps” before “you worship.” Did he perhaps refrain from asserting it absolutely because the pagans, while worshipping the images of their gods, did not direct their attention to the crosses placed beneath them? Or what if he said it ironically, and therefore affirmatively, as Tertullian did?

Before both of them, however, Justin Martyr [Col. 0534D] similarly charged the pagans that “military standards and victorious trophies” were crosses, as Minucius says, or, as Tertullian put it, “robes of crosses” (Justin, *First Apology*, p. 90). Since this is sufficiently well known to everyone, we shall add nothing further except these words of Jerome to Laeta: “The standards of soldiers bear the emblems of the cross” (Jerome, Letter 57 to Laeta, p. 591).

The *cantabra* mentioned by our author and Tertullian were standards adorned with purple and gold, which were carried through the city with images of the gods in processions and solemn ceremonies. There was no other distinction between *cantabra* and banners except that the latter were merely pieces of cloth, readily distinguished by their color; *cantabra*, however, were distinguished from them both by their color and by some verse [Col. 0535A] or name signifying something agreeable and pleasant.

“Trophies,” moreover, were captured spoils of the enemy, customarily hung upon two poles, one upright and the other transverse, and thus presenting the form of a cross. Those appointed to carry trophies were called *cantabrarii*, who are mentioned in the Theodosian Code; Gothofredus’s notes upon that passage should be read, along with Bullinger’s observations *On Gallic Spoils* (Theodosian Code, law 2, *On Guild Members*, vol. V, p. 192; Bullinger, *On Greek Spoils*, ch. 3).

Minucius continues and says that the sign of the cross is naturally seen in a ship driven along by swelling sails and in the yoke with which the earth is customarily plowed. Justin Martyr explains these points still more fully. Tertullian and Jerome likewise observed that a ship’s yardarm [Col. 0535B] is a sign of the cross (Tertullian, *Against the Jews*, ch. 11; Jerome, *Letter to Heliodorus*).

Another natural sign of the cross is a man “with outstretched hands,” as Tertullian says, or, as our Minucius puts it: “A man who, with hands extended, worships God with a pure mind” (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book II, ch. 12).

But to what purpose, you will ask, did he argue against the pagans from this example? Did those pagans also pour out prayers with outstretched hands? Indeed they did; and not only prayers but also their vows in this rite, as may very easily be proved by very many testimonies from writers of every kind. Virgil, for example, sang of Anchises:

[Col. 0535C]

> But father Anchises joyfully raised his eyes  
> To the stars, and stretched his palms toward heaven with his voice:  
> “Almighty Jupiter, if you are moved by any prayers,” etc.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book II, v. 687 ff.)

And in the following book:

> I snatch my body from the bed, and stretch up  
> My upturned hands toward heaven with my voice, and offer  
> Untainted gifts upon the hearths.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book III, v. 176 ff.)

A little later he says that the divine powers were invoked by the same Anchises with open—that is, extended—hands:

> But father Anchises, from the shore with open palms,  
> Invokes the mighty divine powers.  
> (Ibid., v. 263.) [Col. 0535D]

Why say more? Elsewhere he sang of another:

> Unaware of events and ungrateful for his deliverance, he looks back,  
> And stretches both hands toward the stars with his voice:  
> “Almighty Father,” etc.  
> (Ibid., book X, v. 666 ff.)

Ovid likewise writes of a ship’s captain during a storm:

> The helmsman himself, lifting his palms toward the stars,  
> Forgetful of his art, sought help through vows.  
> (Ovid, *Tristia*, book I, elegy 10.)

And elsewhere:

> . . . . . . . . Here he adores the divine power with vows,  
> And, lifting ineffectual arms toward the heaven he cannot see,  
> Begs for help.  
> (Ibid., *Metamorphoses*, book II, 58.)

If you desire more, Brissonius will supply it for you (Brissonius, [Col. 0536A] *On Formulae*, book II, pp. 44 and 118). Not only the Gentiles, however, but Moses also, while the Israelites were fighting against the Amalekites, “when he raised” his hands, namely toward heaven, and prayed to God for them, “Israel prevailed” (Exodus 17:11).

Tertullian plainly teaches that Christians had the same custom: “Looking up there”—that is, to heaven—“Christians,” he says, pray “with hands outstretched, because they are innocent; with head uncovered, because we are not ashamed; finally, without a prompter, because we pray from the heart” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 30). In another book he also writes: “We, however, not only raise our hands but even spread them out, shaping them after the Lord’s Passion, and in prayer we confess Christ” (ibid., *On Prayer*, ch. 11). But these words of Tertullian will be discussed in their proper place.

[Col. 0536B] Ambrose also, when describing the martyrdom of the most holy Agnes, says: “If she were dragged unwillingly to the altars, she would stretch out her hands to Christ amid the flames and, upon the very sacrilegious hearths, trace the trophy of the victorious Lord”—that is, the sign of the Lord’s cross, as we have noted there (Ambrose, *On Virgins*, book II, ch. 2, § 7, p. 148).

Finally, to pass over many others, Prudentius sang that the bonds fell from the hands of the martyrs Fructuosus, Aurelius, and Eulogius as they raised them to God amid the flames:

> The punishment did not dare restrain the palms  
> That were to be raised to the Father in the form of a cross;  
> It released the arms that they might pray to God.  
> (Prudentius, *Hymn on Fructuosus*, etc., p. 187.)

[Col. 0536C] Nor is this surprising, since Paul had written in his First Letter to Timothy: “I desire men to pray in every place, lifting up pure hands” (Paul, First Letter to Timothy 2:8).

Our author therefore rightly concludes from these things: “Thus either natural reason rests upon the sign of the cross, or your”—that is, the Gentiles’—“religion is fashioned by it.”

# CHAPTER XIII. FURTHER HEADS OF CAECILIUS’S MOST UNJUST ACCUSATIONS ARE EXAMINED

## ARTICLE I. With what great injustice Caecilius blamed Christians for abstaining from respectable pleasures, spectacles, processions, circus and gladiatorial games, and the shameful scenes of the theater

[Col. 0536D]

Caecilius charges Christians with other things as though they were disgraces and crimes, although these should have been imputed to them as reproach and fault all the less precisely because they made their piety and holiness all the more manifest to everyone. Yet no one will wonder at the extraordinary insolence of an accusation of this kind if he observes that impious men have always found it difficult to endure having their wicked and utterly shameful works reproved and condemned by the outstanding and exceptional holiness of Christians. But come, let us see what Caecilius dared to reproach them for.

Christians, he said, abstain from respectable pleasures. [Col. 0537A] O great crime! What wonder is it, I ask you, if a Christian, who must carry his cross daily in following his crucified Lord, flees every pleasure? Have we not learned from Clement of Alexandria that pleasure, ἡδονή (*hēdonē*), is defined by the pagan philosophers themselves as ἔπαρσιν ψυχῆς ἀπειθῆ λόγῳ, “an elevation of the soul disobedient to reason” (Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus*, book I, ch. 13, p. 135)? We have also observed that Clement taught that desire is the beginning of every pleasure and that, conversely, desire is generated by pleasure (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book III, diss. 2, ch. 21, art. 2, p. 1024). Therefore, every Christian must always flee it as far as possible. What of the fact that Cicero, although a pagan, concludes the books in which he instructs his son in the cultivation of duty [Col. 0537B] as follows? “We say that every pleasure is contrary to moral worth . . . . For even if we grant something to pleasure, it will perhaps possess a little of seasoning, but certainly nothing of usefulness” (Cicero, *On Duties*, book III, p. 404 ff.).

Nevertheless, so as not to irritate Caecilius’s mind too greatly, our Minucius modestly replied that Christians “abstain from evil pleasures,” which the pagans indulged as though they were respectable and lawful.

Tell us, then, Caecilius, what are those respectable pleasures from which you contend it is wicked for Christians to abstain? “You do not attend spectacles,” he addresses them, “you take no part in processions; public banquets proceed without you; you abhor sacred contests, food previously tasted, [Col. 0537C] and drinks poured as libations upon altars.” But this truly novel and extraordinary kind of charge did not in the least dismay or disturb Minucius. For he freely acknowledges that Christians flee and abhor all these things as harmful, impious, and opposed to modesty and chaste morals.

As regards spectacles and processions, he says: “We, who are judged by our modesty and morals, abstain from evil pleasures, from your processions and spectacles, whose origin in sacred rites we know, and whose harmful allurements we condemn.” Tertullian had earlier spoken in an entirely similar manner: “We likewise renounce your spectacles to the same extent that we renounce their origins, which we know were conceived from superstition. Indeed, we also take no part in the very matters represented in them” (Tertullian, *Apology*, ch. 38, p. [Col. 0537D] 34). What those origins of the spectacles were, and how they arose from idolatry and the impious worship of false gods, he clearly explained in his book *On Spectacles* (Tertullian, *On Spectacles*, ch. 5 ff., p. 91 ff.), where you may read more about those same spectacles and their prohibition, as also throughout other works of the holy Fathers.
