Ὠκύμορος δή μοι τέκος ἔσσεαι, οἷ ἀγορεύεις. [Col. 0501D] That is: “When you speak such things to me, my son, I fear you may not long survive.” Nor did Anaxarchus’s harsh temper go unpunished. After Alexander had received him at a splendid banquet and asked him about the dinner, he is said to have replied: Πάντα πολυτελῶς. ἔδει δὲ λοιπόν κεφαλὴν σατράπου τινὸς παρατεθεῖσθαι. ἁποῤῥίπτων πρὸς τὸν Νικοκρέοντα. That is: “Everything was magnificent; but the head of some satrap ought now to be added”—as he looked toward Nicocreon. Easily foremost among all these was Diogenes, who, when a tyrant asked him what sort of bronze would be best for casting his statue, dared to answer: “The kind from which Harmodius and Aristogiton were cast.” These men, of course, were tyrannicides and were honored with statues for that reason. Hence he is called “the little bitch” by Tertullian, *Against Marcion*, book I, chapter 1.

Some admired the remark he addressed to Alexander as an expression of freedom, but others rightly deride it, and Chrysostom censures it in his book *Against the Gentiles*, or *On Saint Babylas*. His words are:

“You will say, ‘But the philosopher displayed great freedom of speech before the king.’ Let us therefore see what that excellent freedom was, and whether it too is [Col. 0502B] emptier than the tricks of his barrel. What, then, was it? When the Macedonian was setting out with his army against the Persians and earnestly urged the philosopher to tell him whether he needed anything, he replied, ‘Nothing,’ except that he asked the king not to stand between him and the sun; for it happened at that moment that the philosopher was warming himself in the sun’s rays.”

He therefore attacks this folly as follows:

“Will you not here be overwhelmed with shame? Will you not cover your eyes and face? Will you not depart from here and bury yourselves in the earth—you who take pride in precisely those things of which you ought to be ashamed? How much better it would have been for him to take some clothing, moderately thick, and proceed to some useful work, and also to ask the king for something that would be of use, than to sit dressed in a torn cloak, warming himself in the sun’s rays [Col. 0502C] like tiny nursing infants, whom their nurses, after bathing and anointing them, place in the very spot where the philosopher was then sitting, begging the favor of a wretched old woman.”

To this point pertains the wise counsel of Elihu in Job 34:18: “Would anyone say to a king, ‘You are Belial,’ and to princes, ‘You are ungodly’?” Indeed, οὐ πολλῆς τῆς πραγματείας δεῖ τριβώνιον, etc.; that is: “For it is no great undertaking to put on a threadbare garment, hang a wallet from one’s neck, carry a staff in one’s hand, and shout—or rather, bray or bark—and curse everyone,” as Lucian says in *The Runaways*. Those salaries were indeed sufficiently generous, as may be gathered from Lucian. In *The Eunuch* he says: Συντέτακται μὲν, ὦ Πάμφιλε, ὡς οἶσθα, ἐκ βασιλέως μισθοφορά τις, etc. In Latin these words mean:

“You know, Pamphilus, that a salary—and by no means a small one—was established by the emperor for each class of philosophers: namely, for the Stoics, Platonists, and Epicureans, and along with these also for the Peripatetics, so that the compensation was equal for each sect. When any one of them dies, however, someone else must be appointed in his place, approved by the votes of the leading men. Moreover, that compensation was neither a trifling thing, as the poet says, [Col. 0503A] nor a matter of sport, but ten thousand pieces of money each year, which they receive to teach the young.”

A little later in the same work: καὶ πρέποντα ταῖς μυρίαις, ἃς χρὴ παρὰ βασιλέως ἀποφέρεσθαι, that is, “and that he should not appear unworthy of those ten [Col. 0503B] thousand drachmas which were to be received from the emperor.”

The Leiden manuscript, however, reads *solariis*, a reading which, La Cerda testifies, had already occurred to Lipsius; he explains it as referring to taxes levied on buildings erected upon public ground. In this sense it is related in Eunapius of Sardis, in his life of Prohaeresius, that the emperor Julian assigned taxable and tribute-paying lands to learned men so that they might be exempt from burdens. “Ἓμέτρει μὲν,” he says, “γὰρ ὁ Βασιλεὺς τὴν γῆν τοῖς Έλλησιν εἰς τὸν φόρον, ὅπως βαρύνοιντο.” But how can philosophers be said to be rewarded with *solaria*, that is, with taxes or ground rent, when they are freed from the necessity of paying them? For I think that is what La Cerda means.

I therefore explain *solaria* as those sunny places where wealthier persons warmed themselves and, after being anointed, dried themselves in the sun. They adorned such places with statues, and thus he rightly joins *solaria* with statues. One might also read *larariis*. Capitolinus says in *Antoninus the Philosopher*: “He showed so much honor to his teachers that he kept golden images of them in his household shrine.” Lampridius says of Alexander Severus: [Col. 0503C] “In his household shrine, in which he kept the deified emperors—though only the best of them, chosen out—and holier souls, among whom were Apollonius and, according to what a writer of his own times says, Christ, Abraham, Orpheus, and gods of that sort, as well as portraits of his ancestors.”

Men of princely rank evidently had two household shrines: the first containing deified persons, and the second learned and great men. The same writer says a little later in the same place: “He called Virgil the Plato of poets, and kept his image together with a likeness of Cicero in his second household shrine, where he also kept Achilles and other great men. But Alexander the Great he consecrated among the Deified and the Best in the greater household shrine.” Among all the prodigies that preceded Tacitus’s death, Vopiscus says in his *Florianus*: “In the household shrine all the gods fell down, whether because of an earthquake or some other accident.”

Pamelius reads *salutariis*, beside which Scaliger wrote ἀσπαϛικὸν (“greeting” or “salutatory”; recovered spelling uncertain). Our author below, in the final chapter, says: “And yet you cast statues for all of them, engrave inscriptions upon images, and incise titles.”]

[Note: [Col. 0503D] “He ordered at the very end.” At the end of the *Phaedo*, Plato relates the following about Socrates after he had drunk the poison: καὶ ἐκκαλυψάμενος, ἐνεκεκάλυπτο γὰρ, εἶπεν· ὃ δὴ τελευταῖον ἐφθἑγξατο· ὧ Κρίτων, ἔφη, τῷ Ἀσκληπίῳ ὀφείλομεν ἀλεκτρυόνα· ἀλλὰ ἀπόδοτε, καὶ μὴ ἀμελήσατε; that is:

Hence Lucian says that he died without even paying Aesculapius the cock: ἔπιεν ἄθλιος τοῦ [Col. 0504B] κωνείου, μὴ δὲ τὸν ἀλεκτρυόνα τῷ Ἀσκληπίῳ ἀποδεδωκώς, as his words run in *Twice Accused*. Theodoret investigates the reason for this saying in *Cure of the Greek Maladies*, p. 109, and writes: ἐγὼ δὲ οἶμαι καὶ Σωκράτην, etc.; that is:

“I think that Socrates, the son of Sophroniscus, ordered a cock to be sacrificed in order to refute the charge brought against him, since Anytus and Meletus had accused him of denying the existence of the gods.”

Tertullian ridicules this and other things concerning Socrates in *On the Soul*, chapter 1:

“But if Socrates was therefore the wisest of men according also to the testimony of the Pythian demon—which was, of course, rendering a service to its own associate—how much worthier is the assertion of Christian steadfastness and wisdom, before whose inspiration the entire power of demons yields? This wisdom, from the school of heaven, is freer in denying the gods of this age: it does not betray its principles by ordering any cock to be rendered to Aesculapius; it does not introduce new demons, but drives out the old; it does not corrupt the young, but trains them in every good form of modesty. Consequently, for the name of truth it endures the unjust sentence not merely of one city but of the whole world—a truth whose achievement is, indeed, the more arduous in proportion as it is fuller. Thus it does not swallow death from a cup under the appearance of pleasure, but drains it from the gibbet and the living pyre through every device of cruelty. Meanwhile, in this darker prison of the world, among its own Cebeses and Phaedos, if anything concerning the soul must be examined, it directs the inquiry according to the rules of God.”

He also touches upon the cock in *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 10: “For I too sacrifice a cock to myself, no less than Socrates did to Aesculapius.”

Theodoret is seriously mistaken when he investigates the reason for the Socratic cock as though it were to be sacrificed to Apollo. For our Tertullian, who was in a position to know, adapted it to his own argument. Those who had recovered from illness customarily sacrificed a cock or chicken to Aesculapius, as is apparent from the engraving on the Gorlaean gem. This is also why a cock is added to him among the other ornaments in Choul’s very remarkable representation, which Sextus Pompeius Festus describes as follows under the word “Island”:

“A temple was built to Aesculapius on the island because the sick are sustained by physicians chiefly with water. [Col. 0504D] Serpents are under his guardianship because that creature is exceedingly vigilant, a quality especially suitable for guarding a sick person’s health. Dogs are employed in his temple because he was nourished at a bitch’s teats. He carries a knotted staff because it signifies the difficulty of the art. He is crowned with laurel because that tree furnishes a very great number of remedies. Chickens are sacrificed here.”

The reason Socrates wished a cock to be sacrificed to Aesculapius is therefore clear: he had now recovered and was about to depart from this frail and diseased existence for a better life—punished in the judgment of his enemies, but brought to completion in his own. Valerius Maximus speaks elegantly in the same way of Theramenes, book III, chapter 2. HAV.]

I believe this was done in honor of his father, because Apollo had proclaimed Socrates the wisest [Col. 0504A] of all men. O thoughtless Apollo! He bore witness to the wisdom of a man who denied that the gods existed.

[Note: [Col. 0504D] “Theatrically.” The Fulda manuscript incorrectly reads *inimici*; the Codex Agobardinus, *inimicae*. In the Leiden manuscript the copyist was unable to make it out, writing only the letter M and leaving a blank space.

Philo Judaeus censures such people in his book *That the Worse Is Wont to Attack the Better*, in these words: Πεφύκασι δὲ οἱ σοφισταὶ πολεμίοις χρῆσθαι ταῖς ἐν αὐτοῖς δυνάμεσι, etc.; that is:

“For nearly all sophists are accustomed to have their own faculties set at variance with one another, since their words oppose their thoughts and their counsels oppose their words, without any agreement between them. By their discourses they charm our ears with accounts of how social a thing justice is, how greatly temperance benefits everyone, how highly courage is approved, how immense a profit is derived from piety, and how salutary the other virtues are. Conversely, they say that injustice is a turbulent thing, intemperance disease-producing, impiety abominable, and all other wickedness ruinous. Yet they do not cease to think contrary to their own words. After singing the praises of prudence, temperance, justice, and piety, they then display the greatest folly and wantonness and behave unjustly and impiously; in a word, they are discovered to confound and subvert every divine and human law.”

Our author therefore aptly says that philosophers act a mime, since they appear to be one thing outwardly while being another within, and thus sometimes become ridiculous when their vices are exposed. Apply to them Lucian’s statement in *The Cock*: Εἶτ` ἐπειδὰν πέσωσιν, ὅμοιοι μάλιστα φαίνονται τοῖς τραγικοῖς ὑποκρίταις, etc.; [Col. 0505B] that is:

“When they fall down, they appear very like tragic actors. For many, as may be seen, while they are playing Cecropses, Sisyphuses, or Telephuses, wear diadems, swords with ivory hilts, streaming hair, and cloaks interwoven with gold. But if one of them—as frequently happens—stumbles and falls in the middle of the stage, he certainly provokes laughter among the spectators: his mask is crushed together with the diadem itself, while the actor’s real head is bloodied and his legs are largely exposed, so that one can now see how wretched are the cloths of his undergarment and how grotesque the buskins he has put upon his feet, since they do not fit the size of the foot.”

Lucian frequently and wittily ridicules the same thing in the *Dialogues of the Dead*, in the dialogue of Charon and Mercury. There, once the theatrical disguise has been laid aside, everything about the philosopher appears ridiculous. But let us hear Lucian himself. Mercury says to that little-bearded fellow: Κατάθου σὺ τὸ σχῆμα πρῶτον εἶτα καὶ ταυτὶ πάντα. Ὦ Ζεῦ, ὅσην μὲν τὴν ἀλαζονείαν κομίζει, ὅσην δὲ ἀμαθίαν, καὶ ἔριν, καὶ κενοδοξίαν, etc.; [Col. 0505C] that is:

“First put off this costume, and then all these things too. O Jupiter, what arrogance he carries, what ignorance, what a mass of contentiousness and empty glory, how many dubious questions, thorny disputes, and tangled thoughts! Indeed, how many futile labors, no small number of delusions, how much nonsense and inquisitiveness! But, by Jupiter, he also carries this gold with him, as well as pleasure, shamelessness, anger, luxury, and softness; for these do not escape my notice, although you carefully conceal them. Cast away your lies too, and your pride, and that opinion by which you suppose yourself superior to everyone else. For if you board with all these things, what five-banked galley could hold you?”

After these have been cut away, his beard trimmed, and his eyebrow shaved, flattery is also removed: Ἓν ἔτι τὸ βαρύτατον ὑπὸ μάλης ἔχει. Τί, ὧ Μένιππε; Κολακείαν ὦ Ἑρμὴ, πολλὰ ἐν τῷ βιῳ χρησιμεύσασαν αὐτῷ; that is:

“He still carries one very heavy thing under his arms.

“Mercury: What, Menippus?

“Menippus: Flattery, Mercury, which was of great use to him while he lived.”

A little later, when the same philosopher, weeping childishly, is asked the reason and answers that he weeps because he had believed the soul immortal, Menippus mocks him, says that he is lying, and presents the true causes of his grief, namely: ὅτι μηκέτι δειπνήσει πολυτελῆ δεῖπνα, etc.; that is:

“Because he will no longer have such sumptuous dinners; nor will he go out at night, secretly wrapping his little cloak around his head so that no one sees him, and visit all the prostitutes in succession; nor in the morning will he deceive young men with his wisdom and receive money from them. These are the things that grieve him.”

Lucian also describes their conduct in *Twice Accused*, p. 228: Ἑώρα γὰρ οἶμαι, etc.; that is:

“For she saw, I suppose, that those who habitually say much about enduring and bearing hardships privately cultivate pleasure; that they boast like young men to this extent, but at home live in accordance with the precepts of pleasure; and that they are ashamed if they are caught relaxing their firmness in any respect and betraying their profession.”

Likewise in *The Runaways*: Ἃ μὲν [Col. 0506A] γὰρ ἐν τοῖς συμποσίοις, δρῶσι, etc.; that is:

“It would take a long time to recount what they do at banquets and how they become drunk. And they do these things—just imagine!—while they themselves denounce drunkenness, adultery, luxury, and greed. You will therefore find nothing more mutually contradictory than their words and deeds.”

He adds: Οἷον κολακείαν μίσειν φασί, etc.; that is:

“For example, they say that flattery is hateful to them, although they could surpass Gnathonides and Struthias in it; and they exhort others to speak the truth, although they themselves cannot even move their tongues without lying. In words they all hate pleasure, and Epicurus is their enemy; but in practice they will do anything for its sake. Moreover, in the vehemence of their anger, their agitation over the slightest matters, and their readiness to become enraged, they surpass newborn infants. They therefore provoke no little laughter among the spectators when their bile boils up for any reason whatever and they are seen turning livid, displaying in their appearance shamelessness and impotence of mind. Their mouths are filled with foam—or rather, with venom.”

I think τὸν Στρυθίαν (“Struthias,” masculine accusative) should be restored: both were present, not the woman. He continues—for this too is exceedingly ridiculous—as follows: Μὴ σύγε ἐκεῖθι τύχοις, etc.; that is:

“Do not be there when that foul flood is poured forth: ‘By Hercules, I do not wish to possess gold or silver. An obol is enough for me to buy lupines, while a spring or stream will provide my drink.’ Yet a little later they demand not obols or a few drachmas, but entire fortunes, so that a merchant might be loaded with as great a burden as philosophy brings them in profit. Then, after they have scraped together enough to satisfy themselves and secured their livelihood, they cast aside that wretched little cloak and sometimes buy estates, soft garments, long-haired boys, and entire houses, bidding a long farewell to Crates’ wallet, Antisthenes’ cloak, and Diogenes’ barrel.”

He also describes the same men excellently in the *Necromancy*. Theodoret likewise sharply censures the life of Socrates in *Cure of the Greek Maladies*, p. 174, where among other things he says: Κοὶ ὁ Σωκράτης δὲ [The recovered initial spelling “Κοὶ” is uncertain.], etc.; that is:

“Socrates, too, does not command us to beware of every persuasion and enticement to the point that we should not eat when hungry or drink when thirsty; he does, however, command us wholly to avoid the kisses and glances of beautiful persons, since they infuse a venom worse than that of scorpions and venomous spiders. But these are mere words, lacking deeds and actions.”

For in the same place he shows that Socrates constantly frequented the gymnasia in order to feed his eyes upon a shameful spectacle, and that at drinking parties he remained awake through the night after the others had become exhausted, together with many additional details taken from ancient authors now lost, Aristoxenus and Porphyry.

There is also somewhere in Appian a very notable passage about philosophers to this effect, but at present it does not come to mind. In Laertius too, Diogenes rebuked those who praised the just because they despised money, yet strove to imitate the wealthy. Not even Marcus, that excellent emperor and philosopher, could entirely escape his detractors, who smeared him with a similar stain. Julius Capitolinus says: “They charged him with having been insincere and not so straightforward as he appeared, or as either Pius or Verus had been.”

Finally, Martial writes against a sodomite who pretended devotion to philosophy, [Col. 0506D] book IX, epigram 77:

> Of Democrituses, Zenos, and Platos past explaining,  
> Of all that bristles grimly in shaggy portraits,  
> You speak as though Pythagoras’ successor and heir;  
> No lesser beard hangs down from your chin.  
> But—what is shameful even in the hairy, and too late for goatish men—  
> You gladly take something stiff in your soft buttocks.  
> You who know the grounds and weight of every sect,  
> Tell me, Pannychus: what school teaches being circumcised?

Thus the old saying is entirely true: the man who advances in learning but declines in morals declines more than he advances. In Lucian’s *Lapiths*, after the philosophers’ ridiculous fight, the man who speaks as follows rightly concludes: Ἐν ὅσῳ δὲ ταῦτα ἐγίνετο, etc.; that is:

“While this was taking place, Philo, I turned various thoughts over in my mind. One conclusion was readily at hand: there is no value in a man’s learning the disciplines unless he also adjusts the conduct of his life to what is best, since I saw how ridiculous in their actions those men made themselves who surpassed others in words.”

Such a man is Zeno, who in Laertius is publicly proclaimed by decree at Athens to be a true philosopher, since he was ἐπ` ἀρετὴν καὶ σωφροσύνην παρώρμα, πρὸς τὰ βέλτιστα παράδειγμα τὸν ἴδιον βίον ἐκθεὶς ἃπασιν: “a man who, because he strives toward the things that are best, presents his own life to everyone as a model consistent with his words and teaching.”

Eunapius relates in his preface that Porphyry and Sotion gathered from every source the history and lives [Col. 0507B] τῶν φιλοσόφων ἀνάγων “of those who had truly practiced philosophy.” Epictetus also deservedly condemns those philosophers who are ἄνευ τοῦ πράττειν, μέχρι τοῦ λέγειν, that is, “men of words alone, without deeds.” HAV.]

[Note: [Col. 0507B] “Since they seek glory.” Hence our author calls the philosopher “an animal of glory” in *On the Soul*, chapter 1; and in *On Repentance*, chapter 1: “The philosophers, indeed, who are regarded as creatures of some wisdom.” Others read, “of animal wisdom.”

But Plato described the wise man in book XI of the *Republic*: ἄνδρα ἁπλοῦν καὶ γενναῖον, κατ` Αἰσχύλον, οὐ δοκεῖν, ἀλλ` εἶναι ἀγαθὸν ἐθέλοντα, etc.; that is:
