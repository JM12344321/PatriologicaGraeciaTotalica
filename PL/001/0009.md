In the consulship of Tiberius Claudius Severus (not, as some incorrectly say, the emperor Severus III) [Col. 0061A] and Gaius Aufidius Victorinus, the third year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the eighth of Severus: although, while the emperor was still absent on his Parthian and Adiabenian campaign—having received the submission of the Arabs and of Abgar, king of the Persians, in the preceding year—the persecution of Christians at Rome seemed to abate somewhat, perhaps through the aid of his *Apology*, Tertullian nevertheless saw that neither he nor other writers had achieved anything, or very little, toward the conversion of the Gentiles through their apologies for the Christians. He therefore assailed them with another kind of work, demanding testimony against idols from the still-pagan soul itself, and consequently entitled the book *On the Testimony of the Soul*. In it he presses those common expressions: “As God shall grant”; “If God wills”; “God bless you”; “God sees all”; “I commend it to God”; “God will repay”; and [Col. 0061B] “God will judge between us.” It seems very probable to me that in this same year he also composed the book *On Fate*, of which only a single fragment survives, though it is mentioned by the author himself in these words: “Powers indeed preside: according to us,” he says, “the Lord God and his rival the devil; according to common opinion, providence, fate, necessity, and freedom of choice. For the philosophers too distinguish these, and we already know that they must be discussed according to faith under a title of their own.” To this year we also assign the author’s new condition of life, in which he was admitted to the order of presbyters,

> **Note [Cols. 0061B–0061C].** There is certainly no passage in Tertullian’s writings from which it can be concluded that he was a presbyter in the Catholic Church. A passage is indeed produced from *On the Soul*, ch. 9, where the author speaks of a certain sister who was accustomed to be carried into ecstasy and to prophesy. From this passage it could, though only laboriously, be inferred that Tertullian preached in a church—something that at that time belonged only to bishops, or at least to presbyters—but only in a Montanist church. There such a right was readily allowed to those who boasted of prophetic gifts, and especially to Tertullian, since virtually the whole authority of that church had come to rest upon the name of a man otherwise so celebrated. But St Jerome asserts Tertullian’s presbyteral office so positively that there is no longer any room for doubt on the point.

> **Note [Cols. 0061C–0062D].** Since Tertullian excelled equally in sacred and secular letters, he was afterward ordained a presbyter. Ancient writers disagree whether this was in the Roman or Carthaginian Church, and modern critics likewise do not agree. After Ceillier (*Histoire générale des auteurs*, vol. 2, p. 376), Semler defends the common opinion that he was enrolled among the presbyters at Rome, on the following grounds (Semler, vol. 5 of the works of Tertullian, diss. 1 on Tertullian, §2, pp. 222–227). Tertullian, as Jerome writes, remained until middle age a presbyter of the Church—of Africa, or of Rome? We think of Rome, [Col. 0061D] as we shall presently explain more fully. Afterward, however, through the envy and insults of the clergy of the Roman Church—that is, after being attacked and expelled from that Church—he fell into the doctrine of Montanus, etc. Jerome’s account may plainly appear to embody a history not unknown to him, even if he did not wish to describe it more fully and precisely. Unless, indeed, we suppose that Jerome himself discovered these things by conjecture from various passages in these writers, such as the beginning of *Against Praxeas*. From those statements it might appear that Tertullian himself was then at Rome when Praxeas, coming to Rome from Asia, told the Roman bishop such things about the Montanists that the bishop decided it was in the interest of the Roman ecclesiastical community to maintain no further relations with them.
>
> Tertullian certainly writes (*Against Praxeas*, ch. 1): “The bishop of Rome had already acknowledged the prophecies of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, and by that acknowledgment was bringing peace to the churches of Asia and Phrygia. By making false assertions about the prophets themselves and their churches, and by defending the authorities of the bishop’s predecessors, [Praxeas] compelled him both to recall the letters of peace already issued and to desist from his purpose of receiving the charisms. Thus Praxeas managed two works of the devil at Rome: he expelled prophecy”—that is, the supporters of Montanus [Col. 0062A] who were then commending those churches of Asia to the bishop, among whom Tertullian himself was doubtless included—“and brought in heresy”—Tertullian now thinks that the Roman Church itself had fallen into heresy, and accordingly withdrew from it. “He put the Paraclete to flight and crucified the Father.” A little later he adds plainly: “And afterward acknowledgment and defense of the Paraclete separated us from the Psychics.” Here, as we think, Tertullian indicates clearly enough that he had previously lived in ecclesiastical communion with those Romans whom he here calls Psychics.
>
> From this passage, then, Jerome could have inferred as though it were history that envy and insults from the clergy of the Roman Church caused Tertullian to pass over to the Montanists. No more precise-looking account survives in other writers to persuade us that Jerome received these details from elsewhere. The words “expel” and “put to flight” signify clearly enough that this writer suffered an ecclesiastical affront and was removed and cast down from a sacred place and rank—namely, that of presbyter—[Col. 0062B] by the Roman clergy, as Jerome puts it more mildly. It is easy to understand that this was done by the Roman bishop himself through the clergy’s improper zeal. [Col. 0062D: Since Jerome wrote that catalogue at a later date, he seems to have written this judgment against the Roman clergy without the light of true history, in order to avenge himself upon those whose malicious judgments he had experienced after the death of Damasus.] Perhaps this cause may be examined more carefully elsewhere.
>
> That by “presbyter of the Church” Jerome means presbyter of the Roman Church is sufficiently certain even from Eusebius’ account, book 2, ch. 2. For he writes of Tertullian that he was “one of those especially illustrious at Rome”. Tertullian himself also states most clearly that he lived at Rome. [Col. 0062D: *On the Dress of Women*, book 1, ch. 6. Ceillier already used these passages against the *Praedestinatus*, which says that Tertullian, a presbyter of Carthage, wrote against the Montanists.] “At Rome too,” he says, “we saw the nobility of gems blushing before matrons at the disdain shown them by the Parthians, Medes, and the rest of their own Gentiles.” I should like to add other statements here which confirm that Tertullian lived as a cleric in the Roman Church. Chief among them is this passage (*On Prescription*, ch. 30): “Where then was Marcion, the Pontic shipmaster and student of Stoicism? Where Valentinus, the follower of Plato? For it is agreed that they lived not so long ago, about the reign of Antoninus, and at first believed the Catholic doctrine in the Roman Church, until under the episcopate of the blessed Eleutherus, because of their always restless curiosity by which they also corrupted the brethren, they were expelled once and again—Marcion indeed together with his two hundred sesterces which he had brought into the Church. Finally banished into permanent separation, they scattered the poisons of their doctrines. Later Marcion professed repentance and, when he had met the condition imposed upon him—namely, that he would receive peace if he restored to the Church (that is, the Roman Church) the others whom he had instructed for perdition—death overtook him.” [Col. 0062C]
>
> It is sufficiently clear that one who knew these things so precisely had himself been at Rome and had been among the clergy who were present when they occurred. If he had still been at Rome when he wrote this book, he would not have written “in the Roman Church” as though he himself did not belong to it. He had therefore already been expelled from Rome and was living in Africa. This appears still more clearly from chapter 36 of the same book: “Run through the apostolic churches—Achaia is nearest; you have Corinth—if you are near Italy, you have Rome, whence authority is available to us also.” He is addressing Africans who use the Latin language. “That Church (the Roman)—let us see what it learned and taught, and what fellowship of tokens it exchanged with the African churches.” It is altogether plain here that the writer was then in Africa; he must therefore previously have been at Rome, in that Roman Church, among the clergy. No others than clerics could have known such internal ecclesiastical affairs and histories. [Col. 0062D] Many other historical and political details of this kind—*Τοπικὰ* (“local matters”)—readily reveal a man long resident at Rome among other clerics.
>
> Such are Semler’s arguments for Tertullian’s Roman presbyterate. Dupin and Schroeck defend the opposite opinion. Yet their position, as it seems to me, is not sufficiently firm either, since it rests solely upon the testimony of the author of the *Praedestinatus*, who, because of his countless errors, has scarcely any authority among most discerning critics. It matters little for our purpose whether he was presbyter at Rome or Carthage or, as the distinguished Johann Georg Walch prefers (*Church History*, sect. II, ch. I, p. 643), presbyter of no particular church.

That he was distinct from Agrippinus [Col. 0062A], or Agrippa, the bishop whom Eusebius and Nicephorus mention under the emperor Marcus Aurelius, Zonaras under Marcus Antoninus the Philosopher, and the abbot of Ursperg under Antoninus Verus, seems to me to follow from the fact that blessed Cyprian, in Epistle 70 to Januarius, indicates that Agrippinus was one of his predecessors, and blessed Augustine, in book 2 *Against the Donatists*, ch. 7, calls him Cyprian’s predecessor. This is especially so because Egypt, where that Alexandrian held his episcopate, was a province distinct from Africa, and Cyprian, Epistle 71 to Quintus, writes that Agrippinus of blessed memory, together with his fellow bishops—whom he also calls his predecessors in Epistle 70—issued a decree concerning the baptism of heretics.

It also seems probable to me that immediately upon receiving the priesthood the author, by mutual consent with his wife, abstained from marital relations, in accordance with the custom of the Latin Church dating from the decree of Pope St Clement. These words to his wife especially appear to tend in that direction: “Do not suppose that, from resentment at the injury of your bodily integrity having to be reserved for me, I am already insinuating to you a plan of widowhood.” Again: “The time,” he says, “has been shortened; it remains that those who have wives should act as though they had none. If those who have them must forget what they have, how much more those who do not?” (*To His Wife*, book 1, chs. 5–6.) He plainly indicates there that this practice was common among many at the time: “For how many are there who immediately after the washing seal their flesh? How many likewise, by mutual agreement, cancel the marital debt? For voluntary eunuchs, out of heavenly desire, abstinence is endured while the marriage remains intact” (*On Baptism*, ch. 15; vol. I, *To Scapula*, ch. 3).

> **Note [Col. 0063D].** Tertullian’s marriage is beyond doubt. The matter is established, as we have already said, by his books *To His Wife*, preserved to our time. Writers disagree, however, over whether he was exercising priestly office when in those books he urged his wife to preserve continence in widowhood. These words indicate that he did not then wholly abstain from marital relations: “Let us cherish the capacity for continence as far as we can; as soon as it comes, let us drink it in, so that what we could not achieve in marriage we may pursue in widowhood” (book 1 *To His Wife*, ch. 7). He surely would not have said “what we could not achieve in marriage” if they had imposed perpetual continence upon themselves within it, as the distinguished Samuel Basnage rightly observes (*Annales politico-ecclesiastici*, vol. II, p. 215). Some therefore contend that presbyters at that time were not yet bound by the bonds of continence. Yet even if this were true—which remains unproved—the Reformers would still boast in vain, with Dallaeus, of Tertullian’s example while insulting the Catholic Church for prescribing continence to priests. For since the discipline of continence was introduced more by custom and practice than by laws, it is in no way overthrown by one or two examples from that age.

Now that he had become a presbyter, and was mindful of the power granted to him both to absolve penitents and to offer sacrifices, [Col. 0063C] he composed the books *On Repentance* and *On Prayer*. In them he prescribes both the manner of repentance for catechumens and fallen Christians and the rites the faithful must observe in the sacrifice. To this year may also be assigned writings mentioned by blessed Jerome but now lost: *On Circumcision*, *On the Garments of Aaron*, *On Clean and Unclean Animals*, and finally *On the Trinity*; perhaps also the Greek book *On Baptism*, since it seems to have been composed after Agrippinus’ council. The author indicates that it treated the same subject: “But what must be observed concerning heretics,” he says, “someone worthy should reconsider. It is addressed to us; heretics have no fellowship in our discipline,” etc. “But this matter has already been treated more fully in Greek.” More on this point, on Agrippinus, and on the author’s own paradox [Col. 0064A] in the Prolegomena.

In this year too must have occurred what he wrote in the following year in *To Scapula*: “Moreover, the rain of last year showed what the human race deserved; there had also been a deluge in ancient times because of human unbelief and wickedness.” Perhaps also this: “Those who saw them know what the fires lately suspended over the walls of Carthage throughout the night threatened, and those who hardened themselves know what the earlier thunderings proclaimed. All these are signs of God’s impending wrath, which we must, in every way we can, announce and proclaim, while praying that it may be local. Those who interpret its examples otherwise will in their own time experience its universal and final form.”

### [Col. 0064B] § XI. On other writings composed in good faith: *To Scapula*, *To the Martyrs*, *On Patience*, and *On Prescription against Heretics*

19. In the consulship of Lucius Annius Fabianus and Marcus Nonius Mutianus, the third year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the ninth of the emperor Severus: Scapula, a man of consular rank, governor of Carthage and therefore proconsul of Africa, had not yet received a decree of persecution from the emperor—and perhaps persecution had even been forbidden at Rome—but nevertheless raged more cruelly against the Christians at Carthage than did the other governors. Tertullian therefore wrote the book *To Scapula* (*To Scapula*, chs. 2–4). First he seeks goodwill by appealing to the Christians’ innocence and to their concern for the emperor’s safety not only through prayer but also through their sacrifices, from which it is clear that he was already a presbyter. [Col. 0064C] He then attributes the portents of the time and the destruction of Byzantium, already mentioned above, to the shedding of Christian blood. He adduces examples of punishment: Valgius Saturninus, Hilarianus, and Scapula himself in Africa, and Claudius Herminianus in Cappadocia, all persecutors of the Christian faith. He next urges that such cases be overlooked, citing the examples of the emperors Severus and Antoninus given above and Marcus Aurelius on his German expedition, as well as those of the governors and proconsuls Servilius Pudens under Antoninus Verus, Vespronius Candidus under Commodus, Cincius Severus, afterward killed by Severus, and Aper and Arrius Antoninus (the latter called Arrius by Lampridius and Brusius the governor by others), who were consuls under the emperor Antoninus.

[Col. 0064D] Finally he complains that although from the beginning the command had been to punish only by the sword, and this was observed by the governors of Numidia and Mauretania, Christians were also burned at Scapula’s order. To this belongs the statement in the *Apology*: “You call us faggot-men and half-axle-men, because, bound to a stake of half an axle’s length, we are burned within a ring of brushwood.” There too he records the kind of sentence by which a Christian woman was condemned “to the pimp rather than to the lion” (*Apology*, final chapter). Likewise, in the book *To the Martyrs* held in prison, composed at the same time, he adds to the sword and the supreme penalty of fire “the savage fury of beasts and every ingenuity of the executioner in torture” (*To the Martyrs*, ch. 4). He also mentions the custom by which those who did not possess peace in the Church, namely the lapsed, would ask it from martyrs in prison [Col. 0065A] (ch. 1). Although near the end of his life, when already a heretic, he did not approve this custom, it continued in the Church until the time of blessed Cyprian, as we discuss more fully in Cyprian’s *Life*.

He repeats all these punishments of martyrs in *On Patience* (*On Patience*, ch. 13; *To Scapula*, ch. 3; *On the Many Heresies*, ch. 7), which he also composed on the occasion of this persecution. There he even counts flight in persecution as a good belonging to patience, and thus he had not yet become attached to the Montanists, according to whose doctrine he later wrote, though in vain, the contrary work *On Flight in Persecution*. The author expressly attests that under Scapula St Mavilus of Hadrumetum was condemned to the beasts.

That the author was at Rome in this year appears from these words: “At Rome too we saw the nobility of gems blushing before matrons at the disdain shown them by the Parthians, Medes, and the rest of their own Gentiles—except that they are scarcely even displayed. Emeralds lie hidden singly, and only the sword beneath the cloak knows the cylinders on its sheath, while pearls on boots long to emerge from the mud.” [Col. 0065B] We interpret this as having occurred at the triumph of the emperor Severus, concerning which the consular fasti for this year report: “The emperor Caesar Lucius Septimius Severus, son of Marcus, Pius Pertinax Augustus, Arabicus, Adiabenicus, Parthicus Maximus, pontifex maximus, holding tribunician power for the eighth time, consul twice, father of the country, proconsul, acclaimed imperator eleven times; and the emperor Caesar Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, son of Severus Augustus, Pius Felix Augustus, holding tribunician power for the third time, [Col. 0065C] consul-designate and imperator, triumphed together over the Arabs, Parthians, Adiabenians, and King Artabanus.” Spartianus and Herodian also mention this triumph. Our view is supported by the fact that the author describes as an eyewitness the custom observed in triumphs: “Even as he triumphs in that most exalted chariot,” he says, “he is reminded that he is a man. For it is whispered to him from behind: ‘Look behind you; remember that you are a man’” (*Apology*, ch. 33).

20. In the consulship of the emperor Caesar Lucius Septimius, son of Marcus, Arabicus, Adiabenicus, Parthicus for the third time, and the emperor Caesar Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, son of Severus Augustus, in the fourth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, [Col. 0065D] and the tenth of Severus—or certainly in the preceding year—when not only ancient heresies but also several recent ones were already raging even in Africa, Tertullian published that truly golden work *On Prescription against Heretics*. That it was written before all his other books on the same subject is clear from the author’s words: “For the present we have dealt generally with all heresies, rejecting them from debate over the Scriptures by definite, just, and necessary prescriptions. Hereafter, if God’s grace permits, we shall also answer certain of them individually” (*On Prescription against Heretics*, ch. 45, nos. 293–294).

Near its end he reviews a catalogue of the heretics who had torn the Church up to that time, following blessed Irenaeus, though without sufficiently observing chronological order; we have set that order forth as far as possible. First come four kinds of Jewish heretics: Dositheus the Samaritan, the Sadducees, Pharisees, and Herodians. Second come those who wished to be heretics on the basis of the Gospel (ch. 46, heresies 1, 2, 5, 10, and 11), twenty-five in all: Simon Magus, Menander, Nicolaus, Cerinthus, and Ebion (ch. 46, heresies 3–4; ch. 47, heresies 6–8; ch. 48, heresy 6), who spread their heresies under the apostles, and thus under Tiberius, Claudius, Nero, and Trajan. Third come Saturninus and Basilides, the Ophites, Cainites, Sethians, and Carpocrates, under the disciples of the apostles down to the emperor Hadrian. Fourth come Valentinus and his followers Ptolemy and Secundus, [Col. 0066B] Heracleon, Marcus, and Colorbasus. The author relates that Valentinus, their leader, first believed the Catholic doctrine among the Romans under Pope Hyginus, about the reign of Antoninus, and that under the episcopate of Eleutherus, because of his perpetually restless curiosity, he was expelled from the Church once and again together with Marcion (references in the source: ch. 30; chs. 49–53; *On Spectacles*, ch. 27; *On Idolatry*, ch. 25). His followers flourished under the Pius pontiffs and emperors.

Fifth come Cerdo and his disciple Marcion, and among Marcion’s disciples the teachers Lucanus and Apelles. The former began with Valentinus; Marcion and the others flourished under Anicetus, Soter, and Eleutherus, while Pius and Aurelius were emperors. [Col. 0066C] Sixth comes Tatian, whom Clement of Alexandria also says established his school around the twelfth year of Antoninus Pius. What is more, Tertullian explicitly calls the Cataphrygians heretics, into whose heresy he later incautiously fell. Seventh, some of these were called *κατὰ* “according to Proclus” (of whom more below), others “according to Aeschines.” He charges them not only with heresy but also with blasphemy: they said that the Holy Spirit had been in the apostles but not the Paraclete, and that the Paraclete had said more, better, and greater things in Montanus than Christ uttered in the Gospel. More will be said presently about their period. Eighth, finally, come Blastus, discussed above; Theodotus of Byzantium and Theodotus the leather-worker, the Melchizedekian, under Pope St Victor and the emperor Commodus; Praxeas, discussed later; and Victorinus, who tried to strengthen Praxeas’ heresy. [Col. 0066D]

In the same book, from the apostle Paul, mention is also made of other heretics: ninth, Phygelus and Hermogenes, Hymenaeus and Philetus; and tenth, a certain Nigidius, of whom I know nothing, and Hermogenes. More on the latter shortly. The author further alludes to the persecution of Christians proclaimed by the emperor Severus in the same year when, speaking of the amphitheatre, he says: “There daily the lions are demanded against us; thence persecutions are decreed; thence trials are sent forth.” Again: “They naturally want to know the time of the persecution, the place of the tribunal, and the identity of the governor,” with several other remarks in the books listed below.

Here we have proved only that the beginning of the said persecution should be assigned to this tenth year of Severus’ reign rather than to the seventh or eleventh. First, blessed Jerome in his *Catalogue of Ecclesiastical Writers*, under Origen, and after him Sophronius and Nicephorus the Greek, write that when persecution was stirred up against the Christians in the tenth year of Severus Pertinax, Origen’s father Leonides was crowned with Christ’s martyrdom. Eusebius, *Church History*, book 5, lists Leonides among the first martyrs who suffered at Alexandria, where the persecution raged most intensely. Second, Judas continued his chronography down to this year because, on account of this extremely severe persecution, he thought the coming of Antichrist imminent. Finally Spartianus expressly indicates that Severus went to Alexandria only after entering upon his consulship with his son in Syria, which fell in this year, undertaking the journey for devotion to the god Serapis. “On this journey,” he says, “he forbade anyone to become a Jew under severe penalty; he likewise decreed the same concerning Christians.” In this persecution very many martyrs suffered at Alexandria under the prefect Laetus; in Egypt, Libya, and the Thebaid under the tyrant Aquila; and in Gaul, Saints Irenaeus, Andeolus, Benignus, Andochius, and Thyrsus. Most writers also assign to this year the inscription: “Marcus Agrippa, son of Lucius, consul for the third time, built this. The emperor Caesar Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, consul, restored the Pantheon, damaged by age, with all its adornment.” We thought it appropriate to recall this here for the history of the Church.

### [Col. 0067C] § XII. *On Baptism against Quintilla*

21. In the consulship of Publius Septimius Geta, son of Marcus, and Lucius Septimius Plautianus for the second time, the fifth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the eleventh of Severus: although persecution was already in force, Tertullian did not cease writing books against heresies. First among these, we think, should be placed *On Baptism against Quintilla*, since it is established that it was written before he began in any way to incline toward heresy: Quintilla belonged to the Montanist faction, from whom, on Epiphanius’ evidence, we derived there the so-called Quintillians. Whether the author wrote anything else against Montanus himself cannot be proved equally well. It is nevertheless probable both from Augustine’s words in *On Heresies*, “He passed over to the Cataphrygians, whom he had previously destroyed,” and especially from Nicephorus, book 4, ch. 34. Nicephorus relates that until settled middle age the author was a presbyter in the Catholic Church and composed many works against the doctrine of Montanus, but afterward fell into his error and in turn wrote many books in his defense. Since Nicephorus treats the history of the Montanist heresy at length in chapters 18 and 20–25 of the same book, drawing on Apollinaris of Hierapolis, Rhodo, Miltiades, Serapion, and Apollonius, who opposed it in their writings, and following Eusebius, book 5, chs. 3–4 and 14–18, we thought it proper—besides what we noted on *Prescription against Heretics*—to reduce it here to a compendium in fulfillment of our promise (*On Baptism*, no. 2).

### [Col. 0068A] Excursus on Montanism, which Tertullian first attacked in writing

22. There is said to have been a village in Mysian Phrygia called Ardabau, where, according to report, a certain Montanus (*On Prescription against Heretics*, ch. 52, heresy 21), recently converted to the faith, first became inflamed—when Gratus was proconsul of Asia—with an insatiable desire to obtain primacy. As soon as he opened an entrance to the devil, he was seized by an evil spirit and suddenly driven by frenzy and madness. He began to pour out strange utterances and to prophesy contrary to the Church’s custom derived from the tradition and succession of the apostles. Some recognized these utterances as counterfeit and reproved him so that he would chatter no longer. [Col. 0068B] Among them were the bishops Zoticus of Comana, or Ostrenus, and Julian of Apamea, and the martyrs Gaius and Alexander; they refused, to their last breath, to commune with him and his insane prophetesses Maximilla and Priscilla, former prostitutes whom the demon had filled with the same spirit. Yet a few Phrygians began to adhere to him, and the Theodotus mentioned above assumed, as it were, the management of his fabricated prophecy.

Advancing thence into Galatia, Pontus, and Asia, they also attached to themselves a certain Alexander, whom Maximilla habitually received as an intimate table companion, and a certain Alcibiades and Themiso, who falsely boasted that they were confessors and martyrs of Christ. This occurred—with blessed Jerome also agreeing with them—under the emperor Marcus Aurelius, at the beginning of the pontificate of St Eleutherus. Blessed Irenaeus had carried letters to Eleutherus from Gaul, having been sent by the Christians of Lyons with letters from St Attalus and the other martyrs then still confined in prison, to convey to him in Asia and Phrygia the judgment of the Gallic Church concerning their pseudoprophecy. In my judgment, this same Roman bishop, relying on the authority of his predecessors, refused them letters of peace (vol. 3, *Against Praxeas*, ch. 1). Whether Praxeas, then still Catholic, assisted in this is for the author’s reliability to answer. Certainly not only the bishops at Rome and in Gaul, but also those living in Asia, repeatedly assembled in many places, examined their prophecy, showed it to be false and detestable, and deprived the Montanists of communion. [Col. 0068D]

Montanus and his insane prophetesses died by hanging, like Judas, not by martyrdom. That Alexander was condemned for robbery at Ephesus by the proconsul Aemilius Frontinus; when he falsely represented himself as a martyr, he was not received even by his own people, since he was a robber. Finally, that Theodotus, lifted aloft by a demon like Simon Magus, suddenly fell headlong and ended his life miserably. More on the errors of Montanus, into which the author afterward fell, among his paradoxes.

Before the Kalends of June in this year, when Severus’ eleventh year began, I think there occurred what the author describes: “How eagerly this age celebrates those contests, the contentious solemnities and superstitious rivalries of Greek religions and pleasures, Africa too has learned. Even now the individual cities harry Carthage with congratulations because, after the long decline of its stadium, it has been granted the Pythian contest” (*Scorpiace*, ch. 6). [Col. 0069A] For this seems to be the point of the ancient coin inscription “Severus Pius Augustus, pontifex maximus, holding tribunician power for the tenth time,” or “Antoninus Pius,” on one side, and on the other “The indulgence of the emperors; by decree of the senate; at Carthage”; likewise the Greek legends “ΑΥ; ΚΑΙ ΖΕΟΥΑΡΟΣ ΠΕΡΤ; ΚΑΡΤΑΓ ΜΗΤΡΟΠΟΛΙΟΣ” (“AU; and Severus Pert[inax?]; Carthage, metropolis”—the recovered legends are corrupt or abbreviated, so this interpretation is uncertain), signifying that these games were instituted at Carthage by the emperors Severus and Antoninus.

### § XIII. On certain books of doubtful reliability: *On Spectacles*, *On Idolatry*, *On Female Dress* and *On the Adornment of Women*, the two books *To His Wife*, *Against the Apelleians*, *Against Hermogenes*, *On Matter Coeternal with God*, *On the Origin of the Soul from That Same Matter*, *Against the Valentinians*, *On the Soul*, and *On the Hope of the Faithful*

23. In the consulship of Lucius Fabius Septimianus, son of Marcus, for the second time, and Marcus Annius Libo, son of Marcus, the sixth year of St Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, and the twelfth of Severus: partly on the occasion of the Pythian games and other contests held at Carthage, and partly because the Secular Games had already been proclaimed at Rome—games to which heralds customarily summoned everyone on the ground that they had never been seen before and would never be seen again—Tertullian wrote, in both Greek and Latin, that distinguished book *On Spectacles* (*On Spectacles*, no. 1 ff.) in order to deter Christians from making that journey. In it he beautifully derives all four kinds of public entertainment from idolatry and therefore declares them forbidden to Christians. [Col. 0069C] That Severus celebrated the eighth Secular Games in this year is mentioned not only by Censorinus in *On the Birthday* and by Dio and Herodian—the latter says he saw them—but also by the consular fasti: “The eighth Secular Games were held by decree of the senate.” A coin inscription is likewise found: “Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Pius Augustus; the Secular Rites; by decree of the senate.” Poliziano and Onuphrius also think the triumphal arch of Severus, which still survives, was erected on this occasion in this year; its inscription is given above, and both authors treat at great length the origin and every rite of the Secular Games. Indeed, the author discusses so fully all the entertainments customarily exhibited at these games that the curious reader needs no other commentary, especially if he consults our annotations.

It is established that *On Idolatry* was composed at almost the same time, since it mentions both this book and the persecution (*On Idolatry*, ch. 13, no. 164; book 1 *To His Wife*, ch. 7, no. 44; book 2 *To His Wife*, ch. 1), and argues that imperial festivals belong to idolatry, together with many other things in which the worship of idols directly consists. To the same year we assign the books *On Female Dress* and *On the Adornment of Women*: in the former he censures excessive ornament, and in the latter especially superfluous care of the hair and skin. [Col. 0069D]

The two books *To His Wife* also seem to have been composed in this year. Since in the first he merely discourages a second marriage and in the second only forbids marriage with Gentiles, it appears that they were written before the author’s heresy, perhaps at Rome, where he frequently traveled, or at least to his wife while absent. He was apparently in poor health, since he calls the first admonition a legacy and trust made as though he were already dying. [Col. 0070A]
