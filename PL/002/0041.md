Blue Tiber. RIG.] that the Jordan was a sword, whose force and downward course Joshua had plainly taught to stand still while the prophets crossed. What answer is there to this? If he is your Christ, he will not be more powerful than the servants of the Creator.

But I would have used only these examples, had not the prophecy of this maritime expedition also preceded Christ. For when he crosses the sea, the Psalm is fulfilled: “The Lord,” it says, “is upon many waters” (Ps. 28:3). When he scatters the waves of the strait, Habakkuk is fulfilled: “Scattering [Note: [Col. 0406C] ‘You will scatter,’ Pam.] the waters,” it says, “along the way” (Hab. 3:9). When the sea is crushed by his threats, Nahum likewise is accomplished: “Threatening the sea,” it says, “and drying it up” (Nah. 1:4)—the sea, of course, together with the winds by which it was being disturbed. From what do you wish me to establish my claim to Christ: from examples, or from the Creator’s prophets?

Come now, you who suppose that a military and armed warrior was foretold—not figuratively or allegorically—as one who would spiritually wage a spiritual war against spiritual enemies, with spiritual soldiery and spiritual weapons: when you find in one man a multitude of demons professing itself to be a legion, a spiritual legion of course, learn that Christ too must be understood as a conqueror of spiritual enemies, spiritually armed and spiritually warlike; and thus as the very one who was also to fight against a legion of demons. Accordingly, the Psalm may be seen to have pronounced concerning this war as well: “The Lord is strong, the Lord is mighty in battle” (Ps. 23:8). [Note: [Col. 0407D] Others read “combat.”] For when he had fought against the final enemy, death, he triumphed by the trophy of the cross.

[Note: [Col. 0407D] But whose God’s Son did the legion testify Jesus to be? As though pursuing another point, he follows up the words: “What have I to do with you, Jesus, Son of the Most High God?” and there, of course, “pardon of the abyss,” that is, pardon from the abyss, just as above, chapter XII, “pardon from the fast.” PAM.]

But whose God’s Son did the legion testify Jesus to be? [Note: [Col. 0407D] “Did the legion testify,” Seml., Oberth.] Without doubt, the Son of the God whose torments and abyss they knew and feared. For they cannot be thought still to have been unaware that the power of a new and unknown God was at work on earth, since it is unlikely that the Creator was unaware of it. For even if he had once been ignorant of another God above himself, he would certainly by now have discovered one acting beneath his own heaven. And what the Lord had discovered would already have become known to his whole household in the same world and within the same circuit of heaven in which the foreign Divinity was dwelling. Therefore, to the extent that the Creator and his substances would have known that Divinity, had it existed, to that same extent—because there was no such Divinity—the demons knew no other Christ than the Christ of their own God. They do not ask [Note: [Col. 0407D] “For they would ask,” Wouw.] from another what they remembered they must ask from the Creator: namely, exemption from the Creator’s abyss. In fact, they obtained it. On what merit? Because they had lied? Because they had made him the son of a savage God? And what kind of being would he be who aided liars and tolerated those who defamed him?

But because they had not lied, because they had recognized the God of the abyss and their own God, he likewise confirmed himself to be the one whom the demons had recognized [Note: [Col. 0407D] “Recognized,” Pam.]: Jesus, the judge and the Son of the avenging God.

Here is something also concerning those pettinesses and weaknesses of the Creator in Christ. For I too wish to ascribe ignorance to him. Allow me to do so against the heretic. He is touched by a woman suffering from a flow of blood, [Note: [Col. 0407D] “By a woman suffering from a flow of blood.” Thus Jerome, Epistle 131 to Raffinus, spoke of “a woman flowing with blood,” and in Epistle 128 to Fabiola: “If one of the priests has a discharge of semen.” RIG.] and he did not know by whom. “Who touched me?” he says. Even when the disciples offer excuses, he persists in the language of ignorance: “Someone touched me”; and he affirms this from the evidence: “For I perceived power going out from me.” What does the heretic say? Did he know the person? And why did he speak as though ignorant? Surely to elicit a confession, to test her fear. So too Adam had once been sought as though God were ignorant: “Adam, where are you?” You now have both the Creator excused together with Christ, and Christ made equal to the Creator.

But you will also claim that this was because he was [Note: [Col. 0408D] “By which,” Jun.] an adversary of the law: because the law [Note: [Col. 0407D] “Excludes from contact with a bleeding woman,” etc. This alludes to the statement near the end of Leviticus 15: “This is the law,” etc., “of a woman bleeding,” that is, as blessed Jerome puts it, “who continually flows with blood.” In its place the Septuagint is now incorrectly read: καὶ ὁ γονορρυὴς, that is, “the man with a seminal discharge”; but καὶ τῇ αἱμορροούσῃ, that is, “and for the woman with a flow of blood,” must be repeated. For that chapter did not treat of a woman with a seminal discharge, but of a man with a seminal discharge and a woman bleeding for many days outside the time of her menstruation. PAM.] excludes one from contact with a bleeding woman, he was therefore eager not merely to admit her touch but even to grant her health. O God, beneficent not by nature but from rivalry! Yet if we find that the woman’s faith deserved this, since he says, “Your faith has saved you,” who are you to interpret as hostility to the law [Note: [Col. 0408D] “Hostility to the law,” etc. Here he takes *aemulatio* to mean opposition to the law; for shortly before he says “adversary of the law,” and near the end of this chapter, “rival of the law,” in the same way that Pliny called Carthage “the rival of Rome,” book XV, chapter 18. PAM.] an act which the Lord himself shows to have been performed as a reward for faith?

“But,” you say, “this constituted the woman’s faith: that she had scorned the law.” And to whom is it credible that a woman, as yet conscious of no God and initiated into no new law [Note: [Col. 0408D] “Into no law,” Wouw.’s codex.], would violate the law to which she was still subject? By what faith, then, did she violate it? Believing in which God? Despising which? The Creator? Certainly, she touched him in faith. If it was faith in the Creator—a faith that knew no other God—how did she violate his law? For if she violated it, she did so just as truly as she acted from faith in the Creator. How can both propositions agree: that she violated the law, and that she violated it from the very faith on account of which she ought not to have violated it?

I shall explain. First, this was her faith: that she trusted her God to prefer mercy to sacrifice itself; that she was certain this God was at work in Christ; and that she therefore touched him not as a holy man, nor as a prophet whom she knew to be capable of contamination because of his human substance, but as God himself, whom she presumed no uncleanness could pollute. Accordingly, she did not rashly interpret the law for herself as signifying that things capable of contamination were contaminated—not God, whom she trusted to be present in Christ. But she also reflected that the law specified the regular and customary flow of menstrual or childbirth blood, which proceeds from the operation of nature, not one arising from a disorder of health. Her own flow, however, abounded from a disorder of health, for which she knew that not a temporal limit but the aid of divine mercy was necessary. Thus she may be seen [Note: [Col. 0408D] “May be seen” omitted by Seml.] not to have violated the law, but to have drawn a distinction. This was the faith that had also bestowed understanding. “Unless you believe,” he says, “you will not understand” (Isa. 7). By approving the faith of that woman, who believed in the Creator alone, Christ showed himself to be the God to whom the faith he approved was directed.

Nor shall I omit the fact that, when his garment was touched—a garment worn, of course, upon a body, not upon a phantom—the body too was thereby demonstrated. I do not say this as though we were already reopening that question, but because it concurs with the present issue. For if his body was not real, a phantom certainly could not be contaminated, being an empty thing. He who cannot be contaminated because of the emptiness of his substance—how could he have wished to be? As a rival of the law, he was lying, since he was not truly polluted.

## CHAPTER XXI

He sends the disciples to preach the kingdom of God (Luke 9). Did he even here disclose whose kingdom it was? He forbids them to carry anything for food or clothing on the road. Who would have commanded this, except the one who both feeds the ravens [Note: [Col. 0409C] “Fed,” Oberth.] and clothes the flowers of the field, and who had already granted even the threshing ox freedom of the mouth (Deut. 25:4), [Note: [Col. 0409D] “For the privilege of taking food from its work.” He says that the threshing ox was “removed from its work” by the same formula of exemption by which the legal writers spoke of property “removed from the husband’s goods.” RIG.] for the privilege of taking [Note: [Col. 0409D] “Taking,” Wouw.’s codex.] food from its work—because “the worker is worthy of his wages”? Let Marcion delete these words, provided their sense remains intact.

But when he commands them to shake the dust from their feet against those by whom they had not been received, and orders this to be done as a testimony, no one gives testimony concerning something not destined for judgment. He who orders inhumanity to be entered into evidence threatens judgment.

That no new god had been established by Christ was also made plain by the opinion of everyone: some affirmed to Herod that Jesus Christ was John, others Elijah, and others one of the ancient prophets. Whichever of these he might have been, he certainly had not been raised for the purpose of preaching another god after his resurrection.

He feeds the people in the wilderness, in accordance, of course, with the former manner. [Note: [Col. 0409D] “In accordance, of course, with the former manner”: namely, that of the Old Instrument or Testament. Thus in the book *On Baptism*: “We are anointed with blessed anointing in accordance with the former discipline,” that is, the Jewish discipline. RIG.] Or if his majesty is not the same, it is therefore already inferior to the Creator’s; for the Creator sustained not for a single day but for forty years, not five thousand or thereabouts but six hundred thousand people, not from the inferior materials of bread and fish but from heavenly manna. [Note: [Col. 0409D] “But sustained six hundred thousand people.” That is, he continued, prolonged, and extended the tenure of life and strength for six hundred thousand people. RIG.] So fully, however, was it that same majesty that he willed, in accordance with the former example, that the scantiness of the food should not merely suffice but even overflow. For so too, in the time of famine under Elijah, the small and last provisions of the widow of Zarephath had abounded, through the prophet’s blessing, throughout the entire period of famine (1 Kings 17).

You have the Third Book [Note: [Col. 0409D] “The third,” Fran., Jun.] of Kings. If you also unfold the Fourth [Note: [Col. 0409D] “The fourth,” Fran., Jun.; “And if you turn again to the third,” Wouw.], you will find this whole course of Christ’s action surrounding that man of God who, when twenty [Note: [Col. 0409D] “Ten,” Seml., from certain Greek copies and from Ambrose, *Hexaemeron*, book VI.] barley loaves had been offered to him, commanded them to be distributed among the people. His servant, likewise comparing the multitude with the meagerness of the food, answered: [Note: [Col. 0409D] “What, then, am I to give this in the sight of a hundred men?” The exemplar reads: “What, then, am I to give this, the sight of a hundred men?” I think it should be written: “What, then, am I to give this very thing within sight of a hundred men?” RIG.] “What, then, am I to give this [Note: [Col. 0409D] “I,” Paris, Fran.] in the sight [Note: [Col. 0410C] “For the viewing of,” Seml.] of a hundred [Note: [Col. 0410D] Pam. adds “thousand.”] men?” “Give it,” he says, “and they will eat, for thus [Note: [Col. 0410D] “This,” Oberth.] says the Lord.” And they ate and left [Note: [Col. 0410D] “They will eat and leave,” Seml.; “they will eat and leave, and they ate and left,” Jun.; thus the Septuagint.] remnants, according to the word of the Lord. O Christ, ancient even amid new things!

Peter, therefore, had seen these things, had compared them with the former things, and had recalled not only the deeds accomplished in the past but also those already at that time prophesying the future. When the Lord asked who he seemed to them to be, Peter answered for everyone: [Note: [Col. 0410D] “You are the Christ,” etc. Because of that confession, blessed Peter is the foundation and the rock upon which the Church of God stands: ἐπὶ τῇ πέτρᾳ ταύτῃ τῆς ἀσφαλοῦς πίστεως οἰκοδομήσω μου τὴν ἐκκλησίαν, “Upon this rock of secure faith I shall build my Church,” as St. Epiphanius has it, *Heresy* 59. LE PR.] “You are the Christ.” He cannot have understood him to be any Christ [Note: [Col. 0410D] “He cannot have understood him to be a new Christ,” Latinus.] other than the one whom he knew from the Scriptures and whom he was now recognizing in his deeds.

Christ himself confirms this, still remaining patient, or rather even imposing silence. For if Peter could confess him to be none other than the Creator’s Christ, while Christ commanded him not to tell anyone this, then surely he did not wish what Peter had understood to be made public. “No,” you say, “because Peter had understood incorrectly, and Christ did not wish a falsehood to be disseminated.” But Christ declared another reason for the silence: “Because the Son of Man must suffer many things, and be rejected by the elders and scribes and chief priests, [Note: [Col. 0410D] “Elders and scribes and priests.” He preferred to retain *presbyteris* rather than its common translation, “older men.” As for “priests,” that is not particularly admirable, although the Greek is ἀρχιερέων, which the Vulgate renders “chief priests.” LE PR.] and be killed, and rise after the third day.” Since these very things too were foretold concerning the Creator’s Christ, as we shall establish in their proper places, he thus also showed himself to be the one concerning whom they were foretold. Certainly, even if they had not been foretold, the reason he gave for imposing silence would demonstrate not Peter’s error [Note: [Col. 0410D] Paris and Fran. add “but.”] but the necessity of undergoing the sufferings.

“Whoever,” he says, “wishes to save his life will lose it; and whoever loses it for my sake will save it.” Certainly, it was the Son of Man who uttered this sentence. Look, then, with the king of Babylon into his burning furnace (Dan. 3), and there you will find one “like the Son of Man”—for he was not yet truly the Son of Man, since he had not yet been born from man—even then determining these outcomes. He saves the lives of the three brothers who had agreed to lose them for God; but he destroyed the lives of the Chaldeans, which they had preferred to save through idolatry. What new doctrine is this, when its proofs are ancient?

Moreover, prophecies had already run their course concerning martyrdoms—both that they would occur and that they would receive their reward from God. “See,” says Isaiah, “how the righteous man perishes, and no one takes it to heart; and righteous men are taken away, and no one considers it” (Isa. 57:1). When does this occur more than in the persecution of his saints? [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Certainly not an ordinary doctrine, nor one common under the law of nature.” These words can refer to nothing other than the question, “What new doctrine is this?”—certainly not an ordinary doctrine, that is, nor one common under the law of nature, etc. RIG.] Certainly this doctrine is not ordinary, nor common under the law of nature, but distinguished and militant on behalf of faith: in it, the one who loses his life for God preserves it. Thus here also you may nevertheless recognize the Judge, [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Recognize,” Pam.] who will repay an evil gain of the soul with its destruction, and a good loss of the soul with its salvation. [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Repays,” Paris, Fran.]

But he also presents to me a jealous God, [Note: [Col. 0411C] “To me” absent from Paris.] returning evil for evil: “Whoever,” he says, “is ashamed [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Ashamed of me,” Seml., Oberth.] of me, of him [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Of that person,” Franeq.] I too will be ashamed.” Yet the grounds for shame are not applicable to any Christ except mine, whose whole course is the more shameful, [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Whose course is the more shameful.” He means the course of his birth and upbringing. RIG.] since it lies open even to the insults of the heretics as they plead, with all the bitterness they can muster, the entire foulness of birth and upbringing and even the indignity of the flesh itself. [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Of the flesh itself.” That is, of passible and mortal flesh, which certainly seemed unworthy of God. Thus *Against the Jews*: “First he was clothed in filthy garments,” that is, “in the indignity of passible and mortal flesh.” Or understand “indignity” as εἶδος οὐκ ἄξιον τυραννίδος, “an appearance unworthy of sovereignty.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0411C] Seml. conjectures “of those very things.”] [Note: [Col. 0411C] “Of those pleading,” Wouw.’s manuscripts.]

Otherwise, how will that Christ be liable to shame who does not admit it? He was not coagulated in a womb—which, though a virgin’s, was nevertheless a woman’s; nor, though without semen, from a woman’s fluid according to the law of corporeal substance; [Note: [Col. 0411D] “From a woman’s fluid.” In *On the Flesh of Christ* he spoke of “foul clots of fluid and blood.” RIG.] he was not flesh before taking shape; he was not called a “beast” after acquiring a form; [Note: [Col. 0411D] “Not called a beast after acquiring a form.” Julius Firmicus uses the same word in book VII, chapter 1: “The beast, cut limb by limb within the mother’s viscera, will be brought forth by the physicians.” RIG.] he was not brought to completion through the torment of ten [Note: [Col. 0412C] “Nine,” Fran.] months; [Note: [Col. 0411D] “Not brought to completion through the torment of ten months.” Thus the exemplars read. If they have preserved the author’s text intact for us, Septimius will have said that birth is brought to completion as long as the fetus is being perfected in the womb, according to the course of human birth over a period of ten months, just as in the *Apologeticum* he said that conception is brought to completion while the blood is still being developed into a human being. Otherwise, Latinius’s conjecture, “brought to completion through the torment of ten months,” would seem by no means contemptible. For an infant’s position in the womb is such that it seems to be tortured, entirely bent and contracted. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0412C] “Brought to completion,” Fran.] he was not, through a sudden convulsion of labor pains, poured down to the earth through the sewer of the body together with the filth of so long a period; [Note: [Col. 0412C] “Together with the filth of so long a period,” etc. The book *On the Flesh of Christ*, chapter 4, has almost the same words: “Beginning with the birth itself, which you hate, declaim against it; come now, speak of the filth of the genital organs in the womb, the foul clots of substances, fluids, and blood, and of flesh to be nourished from the same mire for nine months.” By “mire” he means what Hippocrates says in *On the Nature of the Child*, no. 136: Τὰ γὰρ καταμήνια οὐ χωρέει, ὁκόταν γυνὴ λάβῃ πρὸς ἑαυτήν, ἢν μέλλῃ τὸ παιδίον ὑγιαίνειν, “The menses do not flow in a woman carrying in the womb, if the child is to be healthy.” In this passage there is an admirable description of human origin and an image of its weakness, which I remember reading in almost the same form in Pliny, who immediately exclaims: “From these beginnings arises the mind of an executioner,” etc. If the book were at hand, I would transcribe the entire passage. LE PR.] nor did he immediately inaugurate the light with tears and with the first wound severing what held him; [Note: [Col. 0412D] “Inaugurated the light with tears,” etc. He begins life amid miseries, namely with wailing; he lives that same life amid labors; and, to complete his evils, must undergo death. LE PR.] nor was he thoroughly washed; nor treated with salt and honey; nor, through swaddling-cloths, initiated already into the wrapping of burial; [Note: [Col. 0412C] “Into the wrapping,” Wouw.’s codex.] nor thereafter rolled amid impurities between embraces, troublesome to the breasts, long an infant, scarcely a boy, slowly [Note: [Col. 0412C] “More slowly,” Seml., Oberth.] a man. Instead, he was set down from heaven, fully grown at once, complete at once, immediately Christ—spirit and power, and nothing but God.

Moreover, because he was not real, since he was not what he appeared to be, neither was he an object of shame because of the curse of the cross; he lacked its reality because he lacked a body. He could not, therefore, have said: “Whoever is ashamed of me.” [Note: [Col. 0412C] “Of me,” Pam., Fran.] It was our Christ who had to make this pronouncement: “Made by the Father a little lower than the angels” (Ps. 8:6); “a worm and not a man; the disgrace of mankind and the contempt of the people” (Ps. 21:6)—inasmuch as he so willed it that “by his bruise we might be healed” (Isa. 53:5), and that our salvation might rest upon his dishonor.

And rightly did he lay himself down for his own human being, for his own image and likeness, not another’s, [Note: [Col. 0412C] Seml. adds “then.”] so that, since man had not been ashamed when adoring stone and wood, he might with the same steadfastness remain unashamed of Christ and, for the shamelessness of idolatry, make satisfaction to God through the shamelessness of faith. [Note: [Col. 0412D] “For the shamelessness of idolatry, make satisfaction to God through the shamelessness of faith.” It was the shamelessness of idolatry when one was not ashamed to adore stones and pieces of wood; the shamelessness of faith is when one is in no way ashamed to believe that God was born and crucified. RIG.] Which of these things belongs to your Christ, Marcion, to furnish grounds for shame? Certainly you ought to be ashamed that you fabricated him yourself.

## CHAPTER XXII

Indeed, you ought especially to have been ashamed of this too: that you permit him to be seen with Moses and Elijah in the seclusion of the mountain, though he had come as their destroyer. This, of course, is what that voice from heaven wished to be understood to mean: “This is my beloved Son; hear him”—that is, no longer Moses and Elijah.

Then the voice alone would have sufficed, without the display of Moses and Elijah. For by determining whom they should hear, it would have forbidden them to hear any others whatsoever. Or did it perhaps permit Isaiah, Jeremiah, and the others whom it did not display to be heard, if it forbade those whom it did display? Even if their presence was necessary, they certainly ought not to have been shown conversing with him, which is a sign of familiarity; nor sharing in his splendor, which is an example of favor and grace; but amid some kind of filth, which would be evidence of their destruction—or rather in the Creator’s darkness, which he had been sent to disperse. They ought also to have been far removed from Christ’s splendor, since he was going to separate their very words and writings from his Gospel. Is this how [Note: [Col. 0413D] “Thus,” Rig., Venet.] he demonstrates that they are strangers—by having them with him? Is this how he teaches that they must be abandoned—by joining them to himself? Is this how he destroys them—by building them up out of his own rays?

What would their own Christ have done? According to the logic of perversity, I suppose, he would have revealed them as the sort of men Marcion’s Christ ought to have revealed—or else would have revealed with himself anyone at all other than his own prophets. But what could be more characteristic of the Creator’s Christ than to display his own heralds with himself; to be seen with those by whom he had been seen in revelations; to speak with those who had spoken of him; to share his glory with those by whom he had been called the Lord of glory; and to be with his chief men, [Note: [Col. 0413D] “With his chief men.” Vatican manuscript I reads, “with his princes.” But the former has more of the flavor of Tertullian’s African style. The author calls them this because Moses and Elijah were the foremost of the ancient prophets, long before those whom we call the greater prophets. PAM.] one of whom had once formed the people and the other would one day reform them, the one the inaugurator of the Old Testament and the other the consummator of the New?

Peter, therefore, rightly recognized the fellowship of his own Christ in Christ’s inseparability from them, and offered the counsel: “It is good for us to be here.” Good indeed, where Moses and Elijah were! And: “Let us make three tabernacles here: one for you, one for Moses, and one for Elijah”—but he did not know what he was saying. How did he not know? Was it through a simple error, or according to the principle [Note: [Col. 0413D] “Which we defend in the cause of the new prophecy: that ecstasy—that is, madness—is compatible with grace.” He seems to have done this in the books *On Ecstasy*, which Jerome mentions. RIG.] which [Note: [Col. 0413D] “According to which,” Jun.] we defend in the cause of the New Prophecy: that ecstasy, that is, loss of reason, is compatible with grace? For when a man is established in the Spirit—especially when he beholds the glory of God or when God speaks through him—he must necessarily fall away from his ordinary consciousness, since he is overshadowed by divine power. This is a question between us and the “psychics.” [Note: [Col. 0413D] “Concerning which there is a question between us and the psychics.” This is the first of all references made by the author to the psychics; from this it is clear that this book was written by the author at the beginning of the heresy. RIG.]

Meanwhile, it is easy to establish Peter’s loss of reason. For how could he have recognized Moses and Elijah except in the Spirit? The people could not have possessed images, statues, or likenesses of them, since the law prohibited them. It was because he had seen them in the Spirit; and thus, being established in the Spirit rather than in his senses, he could not know what he had said.

But if he “did not know” in the sense that he was mistaken because he thought Christ belonged to them, then it is already established that, when Peter was questioned above by Christ concerning who they thought he was, he had spoken of the Creator’s Christ in saying, “You are the Christ.” For if he had then recognized him as the Christ of another God, he would not have been mistaken here. But if he erred here for the same reason that he erred above, it is therefore certain that down to that very day no new divinity had been revealed by Christ, and that Peter had not erred down to that point, since Christ had so far revealed nothing of that kind. Christ must be reckoned as belonging to no one other than the Creator for as long as he exhibited the Creator’s whole established order, as he also did here.

He takes three of the disciples as witnesses of the future vision and voice; this too belongs to the Creator: “By three witnesses,” he says, “every word shall stand” (Deut. 19:15). He withdraws to a mountain; I recognize the pattern of the place. For the Creator had also initiated the former people at a mountain, both by his vision and by his voice. The New Testament had to be sealed upon the same height upon which the Old had been inscribed, and also beneath the same enclosure of the cloud—which no one doubted had been gathered from the Creator’s atmosphere—unless Marcion’s Christ had brought clouds of his own down to that place because he had also broken a path through the Creator’s heaven, or had similarly made borrowed use of the Creator’s cloud.

Accordingly, the cloud was not mute even now. Instead, a voice rose from heaven, the Father’s renewed testimony concerning the Son and the words already found in the second Psalm: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you.” Of him he also says through Isaiah: “Whoever fears God, let him hear the voice of his Son” (Isa. 50:10). Thus, when he now presents him with the words, “This is my Son,” the implied addition is surely, “whom I promised.” [Note: [Col. 0414D] “He promised,” Oberth.] For if he once promised him and afterward says, “This is he,” it belongs to the one who presents what was promised to employ the language of demonstration—the one who once made the promise—and not to one to whom the answer might be given: “And who are you yourself to say, ‘This is my Son,’ when you no more announced him beforehand than you revealed yourself and what you previously were?” [Note: [Col. 0414D] “Was,” Pam.]

“Hear him,” therefore: the one whom from the beginning he had declared must be heard under the title of “prophet,” since the people were going to regard him as a prophet. “A prophet,” says Moses, [Note: [Col. 0414D] “A prophet,” says Moses. He cites this Scripture very differently from the text now found in the Septuagint, but rather according to the citation in Acts 3 and 8. This is not surprising, since the same thing is repeated twice in other words in Deuteronomy 18. The author’s wording, “will raise up for you,” agreeing with the apostle Peter, approaches more closely to the former passage, except that the word “for you” occurs there; while from the latter passage he took the remaining words. PAM.] “God will raise up for you from among your sons”—according, of course, to fleshly descent [Note: [Col. 0414D] “Meaning,” Paris, Fran.]—“one like me; you shall hear [Note: [Col. 0414D] “You shall have heard,” Pam.] him. But every person who does not hear him shall be exterminated from his people” (Deut. 18:15). So too Isaiah: “Who among you fears God? Let him obey the voice of his Son” (Isa. 50:10)—a voice that the Father himself was also going to commend. For he confirms [Note: [Col. 0415D] “For he confirms,” it says, “the words of his Son,” etc. Again, the author renders the words of Isaiah 44, “of his Son,” where the Greek has παιδός σου, for which others read “of his servant.” Consequently, this passage was very difficult to locate, especially since blessed Jerome translates from the Hebrew, “raising up the word.” PAM.] [Note: [Col. 0415D] Pam. adds “it says.”] the words of his Son by saying, of course: “This is my beloved Son; hear him,” he says. [Note: [Col. 0415D] “He says” omitted by Pam.]

Therefore, even if the act of hearing was transferred from Moses and Elijah to Christ, [Note: [Col. 0415D] “To Christ,” Wouw.’s codex.] it was not transferred as though from another god or to another Christ, but from the Creator to his Christ, in accordance with the passing away of the Old Testament and the succession of the New. “Neither an envoy,” says Isaiah, “nor a messenger, but the Lord himself [Note: [Col. 0415D] “God himself,” Seml., Oberth.] saved them [Note: [Col. 0415D] “Will save them,” Oberth.]” (Isa. 63:9), himself now preaching and fulfilling the Law and the prophets.

The Father, therefore, handed over the new disciples to the Son, after first displaying Moses and Elijah with him in the privilege of splendor, and then dismissing them as though their office and honor had now been discharged. This very thing was thereby confirmed against Marcion: that Christ’s splendor too was shared with Moses and Elijah.

We also possess the entire form of this vision in Habakkuk, where the Spirit speaks at times in the person of the apostles: “Lord, I heard your report and was afraid” (Hab. 3:2). What report more than that heavenly voice: “This is my beloved Son; hear him”? “I considered your works [Note: [Col. 0415D] “Your work,” others.], and fell away in mind.” When [Note: [Col. 0415D] “How much,” Paris; “by which,” Fran.] more than when, after seeing his splendor, Peter did not know what he was saying? “In the midst of two living beings you shall be known”—Moses and Elijah. Zechariah too saw them figuratively as two olive trees and two olive branches (Zech. 4:3, 14). For these are the ones of whom it was said to him: “The two sons of richness stand beside the Lord of the whole earth.”

And again the same Habakkuk says: “Power covered the heavens” (Hab. 3:3)—surely by that cloud. “And his brightness shall be like the light”—surely the light by which even his clothing shone.

And if we recall the promise made to Moses, we shall find it fulfilled here. For when Moses desired the sight of the Lord, saying, “If, therefore, I have found favor before you, manifest yourself to me, so that I may knowingly see you,” he desired that sight in which God was to act as man, as the prophet knew. Otherwise, he had already heard: “No man shall see my face [Note: [Col. 0415D] “Me” absent from Rig., Venet.] and live” (Exod. 33). “And this word,” he says, “which you have spoken, I will perform for you.” And Moses again: “Show me your glory.” And the Lord similarly speaks of the future: “I shall pass before you in my glory,” and the rest. And at the end: “Then you shall see my posterior parts”—not his loins or calves, but the glory Moses had desired, which was to be revealed in later times.

In that glory he promises that he will become visible to Moses face to face, [Note: [Col. 0416D] “He had promised,” Seml.] saying also to Aaron: “If there is a prophet among you, I shall be known to him in a vision and speak to him in a vision—not as to Moses; mouth to mouth I shall speak to him [Note: [Col. 0416D] “To him” omitted by Seml.] in a form”—surely the form of a human being, which he was going to bear—“and not in a riddle” (Num. 12:6). [Note: [Col. 0416D] Oberth. inserts “And.”]

Moses departed from Christ illuminated by this glory in no other way than he was accustomed to depart from the Creator, striking [Note: [Col. 0416D] “To strike,” Pam.] the eyes of the children of Israel then, just as he now strikes the blinded eyes of Marcion, who failed to see that this argument too works against him.
