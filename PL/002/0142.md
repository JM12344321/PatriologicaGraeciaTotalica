NICOLAITANS. II, 63 B, “Because that whole heresy of the Nicolaitans,” etc. They are indicated in Acts 20 by these words: “Speaking perverse things” (Λαλοῦντες διεστραμμένα). Tertullian in this passage, Epiphanius, *Heresies* XXV; Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* II and III; Jerome, the letter concerning Fabian and the letter to Ctesiphon; Eusebius, book III; Philastrius of Brescia, *Book on Heresies*; Theodoret, *Heretical Fables*, book III; Augustine, *Heresies* V; and countless others make Nicolaus, one of the seven deacons, the founder of this sect. [Col. 1336C] Ignatius, however, raises a doubt when he writes in his letter to the Trallians: “Flee also the falsely named Nicolaitans” (Φεύγετε καὶ τοὺς νικολαΐτας, τοὺς ψευδωνύμους). Add that most of those Fathers speak doubtfully about that Nicolaus, upon whom it was easy to cast the blame because of the filthiness of his life and his thoroughly corrupt morals.

MARK (*NOTA*). II, 17 B, “From temptations of a worse mark as well.” A metaphor from merchandise or kinds of wine, which were customarily distinguished by marks and recognized from them. II, 254 A, “Neither by the sound nor by the mark of this name,” that is, by the written form of the name. I, 1238 A, “What soldier, after being discharged from his camp, takes pains over his marks?” He means military marks by which a soldier is branded for military offenses—for example, if he has been absent without leave, a deserter, seditious, etc. I, 1248 B, “Since I am a sinner bearing every mark.”

[Col. 1336D] NEWNESS (*NOVELLITAS*), said of what has recently arisen. II, 157 A, “The very newness of yesterday’s Praxeas.” II, 697 C, “Nor does falsehood escape newness.”

NEW (*NOVELLUS*), recent. I, 392 A, “As being somewhat new.”

NOVIAN. II, 1042 B, “Novian’s Fullers.” The poet Novius produced a play entitled *The Fullers*, in which word there is an unspeakable obscenity.

NOVELTY (*NOVITAS*). II, 1030 C, He calls the Carthaginians “fortunate through novelty,” because Carthage had been granted Italic rights through the favor of the emperor Severus, during whose reign Tertullian wrote the treatise *On the Pallium*.

CULPABLE (*NOXIUS*). I, 372 A, “A culpable hireling.” Gothofredus takes *noxius* to mean “liable”; Rigaltius says that he acted dishonestly, because he had so often eluded the teeth of the beasts. In this passage I would rather understand *noxius* as someone who, although under no necessity of fighting with beasts and not condemned to them among the criminals, nevertheless, from desire for a little profit and for an agreed payment, handed himself over to the trainer.

[Col. 1337A] TO MARRY (*NUBERE*). II, 198 A, “She marries continually,” that is, she approves polygamy in every form and enjoins marriage without any distinction. Thus she defends God’s law in the service of lust.

CLOUDS. I, 417 A, “Otherwise, if you think this ought to be done, let him count the clouds while praying.” Christians generally assembled in open courts and, while praying there, looked up to heaven, without altars and without statues. The pagans therefore mocked them as though they were counting clouds in prayer.

CLOUDINESS (*NUBILUM*). II, 597 A, “The truth was overshadowed by a certain cloudiness in each person,” that is, the cloud of ignorance overshadowed the truth in the minds of individuals. II, 247 B, “More obscure than cloudiness.”

WALNUT GROVE (*NUCETUM*) is a place planted with walnut trees, from *nux* (“walnut”), just as *quercetum* comes from *quercus* (“oak”) and *lauretum* from *laurus* (“laurel”). II, 575 A, “Thus in heavenly matters too he presumed walnut groves.”

NAKEDNESS. II, 706 B, “Nor is nakedness granted to the ashes being punished,” because the ashes of assassins are not clothed or enclosed in an urn.

NAKED. II, 247 A, “Naked lust”: not covered, but exposed [Col. 1337B] to the spectators’ eyes. For the contrary statement follows: even when they conceal their lust, they are marked out to the judges by quivers suspended from the yoke of the wagon in which they indulge their lust. I, 301 A, “Naked theaters,” that is, without a roof. For they used to be covered to ward off the sun.

A THING OF NO ACCOUNT (*NULLIFICAMEN*), ἐξουθένημα. II, 330 A, “And the people’s thing of no account,” which at II, 639 A he also calls “the people’s rejection.”

NONE (*NULLUS*). II, 134 A, “The wounds are none,” that is, they are not reckoned as wounds, though they exist. Rigaltius retains the common reading, “the wounds are not none,” in this sense: there are no battles, because there is no enemy; nevertheless, there are not no wounds, because they struggle vigorously and bravely and are wounded.

TO NUMBER, equivalent to “to register.” II, 116 C, “Numbered in blood,” that is, those whose faces were marked with blood.

NUMERICALLY (*NUMEROSE*). II, 167 D, “So that he might speak numerically,” that is, in the plural, as he had previously said, “without the plural number.”

TO DISPLAY FOR SALE (*NUNDINARE*). II, 907 A, “I shall praise their vigor if they have put some example of virginity on sale even among the pagans,” that is, if they possess such constancy in their virginity that they dare also to set forth examples of it from which pagans may take something to imitate. The metaphor is from wares offered for everyone to see at markets.

MARKET-DISPLAYING (*NUNDINATITIUS*). II, 892 B, “Therefore, because of heads of this sort displayed at market,” that is, exposing themselves in public as though at a market.

*NUS*, νοῦς (“mind”). II, 62 A, Basilides says that the supreme god is named Abraxas, by whom was created Mind, which the Greeks call νοῦς. II, 552 A; 573 B, “He would represent that same demiurge as the son, νοῦς.” II, 666 A, “Accordingly, by *animus* also—or mind, which is νοῦς among the Greeks—we understand nothing other than an innate and implanted prompting of the soul,” etc.

NOWHERE (*NUSQUAM*). II, 228 B, “It will never happen,” from Isaiah 34:4, οὐδέποτε γενήσεται (“it will never happen”).

TO WAVER. I, 516 A, “Human scrupulousness, scorning faith, wavered all the more,” that is, by their uncertainty and instability they confused and disturbed even what they had found certain in the Scriptures.

[Col. 1337D] NOD (*NUTUS*). II, 1043 B, “To betray by a nod.” This belongs to people secretly mocking and disapproving something among themselves when it would be neither safe nor seemly to do so openly. Since they are permitted neither to turn their eyes upon the person nor to point with extended fingers, he can at least be indicated by a covert nod.

## O

*OBBA*, a kind of potbellied vessel with which libations were made to the spirits of the dead. I, 347 A.

TO REFUTE (*OBDUCERE*), or to catch and hold someone asserting contrary and mutually conflicting things. I, 501 A, “While he is refuted concerning the goodness of this sect.” II, 232 B, “And you will be refuted for making it corporeal and infinite.” II, 218 C, “The argument of the opposing side is refuted”—overcome and disproved. II, 270 B, “Produce, therefore, someone from among your own who possesses apostolic standing, [Col. 1338A] and you will have prevailed.” II, 344 A, “We have already prevailed concerning the names.” II, 797 A, “Accordingly, we too refute, having thereafter been refuted concerning God.” II, 993 B, “When I shall thus also have refuted the presumption of the opposing side.” It can also mean “to erase,” with a metaphor taken from account books, like “to strike out.” I, 531 A, “But we are erased,” that is, we die in battle, and you cast this against us.

BARRIER (*OBEX*). II, 143 C, “Nor will anyone dislodge the barrier of that prescription.”

TO FASTEN FIRMLY (*OBFIRMARE*). II, 688 C, “Even if he fixes no hunting spear firmly against it.”

TO SILT UP (*OBHUMARE*), II, 1033 B. Rivers are “silted up” when their channels are filled with mud so that they are thereby forced to alter their course. Others read “by overshadowing”; Latinius, “by walking about.”

TO LIE AGAINST (*OBJACERE*). II, 658 A, “Thus, therefore, even if corporeal things lie against corporeal things.”

ACCUSATION (*OBJECTIO*). I, 1297 A, “Whose dowries, through the accusation attached to the name, may provide the reward for silence,” etc.; that is, when the women are informed against because of the name and profession of the Christian religion, their husbands claim their dowries for themselves as payment for the silence they had maintained on their wives’ behalf for so long. Thus the husband himself, who had slyly spied out Christian secrets through the occasion of marriage, became arbiter of the dispute. Unless the wife was entirely ready to die, she necessarily had to forfeit either the faith pledged to Christ or her dowry.

[Col. 1338B] OFFERING (*OBLATIO*). II, 739 A, “What arises from the force of the offering,” that is, of the condition offered.

TO LATTICE (*OBLAQUEARE*), to draw out and extend in a latticed pattern. II, 805 A, “Lest you lattice it with silver as well.”

ENSNARED (*OBLEGATUS*). II, 971 B, “And bound, as I think, by all the enticements of this life as though they were worthier than eternal ones,” that is, baited on every side, nurtured amid pleasures, and growing sluggish in them.

BOND (*OBLIGAMENTUM*). II, 352 B, “When the bonds and burdens of the law were already being altered by evangelical freedom.” I, 684 A, “And every unclean spirit through the bond of consecration.” II, 98 A, “Not even subject to a veil, much less to a bond.”

[Col. 1338C] TO OBLITERATE. I, 1283 B, “But if they possess it, they ought to obliterate what they possess.” II, 933 C, “Therefore, if all those things obliterate the permission to marry.” II, 700 A, “Notorious wastelands have obliterated estates that were formerly most pleasant.” I, 1293 A, “For who could doubt that faith is daily obliterated through commerce with unbelief?”

BOUND (*OBNOXIUS*). II, 900 C, “Two laws bound to one another,” that is, joined to one another by what is, as it were, a covenant of nature. II, 452 A, “Releasing those bound by more violent contracts”; “entangled” (*obnexus*) is better.

ILL-OMENED (*OBSCOENUS*). II, 1031 A, “After the ill-omened portents of Gracchus,” that is, inauspicious and sinister omens: namely, a standard broken by the force of the winds although the standard-bearer resisted strenuously; and sacred offerings placed upon the altars scattered and cast beyond the boundaries of the city.

TO OBSERVE. II, 39 A, B, n. (1), “Since they themselves observed,” that is, since they remained consistent with themselves, having themselves taught the same thing in their letters. For he establishes the form of preaching from the form of the letters.

TO SEAL. I, 1283 B, “Those who immediately after the washing seal [Col. 1338D] their flesh,” that is, from baptism onward devote themselves to virginity and celibacy. I, 1327 A, “And they seal themselves for eunuchhood.” I, 675 A, “He seals the gods themselves with this name.” I, 677 B, “If we speak after faith has been sealed.”

ALTERED FROM USE (*OBSOLEFACTUS*). II, 905 A, “Her voice has been altered from use,” that is, changed and altered from its accustomed quality since it first began, as Nonius puts it, “to crow like a young cock.”

TO WEAR OUT (*OBSOLETARE*). I, 363 A, “They wear out the traces of majesty.” In the Fulda manuscript Havercamp reads “they level the summit to the ground” (*fastigium adsolant*), that is, make it equal to the soil; correctly. Compare I, 577 A; II, 135 A, “Those who had worn out the wedding garment,” that is, had rendered it worn out or had brought it in a worn-out state.

OBSTINACY, in a good sense, for constancy. I, 631 B, “To be trained for this constancy by renouncing pleasures.”

TO OBSTRUCT (*OBSTREPERE*). II, 82 C, “Although neither does extraordinary attire obstruct ordinary use,” that is, stand in its way. I, 630 B, “Such great outward consolations of the eyes or ears offer no obstruction to religion in the mind and conscience.” I, 654 B, “Even with the charm of pleasure obstructing.” I, 1326 A, “And let them obstruct the disciplines of chastity.”

[Col. 1339A] OBSTRUCTION (*OBSTREPITACULUM*). II, 268 A, “Against the obstructions of the opposing side.”

TO BIND (*OBSTRINGERE*). II, 57 A, “Now bound to the world.” He means those who held some prefecture among the pagans. For many Christians who had obtained some dignity or military office nevertheless discharged the duties of those honors; yet it did not seem right that they should be ordained to ecclesiastical offices.

TO BLOCK (*OBSTRUERE*). II, 38 B, “A lamp is not usually blocked under a bushel.” Junius and others prefer “hidden” (*abstrui*). *Abstruere* means to remove the light by placing a pile in front of it.

TO BLUNT (*OBTUNDERE*). II, 454 C, “David was not refuting the scribes’ error,” that is, disproving it. II, 169 B, “So that from this point I may now blunt your faith.”

TO BEFALL (*OBVENIRE*). II, 36 A, “Because Paul had befallen him as his adversary,” that is, had fallen to his lot as a servant given by God to the Church.

[Col. 1339B] HAPPENSTANCE (*OBVENIENTIA*). II, 584 C, “They add an argument from happenstance”: by *obvenientia* he means events that arise from elsewhere.

TO OVERSHADOW. II, 597 A, “The truth was overshadowed by a certain cloudiness in each person,” that is, in the minds of individuals the cloud of ignorance overshadowed the truth, especially if the noise of others who were equally overshadowed caused further confusion. II, 717 B, “Even now it overshadows and corrupts.”

OCCASION. II, 545 A, “From the occasion of divine abundance.” II, 886 B, “These women, however, could not dare this without some pretexts from the Scriptures.”

FIT FOR SLAUGHTER (*OCCISORIUS*). II, 705 B, “And whatever are to be butchered among those fit for slaughter,” that is, among those kinds of animals prepared for killing.

TO TAKE POSSESSION (*OCCUPARE*). I, 376 A, “It is less even than the eyes by which it is taken possession of”; for by his sight a person takes possession of whatever is placed before his eyes. II, 802 A, “Which first took possession of the designation ‘man.’” II, 802 B, “Consider again that God in his entirety has been taken possession of and dedicated to him.”

[Col. 1339C] TO ENCOUNTER (*OCCURRERE*). I, 500 A, “Deservedly, we alone encounter innocence,” that is, once we find it, we alone will embrace it. II, 42 B, “When he meets the condition of what was granted to him”—namely, repentance—that is, when he voluntarily presents himself anew to the Church.

EIGHT-HORSED (*OCTOJUGIS*). The Valentinian Ogdoad is so called; for he alludes to the circus games. II, 500 B, “Once, as they say, the starting-cloth has been dropped for that eight-horse team.”

TO GIVE SIGHT (*OCULARE*). Scaliger: ἐξομματοῦν (“to give eyes to”). I, 404 A, “Who would give sight to those already refined for recognition of the truth.” I, 1248 A, “He knows how to give them sight again.” II, 994 C, “In garments given sight by purple,” that is, illuminated by it. Thus Pliny says of the sun: “It gives sight to the other heavenly bodies.” The brilliance or radiance proper to purple is called “light.” Fabius, *Institutes* VIII: “A broad stripe and pieces of purple inserted in the proper place will bring light.” Horace, *Art of Poetry*: “One or two purple patches, to shine broadly, are sewn on.”

KEEN-SIGHTED (*OCULATUS*). “I think God is too keen-sighted from his higher position for anything below him to escape his notice.”

ODEUM. II, 851 C, “But very recently, in this city, [Col. 1339D] the foundations of an odeum were sacrilegiously laid upon so many ancient tombs.” He means either Carthage or Rome. Odea were places to which spectators sometimes withdrew from the theater if rain came on. Poems were also recited there, and grain for sale was customarily displayed. The foundations built upon the tombs are called sacrilegious because burial grounds were regarded as religious and sacred things. It followed from this that those who dug them up were guilty of sacrilege, and that the foundations of the odeum were sacrilegious, since for its sake things held sacred under the law of nations were seized for profane uses.

Οἰκονομία (“dispensation”). II, 156 B, “Yet under this dispensation, which we call οἰκονομία.” II, 158 A, “The mystery of the οἰκονομία,” concerning the doctrine of the Trinity.

OENOTRIA. Italy was so called from wine, not from any king of that name. This opinion agrees with Aristotle, *Politics* VII, and Strabo, book VI, who indicate it without making any mention of Oenotrius. I, 330 A.

[Col. 1340A] TO STUFF (*OFFARCINARE*). II, 418 C, “Besides being stuffed with loads of dough.” Exodus 12:34: “And the people took up their dough before it was leavened, their kneaded portions bound up in their garments upon their shoulders” (Ἀνέλαβε δὲ ὁ λαὸς τὸ σταῖς αὐτῶν πρὸ τοῦ ζυμωθῆναι, τὰ φυράματα αὐτῶν ἐνδεδεμένα ἐν τοῖς ἱματίοις αὐτῶν ἐπὶ τῶν ὤμων).

PRESENTATION (*OFFERENTIA*). II, 421 A, “Consider how the causes present themselves,” that is, according as the different causes present themselves.

TO OFFER. II, 922 B, “You both offer and baptize.” II, 942 C, “And she makes an offering on the annual days of his falling asleep.” II, 984 B, “And offering their affliction to God.”

OFFICIAL (*OFFICIALIS*). II, 93 A, “And the other reasons for official crowns,” which those who discharged certain offices and honors wore because of the necessity of their office.

OFFICE (*OFFICIUM*). II, 970 A, “He sends her forth to it with an official staff and retinue.” Among the jurists, *officium* is the very body of those who serve a magistrate; hence their expressions “to relinquish one’s staff” and “to hand over to the staff.” Tertullian uses it here in this sense. II, 137 B, “When the dedication of the imperial image compelled the crowd to its duty,” that is, [Col. 1340B] to the duty of idolatrous worship. II, 119 A, “If you have bought off the military offices,” that is, if with money you have won over to yourself soldiers exercising public authority. II, 713 A, “We believe that angels are divine offices,” that is, officials and administrators of offices. II, 719 C, “The resentment against the governor is more severe when his officials are assaulted.”

WORKSHOP (*OFFICINA*). II, 336 A, “Call the womb a sewer—the workshop for producing so great a living creature, namely, a human being.”

TO POUR DOWN THE THROAT (*OFFOCARE* or *OFFUCARE*), to give water to be swallowed down the throat, as Festus explains it. I, 695 A, “Whatever waves of it choke [him].”

TO AGGRAVATE (*ONERARE*). I, 1293 A, “The charge of contumacy is so greatly aggravated,” that is, it is so much graver and more atrocious. II, 928 A, “It is also recognized by outsiders and burdened with testimony.” II, 988 A, “He therefore aggravated adultery,” that is, he described adultery as a graver and more atrocious crime. This reading seems preferable to Latinius’s conjecture, “honored” (*honoravit*).

BURDENSOMENESS (*ONEROSITAS*). I, 1323 A, “So great a burdensomeness in arranging the head.” [Col. 1340C]

ONESIMUS. II, 588 A, “And perhaps you may give birth to some aeon named Onesimus.” He alludes to Philemon’s Onesimus, whom Paul begot in chains, as he himself writes to Philemon—that is, by an altogether heavenly birth, making a Christian from a Gentile and an upright man from a runaway. Septimius says mockingly: We expect that you, who are Marcus or Gaius—or at least, which suffices, a male—will give birth amid those unspeakable chains or embraces of the Valentinian angels. But what will you give birth to? Some Pauline Onesimus, no doubt? No, rather some monstrous scoundrel of Satan.

*ONOKOITĒS* (ὀνοκοίτης). I, 372 A, Properly one who copulates with an ass; then, that which is conceived from an ass and a human being. Thus the Septuagint translators frequently used κοίτης (“bed, intercourse”) for offspring, as did Paul in Romans 9:10. From the appearance of the body they mark this fictitious god as a kind of ὀνοκοίτης, shamefully begotten, as it were, of an adulteress. See Juvenal, *Satire* VI, verse 330; Suetonius, *Life of Domitian*, chapter 12; Martial I, 5.

BURDEN (*ONUS*). I, 515 A, “If it were not already a burden, I would regulate the size of the volume,” that is, its bulk and magnitude (ὄγκος, μέγεθος). [Col. 1340D]

TO WORK AT (*OPERARI*), to devote effort. I, 1520 A, “In which we work.” II, 762 C, “While he worked at curing weaknesses and defects.” I, 695 A, “Let it give the brethren an opportunity to work,” that is, an opportunity to do us good, so that through their assistance we may be protected from that necessity.

WORKER (*OPERARIUS*). I, 1271 A, “And so the devil is the worker of that victory of God.” I, 1274 A, “Workers for the belly”: he calls parasites this. II, 705 B, “And those to be wearied among working animals,” that is, among those kinds of animals prepared to perform labor.

OPERATION (*OPERATIO*). II, 907 A, “Or any operation of almsgiving”; so also “the operation of fasting.”

OPERATIVE (*OPERATRIX*). II, 739 A, “That very principle which produces death, though simple, is a force.”

WROUGHT (*OPERATUS*). I, 259 A, “A wrought persecution,” that is, a more violent and bitter one. Perhaps “aggravated” (*onerata*) would be better, so that the sense is: What prevents you from permitting the Christian sect to be defended [Col. 1341A] is the prejudgment by which you have condemned it so often.

ELABORATENESS (*OPEROSITAS*). I, 1328 A, “And elaborateness joined with rarity,” that is, workmanship and the craftsman’s fee. Irenaeus uses this word for labor and hardship in book V, chapter 19. I, 1184 A, “And elaborateness, the pander of the hair”;“ similarly I, 1151 A, “With the Gospel superimposed as the canceller of all former antiquity.” II, 1045 B, “The priestly apparatus,” etc. II, 651 A, “So great an elaborateness of persuasion.”

SKILLFULLY (*OPIFICE*). I, 158 A, “And so skillfully.” Rigaltius prefers “even craftsmen” (*etiam opifices*), which seems to agree better with what follows. Others read “even Opici” (*etiam opici*), that is, barbarians, because they spoke *opice*, that is, barbarously rather than in Latin, according to Pliny, book XXIX, chapter 1.

FULLNESS (*OPIMITAS*). II, 683 B, “Fullness hinders wisdom; thinness assists it.” II, 998 C, “And thus thereafter he feeds upon the fullness of the Lord’s body.”

OPINIONATED (*OPINIOSUS*), one who forms opinions. II, 447 A, “Let him see whether Christ will appear so opinionated to anyone”; so Cicero, *Academics*.

WORK (*OPUS*). I, 339 A, “In the very working,” that is, when they are cast, hammered, and engraved, and thus injured and wounded. [Col. 1341B]

PRAYER (*ORATIO*). II, 105 B, “But in the lawful prayer.” He calls that prayer “lawful” which Christ, skilled in heavenly law, prescribed for us. Thus Augustine spoke of “the lawful prayer” in *Sermon 125, On Time*.

ROUND TABLE (*ORBIS*), which the ancients called a *cilliba*. II, 1048 A, “For which Marcus Tullius bought a round citrus-wood table for five hundred thousand sesterces.” Citrus-wood tables were brought from Mauretania to Rome at incredible expense. Pliny, book XXXIII, chapter 11.

ORDER (*ORDO*). II, 39 B, “The whole order of the rule,” that is, an ordered form of Christian doctrine. In the same sense, II, 40 B, “The order of the Churches’ doctrine.” II, 947 B, “So that the people might know all the more that they must observe for themselves the order which produces the presiding officers.” He means the order of the once-married, from whom the presiding officers are chosen. Accordingly, he wished all the people to be once-married.

THE RISING (*ORIENS*). II, 545 A, “The Rising, a figure of Christ”; supply [Col. 1341C] “he loves.” For Christ is often understood by the word “Rising” in the sacred writings, as in Zechariah. He is called the Rising because he is the sun of righteousness rising from on high.

ADORNMENT (*ORNATUS*). I, 1309 A, “We call that adornment which ought to be called feminine uncleanness,” namely, care of the hair and skin.

MOMENTARY (*ORTANEUS*). II, 483 B, D, n. (1), “And certain signs even of momentary things.” *Ortanea* are things that are momentary and possess their being only in their arising: they arise and perish at once.

MOUTH (*OS*). I, 654 A, “But let him revisit the man, now exhausted, even for the spectacle of his mouth,” so that he may wish to look even upon the mouth and face of the dying man.

OSCILLATING DEVICE (*OSCILLUM*), an oscillation, αἰώρα, said of a machine poised for striking and assault. See Festus under this word. II, 1032 A.

OSTENTATIOUS (*OSTENTATITIUS*). II, 892 B, “The shamelessness of ostentatious virginity.”

IDLE (*OTIOSUS*). II, 76 A, “With an idle crown in his hand.” By “idle” he means useless and inappropriate. Thus in *To His Wife*: [Col. 1341D] “For us it is idle to beget children,” that is, it is useless and inappropriate for Christians to beget children while a tyrant rages against the Christian name.

## P

FOOD (*PABULUM*). I, 306 B, “And food from it,” that is, from the flesh of the slain infant. Havercamp proposes: “And with the food raw, from it and afterward,” etc.

PACIFYING (*PACATORIUS*). II, 435 C, “Judge a just and pacifying judgment.”

TRAINING (*PAEDAGOGATUS*). II, 563 A, “The training of the Holy Spirit,” that is, his ministry.

TRAINING-HOUSE (*PAEDAGOGIUM*), an assembly. I, 348 A, “Then concerning the imperial training-houses.” II, 554 A, “And a thousand other names from the training-house.” Following the custom of the imperial palace, he calls it a *paedagogium*, in which court boys were reared and instructed under the supervision of tutors.

[Col. 1342A] WINNOWING SHOVEL (*PALA*), an implement with which grain is winnowed and cleaned. II, 103 C, “This is that shovel which even now cleanses the Lord’s threshing floor.”

CLOAKED (*PALLIATUS*). II, 1049 A, “Cloaked discourse,” that is, philosophical and Christian discourse.

PALLIUM. II, 1031 B, “Of the now rounded pallium,” etc. You have exchanged your square cloak for a rounded cloak—that is, the Roman toga. Salmasius adduced as a witness particularly suited to this interpretation the ancient scholiast on Persius, who says that the toga is an unadorned pallium of rounded shape, with a broader and flowing fold.

STAKE (*PALUS*). II, 969 B, “But again you fix boundary stakes for God.” Writers on the regulation of boundaries teach that in certain regions stakes of holm oak, olive wood, and so forth were set up as boundary markers.

*PANGAPIPAN NIRAPIAM*. II, 563 A. The sense of this passage is perhaps: “Which miscellaneous work of Ptolemy would more properly have been called, after certain Attic *Curae*, a *Pankarpon* or *The Whole* (πάγκαρπον ἢ τὸ πᾶν).” [The lemma is textually corrupt.] By “Attic *Curae*” he means Greek books which, because their titles were devised with ambitious cleverness of this kind, were deservedly mocked in Pliny’s preface addressed to Vespasian. Πάγκαρπον means fruits of every kind. See Gellius, book XX, final chapter.

[Col. 1342B] BREAD (*PANIS*). II, 461 A, “Why, however, does he call bread his body?” I, 1160 A, “Then because his body is also reckoned to be in the bread.” II, 262 A, “Nor the bread by which he represents his own body.” II, 274 A, “Over another’s bread he performs thanksgiving to another God.” II, 370 B, “Behold, he first offers the bread of his teaching to the Israelites.” II, 80 A, “We suffer with distress if any portion even of our cup or bread falls to the ground.” II, 489 A, “Accordingly, we have already established in the Gospel the sacraments of the bread and the cup,” etc.

PAPA. II, 103 C, “Good shepherd and blessed papa,” that is, father—an appellation already at that time honorifically attributed to God’s devout servants and the ministers of the Church.

TENT (*PAPILIO*). I, 624 A, “But concerning tents spread out and tied down.” Tents and pavilions are spread out from their rolled-up state when erected in camp, and they are tied and fastened by ropes to stakes driven into the ground [Col. 1342C] so that they do not collapse when disturbed by the wind.

EQUAL (*PAR*). II, 204 A, “Equally” (*ex pari*), ἐξ ἴσου—that is, equally and alike. II, 398 A, “As one made equal to the law of my will,” that is, as though it were within my power to do whatever might be in my will and mind. II, 920 B, “And had provided for him”—Adam—“a necessary equal.” II, 107 C, “Which even amid evils is a corresponding good,” that is, the combination of good with evil, so that good and evil appear to make an equal pair in the same matter. Thus Festus says that the ancients fought “with matched horses,” meaning with two horses in a team.

PARACLETE. II, 156 A, “He put the Paraclete to flight,” that is, the spirit who is the author of special revelations. Montanus, if others are to be believed, proclaimed that a greater fullness of the Spirit dwelt in him than in the Apostles. But Baronius already observed that the school of Montanus remained harmless for some time and had disciples so commendable for holiness of conduct and so steadfast in martyrdom that nowhere else did anyone recognize more immediate powers of the divine presence. Indeed, that most learned cardinal rightly judged that Anicetus, then Roman pontiff, recognized these gifts of the divine Spirit in people who did not yet dissent in any respect from the rule of faith, granted peace to their churches, and, as Tertullian says, sent letters of peace. Praxeas, therefore, as Tertullian depicts him, did not cease from burdening Montanus’s disciples before the pontiff with fabricated charges—and even with accusations diminishing pontifical authority—until the already issued letters of peace or communion were recalled. Perhaps, then, some who were more than reasonably offended and indignant began to exalt and proclaim their Montanus to such a degree that they said that the Apostles indeed had possessed the Holy Spirit, but had not possessed the Paraclete; that this Paraclete of theirs had said more through Montanus than in Christ’s Gospel, and not merely more, but also better and greater things. We read these things concerning those called Cataphrygians in the *Catalogue of Heretics* customarily appended to Tertullian’s book *On Prescription against Heretics*. These reports sufficiently show that those Phrygians [Col. 1343A] attributed to their Montanus many things that he himself had never claimed. It thus becomes probable that Tertullian held Montanus’s doctrine as it originally was—indeed acceptable in its beginning to the more austere Christians—but not as it later became, when, embellished with Phrygian embroidery by the impostures and deceits of certain followers, it began everywhere to be spurned by the catholic Churches.

PARADISE. II, 856 A. “Namely, destined to lodge in paradise, not in the lower regions”; see LOWER REGIONS.

PREPARATION (*PARATURA*), constitution. I, 409 A, “To discern constitutions.” The sense of the whole passage is: beings dwelling and having their abode in the air, close to the stars and accustomed to the clouds, can know the constitution of the air and thus predict rains, drought, pestilence, and famine. I, 433 A, “That spirit belonging to the constitution of demons and angels.” I, 519 A, “This newly introduced preparation.” La Cerda understands the new sect of the Christians; Havercamp, more correctly with Rhenanus, the New Testament. II, 908 B, “According to the constitution of each Testament.” II, 570 B, “Into the corporeal constitution of matter.” II, 259 B, “He provided no preparation for himself by which he might be recognized,” that is, an adornment or outstanding element of creation through which he might be recognized by what was, as it were, his own apparatus and peculiar glory. [Col. 1343B] II, 284 C, “Thus one builds who lacks his own preparation,” that is, material made ready for building. II, 321 C, “To such an extent he lacked preparation that it could be believed.” II, 361 A, “Every thought and every preparation,” that is, every subject matter and apparatus of arguments. II, 906 B; 907 A, “But only those who are manifestly prepared parade their entire womanhood,” that is, they walk with uncovered heads, the principal part of manifest feminine preparation, which women customarily cover. II, 937 B, “So that we may discuss in order the whole of our preparation”: he means the writings of both Testaments. II, 769 A, “I must strike them back with their own preparation.” II, 723 B, “In these”—food and drink—“lies most of the preparation for sleep.” II, 77 A, “Shod with the preparation of the Gospel.”

TO SPARE (*PARCERE*). I, 1283 B, “Since most pagan women spare the memory of their dearest husbands,” that is, [Col. 1343C] preserve it inviolate—namely, by abstaining from remarriage. are always in attendance upon someone and are familiar with him, II, 698 B.

TO PERFORM FUNERAL RITES (*PARENTARE*), to celebrate the due rites for parents. II, 927 B, “Perhaps those who perform funeral rites for him.” II, 795 B, “They perform funeral rites for the dead.” Pagans indeed did this, but Augustine establishes that Christians too in some places in his time repeatedly did so, in *City of God* VIII, chapter 27. They brought their meals to the memorials of the martyrs, prayed and made offerings so that they might eat, or even distributed some of the food to the needy. This practice, he says, is not followed by better Christians, and in most regions no such custom exists.

TO DISCHARGE (*PARERE*). II, 807 A, “Which can discharge so great a debt before Christ the Lord,” that is, render and pay so great a debt. A debtor obeys the summons of his creditor when he gives satisfaction. The one who demands commands; the one who provides what he promised obeys. II, 813 B, “Furthermore, to present the whole human being as a concretion of both substances,” that is, to make him present and produce him. I, 639 C, “Before these, three altars stand ready for three gods,” that is, they are at hand. I, 1203 A, “He made it present even in its own sacraments.” II, 654 B, “And his efforts are present externally, outside,” that is, they appear and are visible. [Col. 1343D] II, 191 C, “Such distinct proofs of each substance would not be presented”—that is, would not appear, in a forensic sense. II, 263 A, “How did the Lord appear from him in this world?”—that is, ὤφθη, ἐπεφάνη (“was seen,” “was manifested”): if the Lord could not exist here in substance, how could he exist in the appearance of substance?

TO MAKE EQUAL (*PARIARE*). II, 433 E, “Than one to whom they have made him equal in all respects.” II, 802 C, “He did not regard being equal to God as robbery,” τὸ εἶναι ἴσα Θεῷ (“to be equal to God”). *Pariari* is a forensic term meaning “to become equal,” while *pariare* means “to make equal” or “to equate.” II, 700 C, “Because this supply would not have matched it,” that is, would not have made itself equal or corresponded to it on equal terms. II, 705 A, “Their natural qualities correspond; their substantial qualities do not.”

EQUALITY (*PARILITAS*). II, 252 B, “By this very fact supplying testimony to their equality and unity.” II, 804 A, “Moreover, is an example composed from diversity or from equality?”

EQUALLY (*PARITER*). I, 386 A, “He is equally of age,” a Hellenism: τὴν ἡλικίαν ἴσα ἐστί (“he is equal in age”).

[Col. 1344A] SMALL SHIELD (*PARMULA*). I, 321 A, “Blood caught in a small shield from the slashed thigh.” In Gorlaeus’s *Dactyliotheca*, volume II, no. 664, there is an engraving of a gem in which a small shield, or *parmula*, leans against Bellona’s altar and is intended to catch the blood. But it is also evident from the gem in Leonardo Agostini I, 171 that a small palm-shaped vessel (*palmula*), which others substitute in the text, was used in these rites. There a naked priest of Bellona is seen piercing his side with the blade held in his left hand and raising before Bellona’s image his right hand filled with the gore flowing from his slashed thigh.

PARRICIDE (*PARRICIDIUM*). I, 1282 B, “They are assaulted by abortive parricides, the conception being dissolved by drugs.” Similarly, in the book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, “infants fought down for some time by their mothers.”

PARTNER (*PARTIARIUS*). II, 274 B, “A partner in destruction.” II, 343 A, “Learn here also, together with the Jews who share your error.” II, 796 B, “Partners in their opinion,” κοινωνούς.

PASTURE (*PASCUA*), food. I, 407 A, “Which is a more carefully attended pasture for them,” that is, the food for which they have greater concern.

INDISCRIMINATELY (*PASSIVE*), everywhere, in common, according to each person’s discretion. II, 956 A, “Yet let them circulate indiscriminately.”

PROMISCUITY (*PASSIVITAS*), indiscriminate frequency. II, 1044 A, “How many freedmen promiscuity brings forward for censure.” I, 325 A, “The promiscuity of luxury is a wandering Venus, roaming everywhere through lawful and unlawful things.” II, 234 B, “And it is not promiscuity,” that is, an affection flowing abroad indiscriminately and exposed to movements—dissolution and confusion. II, 585 B, “Concerning the promiscuity of life,” that is, a disorderly life spent according to lust, for which Irenaeus attests that the Valentinians were notorious. II, 397 C, “He permitted the promiscuity of my opinion,” that is, a common and indiscriminate principle, free to embrace any opinions whatsoever. II, 652 B, “They sometimes also possess intercourse of promiscuity,” [Col. 1344C] that is, the right of moving and passing back and forth indiscriminately, so that one is taken promiscuously in place of the other. II, 726 B, “And scattering all things in promiscuity,” that is, in the indiscriminate confusion and disturbance of things. II, 88 A, “Because promiscuity deceives,” that is, common usage, which prevails everywhere, deceives, so that corruption cannot be distinguished from the original condition, being in some fashion overshadowed and concealed by the indiscriminate use of everything.

PROMISCUOUSLY (*PASSIVITUS*), everywhere, commonly and indiscriminately among everyone. II, 1039 B, “Some, however, are promiscuously useful to everyone.”

COMMON (*PASSIVUS*), occurring everywhere, ordinary. II, 548 A, “Not with contrived leaders of common disciples.” II, 252 A, “An indiscriminate mode of life, of course.” I, 1277 B, “Through the permission then universally available,” that is, multiple marriages permitted everywhere. II, 936 B, “For your source in that passage is not indiscriminate,” that is, it is not wandering or unlimited, to be applied indiscriminately by you from any portion of Abraham’s history. II, 791 B, “Another common to all the others”—that is, ignoble, ordinary, shared. II, 985 A, “Without indiscriminate permission.”

[Col. 1344D] *PATAGIUM*, a golden stripe commonly sewn onto the upper part of a tunic. For married women it was called a *patagium*, and for men a *clavus*. II, 1036 B, “More gilded with a *patagium*.”
