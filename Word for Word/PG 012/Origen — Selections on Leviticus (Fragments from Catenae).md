## Source locus 1

“And he called Moses.” It is called *Leviticus*, because that which God ordained concerning the priesthood, the matters of the sacrifices, and as many things as are of this kind, is contained in this *Leviticus*. Exodus, then, also has legislation from God; but Leviticus relates how the priests, having been anointed, were consecrated, how the tabernacle was made and received a harmonious joining together, and many other things. If it also contains certain other things, this is not surprising: for the title, *Leviticus*, indicates the more important matter in it.

## Source locus 2

Of the same author. Those who do not rightly look into the discourse concerning sacrifices despise it either as legislation of little worth, containing nothing worthy of the Creator, or they impiously condemn the Creator himself as one who legislates trivial things. But the person who understands the saying of David and of the Apostle—the one saying, “Uncover my eyes, and I shall perceive the wonders from your law,” and the other crying, “The law is spiritual”—is able to see that lessons worthy of God are hidden in these things.

<!-- source-locus: 3 -->
## Source locus 3

“A human being from among you, if he brings gifts to the Lord.” We have observed in many places of the Scriptures that “gifts” are spoken of living creatures, but “sacrifices” of inanimate things, according to what is said in Genesis: “And God looked upon Abel and upon his gifts; but upon Cain and upon his sacrifices he did not look.” One must say this also: in the case of living things, “A human being,” it says, “from among you, if he brings gifts”; but in the case of inanimate things, “if a soul [brings].” The soul is the inanimate [element], so that from the irrational creature only the rational may be left, and from the inanimate thing, when conquered by the fire, only the living may remain.

<!-- source-locus: 4 -->
## Source locus 4

“They shall pour out the blood upon the altar.” The altar upon which are sacrifices of whole burnt-offering is called an altar of offering; but the altar of incense is the inner altar.

<!-- source-locus: 5 -->
## Source locus 5

Of the same author. Since the Jews delighted in sacrifices, having become accustomed to them in Egypt—as the making of the calf in the wilderness also testifies—God permitted them to offer these to him, thereby restraining the disorderly impulses of polytheism and preventing sacrifice to demons.

<!-- source-locus: 6 -->
## Source locus 6

“If one soul should sin involuntarily.” Involuntary is whatever evil a person may do. It should be noted that Scripture calls the involuntary inadvertence a sin. Blessed Paul also makes mention of certain other things, saying: “For the law had a shadow of the things to come.”

<!-- source-locus: 7 -->
## Source locus 7

Of the same. “If a soul should sin involuntarily,” he says, “and should not do from all the commandments of the Lord” [Source text: the syntax of κατὰ φύσιν ἔχει is uncertain], it has this according to its nature. For perhaps there are also certain ordinances of the Lord which ought not to be done, according to: “I gave them ordinances which were not good.” A soul therefore sins involuntarily whenever, under the pretext of piety, it keeps what is not required. And it needs a sacrifice for forgiveness; but the one acting deliberately does not need sacrifice, as the one who became a Jew in order that he might gain Jews, and who circumcised Timothy.

<!-- source-locus: 8 -->
## Source locus 8

“That soul which touches any unclean thing, or a dead thing.” By “dead thing” he means the human being dead to God, with whom one ought not even to eat; for the one touching him is unclean. And that is unclean which has been hunted by our adversary, the devil. Unclean cattle might be those who approach the Word in a beastly manner, who have sinned unto death and have not become worthy to be saved even with cattle, according to the saying: “You will save human beings and cattle, Lord.”

<!-- source-locus: 9 -->
## Source locus 9

“Everyone who touches them shall be sanctified.” Some say that “shall be sanctified” means “shall be defiled,” I suppose not because of the sacrifice, but because of the boldness of the one touching it. For Haggai says: “Whoever touches [it] shall be defiled.”

<!-- source-locus: 10 -->
## Source locus 10

Of the same. We say that sacrifices were rather an occasion of sustenance for the priests and a shadow of piety. But the profit from sacrificing is twofold: both because honour is offered to God, and because the things which someone sacrifices he would neither worship nor suppose to be God.

<!-- source-locus: 11 -->
## Source locus 11

“And you shall certainly not die.” The discourse seems to threaten death to priests who drink wine and strong drink whenever they enter the tabernacle. For bishops who pray on behalf of the people must turn away from drunkenness, not only that from wine but also that from things and human reasonings. It does not forbid this as though it were absurd, but because it knew the lack of self-control of the Israelites: whenever they partook, they went into excess; so that, when they enter the holy things, they may have the exactness of the soul sober and a subtle remembrance of God.

<!-- source-locus: 12 -->
## Source locus 12

“Why did you not eat the sin-offering in a holy place?” It shows that, if the priests do not eat, the sacrifice is incomplete and the sin remains; for it was necessary both for blood to be brought in and for them themselves to eat.

<!-- source-locus: 13 -->
## Source locus 13

“If today they have offered the things concerning their sin.” Since, he says, it happened that those involved with Nadab and Abihu, who offended God, died at the time when they had to eat the people’s sin-offering so that what it had sinned might be forgiven, how shall we, exposed today as being in sins, procure forgiveness through eating? For the priest must first make expiation concerning himself [Source text: `ἐξῶ άσασθαι`].

<!-- source-locus: 14 -->
## Source locus 14

“These are the cattle which you shall eat.” Wild beasts, then, are in no way clean; for all are unclean according to the law that says: “Everything which walks upon its hands among all the wild beasts shall be unclean for you.” In a general sense, wild beasts might be all the most savage human beings, concerning whom the Prophet says: “When evildoers draw near against me to eat my flesh,” and what follows.

<!-- source-locus: 15 -->
## Source locus 15

“Every beast dividing the hoof.” The cloven-hoofed one is the person who lives piously in this age and hastens toward that other age because of the age to come; and, in dividing the hoof and trimming the claws, removes the superfluous and dead things from the hands—that is, from actions—and from the feet—that is, from the course of life. Besides this, in rumination, he does not merely store up spiritual food, but also remembers it and studies it continually.

<!-- source-locus: 16 -->
## Source locus 16

“This is the law concerning cattle and birds and every soul.” It makes much discourse concerning clean and unclean things; first it trains the soul to be holy. For if what is natural, having come into being by God’s ordinance, is unclean, how much more what comes from a wicked choice and has not come into being by God’s ordinance? If someone should think unclean that of which God is maker, how much more what stands contrary to God’s will and does what he forbade?

<!-- source-locus: 17 -->
## Source locus 17

“In a human being, if some scar should arise in the skin of his flesh” [Source text: the opening quotation is badly distorted]. These things are referred to certain blemishes of the soul. And just as, in bodily wounds, after treatment, a trace of what the places suffered sometimes remains in what is called a scar, in the same way the soul which has received a wound of sin, even if it receives healing, sometimes has, as it were, a scar left behind. One must also understand the manner of its purification or non-purification.

<!-- source-locus: 18 -->
## Source locus 18

“In a man or woman in whom a [source text: `ἀρἡ`, probably `ἁφή`, “lesion”] of leprosy should arise.” It should be observed in which cases it is said of human beings: “If something should arise in someone in the skin of his flesh”; or, “In a man or woman, in whom a [source text: `ἁρὴ`, probably `ἁφή`] of leprosy should arise”; and in which cases: “If flesh should have an ulcer in its skin,” or, “If flesh should have a burn of fire in its skin.” These are wounds.

<!-- source-locus: 19 -->
## Source locus 19

“His garments shall be loosened.” By these words it intimates that the one whose soul has become leprous ought not to conceal his sins and cover them. Just as the garments of the one whose ugliness is covered are loosened, so it wishes him to be exposed to all, not having become a whitewashed tomb. To have the mouth covered means not to have freedom to open the mouth; and to go out from the camp means no longer to lodge oneself with the choirs of the saints.

<!-- source-locus: 20 -->
## Source locus 20

“And they shall slaughter the one little bird into an earthen vessel.” The little bird is sacrificed into an earthen vessel, living water having first been put into it, so that the cleansing may occur by water and blood, which came out from the side of the Saviour. By cedar wood it means the wood of our salvation. The twisted scarlet was a type of the precious blood through which the whole world is cleansed. The hyssop, I take it, signifies the warmth through the Spirit; and the scarlet cord signifies the Word’s union with flesh.

<!-- source-locus: 21 -->
## Source locus 21

“He shall shave his head and his beard.” He shaves the head with respect to the most fundamental and chief doctrines; with respect to the beard, which is a symbol of the man, he puts away the sins of the man; and with respect to the eyebrows, he puts away every conceit and, if I may so name it, brow-raising. These things which have grown upon and blossomed from the soul, he says, are symbols of deadness, being called hairs.

<!-- source-locus: 22 -->
## Source locus 22

“He shall take two unblemished year-old lambs.” The discourse divides the things offered into three: one for the release of former sins; one for being offered whole to God; and another, offered on behalf of the person being cleansed, for the things done by him. Perhaps also the three tenths of fine flour are taken, so that one of the tenths may be joined to the lamb being slaughtered, the second to the remaining [lamb], and the third to the sheep. Already, then, at the fifth cleansing it has not meal but fine flour, which is a symbol of the material of pure bread, and this mixed with oil. The oil is employed as feeding light, serving the need of light and not of fire. It also has, by itself and apart from the oil taken for mixing the fine flour, oil which is offered through the priest, amounting to a cotyle measure. And the saying, “The priest who cleanses shall set the person being cleansed, and these things, before the Lord,” seems very much to me to require a priest.

## Source locus 23

“And [Source text: the clause about the householder is damaged] shall come concerning his house and report to the priest.” We never say that anyone ought to go to the priest in order to accuse his own house, as the lawgiver says, but rather, as it were, to request medicine from a physician for what has happened. We say, then, that the synagogue of the Jews has become leprous. The person of the one reporting is the choir of the holy prophets; they report to the great high priest, that is, before indignation, the sufferings caused by the Jews’ reckless folly—not with hatred or hostility, but with reverence toward the Benefactor, confessing their transgressions yet nevertheless asking that they be pitied.
