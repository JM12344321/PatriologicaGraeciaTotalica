Amen. Here, at the end, the subject itself and the just expectation of the learned reader demand that we add something concerning the date of this most celebrated martyrdom. I understand from various notes of the most learned Holstenius that he investigated the matter at great length; nowhere, however, do I find that he definitively determined what conclusion he judged should be followed. After carefully considering everything, it seems to me that one may plausibly affirm that the date of the contest of these Thuburbitan martyrs should be fixed in the year of Christ 203. For these very Acts, which are now appearing for the first time, teach us that they fought in the arena on Geta Caesar’s birthday. This is stated most explicitly [Col. 0057C] in chapters II.3–4 and V.3 of our edition.

Geta was the younger son of the emperor Severus. Herodian, Book III, in agreement with Spartian and Xiphilinus’ excerpts from Dio, relates that Severus, while still alive, declared Geta and his elder brother Antoninus Caracalla joint participants in the imperial power. The most exact Petavius assigns this event to the seventh year of Severus’s reign, while aptly warning that the same honor was not conferred upon both brothers. The prerogative of age was preserved for the elder, who was made entirely equal to his father in the title and dignity of Augustus; whereas the younger brother Geta—then still only ten years old, since he is said to have been born in the consulship of the Silani—received only a share and merely the second rank of supreme dignity, with the appellation Caesar, as Spartian teaches more distinctly.

This distinction between the brothers remained until the twelfth year of Severus’s reign, which was the year of Christ 204, when Petavius observes from Zosimus and Censorinus that the Secular Games were celebrated at Rome. That in this year the name Augustus [Col. 0057D] was also bestowed upon Geta—Severus the father thinking, among the many other measures which Herodian records him as attempting to reconcile the perpetually discordant brothers, that this equalization in supreme power should also be tried—is securely confirmed by Scaliger, *Annotations on Eusebius*, p. 29, both from other distinguished testimonies and from this inscription on an ancient coin:

> IMP. CAES. P. SEPT. GETA. PIUS. AUG. SAECULARIA SACRA. S. C.  
> Emperor Caesar Publius Septimius Geta Pius Augustus. The Secular Rites. By Decree of the Senate.

Since, therefore, the holy martyr wrote in chapter V.3—with a credibility that everyone ought to regard as inviolable—that she had been destined for the arena in celebration of the birthday of Geta, who was still *Most Noble*, still Caesar, and not yet Augustus, we have the limits beyond which conjecture cannot wander in investigating the year of her death: precisely those which reliable history defines for us as the period during which Geta occupied the second rank of supreme authority. This period amounts [Col. 0058A] to exactly four years, namely from AD 199 to 203; for in the following year, 204, the year of the Secular Games, Geta was already Augustus, as we have seen.

But lest we should be free to select any one of the first three [Col. 0058B] years of this period for the martyrdom of the saints, we are restricted by the date assigned by the most exact chronologists to the persecution initiated against the Christians by that emperor: Severus’s tenth year, AD 202. The *Roman Martyrology* and these same Acts demonstrate that the passion of Saints Perpetua and Felicity occurred after this persecution had begun, since in chapter IV.1 they expressly recall that the persecution had already been proclaimed and put into effect—a general persecution, certainly, and one commanded by the Augusti—in which Jocundus, Saturninus, and Artaxius had already earned the palm through being burned alive.

I see that one might oppose to this the authority of most learned men: Pamelius, in his notes on Tertullian’s *To Scapula*; Petavius, in his *Doctrine of Times* and in his *Chronicle* at the year of Christ 201; and finally Philip Labbe, in his work *On Ecclesiastical Writers*, under Tertullian. They judge that Tertullian’s book *To Scapula* was written before Severus proclaimed the persecution. From this it follows that the prefecture of Hilarianus, under whom Saints Perpetua and Felicity suffered, must be carried back to a considerably earlier [Col. 0058C] date, namely AD 195 or even earlier. But then those holy martyrs could not have fought in the arena on the birthday of Geta Caesar, as these Acts testify that they did, since, as has been said—and as Petavius himself also holds—Geta was declared Caesar in the seventh year of his father Severus, which was AD 199.

I therefore find it credible that, if those most learned men had been able to see these Acts, which are now being published for the first time, they would never have assigned the composition of Tertullian’s *To Scapula* to AD 199 or 201, before Severus issued his edict against the Christians. For in that book Tertullian indicates that Hilarianus—under whom Saint Perpetua writes in chapter II.2 that she suffered, when the persecution had already been raging—preceded Scapula in the prefecture by an interval of at least several years. These are the holy martyr’s words: “The procurator Hilarianus, who had then received the power of the sword in place of the deceased proconsul Minucius Timinianus,” etc.

What other Hilarianus can we imagine here than the one whom Tertullian mentions when addressing Scapula [Col. 0058D] in these words?

> Just as under the governor Hilarianus, when they had shouted concerning the areas of our burial grounds, “Let there be no areas,” their own threshing floors were no more—for they did not gather in their harvests.

What he immediately adds concerning the rain of the preceding year plainly shows that Hilarianus’s prefecture was earlier than the time of Scapula by more than one or two years. Indeed, it is likely that the African prefects whom Tertullian mentions a little later in the same book—Cincius Severus, Vespronius Candidus, Asper, and Pudens—were, either all or most of them, successors of Hilarianus and predecessors of Scapula.

Anyone who carefully considers these points will be compelled to admit that the book *To Scapula* was by no means written before the beginning of the persecution proclaimed by Severus. Indeed, there are arguments suggesting that the book was written after Severus’s death. This is intimated by the manner in which Severus is spoken of whenever he is mentioned there, which is precisely the manner ordinarily used not of present events, but of matters recalled from an earlier period.

Not content with this, Joseph Scaliger, on p. 110 [Col. 0059A] of his *Annotations on Eusebius*, places Scapula’s prefecture—or at least the composition of the little work by Tertullian with which we are concerned—even after the death of Antoninus, Severus’s son. His conjecture, in my judgment, is by no means contemptible. It is drawn from the words of the same book, where, after naming a certain Christian who was dear to the emperor Severus and retained in the palace until Severus’s death, Tertullian adds:

> Antoninus also knew him very well, having been reared on Christian milk.

Scaliger thinks that no person of sound mind could believe that Tertullian wrote these words while Antoninus Caracalla was still alive.

But why do we struggle with slender subtleties extracted from a single word, when testimony clearer than the noonday sun and irrefragably marked by the heavens is available? In that very same book of Tertullian *To Scapula*, these words are found:

> For that sun also, in the assembly at Utica, was so nearly extinguished in its light and was so great a portent that it could not have suffered this through an ordinary eclipse, being situated [Col. 0059B] in its own *hypsoma* and house. You have astrologers.

These words indicate a great solar eclipse that was witnessed at Utica with public terror some time before Tertullian wrote them. The first—and, so far as I know, the only—man to calculate this eclipse was the most learned Antonius Lalovera of our Society, in an unpublished letter to Joannes Bessonus, likewise of our Society. I shall therefore transcribe his calculations here:

> In the year of Christ 210, on the eleventh day of April, there was an eclipsing new moon at Utica in Africa, at 11 hours, 57 minutes, and 14 seconds after midnight. This was the midpoint of the eclipse:
>
> Beginning: 10 hours, 44 minutes, 44 seconds. Time of immersion: 1 hour, 12 minutes, 30 seconds. End: 1 hour, 12 minutes, 1 second after midday. Time of emergence: 1 hour, 14 minutes, 47 seconds. Duration of the entire eclipse: 2 hours, 27 minutes, 13 seconds. Magnitude: 8 digits, 57 minutes, 30 seconds.

The “house of the sun” mentioned by Tertullian should not here be understood as the sign of Leo, but according to the method of the horoscope, as the portion or cusp [Col. 0059C] of the sign Aries, which the sun then occupied, touching the ecliptic. For, as Jovianus Pontanus observes in Book II of *Celestial Matters*, chapter 4, p. 75, and chapter 5, p. 79, such a place in any *dodecatemorion*—that is, any zodiacal sign—is the sun’s proper house and is often called by the ancients “the place of the sun,” or “of the god.”

At that time the sun was also in its own *hypsoma*, or exaltation, for this is the name that certain astrologers give to the sign Aries. Although—and Lalovera inclines still more strongly to this explanation—it can readily be understood that by the sun’s *hypsoma* Tertullian intended to indicate nothing other than the vertical altitude [Col. 0060A] of the sun as it culminated at midday, when it underwent that eclipse. If Tertullian seems to have denied that this could occur naturally, he erred in accordance with common opinion, with no serious fault, since the art was foreign to his studies and profession. Indeed, he himself appears to intimate as much by immediately referring the reader to astrologers, who concern themselves with such matters. In truth, nothing in the event lay beyond the ordinary course of nature.

Thus far the calculation of this eclipse. From it the point that we have long been striving to establish is manifestly demonstrated: Tertullian’s *To Scapula* was written long after AD 202, Severus’s tenth year, since it recounts a solar eclipse that occurred in AD 210, Severus’s eighteenth and final year, and recounts it in such a manner that it appears to have taken place several years earlier.

This is the place to present a conjecture of the most illustrious Holstenius concerning the date when the book *To Scapula* was written, which I found noted somewhere. After reporting Scaliger’s opinion [Col. 0060B] that it was written under Elagabalus, he says that he himself thinks it was written under Macrinus, since Sulpicius Severus testifies that the clergy were persecuted by Macrinus.

However this matter may stand, from what has thus far been said we seem entitled to draw the following conclusion concerning the subject in hand: we judge with sufficient probability that the martyrdom whose original Acts we now publish occurred in AD 203, when Lucius Septimius Geta Caesar and Lucius Septimius Plautianus were consuls. The fact that Geta Caesar was consul in that year seems to have been the reason why his birthday was honored with gladiatorial games and spectacles in the arena. Our chronology is therefore confirmed by this very indication of Geta’s consulship.

Baronius also agrees with it, relating at length that the holy martyrs Perpetua and Felicity suffered under the same consuls and in the same year of Severus, at no. XXI and following under the year 205. He reckons in this way because of his customary addition of two years, which he subsequently brings into agreement with the common computation by subtracting two years from the reign of the emperor Probus, as all learned chronologists have long observed: [Col. 0060C] Scaliger, Decker, Petavius, Bucherius, Bollandus, Henschenius, Philip Labbe, and others.

Accordingly, by every class of argument—namely, the authorities of weighty writers, reasons drawn from the matter itself, and finally celestial indications—the position we have proposed concerning the date of the triumph of the holy Thuburbitan martyrs Perpetua and Felicity remains confirmed with every degree of certainty: on the Nones of March, AD 203, they attained the palm of immortal glory.

Peter POSSINUS, of the Society of Jesus.]

She presented her own throat [Note: [Col. 0057A] Holstenius translated this.]. Perhaps so great a woman could not otherwise have been killed, because she was feared by the unclean spirit, unless she herself had willed it.

O most valiant and most blessed martyrs! O you who were truly called and chosen for the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ! Whoever magnifies, honors, and adores that glory ought surely to read these examples too, no less than the ancient ones, for the edification of the Church, [Col. 0058A] so that these new deeds of power may also testify that the one and the same Holy Spirit continues to work even to the present day, and may bear witness to almighty God the Father and his Son Jesus Christ our Lord, to whom belong splendor and immeasurable power for ever and ever. Amen.

# AN APOLOGETIC DISSERTATION IN DEFENSE OF THE ORTHODOXY OF SAINTS PERPETUA, FELICITY, AND THEIR COMPANIONS

## By Joseph Augustine Orsi, O.P., Cardinal-Priest of the Holy Roman Church

## CHAPTER ONE

[Col. 0061A]

### The orthodoxy of the holy martyrs Perpetua, Felicity, and their companions is vindicated by external arguments drawn from the authority of the Church and the ancient Fathers

I. The testimony of Tertullian, a contemporary writer, concerning Perpetua and her fellow martyrs.  
II. The deacon Pontius praised the Acts of these same martyrs in terms that are not obscure.  
III. The very ancient veneration of Perpetua and Felicity in the Roman Church is demonstrated from Bucherius’s extremely ancient calendar.  
IV. Saint Augustine mentions these martyrs with praise in various places and celebrated them in three treatises. Basnage, on a very slight conjecture, denies that the first of these as published belongs to the holy Doctor, and strives unsuccessfully to diminish and undermine it.  
V. The most celebrated of the churches of Carthage, [Col. 0061B] and the mother church of the others, was adorned with the relics of Perpetua and Felicity and named after them.  
VI. In the daily Canon of the Mass, their names are numbered among the more celebrated martyrs.  
VII. The argument established from these testimonies; Basnage—in vain. [The source heading breaks off here.]

There is absolutely no mark of religious veneration and observance which, from ancient times onward, the piety of the Catholic Church, the unanimous Christian affection for these martyrs throughout the world, and the devotion of the peoples did not immediately bestow upon these holy and most celebrated martyrs. Their names, indeed, appear in the most ancient ecclesiastical registers and are recorded in almost all the martyrologies; their solemnity and feast day were celebrated by great gatherings of the Christian people; their Acts were publicly read in churches; churches and altars were consecrated to God in their [Col. 0061C] honor and over their relics; panegyrical addresses were delivered by the Fathers on their annual festival; and finally, a special commemoration of them during the daily sacrifices has continued down to our own time.

I shall demonstrate all these things by the clearest testimonies. I shall arrange them so that it may be plainly and openly established that, from the time of their [Col. 0062A] death onward, they were regarded as true martyrs by a perpetual and uninterrupted tradition of the Church, and therefore died in the peace and communion of the Catholic Church.

### I

Tertullian, who was Perpetua’s contemporary and an inhabitant of Carthage, where Perpetua and her fellow martyrs were put to death, mentions her in chapter 55 of his book *On the Soul* in these words:

> Perpetua, the most valiant martyr, on the day of her passion, in a revelation of paradise, saw there only her fellow martyrs.

He does not indicate by even a single word that she was attached to the Montanist sect. That exceedingly vain man would certainly never have omitted such a thing, since he was accustomed to proclaim with puffed-up cheeks the glories of that wicked faction.

In chapter 9 of the same book he mentions a certain fanatical woman accustomed, as she believed, to visions of angels and of the Lord himself; nor does he neglect to warn that she belonged to his own communion:

> There is today among us, he says, a sister who has received charisms of revelations,

etc.

Near the end of his book *On the Veiling of Virgins*, he mentions another woman, whom he likewise calls a sister, [Col. 0062B] and declares in explicit words to have been a Montanist:

> For us—that is, the disciples of the Paraclete, who do not despise new prophecies, who honor charisms, and who receive visions and revelations—the Lord has also measured out the dimensions of the veil through revelations. For an angel said in a dream to a certain sister of ours,

etc.

He relates nothing of this kind where he speaks of Perpetua and boasts of nothing of this kind. He calls her only a martyr, and a most valiant one; he does not proclaim her his sister or his martyr, nor does he indicate by even a single word that she had been imbued with the superstitions and delusions of the Montanists.

Is it truly credible that he neglected to claim so singular an ornament of his sect for himself and his followers in explicit words, when he was so anxious to inform us that even insane little women—distinguished by no virtue but devoted solely to dreams—had emerged from the school and instruction [Col. 0062C] of Montanus? I shall refute below the arguments that Basnage brings against this and the case that he fashions out of these words of Tertullian in support of his own opinion.

### II

The deacon Pontius, who flourished approximately fifty years after the death of Perpetua and Felicity, has the following words in his *Life of Saint Cyprian*. In my judgment, they plainly refer to their Acts, as [Col. 0063A] the most illustrious Lucas Holstenius also observed long ago [Note: [Col. 0063D] In the *Notes on the Passion of Saints Perpetua, Felicity, etc.*, § II, verse 1.]:

> Our forebears bestowed so great an honor, out of reverence for martyrdom itself, upon laypeople and catechumens who had attained martyrdom that they committed many things concerning their passions—or, I might almost say, nearly everything—to writing, so that these accounts might come also to the knowledge of us who had not yet been born.

These words of Pontius point, as though with a finger, to the Acts of Perpetua and Felicity and fit them perfectly.

“Laypeople” in this passage, as Holstenius likewise observed, must be understood as laity who had attained no rank in the clergy. For Pontius himself says a little later:

> Many are the things that he did while still a layman, and many those he did when already a presbyter.

That both the women themselves and their companions in the passion were catechumens was carefully noted by the compiler of the Acts at their very beginning:

> [Col. 0063B] The young catechumens were arrested: Revocatus and his fellow slave Felicity, Saturninus and Secundulus, and among them Vibia Perpetua, well born, liberally educated, honorably married, having a father and mother and two brothers, one of them likewise a catechumen,

etc.

This latter appears to be Saturus, whom Perpetua calls her brother in these Acts:

> Then my brother said to me, “Lady sister,”

etc.

And below:

> And I immediately told my brother, and we understood that a passion was to occur.

She is speaking of Saturus, her fellow soldier in the same contest.

Finally, these Acts were written with the sort of care praised by Pontius in the words quoted above. They contain many things—or, I might almost say, nearly everything—pertaining to their passions, down to the smallest circumstances, [Col. 0063C] so that I know of no Acts of any ancient martyrs that were described with greater accuracy and care.

Pontius’s words therefore fit the Acts before us so beautifully that they fit none now extant better. I very much doubt whether, in Pontius’s time, any others existed to which his words could more aptly be referred, since it does not appear to have been so much the custom of the earlier age to commit the deeds of the martyrs to writing with such meticulous care.

Dodwell is therefore mistaken when he concludes from the words quoted [Note: [Col. 0063D] *Cyprianic Dissertation XI, On the Small Number of Martyrs*, §§ 3 and 5.] that:

> The ancients were so diligent in committing the deeds of the martyrs to memory that almost nothing escaped them,

and hence infers the small number of martyrs from the small number of Acts that survive. [Col. 0063D] Pontius does not assert that the ancients exercised equal care in committing the deeds of every individual martyr to memory. For his purpose, the very clear example of these Acts was sufficient, through which the memory of lay and catechumen martyrs had been so diligently transmitted to posterity. From this everyone could readily understand how much more diligently Pontius himself ought to commit to literary monuments the illustrious deeds of a bishop and martyr.

I therefore have no doubt that Pontius had before his eyes the Acts of Perpetua, Felicity, and their companions. They concern both laypeople and catechumens; the period fits readily, since [Col. 0064A] the events occurred before Pontius himself was born; and they were composed with precisely the zeal and care that he describes. Thus, Pontius’s quoted words provide no obscure indication that by the middle of the third century Perpetua and her companions were regarded at Carthage, where they had shed their blood, as true martyrs.

### III

No one will wonder at this who reflects that in the following century—that is, the fourth century of the Church—the festival of Perpetua and Felicity was also celebrated at Rome. This is established by the most ancient calendar of the Roman Church published by Bucherius, which scholars unanimously appear to assign to the time of Liberius. In this monument of venerable antiquity, therefore, the names “Perpetua and Felicity, in Africa” are entered under the Nones of March. [Col. 0064B] This is surely an indisputable proof of exceedingly ancient veneration in the Church.

For when learned men assign the Bucherian calendar to the time of Liberius, they do not mean that the solemnities of the martyrs listed in it were first instituted at Rome at that time. Such a thought would be absurd. This Bucherian or Liberian list was copied from more ancient diptychs and registers. These were not something the churches—especially the Apostolic Church, and above all and preeminently the Roman Church—possessed for the first time at that date. Rather, as we learn from Tertullian [Note: [Col. 0064D] *On Prescription*, chapter 32, and *On the Soldier’s Crown*, chapter 13.] and other authors, they had sacred calendars from the very beginning, arranged with the greatest possible accuracy according to the conditions of those times.

It could therefore very well have happened that the feast day of Perpetua and Felicity had begun to be celebrated in the city several years before Liberius. And is it not extremely probable that their veneration was received and disseminated at Carthage [Col. 0064C] before it was at Rome? Since Liberius, under whom the quoted calendar was compiled, began his pontificate in the middle of the fourth century, it would be the act of an excessively captious critic to deny that the memory of our martyrs was celebrated at Carthage at least at the beginning of the fourth century.

There is another point concerning this Liberian list of martyrs that I should like to have carefully observed, since from it one may readily understand not only the antiquity but also the renown of the veneration of our martyrs. Apart from our martyrs and Cyprian, all the martyrs listed in this calendar are Roman; except for these three, no foreign martyr has a place in it. This certainly demonstrates clearly and openly how renowned [Col. 0064D] those martyrs had been from ancient times.

The Oxford editor of Cyprian’s works aptly observed that, from the earliest centuries of the Christian faith, the holy martyrs were considered to belong not to one particular church but to all. Yet, given the conditions of the periods about which he speaks, there is something in his words that is not expressed with sufficient precision:

> We see, he says [Note: [Col. 0064D] In the notes on Celerinus’s Letter, no. 21 among the Cyprianic letters.], the Acts of martyrs transmitted from one church to others, and the martyrologies of individual churches containing not only the names of their own people but also those of persons living far away.

[Col. 0065A] If he is speaking of later martyrologies—the Hieronymian, for example, and the others derived from it—there is no reason for us to raise any dispute with the learned man. In the most ancient calendars, however, each church presents only its own martyrs, with a few exceptions, or those of neighboring provinces.

Thus, in Bucherius’s Roman calendar, apart from our martyrs and Cyprian, as has been said, all are Romans. In Mabillon’s Carthaginian calendar, nearly all are Carthaginians or Africans, with only a few from certain other provinces with which the Christians of Carthage formerly maintained considerable relations. That more extensive martyrologies were always compiled as time progressed—containing not merely the names belonging to individual churches but also those of people living far away—is clear and evident, [Col. 0065B] as is the fact that the number of festivals greatly increased in later centuries and that solemnities were also adopted in which churches extremely remote and widely separated from one another celebrated one another’s martyrs.

But to return to Perpetua and Felicity: will anyone deny that it must be attributed to their fame and renown that, alone with Cyprian among the African martyrs—and indeed among the martyrs of other provinces as well—they had a place in the Roman calendar during those ancient times, when each church was content with its own martyrs and recorded the solemnities of foreign martyrs only in the rarest cases?

In Cyprian’s case, it is readily understood whence came so distinguished an honor that his name should be recorded with those of the Roman martyrs in the Liberian list. This was granted because of his most illustrious [Col. 0065C] services, which extended far and wide not only throughout the Church of Carthage and all Africa, but throughout the entire Church wherever it reaches, and especially the Roman Church.

But whence did our martyrs obtain the same glorious renown as Cyprian? How was it that, out of all the martyrs who watered the arenas of Africa with their glorious blood, they alone, together with that most holy and celebrated bishop, obtained a special solemnity at Rome at so early a date—that is, not long after the beginning of the fourth century? Surely either from their Acts or from the frequency of miracles at their memorials. Basnage will certainly not say that it came from miracles. It must therefore have come from the Acts, which accordingly were then in everyone’s hands.

Is it not absurd to suppose that within the space of a single century it could have fallen out of human memory that these most celebrated [Col. 0065D] and well-known women, whose Acts Christians read with extraordinary delight, had been tainted by Montanist errors? This is all the harder to believe in proportion to the eminence of their fame. Matters pertaining to obscure persons are readily erased from the memory of posterity through forgetfulness. But so singular a fact—namely, that women admired by all for the glory of their unconquered courage and their heavenly revelations had been devoted to Montanist superstitions—could not, after so short an interval, have been permitted to pass into oblivion and disappear from the memory either of Catholics or of heretics.

It could not have disappeared from the memory of Catholics, for just as they avoided association even with heretics distinguished by the glory of confession, so they took anxious precautions lest false martyrs be received into the sacred diptychs in place of true ones—that is, heretical martyrs in place of Catholic martyrs. Although the names of heretics sometimes slipped into the martyrologies, this happened, as will be demonstrated below, in later centuries, [Col. 0066A] extremely remote from the memory of the events. Yet what we are now discussing would have occurred scarcely a century after their passion, something that certainly lacks any parallel among martyrs of equal renown and reputation. Nor would the Montanists, a far from uncommon heretical faction in Africa, have allowed the Catholics to appropriate their martyrs. But there will be a fuller and more appropriate place to discuss this elsewhere.

### IV

At the beginning of the fifth century Augustine flourished. In his catalogue, Possidius lists three treatises by him *On the Feast Day of Perpetua and Felicity*. [Col. 0066B] In the most recent edition of Augustine’s works, prepared through the efforts of the Benedictine Fathers of the Congregation of Saint-Maur, these appear among the sermons as nos. CCLXXX, CCLXXXI, and CCLXXXII.

Basnage calls the first—that is, Sermon 280, otherwise Sermon 103 *On Various Subjects*—into doubt. Indeed, on the basis of a very slight conjecture, he judges that it is not Augustine’s:

> We do not believe, he says [Note: [Col. 0066D] At the year 203, no. VI.], that the sermon *On the Feast Day of the Holy Martyrs Perpetua and Felicity* is Augustine’s, where these words are read: “As their exhortations in divine revelations and the triumphs of their passions were being read, we heard them.” These words surely do not accord with Augustine’s position, since in explicit terms he excludes Perpetua’s visions from Holy Scripture [Note: [Col. 0066D] Book I of *The Soul and Its Origin*, chapter 10.]: “As for Saint Perpetua’s brother Dinocrates, the writing itself is not canonical.” But what could be more canonical than divine revelations, as the author of the sermon considers them?

[Col. 0066C] Yet not only Augustine, but no Catholic writer whatsoever, would have understood “divine” here to mean “canonical.” For who, apart from the Montanists, regarded private revelations of this kind, committed to writing on private authority, as canonical? Will Basnage therefore say that the author of the sermon was some declaimer from the Montanist sect? I do not think the learned man will say so.

Whoever the author of the sermon may have been, he was certainly learned, Catholic, and thoroughly Augustinian. He called the revelations “divine” because, as he piously believed, they had been sent by God, not because he believed that they had been written under divine authority. For it is one thing for a revelation to be divine, and another for the writing in which such a revelation is presented to us to be divine and canonical.

For us to call a revelation divine, [Col. 0066D] it is sufficient that we judge it probable that the revelation had God as its author. But for us to call the writing in which such a revelation is contained canonical, it must have had a canonical author inspired by the divine Spirit.

Augustine, therefore, as we shall subsequently see, judged it probable that Perpetua’s revelations came from God, and could accordingly have called them divine in this sense and meaning. Yet it did not even enter the author of the sermon’s mind that the Acts of Perpetua in which such revelations are read were canonical Scripture, or that their author had been inspired by the divine Spirit and endowed with divine authority.

There is therefore complete agreement between the author of the sermon, whoever he may have been, and Augustine. On so slight and empty a conjecture the quoted sermon ought not to have been denied to Augustine.

[Col. 0067A] But let it be granted that neither this sermon nor the two following sermons for the feast day of the martyrs Perpetua and Felicity, which now stand among Augustine’s works, is Augustine’s. Will Basnage deny the explicit testimony of Possidius that the holy Doctor delivered three treatises on the feast day of Perpetua and Felicity? Will he therefore deny that the holy Doctor regarded them as true and Catholic martyrs and that, at the beginning of the fifth century, their solemnity was celebrated in Africa?

Will he deny that Augustine wrote the exposition of Psalm 47, where he mentions Saint Perpetua and indicates that her Acts were publicly read in the churches?

> As we know, he says, [Col. 0067B] and have read in the Passion of blessed Perpetua.

Moreover, in the books *The Soul and Its Origin* [Note: [Col. 0067D] See Book I, chapter 10; Book III, chapter 11; and Book IV, chapter 18.] he everywhere calls Perpetua holy. Although he excludes the Acts of her passion from the canon of Scripture and refuses to have testimony from them advanced against the mysteries of faith, he nevertheless places them at the same rank and value as the Acts of the other martyrs and gives them the same credit as the others. Nowhere does he regard Perpetua’s visions as the dreams of silly women, reject them as Montanist inventions, or dismiss them as deceptions of the human spirit. He therefore believes, with the degree of confidence that prudent and judicious men customarily grant to monuments of this kind, that they were sent by God and were, in this sense, divine.
