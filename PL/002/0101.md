For as regards fasts, they set against us the fixed days appointed by God, as when in Leviticus the Lord commands Moses that the tenth day of the seventh month is the Day of Atonement, saying: “It shall be a holy day for you, and you shall afflict your souls”; [Col. 0955B] and “every soul that has not been afflicted on that day shall be cut off from his people” (Lev. 16:29).

> **Note [Col. 0955C].** Certainly they suppose that in the Gospel those days were designated for fasting on which the Bridegroom was taken away, and that these alone are now the lawful days for fasting. “In the Gospel,” that is, today, among Christians. So think, namely, the Catholics, whom he calls the “Psychics.” Yet in his book *On Prayer* he expressed the view that on the day of the Passion there is a common and, as it were, public observance of fasting—as though among Catholics, apart from the day of the Passion, or apart from those days on which the Bridegroom was taken away, there were no lawful days for fasting, nor any common or public observance of fasting, but, as he will soon say, one practiced freely at each person’s discretion. And yet the testimony of Jerome in his *Epistle to Marcella* ought to carry the greatest authority: that the Catholic Church, by apostolic tradition, fasts one Lent (*Quadragesima*) each year at the appropriate season. For who would listen to someone saying that those days on which the Bridegroom was taken away—Good Friday and Saturday, a period of forty-eight hours—constituted that one Lent? Or would it be more truthful to say that the ancient Christians, formed by divine [Col. 0955D] and apostolic institutions, called their fasts “Lent” after Christ’s forty-day fast, so as to signify that they were not Jewish but Christian, and that the punctilious observances of the Jewish law—pure meals, great days, and sabbath fasts—had been abolished by the benefit of Christian liberty?

> Accordingly, mindful of the liberty obtained through the Lord’s Passion, which had occurred on the day of Preparation, they consecrated the day of Preparation to fasting, so that on that day there might be what Septimius calls in his book *On Prayer* “a common and, as it were, public observance of fasting.” Soon afterward, in their desire to manifest the grief conceived from the Lord’s Passion, they extended this kind of fast into Saturday. They nevertheless continued to observe other fasts useful for commending their prayers, though these varied according to each person’s discretion; above all, however, they remained free from the yoke of that sabbath rite. Indeed, lest they appear to Judaize in any respect, they did not call the fast of the Christian day of Preparation, or of the Pascha of the Crucifixion (*πάσχα σταυρωσίμου*), and of the following Saturday either “the Preparation” or “the Sabbath,” but “Lent”; so that it might now be a Christian [Col. 0956B] fast—not a sacrament of the Preparation or the Sabbath, but of that Lent which Christ had completed by an altogether divine fast.

> Certainly, in this sense, what Catholics in Tertullian’s age said—that those days on which the Bridegroom was taken away were days designated for fasting, while at other times one should fast differently, by discretion rather than command, according to each person’s circumstances and reasons; and that the apostles had observed the same rule, imposing no other yoke of fixed fasts to be undertaken by everyone in common—will be confirmed by Jerome’s statement in his letter to Marcella that the Catholic Church, by apostolic tradition, fasts one Lent each year at the appropriate season. For whatever Christians fasted—which Irenaeus, as quoted by Eusebius in *History* V, relates was observed with such variety and diversity that some believed they should fast for one day, others for two, others for more, while still others prolonged their own day of fasting for forty hours, day and night—whatever, I say, and in whatever manner they fasted, they professed that they were fasting Lent. Socrates too, in the fifth book of his *History*, says that this seemed remarkable to him. He certainly would have had less cause for wonder had he remembered that this was the apostles’ intention: once the Jewish sabbaths had been effaced, Christians were to name the fasts that they offered to their Lord—such little things in return for such great things—after the duration of the Lord’s fast, so that those who were less capable [Col. 0956D] of attaining it in actual practice might at least honor it in remembrance. Thus some, by a fast of one day, others of two, and others of several days, alike honored Christ’s Lent while nevertheless maintaining peace among themselves. For this is what Irenaeus says: *ἡ διαφωνία τῆς νηστείας τὴν εὔνοιαν τῆς πίστεως συνίστησι*—“Difference in fasting commends concord in faith.” RIG., following the editions.

Certainly, in [Col. 0956A] the Gospel they suppose that those days were designated for fasting on which the Bridegroom was taken away, and that these alone are now the lawful days for Christian fasts, once the ancient observances of the Law and the Prophets have been abolished. For when it suits them, they acknowledge the meaning of “the Law and the Prophets were until John” (Luke 16:16). Accordingly, thereafter fasting is to be undertaken without distinction, [variant: “differently,” Rig., Venetian ed.] at one’s discretion, not by command of the new discipline, according to each person’s circumstances and reasons.

As for xerophagies, [Col. 0956B] they regard the term as a new name for an affected observance and something very close to pagan superstition. Such are the purificatory rites that cleanse Apis, Isis, and the Great Mother through the exclusion of specified foods. For faith, being free in Christ, owes no abstinence from particular foods even to the Jewish law, since the Apostle has once for all admitted it to the entire meat market. He denounces those who, just as they forbid marriage, command abstention from foods created by God; and thus, they say, we were already marked out at that time as those who in the last days would depart from the faith, giving heed to the seductive spirits of the world and to the teachings of liars, having their conscience branded with a hot iron (1 Tim. 4:1). With what fires, I ask you? I suppose with those by which we so often conduct weddings and cook our dinners every day.

Likewise they say that we too are struck by what was said to the Galatians (Gal. 4:10), as observers of days and months and years. Meanwhile they also hurl at us Isaiah’s declaration: “The Lord has not chosen such a fast” (Isa. 58:4–5)—that is, not abstinence from food, but the works of justice that he adds afterward. They say too that the Lord himself in the Gospel gave a concise answer to every [Col. 0957B] scruple concerning diet [variant: “of diet,” Semler]: a person is not defiled [text: *communicari*; variants: *coinquinari*, Semler and Leopold] by what is put into the mouth, but by what proceeds from the mouth (Mark 7:15). He himself ate and drank, even to the point of being reproached: “Behold, a gluttonous man and a drinker” (Matt. 11:19). Likewise, they say, the Apostle teaches that food does not commend us to God: we do not abound if we eat, nor are we deficient if we do not eat (1 Cor. 8:8).

By these and similar arguments they now subtly aim at enabling everyone more inclined toward his belly to regard as superfluous and not particularly necessary the observances connected with food withheld, reduced, or delayed—

## CHAPTER III

Accordingly, we must first affirm that which is in danger of being covertly undermined: how much this emptiness avails before God, and, before everything else, whence arose the very principle of meriting God’s favor in this manner.

> **Note [Col. 0958D].** “For the necessity of the observance will then be acknowledged when the authority of its principle has become evident.” In his book *On the Crown*, Tertullian himself prescribed the rule by which a custom or observance is to be approved: namely, that a custom must be honored when it is found consonant with reason, and that the rationale of Christian observances becomes stronger when they are also defended by nature, which is the first discipline of all. Here, however, he appeals only to the authority of reason. RIG.

For the necessity of the observance will then be acknowledged when the authority of its principle, which must be traced from the beginning, has become evident.

[Col. 0958A] Adam had received from God the law that he was not to taste of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, and that he would die if he tasted it. But then, after the spiritual ecstasy in which he had prophesied that great mystery concerning Christ and the Church, he himself reverted to the psychical state [variant: “was overthrown,” Junius]. No longer comprehending the things of the Spirit, he yielded more readily to his belly than to God, consented to food rather than to the commandment, and sold salvation for gluttony. In short, he ate and perished; otherwise he would have been saved if he had preferred to fast from that one little tree [Semler also reads “had preferred”].

> **Note [Col. 0958D].** “If he had preferred to fast from that one little tree.” What sort of fast was that, seeing that God had permitted Adam to eat from every tree, excepting only one little tree? In truth, Adam was destroyed not by gluttony but by desire for a premature divinity. RIG.

Thus from this point psychical faith may recognize its own seed, deriving from it its appetite for fleshly things and its rejection of spiritual things.

I therefore hold that from the beginning gluttony has been a murderer, destined to be punished with torments and penalties on the Day of Judgment, even if God had commanded no fasts. Nevertheless, by showing me whence Adam had been slain, he who had revealed the offense had left me to understand its remedies: of my own accord, in whatever ways and at whatever times I could, I was to regard food as poison and take hunger as its antidote. Through hunger I was to purge away the cause of death transmitted from the beginning [variant: “the fall,” Semler] into me together with the whole race. I was certain that this was God’s will, since he had willed the contrary not to be done, and sufficiently confident that concern for self-restraint would please him from whom I had learned that the fault of self-indulgence was condemned.

Moreover, since he himself commands fasting and specifically calls the soul afflicted by restrictions of food a sacrifice (Ps. 50:18), who will now doubt that this was the rationale of every mortification concerning diet: that through food again being forbidden and the commandment being observed, the primal sin might now be expiated, so that a human being might make satisfaction to God through the same material that had occasioned his offense, [Col. 0958C] that is, through the prohibition of food; and thus rekindle salvation by abstinence, in a manner corresponding to that in which he had extinguished it by overfeeding, now despising many lawful foods in compensation for the one unlawful food?

## CHAPTER IV

This principle was preserved in the providence of God, who regulates [variant: “governs,” Semler] everything according to its proper seasons. Let no one, therefore, argue contrariwise in order to overthrow our proposition: “Why, then, did God not immediately establish some restriction of diet? Why did he, rather, even enlarge the permission?”

For in the beginning he had indeed assigned to humankind only the produce of plants and trees [Col. 0959A]: “Behold, I have given you every seed-bearing plant that sows seed upon the earth, and every tree that has within itself the fruit of seed fit for sowing; it shall be food for you” (Gen. 1:29 [printed here as Gen. 9:29]). But afterward, when he had enumerated to Noah the subjection of all the beasts of the earth, the birds of heaven, the creatures moving upon the earth, the fish of the sea, and every living creature [variant: “creeping creature,” Semler], he said: “They shall be food for you; as I gave you all the green plants, so have I given you them all. But you shall not eat flesh with the blood of its life” (Gen. 9:2). For by the very fact that he excludes from consumption [Semler adds “removes”] only that flesh whose life is not poured out through its blood, it is manifest that he permitted the use [variant: “consumption,” Junius] of every other kind of flesh.

To this we reply that it was not fitting for a human being to be burdened at that point with any law of abstinence, when he had just proved incapable of enduring so slight a prohibition as that of a single fruit. He was therefore to be relaxed and strengthened by liberty itself. Likewise, [variant: “and,” Semler and Leopold] after the flood, in the restoration of the human race, it was enough for the time being to impose the single law of abstinence from blood while permitting [variant: “by permission,” Semler] the use [Junius thinks “consumption” had perhaps dropped out] of everything else. For the Lord had already displayed judgment through the flood; he had also issued a further threat through the demand for blood from the hand of a brother and from the hand of a beast (Gen. 9:5).

> **Note [Col. 0959D].** “From the hand of a beast, every…” Thus Junius and Leopold.

Therefore, in administering beforehand all that was necessary for the justice of judgment, he released the material of liberty, preparing discipline by indulgence: permitting all things so that he might remove certain things; intending to demand more if he had entrusted more; and intending to command abstinence after he had first granted indulgence [variant: “permitted,” Semler]. Thus, all the more, as we have said, the primal sin would be expiated by the practice of greater abstinence amid the occasion of greater license.

## CHAPTER VI

Now, if we have rashly referred the rationale of diet restricted by God, and to be restricted by us for God’s sake, back to the experiences of the beginning, let us consult the common consciousness. Nature herself will declare what sort of persons she customarily presents us as being before food and drink, while our saliva is still untouched—at least in conducting those affairs of the senses [variant: “by sense,” Gelenius] in which divine things are treated: how much more powerful in mind and how much more lively in heart than when that whole dwelling of the inner person, packed with food, flooded with wine, [Col. 0960C] and heated by excrement already being digested [variant: “seething,” others], becomes a preliminary workshop for latrines—

> **Note [Col. 0960D].** “A preliminary workshop.” Blessed Jerome also imitated this: “What kind of fast,” he says, “or what kind of refreshment after a fast is it? We distend ourselves with the previous day’s feasts, and our throat becomes a workshop—or rather a preliminary workshop—for latrines.” He too cites the Scriptures quoted here: Exodus 31, Deuteronomy 33 and 8, and Isaiah 6; except that there too the reading should be “God,” which in Greek is *θεόν*, and that the author uses his own expression *salvificatorie* for “saving.” PAM.

—in which, plainly, nothing remains nearer at hand than to think in the direction of lust. “The people ate and drank and rose up to play” (Ex. 32:6). Understand the modesty of Holy Scripture: it would not have censured the play unless it had been indecent.

Besides, how few will remember religion when the places of memory are occupied and the faculties of wisdom obstructed? No one will remember God as he ought, as is fitting, and as is useful, at the time—

> **Note [Cols. 0960D–0961D].** “At the time when it is customary for a person to fall away from himself.” For through drunkenness wisdom dies for a time. RIG.

—when it is customary for a person to fall away from himself. Food [Col. 0961A] either kills or wounds every discipline.

I am lying if the Lord himself, when reproaching Israel for forgetting him, does not attribute [variant: “reckon,” Semler] the cause to fullness: “The beloved grew fat and stout and broad; and he abandoned God who made him and withdrew from the Lord his Savior” (Deut. 32:15). Finally, in the same Deuteronomy, when commanding that precaution be taken against the same cause, he says: “Lest, when you have eaten and drunk and built excellent houses, and your sheep and cattle have multiplied, and your silver and gold, your heart be exalted and you forget the Lord your God” (Deut. 8:12).

He placed before the corrupting influence of riches the monstrousness of voracity, which riches themselves procure. Through them, of course, the people’s heart had grown fat, so that it could not see with its eyes, hear with its ears, or understand with its heart, obstructed by the fat that God specifically removed from consumption, thereby unteaching humanity to pursue fattening.

By contrast, in whom was the heart found to be uplifted [variant: “carried upward,” Francis] rather than fattened? The man who maintained a continuous fast for forty days and as many nights, beyond the capacity of human nature, while spiritual faith supplied the strength. He saw the glory of God with his eyes, heard the voice of God with his ears, and understood with his heart the law of God, who was already teaching at that time that a person lives not by bread alone but by every word of God (Luke 4:4). Meanwhile the fatter people were unable steadfastly to contemplate even Moses himself being fed by God and his abstinence being fattened by God’s name [variant: “divine power,” Wouwer’s manuscript and Leopold].

Deservedly, therefore, the Lord later showed himself in the flesh to Moses as a companion in his fasts, and no less to Elijah. For Elijah too, by the very fact that he first called down a famine, had already dedicated himself sufficiently to fasting: “As the Lord lives,” he said, “before whose sight I stand, there shall be neither dew nor rain in these years” (3 Kings 17).

Then, fleeing from Jezebel’s threat, after a single portion of food and drink that he had found when awakened by an angel, he too, for forty days and nights, with empty stomach and dry mouth, reached Mount Horeb. There, when he had turned aside into a cave [variant: “into the cave,” Junius], with what intimate fellowship from God was he received! “What are you doing here, Elijah?” (3 Kings 19:9). That voice was far friendlier than “Adam, where are you?” (Gen. 3:9). For the latter threatened a man who had eaten; the former caressed [variant: “the fasting man,” Venetian ed.] one who had fasted. So great is the privilege of restricted diet that it gives God to a human being as his tent-companion—truly, like with like. For if the eternal God does not hunger (Isa. 1:11), as he testifies through Isaiah, then the time when a human being is made equal to God will be [Col. 0961D] when he lives without food.

## CHAPTER VII

[Col. 0962A]

> **Note [Col. 0961D].** “We have therefore now proceeded.” He has shown what intemperance produces, so that he may demonstrate the effects of fasting, which he pursues here through various examples. LE PR.

We have therefore now proceeded to examples, in order to review the effective powers [variant: “power,” Venetian ed.] of the usefulness of this [variant: “that very,” Semler] observance, which even reconciles an angry God to humankind.

Israel had sinned; but when it was gathered by Samuel at the drawing of water at Mizpah [variant: “Mizpah,” Semler; “but” absent in Francis], it immediately washed away the sin by fasting, with the result that it simultaneously escaped [variant: “routed,” Semler] the danger of battle. At the very time when Samuel was offering the burnt offering (1 Kings 7:9), we hear that God’s clemency was procured by nothing more than the people’s abstinence.

And the Philistines were moving into battle [conjectures: “so that the Philistines moved into battle,” Junius; Leopold conjectures *admovedbantur*]; then at last [variant: “in that very place,” Francis] the Lord thundered with a mighty voice over the Philistines, and they were thrown into confusion and fell before Israel. The men of Israel went forth from Mizpah and pursued the Philistines, and as far as Beth-car the unfed struck down the well-fed, and the unarmed [Col. 0962B] the armed. Such will be the strength of those who fast for God: heaven fights on behalf of such people. Here you have the pattern of protection necessary also for spiritual wars.

Likewise, when Sennacherib, king of the Assyrians, after already capturing many cities, directed blasphemies and threats against Israel through Rabshakeh (4 Kings 18–19), what but this diverted him from his purpose toward Ethiopia? Then what but King Hezekiah’s humiliation destroyed one hundred and eighty thousand of his army through an angel? For when the enemy’s cruelty had been reported, the king tore his garment, put on sackcloth, and ordered the elders of the priests to approach God through Isaiah in the same dress, prayer of course being accompanied by fasting. For there is no time for food in danger,

> **Note [Col. 0962D].** “Nor any display of fullness in sackcloth.” He calls it a display of fullness because gladness arises from fullness, and from gladness concern for more luxurious adornment; this is incompatible with the horror of ashes and the roughness of sackcloth. RIG.

nor any display of fullness in sackcloth. Abstinence always follows grief, just as overfeeding attends gladness.

Through this attendant of grief—abstinence—even that sinful city Nineveh was delivered from the destruction proclaimed against it. For repentance for its crimes had sufficiently commended it to God when it had fulfilled a three-day fast, even starving the cattle, with whom God was not angry (Jonah 3). Sodom and Gomorrah too would have escaped if they had fasted.

Ahab also recognizes this remedy when, after his transgression, idolatry, and the murder of Naboth—whom Jezebel had killed because of the vineyard—Elijah reproached him: “How have you killed and taken possession of the inheritance? In the place where [variant: “in which place,” Semler] the dogs licked Naboth’s blood, they shall lick yours as well” (3 Kings 21:19). He humbled himself, put sackcloth upon his body, fasted, and slept in sackcloth. Then the word of the Lord came to Elijah: “Have you seen how Ahab has shown reverence [Col. 0962D] before my face? Because he has shown reverence, I shall not bring the affliction in his days, but in the days of his son [Col. 0963A] I shall bring it” (ibid., 29)—for the son was not going to fast.

Thus fasting is an act of reverence toward God. Through it Hannah too, Elkanah’s petitioning wife, previously barren, easily obtained from God that her stomach, emptied of food, should be filled with a son—and indeed a prophet.

But fasts will merit from God not only a change in nature, the averting of dangers, or the obliteration of sins, but also the recognition of mysteries.

> **Note [Col. 0963D].** “Recognition of mysteries.” Thus he calls skill in interpreting sacred figures and images—those, namely, that are divinely presented in dreams. “Mysteries,” therefore, are dreams sent by God. RIG.

Look at Daniel’s example. Concerning the king of Babylon’s dream, all the sophists are thrown into confusion.

> **Note [Col. 0963D].** “All the sophists are thrown into confusion.” He calls those “sophists” whom elsewhere he calls sages, interpreters of dreams, and conjecturers. RIG.

They deny outright [variant: “further,” Junius] that it can be known by human excellence.

> **Note [Col. 0963D].** “They deny outright that it can be known by human excellence.” That is, they deny that an interpretation of the royal dreams can be furnished by human ability. RIG.

Daniel alone, trusting in God and knowing what would avail to merit God’s favor, asks for a period of three days. He fasts with his brotherhood, and so, once his prayers have been commended, he is instructed in every detail of both the sequence and meaning [Col. 0963B] of the dream. The tyrant’s sophists are spared, God is glorified, and Daniel is honored.

Later too, in the first year of King Darius, he was to obtain no lesser favor from God, when, after reconsidering the times foretold by Jeremiah, he set his face toward God in fasting, sackcloth, and ashes.

> **Note [Col. 0963D].** “He set his face toward God.” He turned toward God. RIG.

For the angel sent to him immediately declared that this was the reason for the divine favor: “I have come,” he said, “to show you how deserving of mercy you are”—

> **Note [Col. 0963D].** “How deserving of mercy you are.” How great a grace of compassion you have obtained. RIG.

—namely, through fasting. If he was deserving of mercy from God, he had been terrible to the lions in the pit, where indeed an angel provided a meal for him after he had fasted six days.

## CHAPTER VIII

We also set forth the remaining examples [variant: “We have set forth the ancient examples; now to the new,” Latin translation; “also now to the new,” Junius], for we are now hastening to the new testimonies.

At the threshold of the Gospel (Luke 2:37), [Col. 0963C] Anna the prophetess, daughter of Phanuel, who both recognized the infant Lord and proclaimed many things about him to those awaiting the redemption of Israel, is further honored by the testimony of her fasts after the distinguished title of her long widowhood following a single marriage. She shows by what observances one ought to remain in attendance upon the Church, and that Christ is understood by none more than by those who marry once and fast often.

Soon afterward the Lord himself dedicated his baptism—and in his own baptism that of all people—by fasting, though he possessed the power to make loaves from stones and perhaps even to make the Jordan flow with wine, had he truly been a glutton and a drinker. Rather, to the reproach of the old human being, he was initiating the new human being by the power of disdaining food, so that when the devil sought once more to tempt humanity through food, he might display the new human being to him as stronger through complete hunger.

From that point he established in advance a law that fasts were to be completed without sadness. Why should that which is salutary be sad? He also taught that fiercer demons must be fought by fasting. For what wonder is it if [variant: “by,” added by Semler] the unclean spirit is driven out by the same operation through which the Holy Spirit is brought in?

Finally, in order that the favor of the Holy Spirit, together with the further gift of prophecy, might hasten to Cornelius the centurion even before he was baptized, we read that his fasts were heard. I think, moreover, that in Second Corinthians the Apostle too, among his labors, dangers, and hardships, after hunger and thirst, enumerates his many fasts as well (2 Cor. 11:27).
