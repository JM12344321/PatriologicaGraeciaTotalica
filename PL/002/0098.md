I know with what excuses we give a respectable appearance to the insatiable desire of the flesh: we plead the necessities of assistance—a household to administer, a family to govern, money-boxes and keys to guard, wool-work to assign, provisions to procure, and domestic cares to be attended to [Note: [Col. 0927C] “To be shared,” Rig.]. As if, indeed, only married men’s households prospered! The households of the unmarried, the possessions of eunuchs, and the fortunes of soldiers or travelers without wives have all perished, apparently. Are we not soldiers too—and soldiers under a discipline all the greater in proportion to the greatness of our Commander? Are we not also travelers in this world? Why, Christian, have you arranged your life so that you cannot do without a wife?

Now, however, a partner in domestic burdens is said to be necessary. Then have some spiritual wife: take one from among the widows, beautiful in faith, endowed with poverty, and distinguished by age. You will have made an excellent marriage. To have even several such wives is pleasing to God. Yet Christians give thought to posterity, though for them there is no tomorrow. Will a servant of God desire heirs when he has disinherited himself from the world? And is this why a man should marry again, if he has no children from his former marriage? His first concern, then, will be to wish to live longer, although the Apostle himself hastens toward the Lord! No doubt he will be entirely unencumbered in persecutions, utterly steadfast in martyrdoms, exceedingly ready in acts of fellowship, and thoroughly restrained in acquiring possessions. Finally, he will die free from anxiety, having perhaps left children who will perform funeral rites for him.

[Col. 0927C] Are marriages of this kind contracted, then, out of concern for the commonwealth as well, lest cities fail if offspring are not reared; lest laws, legal rights, and commerce collapse; lest temples be abandoned; and lest there be no one to shout: “THE CHRISTIANS TO THE BEASTS!” [Note: [Col. 0927C] “A lion for the Christians,” Rig.]? For this is what those who seek children desire to hear.

Even this may suffice as counsel in favor of widowhood, especially among us: the troublesome burden of children, whom human beings are compelled by law to rear—for every wise person would never willingly have desired children. What, then, will you do if you knowingly impregnate your new [Note: [Col. 0927C] “If unwillingly, a wife,” Ciaccon.; “if you are unwilling, if a wife,” Paris, Pam.] wife? Will you dissolve the conception with drugs [Note: [Col. 0927C] “You will dissolve it with a drug,” Fran., Par.]? I think it is no more lawful for us to kill one being born than one already born.

But perhaps, while your wife is pregnant, you will dare to ask God for a remedy to so grave an anxiety [Note: [Col. 0927D] “Will you dare to ask God amid such anxiety,” etc. I have read this passage, though with the wording altered slightly: “Will you dare amid such anxiety to ask for what you refused when it lay in your own power?” This is what Aristides says *On Concord, to the Rhodians*: ἄτοπον τοῖς θεοῖς ἐνοχλεῖν, αὐτοῖς δὲ μὴ βούλεσθαι πράττειν ἃ ἡμῖν εἶναι δοκεῖ τοῖς θεοῖς αὐτοῖς [text materially uncertain]. “It is ridiculous to weary the gods, yet to be unwilling oneself to do those things which the gods themselves know to lie within our power.” [Col. 0928D] Thus Demosthenes rebukes the Greeks in *Philippic III*: they behaved like those who watch hail about to fall and wish themselves safe from it, though no one makes any effort to avert it. Le Pr.]—something which you refused when it lay within your own power [Note: [Col. 0927C] “In such solitude,” Rhen.]?

Some woman believed to be barren, I suppose, will be chosen, or at least one of more advanced and colder age. A sufficiently prudent plan—and, above all, a faithful one! For we have believed that no woman is barren, if God wills otherwise, nor any old woman incapable of giving birth. Indeed, this may happen all the more readily if someone, through presumption upon such foresight of his own, provokes God’s jealousy. We know, in fact, a certain one of the brethren who, because of his daughter, sought a barren wife in a second marriage, and became a father a second time just as surely as he became a husband a second time.

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN: ON MONOGAMY

[Note: [Col. 0929D] In this book, and likewise in those that follow, he openly defends Montanus’s doctrine. He believed that the day of the Lord was imminent and wished the faithful to be as fully prepared as possible for martyrdoms. ## BOOK (C)

## ARGUMENT

[Col. 0929B]

Throughout this work he calls Montanus the Paraclete [Col. 0929C] and denies that he is a heretic, although he himself follows the sect of the Cataphrygians. He claims, however, to hold a middle course between those who reject marriage and others who introduce repeated marriages in plurality. He accordingly defends monogamy and teaches that this discipline is not new, but ancient and proper to Christians.

## CHAPTER II

They therefore reproach the discipline of monogamy as heresy [Note: [Col. 0931D] “Reproach,” Rhen.]. Nor are they compelled to deny the Paraclete for any reason more than because they suppose him to be the founder of a new discipline—one exceedingly harsh [Note: [Col. 0931D] “Most dreadful,” Rhen.] to them. Consequently, we must first take our stand on this point in [Note: [Col. 0931D] “That,” Rhen.] a general examination: whether it is possible that the Paraclete taught anything of such a kind that it could either be reckoned new in opposition to catholic tradition, or burdensome in opposition to the Lord’s light burden [Col. 0931C] [Note: [Col. 0932D] “Of the Lord” is absent from Rhen. and Par.].

On both points, however, the Lord himself pronounced judgment. For when he said, “I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot yet bear them. When the Holy Spirit comes, he will lead you into all truth” (John 16:12), he surely gave sufficient advance notice that the Spirit would do things which might be thought both new, because they had never previously been disclosed, and somewhat burdensome, since this was why they had not been disclosed.

“Then,” you say, “by this argument anything whatever that is new and burdensome could be ascribed to the Paraclete, even if it came from an adversarial spirit.” Certainly not. For an adversarial spirit would become evident from the divergence of its proclamation [Note: [Col. 0932D] “Becomes evident,” Leop.], first corrupting the rule of faith and then corrupting the order of discipline. For the corruption of that which occupies the first rank must come first—that is, the corruption of faith, which precedes [Col. 0931D] discipline. A person must first be a heretic concerning God, and only afterward concerning conduct.

The Paraclete, however, having many things to teach which the Lord deferred to him, will, in accordance with that prior definition, first bear witness to Christ himself as we believe him to be, together with the entire ordering of God the Creator; he will glorify Christ himself and bring Christ himself to remembrance. Once recognized by this principal rule, he will thus reveal those many matters belonging to disciplines, the integrity of his proclamation providing assurance for them: although new, because they are only now revealed; although burdensome, because even now they are not borne. Yet they belong to no other Christ than the one who said that he had many other things to be taught by the Paraclete, things no less burdensome to these people than they were to those who could not yet bear them at that time.

## CHAPTER III

Whether monogamy is burdensome may still be considered by the shameless weakness of the flesh. For the present, let it be established whether it is new. Indeed, we go further and say that even if the Paraclete today had prescribed complete and absolute virginity or continence, so as not to permit the heat of the flesh to spend itself even in a single marriage, he would still appear to introduce nothing new. For the Lord himself opened the kingdoms of heaven to eunuchs (Matt. 19:12), [Note: [Col. 0931D] “Being himself also a eunuch.” Although that term, by which Christ the Lord is designated, agrees poorly with his extraordinary integrity, no one doubts that he was a virgin [Col. 0932D] and born of a virgin. Le Pr.] being himself also a eunuch; and the Apostle, looking toward him and therefore likewise castrated, prefers continence.

“But,” you say, “with the right to marry preserved.” Certainly preserved; and we shall see for how long. Nevertheless, it is already abolished on that side on which continence is preferred. “It is good,” he says, “for a man not to touch a woman” (1 Cor. 7:1). Therefore, it is evil to touch one. [Note: [Col. 0932D] For nothing is contrary to good except evil. Here he treats at somewhat greater length what he touched upon in the preceding book, ch. 3. From both passages the blessed Jerome transcribed nearly the very words in book I *Against Jovinian*. Accordingly, it remains that even those who have wives should be as though they had none; all the more, those who have none ought not to acquire them. He also gives the reasons why he offers this counsel: the unmarried think about God, whereas the married think about how each may please his spouse in marriage (1 Cor. 7:32).

I can also maintain that what is permitted is not purely good. For what is purely good is not permitted as an indulgence; it is lawful of itself. Permission has a cause, sometimes even one of necessity [Note: [Col. 0932D] “Necessity,” Seml.]. Indeed, in the present instance it is not the will of the one granting permission that people marry. For he wills something else: “I wish,” he says, “that all of you were as I am” (1 Cor. 7:7). And when he shows that something is better [Note: [Col. 0932D] “That something is better, certainly,” Seml.], what does he thereby confirm that he wills, if not what he had already declared to be better? Thus, if he permits something other than what he willed, granting it not from willingness but from necessity, he shows that what he reluctantly conceded is not purely good.

Indeed, when he says, “It is better to marry than to burn,” what kind of good must that be understood to be which is better than a punishment? How can it appear better except in comparison with something worst? That is good [Col. 0933A] which possesses this name in itself, without comparison—not merely with evil, but even with some other good—so that, even if it is overshadowed by comparison with another [Note: [Col. 0933D] “Another,” Seml.] good, it nevertheless retains the name of good. But if something is forced to be called good through comparison with evil, it is not so much good as a lesser kind of evil which, obscured by a greater evil, is driven into receiving the name of good.

Remove, then, the condition, so that you do not say, “It is better to marry than to burn,” and I ask whether you dare say simply, “It is better to marry,” without adding what it is better than. It is therefore no longer “better”; and since it is not better, neither is it good once the condition has been removed—the condition which, by making it better than something else, forces it thus to be regarded as good. It is better to lose one eye than two. Yet if you withdraw from comparison between both evils [Note: [Col. 0934D] “Evils” omitted by Seml.], it will not be better to have one eye, because neither is it good.

What if, then, he grants every indulgence for marrying on his own authority—that is, from human judgment—because of the necessity we have mentioned, since “it is better to marry than to burn”? Indeed, when he turns to the other case and says, “To the married, however, I give this command—not I, but the Lord,” he shows that what he had said above did not possess the Lord’s authority, but rested upon human judgment. Yet when he redirects their minds toward continence—“I wish, however, that all of you were so; and I think,” he says, “that I too have the Spirit of God”—it is as though he were revoking by the Holy Spirit’s authority whatever he had granted out of necessity.

## CHAPTER IV

Let mention of the Paraclete now withdraw, as though he were an authority belonging only to some of us. Let us unroll the shared documents of the earlier Scriptures. Our very point is demonstrated from them: that the discipline of monogamy is neither new nor foreign but, rather, both ancient and proper to Christians, so that you may regard the Paraclete as its restorer rather than its founder.

As far as antiquity is concerned, what pattern can be produced more ancient than the very origin of the human race? God fashioned one woman for the male, taking one of his ribs, though certainly he had several. In the preface to this very work he also said: “It is not good for the man to be alone; let us make a helper for him.” He would have said “helpers” if he had destined him for several wives. He also added a law concerning the future. For it was said prophetically: “And the two shall become one flesh” (Gen. 2:18, 24)—not three or more. Otherwise, they would no longer be two if there were more.

The law stood firm. Indeed, the union of marriage endured in the founders of the race right to the end—not because there were no other women, but because they did not exist for this very purpose: that the firstfruits of the race should not be defiled by double marriage. Otherwise, had God willed it, they could have existed. Adam certainly could have taken a wife from the number of his daughters, having Eve no less from his own bones and flesh, if this could have been done piously.

But when the first crime, homicide, was inaugurated in fratricide, no crime was so worthy of second place as two marriages. [Note: [Col. 0934D] For it makes no difference whether a man had two wives one after the other, or both together made two. Indeed, because it makes a very great difference, an edict of the praetor marks with infamy the man who has contracted two betrothals or two marriages at the same time. Rig.] For it makes no difference whether a man had two wives one after the other or had two together: the number is the same, whether the persons joined are together or separated.

Although God’s institution was once violated by Lamech, it thereafter remained firm until the end of that race. No second Lamech appears as the husband of two women. [Note: [Col. 0934D] Scripture denies what it does not record. When I first read these words, though only in passing and superficially, they seemed rather harsh in an author who had defended tradition so strongly. Moreover, the re-formation of the second human race is reckoned through monogamy as its mother. Again there are two in a single marriage. Monogamy is recognized even among the animals, lest even beasts should be born from adultery. “From all the beasts,” he says, “from every kind of flesh, you shall bring two into the ark so that they may live with you: male and female. What more shall I say? Even unclean birds were not permitted to enter with two females.

## CHAPTER V

So much for the testimony of the beginnings, the advocacy of our origin, and the prescriptive authority of the divine institution. This is assuredly a law, not a mere memorial. For if it was established thus from the beginning, we find ourselves directed back to the beginning by Christ. Thus, in the question concerning divorce, when he says that it was permitted by Moses because of their hardness but had not been so from the beginning, he undoubtedly recalls the indivisibility of marriage to its beginning. Therefore, the two whom God joined from the beginning into one flesh, man shall not separate today (Gen. 2:24).

The Apostle too says, writing to the Ephesians, that God purposed in himself, for the dispensation of the fullness of the times, to bring all things back to the head—that is, back to the beginning—in Christ, the things above the heavens and the things upon the earth, in him (Eph. 1:9–10).

Thus the Lord also assumed for himself the two Greek letters, the first and the last, as figures of the beginning and the end converging in himself. Just as α rolls onward to ω and ω in turn folds back to α (Rev. 1:8; 22:13), so he showed that in himself are both the course of the beginning toward the end and the return of the end to the beginning. Thus every dispensation, ending in him through whom it began—namely, through the Word of God, who became flesh—likewise ends just as it began.

And to such an extent are all things recalled [Note: [Col. 0935D] “Were recalled,” Rhen.] to the beginning in Christ that faith has returned from circumcision to the integrity of the flesh, as it was from the beginning; the freedom of foods and abstinence from blood alone have returned, as they were from the beginning; the indivisibility of marriage has returned, as it was from the beginning; divorce has been restrained, since it did not exist from the beginning; and, finally, the whole human being is recalled to paradise, where he was from the beginning. Why, then, should Christ not restore Adam there as at least a monogamist, when he cannot present him as wholly unblemished as when he was sent from there?

As far as the restoration of the beginning is concerned, therefore, the principle of both your dispensation and your hope requires of you what existed from the beginning, according to the beginning which is reckoned for you in Adam and reckoned anew in Noah. Choose in which of them you reckon your beginning: in both, the rule of monogamy claims you for itself.

But if the beginning passes onward to the end, as α to ω, and the end passes back to the beginning, as ω to α, and our origin is thus transferred into Christ—from the natural to the spiritual, because the spiritual does not come first, but the natural, and afterward the spiritual—then let us see whether you owe this same obligation to this second origin also, and whether it is fitting for you to exist in the same form as the last Adam and the first. For the last Adam, that is, Christ, was entirely unmarried, as the first Adam also was before his exile.

Yet, by the example of his own flesh, the more perfect Adam—that is, Christ, more perfect in this respect also because more inviolate—makes a concession to the weakness of your flesh. If you are willing, he meets you in the flesh as a eunuch; but if you are not equal to that, he meets you in the spirit as a monogamist, having one bride, the Church, according to the figure of Adam and Eve. The Apostle interprets this as that great mystery concerning Christ and the Church, showing that fleshly monogamy corresponded to the spiritual.

You see, therefore, how, even when renewing your origin in Christ, you cannot establish it without a profession of monogamy—unless you are in the flesh what he is in the spirit, although you ought equally to have been what he was in the flesh.

But let us seek still other founders of our origin. For certain people are not pleased with monogamous parents—Adam and Noah, and perhaps not even Christ. They appeal instead to Abraham, although forbidden to acknowledge any father other than God. Let Abraham now be our father, then; and let Paul be so too: “In the gospel,” he says, “I begot you” (1 Cor. 4:16).

Show yourself also to be Abraham’s son. For your descent from him is not indiscriminate [Note: [Col. 0935D] Indiscriminate descent, etc. Rhenanus says that he calls “indiscriminate descent” an unlimited, wandering, unrestricted, and permissive—or licentious—descent. Pam.]. There is a definite time at which he is your father. For if we are reckoned Abraham’s children by faith, as the Apostle teaches when he says to the Galatians, “You know, surely, that those who are of faith are the children of Abraham” (Gal. 3:7), when did Abraham believe God and have it reckoned to him as righteousness? While still, I think, in monogamy, because he was not yet under circumcision.

But if he was afterward changed in both respects—into double marriage through concubinage with the slave-woman, and into circumcision through the seal of the covenant—you cannot acknowledge him as father except at the time when he believed God, since you are his child according to faith, not according to the flesh. Or if you follow the later Abraham as your father—that is, the twice-married Abraham—accept him also as circumcised. If you reject the circumcised Abraham, you must likewise reject the twice-married one. You cannot combine his two dispensations, which differ from each other in two corresponding ways. He began to be twice-married together with circumcision, and was monogamous together with uncircumcision.

Do you accept double marriage? Admit circumcision too. Do you defend uncircumcision? You are bound to monogamy as well. So fully are you the son of the monogamous Abraham, just as you are of the uncircumcised Abraham, that if you are circumcised, you are no longer a son, because you will not be a son by faith but by the seal of the faith that was justified in uncircumcision. You have the Apostle; learn together with the Galatians.

Accordingly, even if you bring double marriage upon yourself, you are not the son of that Abraham whose faith came first during monogamy. For although he was afterward called the father of many nations, these were the nations whose members, through the faith that preceded his double marriage, were accounted Abraham’s children. Let what followed take care of itself.

Figures are one thing, forms another; images are one thing, definitions another. Images pass away when fulfilled; definitions remain to be fulfilled. Images prophesy; definitions govern. What Abraham’s double marriage foreshadows, the same Apostle teaches, as interpreter of both Testaments, just as he determines that our seed was called in Isaac.

If you belong to the free woman and pertain to Isaac, he certainly maintained one marriage. These, then, are the persons, I think, among whom I am reckoned. The rest I do not know.

If you look about for examples from those others—some David forcing marriages upon himself even through bloodshed, some Solomon rich even in wives—you have been commanded to follow better examples. You have Joseph, the husband of one wife, and by this title, I dare say, better than his father. You have Moses, the one admitted close to God’s counsel. You have Aaron, the chief priest. [Note: [Col. 0937D] A second Moses. He means Joshua the son of Nun, concerning whose marriages Scripture relates nothing. Accordingly, nothing can be inferred from him either for or against marriage, whether first or second. Nevertheless, Jerome follows Septimius in book I *Against Jovinian*: “Show me,” he says, “that Joshua the son of Nun had either a wife or children.” Rig.] The second Moses too, of the second people—the one who led our figure [Col. 0937B] into God’s promise, and in whom the Lord’s name was first inaugurated—was not twice married.
