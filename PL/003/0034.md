Thus, although among all nations the account or origin of the immortal gods may be uncertain, a firm consensus concerning them nevertheless remains; accordingly, I cannot tolerate anyone swelling with such audacity and with some sort of prudence—though I know not what sort—so irreligious that he attempts to dissolve or weaken this religion, so ancient, so useful, so [Col. 0256B] salutary. Let him be Theodorus of Cyrene, if you please, or the earlier Diagoras of Melos, to whom antiquity gave the surname “Atheist” [Col. 0257A]: both of them, by asserting that there were no gods, utterly abolished all fear by which humanity is governed, and all reverence. Yet they will never enjoy influence in this school of impiety under the name and authority of counterfeit philosophy, seeing that the men of Athens expelled the Abderite Pythagoras [Protagoras] from their territory and burned his writings [Col. 0258A] in the assembly, merely because he discussed the divine more cautiously than profanely. Why, then—permit me to express more freely the vehemence of the case I have undertaken—why, I say, should we not groan that men belonging to a ruinous, unlawful, and desperate faction are assailing the gods? Gathering the more ignorant from the dregs of the populace, together with credulous women who, through the susceptibility of their sex, [p. 11] readily fall, they organize a rabble of profane conspiracy, bound together by nocturnal gatherings, solemn fasts, and inhuman [Col. 0259A] foods—not by some sacred rite, but by an act of sacrilege. A skulking, light-shunning tribe, mute in public but talkative in corners: they despise temples as though they were tombs; they spit upon the gods and ridicule sacred rites. Miserable themselves, they pity—if one may say so—the priests; half-naked themselves, they despise offices of honor and robes of purple. What astonishing folly and incredible audacity! They scorn present torments while fearing torments uncertain and yet to come; and while they fear to die after death, meanwhile they do not fear death. Thus a deceptive hope soothes their fear with the comfort of renewed life.

## CHAPTER IX

[Note: [Col. 0260D]

**CHAP. IX.—“Shrines . . . increase.”** A *sacrarium* is a small temple, or even a place where sacred objects are stored. Here the term is applied to a house in which Christians assembled to pray. Places “increase” when they are frequented and a great number of people gathers there. Thus Velleius, II.30.6: “The number increased to such an extent.” Tacitus, *Histories* II.34: “Cremona increased through the number of its colonists, the advantageous position of its rivers, the fertility of its soil, and its connections and intermarriages with other peoples.” Behold [Col. 0261B] the Church of Christ disseminated throughout the world even before the age of Christian emperors! LINDNER.

**“Must be utterly uprooted.”** Heumann suspects that *ejicienda* (“must be cast out”) was written, and that Minucius had before his eyes Cicero, *On Duties* I.41: “Indeed, the entire tribe of Cynics must be cast out.” But why may one not speak of “uprooting a consensus” with the same propriety with which we say: to uproot weeds, a tree, eyes, or a city? LINDNER.

**“They recognize one another by secret marks and signs.”** On this passage see Dodwell, *Dissertations on Cyprian* II, §11, p. 19.

**“And indiscriminately call one another.”** I have printed this reading at the manuscript’s direction. All printed editions have *promiscue*, as though the alternative form were not Latin. Gellius, in the preface to the *Attic Nights*: “I used to make notes without distinction and indiscriminately”; hence the same writer spoke of “miscellaneous reflection” in Book XI, chap. 16, and “miscellaneous questions” [Col. 0261C] in Book XIII, chap. 9. DAVIS.—*Promisce* is the manuscript reading, which Davis supports by Gellius’s authority. Heumann, however, observes that Gellius’s manner of speaking is one thing and Minucius’s another: the former is marked by an antiquarian affectation, whereas the latter avoids every affectation. LINDNER.

**“Especially abominable in the highest degree.”** Fulvius Ursinus correctly restored this reading, since the manuscript has *maxima et varia* (“very many and of many kinds”). LINDNER.—Since the copyist’s error is manifest, I wonder that Davis retained this reading. Wopken rightly rejects Burmann’s correction, *maxima et valida*, that is, “certainly true,” which Burmann directed to be written in his note on Suetonius, while at the same time approving Ursinus’s emendation. HEUMANN.

**“The head of an ass.”** The most learned Davis thought that the order of the words recommended deleting, with Cuper (*On Caesar*, Book III, *Gallic War*, chap. 1), the word *asini* (“of an ass”) as a gloss. The distinguished Heumann likewise deletes it, supposing that someone wrote the word in the margin from chap. 28.8. Wrongly, as we saw in *Tullian Readings* II.6, *Prodigies*, chap. [Col. 0261D] 128: “before the house of Lepidus the Pontifex Maximus.” Thus this manuscript reading seems worthy of emphatic approval. Valerius Maximus III.2.3: “and, after routing the enemy, he brought back to the god the *spolia opima*, to Jupiter Feretrius”; where the common reading is *de eo*. Nor should the reading in Florus IV.1 be rejected: “Then the consul, after holding a meeting of the senate, Cicero delivered a speech against the man while he was present.” Here the word *Cicero* seemed to Graevius to have crept in from a gloss, or at least to have been put in the wrong place. Compare Book II.2: “The other consul, Asina Cornelius, was intercepted.” WOPKEN.—Thus our author in chap. 26 has “the blind Tiresias”; 19.2, “God, the parent of all”; 28.9, “the bull Apis”; 31.9, “the one God and Father”; 34.3, “the craftsman himself, God alone”; 35.3, “the fires of Etna and Mount Vesuvius.” LINDNER.

**“A religion worthy of and born for such morals.”** Heumann thinks that Minucius wrote: “Surely such a religion is worthy of such animals.” But take care not to alter anything in a perfectly sound passage. Caecilius had branded the Christians as lustful fornicators and incestuous persons. He therefore says most appropriately that the worship of the ass, an exceedingly lustful animal, accords with these morals. Phaedrus, Book I, Fable 29. He then alludes to the asinine patience of Christians. For the ass treats [Col. 0262B] with honor and dutiful service the very person by whom it is beaten and held in contempt; hence the rabbinic proverb: [Hebrew text absent in the supplied source]. See Buxtorf’s *Chaldaic-Talmudic Lexicon*, p. 788. It is noteworthy that from the Hebrew [word absent], “ass,” derives [word absent], “shameful.” LINDNER.

**“Nature.”** Jerome, Letter LXI: “The more private part of our body is called by us ‘nature,’ *physis* (φύσις).” WOWER.

**“Who [say] that a man.”** Behold the nature of slander and falsehood! They omit certain essential things and add others of their own invention: they say “a man” but omit “God,” or *God-man* (θεάνθρωπος). They speak of the supreme punishment as though it were infamous and servile, not as foretold by prophets and necessary. They say that he was punished for a crime, but omit whose crime, so that his own crimes may be understood, whereas they ought to have said the crimes of others. LINDNER.

[Col. 0262C] **“They tell tales that their rites are such.”** This indeed is the reading of the royal manuscript; but one must read *caerimonias fabulatur* in the singular, as Sigonius, Gelenius, Desiderius Heraldus, and others printed it, for without a singular verb the syntax (σύνταξις) does not hold together. J. Meursius, however, labors in vain when he substitutes *caerimonia fabulantur*. For among the Latins *caerimoniae* means all sacred rites, as Isidore says in *Origins* VI.19, and Servius before him on Virgil, *Aeneid* IV.302. Caecilius therefore reproaches the Christians for worshiping both Christ and the cross. DAVIS.—Octavius himself below, in chaps. 28 and 30, certainly seems to indicate that someone in particular is being named. HERALD.

**“Is to be initiated.”** Thus Heraldus from the royal manuscript. Others have *imbuitur* (“is initiated”), including Lindner, who cites Heraldus’s reading and says: “I do not know whether it comes from the manuscript or from a printer’s error.” We therefore present both readings of the codex. EDITORS.

**“That infant.”** On this repetition, compare chap. VIII. LINDNER.

**“It is known; everyone everywhere talks of it; even . . . a speech.”** The distinguished Heumann should not be followed in thinking that one must read: “Is not that of which everyone everywhere speaks also . . . a speech?” For the broken-off sentence most effectively expresses the emotion of indignation. Thus chap. XI: “Many things are available in reply to this—let my speech not hurry.” “That they themselves are more unjust I no longer labor to prove; I have already demonstrated it.” LINDNER.

**“Our fellow citizen of Cirta.”** No one doubts that our author has here indicated Marcus Cornelius Fronto, born at Cirta in Numidia, an orator by no means contemptible among the Latin writers of that age and a most bitter enemy of the Christians, whom the author mentions again in chap. XXXI. From this and other evidence it is established that he wrote a speech against the Christian sect. I would not have you rashly suppose him to be the other Fronto praised by Juvenal, *Satires* I.12; Pliny the Younger, *Letters* II.11; and Martial, *Epigrams*. According to Eutropius VIII.6, Fronto of Cirta himself was the instructor in Latin literature of the emperors Marcus Antoninus and Lucius Verus. Eumenius extols him with great praise and proclaims him not the second but the other glory of Roman eloquence. If any credit is due to John of Salisbury, *Policraticus* VIII.19, on his mother’s side he descended [Col. 0263B] from the eminent Plutarch of Chaeronea. Nearly all his works had seemed lost, with the exception of a few fragments concerning grammar, when the most illustrious Angelo Mai, cardinal-priest of the Holy Roman Church, successfully brought most of Fronto’s works back to light from within a very ancient palimpsest codex (παλίμψηστος) in the Ambrosian Library, and published them under this title: *Unpublished Works of M. Cornelius Fronto, together with Unpublished Letters of Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius, Lucius Verus, and Appian, as well as Fragments of Other Ancient Authors*. Milan, 1815, octavo. The declamation against the Christians is missing. EDITORS.

**“With all their children.”** This passage should be noted: from its mention of a light it indicates that there was a supper; and, since it attests that all the children and persons of every age were present, are we to believe that none of those children had been baptized? To me, certainly, the narrative seems instead manifestly to imply that baptism was administered even in earliest childhood. [Col. 0263C] GRONOVIUS.—This reflection is pious, but I doubt whether it is also sound. From the presence of persons at the supper, the very learned man concludes that they had all been baptized. Many—including Constantine the Great—deferred baptism until death, yet it is hardly probable that throughout all that time they abstained from the Christians’ love-feasts. Furthermore, the very word *missa* directs us to think of the dismissal of the people. But grant that matters are as the learned gentleman wishes, and that Justin, *Apology* I, chap. 85, attests: “Those called deacons among us give to each of those present a share of the bread, wine, and water over which thanksgiving has been offered, and carry portions to those who are absent” (Οἱ καλούμενοι παρ’ ἡμῖν διάκονοι, διδόασιν ἑκάστῳ τῶν παρόντων μεταλαβεῖν ἀπὸ τοῦ εὐχαριστηθέντος ἄρτου καὶ οἴνου καὶ ὕδατος, καὶ τοῖς οὐ παροῦσιν ἀποφέρουσι). The same, chap. 86: “No one else is permitted to partake of the Eucharist except one who believes and has been washed with the washing for the forgiveness of sins and for regeneration” (τῆς εὐχαριστίας οὐδενὶ ἄλλῳ μετασχεῖν ἐξόν ἐστιν, ἢ τῷ πιστεύοντι καὶ λουσαμένῳ τὸ ὑπὲρ ἀφέσεως ἁμαρτιῶν καὶ εἰς ἀναγέννησιν λουτρόν). Nevertheless, testimony from the mouth of an enemy remains weak, since he did not himself witness what he attests, but knew it only by report—and that uncertain and doubtful. For if this passage could prove that children were present during the administration of the sacred supper, I do not see why it could not with equal right prove that a dog was [Col. 0263D] truly tied to the lampstand. With children present, I believe, the mysteries of the Christians would not long have remained secret. Nor can the use of a supper properly be inferred from the lighting of a lamp, since during times of persecution they generally held their meetings at night. LINDNER.

**“The banquet grew heated.”** Sulpicius Severus also uses the same expression, *Sacred History* II.13: “Therefore, when after the meal and many cups the banquet had begun to grow heated.” WOPKEN.—I do not doubt that *incaluit* (“became heated”) should be restored. Curtius V.7.5; VIII.1.22: “When the king had become heated with much wine.” Nor am I moved by the passage from Sulpicius that Wopken adduces; for the preceding *m* easily persuades me that here too a copyist wrote *calere* for *incalere*. HEUMANN.—But what will he do with Horace, *Odes* III.21.12:

> It is told that even old Cato’s virtue often grew heated with wine.

LINDNER.

**“The heat of incestuous lust blazed up.”** The manuscript has: “the heat of the drunkenness of incestuous lust [Col. 0264B] blazed up.” Gelenius: “Through the drunkenness of incestuous lust, the heat blazed up.” Meursius substitutes *ebriatis* (“when they had become drunk”). Rigaltius: “And the heat of drunkenness blazed up for incestuous lust.” Gronovius: “And the heat of drunkenness blazed up into blind lusts.” Valerius Maximus II.1.5: “The step nearest to Father Liber in intemperance was customarily toward forbidden sexual intercourse.” Hence Isidore, *Origins* VIII.8, attests that wine was attributed to Liber because it arouses lust.

**“The dog is provoked.”** *Carnis* (“of flesh”) is unquestionably an old antiquarian’s error for *canis* (“dog”). EDITORS.—It is difficult to conjecture whence the pagans obtained this slander. Yet I would believe that its origin was as follows. Those employed to track and sniff things out were called “dogs,” as the venerable Ernesti teaches in his *Key to Cicero*; Plautus likewise used the word for a watchman. Since in the early Church there were many who plotted against the Christians, they also maintained certain persons who would inspect gatherings [Col. 0264C] to see whether a spy was present to eavesdrop upon their discussions and mysteries. They called such a person a “dog.” Perhaps it was his task to extinguish the lamps whenever they feared an ambush, or at least to block the windows through which those unwelcome observers might look in. LINDNER.

**“By deed.”** The reading is sound; the distinguished Heumann should not have troubled it and restored *opere*. Cicero, *On Duties* I.35: “To devote one’s effort to children is honorable in fact but obscene in name.” Plautus, *The Merchant* IV.5.10: “By Pollux, I did not think their father was engaged in such activities.” LINDNER.]

**ARGUMENT.—**The religion of the Christians is foolish, since they venerate a crucified man and the very instrument of his punishment. They are said to worship the head of an ass and to adore the “nature” of their own father. They are initiated by the murder and blood of an infant, and in shameless darkness they all mingle together by an uncertain lot.

And now, just as the worse things grow more prolific, with corrupt morals spreading daily, those most loathsome shrines of an impious association are increasing throughout the entire world. [Col. 0261A] This confederacy must be utterly uprooted and accursed. They recognize one another by secret marks and signs, and love one another almost before they know one another. Everywhere among them, moreover, something like a religion of lust is intermingled; and they indiscriminately call one another brothers and sisters, so that even an otherwise ordinary act of fornication, through the intervention of the sacred name, becomes incest. Thus their empty and insane superstition glories in crimes. Nor would shrewd rumor speak about them—unless truth had some foundation—of numerous and various deeds, especially abominable in the highest degree and to be mentioned only after an apology. I hear that, by some absurd persuasion or other, they venerate the consecrated head of that most disgraceful beast, the ass: a religion worthy of and born for such morals. Others report that they worship the genitals of their own presiding minister and priest and adore, as it were, their father’s “nature.” [Col. 0261B] I do not know whether this is false; [p. 12] it is certainly a suspicion suited to secret and nocturnal rites [Col. 0262A]. And whoever tells the tale that their rites center on a man punished with the supreme penalty for a crime, and on the deadly wood of the cross, assigns to abandoned and criminal people altars suited to them, so that they may worship what they deserve. (VI) The story concerning the initiation of novices is as detestable as it is well known. An infant covered with meal, to deceive the unwary, is placed before the person who is to be initiated into the rites. The novice is induced, by the surface of the meal, to strike what seem to be harmless blows; the infant is killed by unseen and concealed wounds. Then—horrible!—they thirstily lick up its blood and eagerly divide its limbs among themselves. By this victim they are bound in covenant; by this shared consciousness of crime they mutually pledge themselves to silence. These rites are fouler than every sacrilege. (VII) And their banquet—it is known; everyone everywhere talks of it; even [Col. 0262B] a speech by our fellow citizen of Cirta attests it. On a solemn [Col. 0263A] day they assemble for a feast with all their children, sisters, and mothers—persons of every sex and every age. There, after many courses, when the banquet had lacked heat [had grown heated], and the heat of the drunkenness of incestuous lust had blazed up, a piece of food is thrown beyond the reach of the line by which the flesh [dog] tied to the lampstand is bound, provoking it to charge and leap. Thus, when the witnessing light has been overturned and extinguished, in the shameless darkness they envelop themselves in the bonds of unspeakable desire according to the uncertainty of chance. And even if not all are incestuous in deed, all are nevertheless equally so in complicity, since whatever can occur in the act of individuals is sought by the desire of all.

## CHAPTER X

[Note: [Col. 0264C]

**CHAP. X.—“Most of all things.”** Wopken proposes *pleraque omnia* (“almost everything”), like *plerique omnes* in Terence and Nepos. Heumann proposes *pleraque omnino* (“almost everything altogether”). I think either the received reading should be retained or *omnium* (“of all things”) deleted. LINDNER.

**“Whatever that is which they worship.”** Heumann restores: “whatever it is that they worship.” To me, *illud* (“that”) seems to have been used pleonastically. LINDNER.

**“Why do they have no altars?”** Thus there is profound silence concerning the sacrifice of the Mass in those times! LINDNER.

[Col. 0264D] **“No known images.”** Nicolaas Heinsius thinks that *vota* (“vowed”) should be written for *nota* (“known”): *Adversaria* I.18, p. 185. But nothing should be changed. The pagan implies that Christians have some image which they worship but which is generally unknown; for shortly before, he said that they conceal and hide what they worship. HEUMANN.

**“Endure.”** Cellarius inserted this verb, which is absent from the manuscript, and because it is absent placed it in brackets. Certainly either this or a similar verb has fallen out. HEUMANN.

**“Whence, moreover, or who is he, or where?”** Unknown persons are customarily questioned in this way. Florus I.18.5: “For who, or from where, were the Romans?” Seneca, *The Death of Claudius Caesar*, p. 811 of my edition, from Homer, *Odyssey* I: “Who are you among men, and whence? Where is your city?” (Τίς, πόθεν εἶς ἀνδρῶν, πόθι τοι πτόλις . . .). LINDNER.

**“A free nation, Roman superstition, have known.”** With great confidence I restore “free nations”; for “kingdoms” follows, and then the plural “have known.” Therefore I also restore, without hesitation, “Roman superstitions.” HEUMANN.—For my part, I interpret this passage through the customary syllepsis as follows: “a free nation [Col. 0265B] has known him; kingdoms have not known him; at least Roman superstition has not known him.” Singulars and plurals are thus interchanged: “Wealth, honor, and glory are situated before the eyes.” What wonder is it if a verb referring to several preceding subjects is put in the plural? LINDNER.

**“The Jewish . . . nation.”** *Gentilitas* here denotes a nation. Tertullian, *On the Soul*, chap. 30: “Aborigines now remain in their own settlements and elsewhere have lent out their nation at interest more extensively.” In the same sense Prudentius wrote “the nation of the Vascones,” *On Crowns* (περὶ στεφάνων), Hymn I.94. DAVIS.

**“To Roman divinities.”** The manuscript has *nominibus* (“names”), which Wower, Rigaltius, Heumann, Cellarius, and many others emend to *numinibus* (“divinities”). Thus Heumann restored *numinis* for *nominis* at Lactantius III.29.14. But the Roman edition has *hominibus* (“men”), which Wopken retains and defends, because otherwise [Col. 0265C] the statement would seem far too feeble and, according to Caecilius’s thinking, would not demonstrate the weakness of the Jews’ God as forcefully if he were captive to Roman divinities as if he were captive to Roman men; compare chap. 33.6. Livy III.17.3 also supports this opinion: “But would you fear your ancestral gods after their capture by the enemy?” LINDNER.—I side with those who regard “to Roman men” as genuine. The preceding words, “whose power and might are so utterly nonexistent,” absolutely require it. Furthermore, nowhere will you find it said that the gods of a conquered nation were held captive with their nation by Roman divinities. But it is often said that a victorious people captured and led in triumph the gods of a defeated people. Tertullian, *Apology* 25: “There are as many triumphs over gods as over nations, as many spoils as there remain images of captive gods.” Minucius himself speaks in the same sense below, chap. 25. GRUNER, cited work, pp. 222 ff.

**“But even indeed.”** Cellarius placed brackets around this word to indicate that it would be better omitted. But since it occurs both in the manuscript and in the Roman edition, I restore *enim* (“indeed”). HEUMANN.—Terence combines these particles under the influence of indignation, *The Eunuch* V.7.10: “But indeed at first I believed the man clever and eloquent.” Cicero, *Philippics* II, p. 438C of my edition: “But indeed he read out letters.” Likewise p. 442A: “But indeed at one point you wished to be witty.” Likewise p. 441: “But indeed you dared to say.” The same, *On the Nature of the Gods* I.41: “But indeed Epicurus wrote books about holiness and piety toward the gods.” LINDNER.

**“What monstrous things.”** The old copy has *quam ta nra*. RIGALTIUS.—Here Rigaltius, too attentive to the obscure writing of the manuscript codex, printed *qua in tam nostra* and, scarcely credibly, even defended it vigorously, explaining: “Christians, insofar as they are spread into the cities of Italy and into the provinces of the Roman people, into so much of our territory.” To such an extent is he held by blind love of that codex. Scheffer recognizes the copyist’s error but is less successful as an emender, judging that the passage should be restored thus: “But now the Christians—what, astonishingly, monstrous things . . .” Yet “astonishingly” before “monstrous things and prodigies” is remarkably feeble and vulgar. I subscribe to DAVIS, who thinks one should read “what monstrous things”; for this comes closest to the writing of that ancient codex. HEUMANN. [Col. 0266B] According to Rigaltius, Ferrarius and the Priscian manuscript read: “what sort of monstrous things?” Ouzel: “But the Christians—what monstrous things?” Gronovius: “How empty are the things, what prodigies, that Christians fabricate?” He appeals to Prudentius on Cassian: “It is no empty or old-womanish tale.” Gruner: “What things contrary to nature, what prodigies they fabricate,” and supports this from Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II.5: “Cleanthes posited a third conception of the gods, the one that terrifies human minds through prodigies contrary to the nature of human beings and animals.” LINDNER.

**“Into the conduct of all.”** Gronovius wonders how Caecilius, a pagan, could have said this, since “the eye of Justice that sees all things” (Δίκης ὀφθαλμὸς τὰ πάνθ’ ὁρῶν) was celebrated throughout pagan literature. But I know very well how he could say it: he spoke, namely, as an Epicurean, not as a pagan. He attacked providence in the same manner as Velleius in Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* I.20. LINDNER.

[Col. 0266C] **“For the whole world.”** Other editions have *toti orbi*, but the reading we adopt, *toto orbi*, occurs in the royal manuscript, as Nicolas Rigaltius attests; nor ought it to have been altered, since Apuleius and others used the same form. Propertius, Book III, Elegy IX.57:

> The city high upon seven hills, which presides over the whole world.

Lactantius, *The Phoenix*, verse 139:

> A radiant crown is fitted evenly to the whole head.

Hyginus, *Fables* V: “For this reason Juno was hostile to his whole race.” G. J. Vossius collected many similar examples involving other words of this form in *Analecta* IV.6. See also Janus Gulielmus on Cicero, *For Quinctius*, chap. 1. DAVIS.

**“The fabric divided.”** Wopken rightly refutes Burmann, who at Valerius Flaccus I, p. 139 directed that *divulsa* (“torn apart”) be written, by adducing passages from Hegesippus II.9, at the end: “Having demolished and divided the colonnades, you separated the temple”; Cyprian, *On the Unity of the Church*, p. 110: “He who rends and divides the Church of God”; Firmicus, *On the Error of Profane Religions*, p. 19: “Who tore him? Who divided him? Who feasted upon his limbs?” [Col. 0266D] Thus Prudentius, *Peristephanon*, Hymn X.494 and XI.93, and others everywhere; and above, chap. IX, *dispertire* is similarly used for “to pull apart, lacerate, tear to pieces.” LINDNER.

**“By which it is held together and encircled.”** Supply from the preceding words “the whole world and the cosmos itself.” Thus Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II.57: “Nature first clothed and enclosed the eyes with the thinnest membranes; she first made these transparent, so that one could see through them, but firm, so that they might be held together”—supply “the eyes by those membranes.” Compare Wopken, *Tullian Readings* II.17. Caecilius concludes that it is impossible for the whole world to collapse from the fact that it is not open and exposed to ruin on every side, but rather, like a most strongly fortified citadel, is held together and encircled on every side by so vast a mass. LINDNER.—**“By which it is held together and encircled.”** Meursius emends this to “by which we are held together and encircled.” Cellarius printed it thus. We, however, prefer the slighter alteration, “which is held together and encircled.” Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* II.36: “Earth, situated in the central part of the world, is surrounded on every side by this animate and breathable substance whose name is air”; [Col. 0267A] and chap. 40: “That which surrounds and restrains everything is called the embrace [Col. 0267B] of heaven.” Lucretius V, p. 614:

> Finally, look now upon this around and above us, which  
> Holds the whole earth in its embrace.

DAVIS.]

**ARGUMENT.—**Whatever they worship, they strive with great effort to conceal. They have no altars, no temples, [Col. 0264A] no known images. Their God, like that of the Jews, is said to be unique; although they can neither see nor display him, they nevertheless consider him meddlesome, restless, and perversely inquisitive.

I deliberately pass over many things, for even these are far too many; and the very obscurity of their depraved religion declares that either all or most of them are true. For why do they strive so greatly to conceal [p. 13] and hide whatever that is which they worship, when honorable things always rejoice in publicity, while crimes are secret? Why do they have no altars, no temples, no known images? Why do they never speak openly and never [endure] assembling freely, unless that which they worship and suppress is either punishable or shameful? Whence, moreover, or who is he, or where is this one, solitary, forsaken God, whom no [Col. 0265A] free nation, no kingdoms, not even Roman superstition have known? The sole and wretched Jewish nation also worshiped one God—but openly, with temples, altars, victims, and ceremonies. His power and might are so utterly nonexistent that he is captive to Roman divinities together with his own nation. But indeed, what monstrous things, what prodigies do the Christians fabricate? They maintain that this God of theirs, whom they can neither show nor see, diligently investigates the conduct of all people, everyone’s actions, and even their words and secret thoughts—running to and fro, of course, and present everywhere. They would have him be meddlesome, restless, and even shamelessly inquisitive, since he stands by every deed and wanders through every place, though he can neither attend to individuals while distracted throughout the universe [Col. 0266A], nor suffice for the universe while occupied with individuals. (VIII) What of the fact that they threaten the whole world, and the cosmos itself with its stars, with fire, and devise its destruction? As though the eternal order established by the divine laws of nature could be disturbed; or, when the compact of all the elements had been broken and the fabric of heaven divided, this mass [p. 14] by which it is held together and encircled could collapse.

## CHAPTER XI

[Note: [Col. 0267B]

**CHAP. XI.—“Both ashes and cinders.”** That is: although after death they are ashes and cinders, by apposition. For in that persecution by tyrants many were either burned alive or, after a violent death, cast into the fire and reduced to ashes and cinders. What appeared so astonishing to the pagans was that Christians believed they would be raised again even after being reduced to ashes. Heumann does not hesitate to write “from ashes and cinders,” but he does so without necessity. I have therefore also placed a comma after “they assert.” LINDNER.

**“You would think they had already returned to life.”** The French translator renders this: *Vous diriez qu’ils sont déjà ressuscités* (“You would say that they have already risen again”). Appel, however, in his German translation blindly followed the Frenchman and rendered it: *Woltet ihr sagen, es waren bereits etliche Wieder auserwecket* (“Would you say that some had already been raised again?”). By this very rendering he declares that he understands neither French nor Latin. [Col. 0267C] LINDNER.

**“For themselves when dead, extinguished.”** There is no need to substitute, with Fulvius Ursinus and Christoph Cellarius, “for themselves when dead and extinguished.” For our author speaks in the same manner in chap. 3.7: “Would flash out, would emerge, after the waves had been shorn,” where J. Wowerus vainly suspects the final word of being a gloss. Arnobius I, p. 8: “It is accustomed to freeze and constrict the heat of its blood.” And shortly afterward: “You cannot endure and withstand the most blazing suns.” Also p. 11: “They are able to repel and defend themselves against insults.” Thus that writer everywhere heaps up synonyms (συνώνυμα). DAVIS.—Heumann thinks Minucius wrote “extinguished by death,” as in Curtius III.5.10: “to be extinguished by an ignoble death.” LINDNER.

**“With years and ages.”** That is, “with the passage of years and ages”—*mit der Zeit und mit den Jahren*. Nicolaas Heinsius, *Adversaria* II.19, p. 366, wrote “by the force of age” in place of “with ages.” LINDNER.

**“Is dissolved into earth.”** Gronovius says one should consider whether the author published “is dissolved into air,” because this discussion does not concern the material into which the corpse is dissolved, but rather its reduction to nothing, so that nothing can be hoped for from it. But who does not see that this is far too remote? LINDNER.—There is no reason to alter anything, for many pagans maintained that the bodies of the dead become earth. Thus Sophocles, *Electra* 246. Epicharmus, in Plutarch, *Consolation to Apollonius*, p. 110, says: “Earth indeed departs into earth” (γᾶν μὲν εἰς γᾶν ἀπελθεῖν). Euripides, *Hypsipyle*:

> He himself dies, and mortals grieve at these things,  
> Bearing earth into earth  
> (Αὐτός τε θνήσκει καὶ τάδ’ ἄχθονται βροτοί,  
> Εἰς γῆν φέροντες γῆν).

Cicero rendered those verses into Latin in *Tusculan Disputations* III.25. See Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* VII.12. DAVIS.

**“Nor does it matter.”** Davis thought that one absolutely had to read “and it matters,” because of the preceding “as though . . . not.” No wonder. Yet he would have thought differently if he had observed our Minucius’s mnemonic error (ἁμάρτημα μνημονικόν), examples of which I long ago warned critics must be observed in my *Critical Art*, §§40–43 [Col. 0268A]. Minucius evidently thought that he had previously [Col. 0268B] written, “although every body . . . is dissolved.” Wopken therefore rightly declined to subscribe to Davis’s view. HEUMANN.—The learned gentleman’s observation is indeed highly erudite, but it is less necessary in this passage. For in a question “as though . . . not” produces the affirmative proposition that Heumann supposed Minucius to have had in mind. LINDNER.

**“Every burial.”** I once thought one should read “burial by fire,” because of its speed in consuming the body, since no form of burial consumes a body more rapidly than fire. It was indeed an obstacle that “burials by fire” had already occurred in §3. It was an obstacle that *quidem* had then to be supplied after *paena*, and the adversative *sed* before *si*. It was also an obstacle that I saw “every burial” included burial in the bellies of wild beasts, at sea, in the ground, and in flames. But these objections were too slight to suppress the memory of so fine an emendation. Now, however, I see that “every burial” is here contrasted with the preservation of corpses by spices and ointments according to Egyptian custom, which caused corpses to be consumed only very late, although, as our author said above, they were dissolved into earth [Col. 0268C] over years and ages. LINDNER.

**“Unending life.”** *Perpetuam*, as some editions have, comes from a gloss. Festus: “The poets said *perpetem* for *perpetuo*.” Plautus certainly speaks thus not infrequently; nor did only poets do so, but prose writers as well. Claudius Mamertinus, *Panegyric* I.3.4: “To pass all nights and days in unending anxiety for the safety of all.” And *Panegyric* II.3.4: “Though the empire has been brought into order, he governs it with unending care.” See also Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 18. DAVIS.

**“That they themselves are more unjust I no longer labor to prove.”** These words yield a double meaning according as *magis* (“more”) is connected with what precedes or with what follows. *Injustus magis* means “more unjust,” a manner of speaking sufficiently [Col. 0268D] familiar even from the lexicons. Hence the sense is: “I shall not further demonstrate that Christians are more unjust than pagans, since I have already shown it.” This explanation accords very well with Octavius’s reply in chap. 35: “Yet if Christians are compared with you . . . we shall be found much better than you.” Furthermore, *magis nec laboro* means “I labor no longer,” namely to demonstrate that they themselves are unjust. Heumann thinks the author’s true text was: “I have already shown that they themselves are unjust and by no means good.” LINDNER.

**“Yet the guilt or innocence is attributed to fate by the opinions of most people, and this is your consensus.”** This is the reading of the royal manuscript. Gelenius had printed: “This is also your consensus.” The Roman edition has: “And this your . . .” He thus says that neither good nor evil deeds should be imputed to anyone, since they are attributed to fate. On this matter we made several observations at Tertullian, *Apology*, chap. 2. HERALD. Heumann reads: “The opinion of most people attributes it. And this is also your consensus.” LINDNER.

**“Thus you assign to God.”** Cellarius, following others, printed *addicitis* (“you consign”) instead of *dicitis*. With a slighter alteration one might substitute *dicatis* (“you dedicate”), that is, “you attribute.” Tertullian, [Col. 0269A] *Apology*, chap. 13: “You dedicate the honor of divinity to deceased emperors.” Again, chap. 21: “Yet the Romans did not dedicate so much honor to the Fates.” See, if you please, chaps. 28 of the same book and Sedulius, *Poem* III.88. But there is no reason to depart from the manuscript reading, as will be shown by what Heinsius noted concerning the verb *dicere* at Ovid, *Fasti* VI.594, and Graevius at Cicero, [Col. 0269B] *For Flaccus*, chap. 34. DAVIS.—Let no difficulty be raised against excellent Minucius because he preferred to use the simple verb with the meaning of the compound; the paternal *Observations*, chap. 3, “To Ecclesiastics,” prove that Cyprian likewise favored this usage. Cicero had already published in *For Flaccus*: “Valeria, he says, had assigned all the money as dowry.” And Plautus, *The Pot of Gold* IV.9.14: “Surely he assigns half to this man.” GRONOVIUS.—I do not see how Davis could defend *dicatis* by adducing passages from Tertullian, *Apology* 13, 25, and 27, nor how Heumann, with Cellarius and others, could defend *addicitis* from chap. 14 of the same Tertullian. LINDNER.

**“Thus you say that those who desire your sect are not volunteers.”** Rigaltius says this is a Hellenism, and for this purpose adduces the following words as though from the Suda: “Desiring, with the genitive, means having a deliberate inclination” (Ἐπιθυμῶν γενικῇ προαιρετικῶς ἔχων). But, apart from the fact that the antepenultimate word does not occur in that writer, the phrase is thoroughly Latin and governs not the genitive but the dative. Caesar, *Gallic War* I.18: “To favor and support the Helvetii.” [Col. 0269C] Tullius Tiro in Gellius, *Attic Nights* VII.3: “They were accused because, against the Roman people, they had supported and favored the king instead.” Likewise Terence, *The Woman of Andros*, Act V, scene IV.2. DAVIS.

**“But chosen.”** Paul, *To the Romans* IX.16: “It depends not on the one who wills nor on the one who runs, but on God who shows mercy” (Οὐ τοῦ θέλοντος οὐδὲ τοῦ τρέχοντος, ἀλλὰ τοῦ ἐλεοῦντος θεοῦ). RIGALTIUS.—He recasts this entire passage as follows: “Thus, fabricating that those who desire your sect are not volunteers but chosen, you fabricate an unjust judge,” etc. But there is no need, provided that after “chosen” you understand from what precedes “you say,” though with a different sense. Thus Cicero, *Letters* IX.17.6: “We serve him [Caesar], he the circumstances”—supply “serves.” The verb has one meaning when one says “to serve Caesar,” and another when one says “to serve the circumstances.” LINDNER.

**“Whether without a body, or.”** Heraldus, from the royal manuscript, printed: “Whether people rise again with bodies—and with what bodies: the same ones, or renewed ones? Without a body? Without this body . . .,” etc. Here the twice-repeated “without a body” indicates that the first occurrence was placed where it does not belong. Thus [Col. 0269D] Fulvius Ursinus, by inserting these two words between “whether” and “with,” restored them to the place from which they had been moved; he did not supply them from his own conjecture. LINDNER.—**“Whether with bodies?”** More recent editors have: “Whether without a body, or with bodies?” The sentence thereby indeed becomes fuller and more rounded. But since that reading rests solely on Fulvius Ursinus’s conjecture, we have decided that the manuscript’s authority should be followed. DAVIS.
