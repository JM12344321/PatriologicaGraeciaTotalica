But, although, led by these arguments, I contend that the book *On the Crown* was written in A.D. 201, I see that most scholars disagree, though they pronounce judgments that diverge entirely from one another. For Orsi, the most learned monk of the Dominican order, and his supporter Hoffmann assign it to A.D. 198; Baronius to A.D. 199; Pagi to A.D. 202; Pamelius and Allix to A.D. 210; and finally Tillemont thinks that it was [Col. 0126D] published under the emperor Maximinus, in A.D. 235. But all of these men, if one excepts Tillemont, erred without exception in understanding the soldiers’ donative given by Severus to be something other than the one that ought to be understood here. We might therefore refrain from refuting them, since we have shown what sort of donative Tertullian indicated here. Yet because the chronology of very many of his books depends upon the date of this little work, and because refuting those who think otherwise contributes greatly to confirming our judgment, come, let us examine these opinions as briefly as possible.

Here there is no need to say anything about Tillemont’s opinion, because the whole matter appears to have been adequately treated by Hoffmann (*In the Dissertation on All Tertullian’s Writings Composed during His Montanist Period*, § 6, p. 26 ff.); moreover, it plainly follows that hypothesis which maintains that Tertullian went over to the Montanists neither in A.D. [Col. 0127A] 199 nor shortly afterward—a matter we have discussed elsewhere. In this part of the discussion, indeed, Hoffmann handled the matter more correctly than when he advanced his own opinion. For he maintains that this book was composed at a time when the Christians’ peace had not yet been disturbed even by provincial governors, much less by an imperial edict, and specifically in the year after the birth of Christ 198. He believes the former to be established by the fact that Tertullian reports that the Christians believed their peace would be disturbed by the imprudent act of the soldier whom he defends here; the latter, he says, was demonstrated by Orsi with the most powerful arguments.

When Hoffmann employed these considerations in order to undermine Mosheim’s opinion—of blessed memory—concerning the true date of the *Apologeticum*, Mosheim indeed (*Additions to vol. I of his Dissertations Pertaining to Ecclesiastical History*, p. 741 ff.) [Col. 0127B] concedes that the matter had been correctly explained by Orsi; but he denies the correctness of the inference that Christians could not at the same time enjoy peace and nevertheless be subjected to various injuries by their enemies. And although I do not agree with Orsi, and indeed shall refute him below, I nevertheless acknowledge that Mosheim judged wisely when he said that, while Christian affairs were tranquil in some provinces and cities, they were very frequently disturbed by the governors in others, and that such was in fact the condition of Christianity when this book was written. This can be understood very well from Tertullian’s book *To Scapula*, which most of those who have attempted anything in this field acknowledge to have been published during Caracalla’s reign; and this will indeed become evident from what we shall demonstrate below.

Now who does not see from that book [Col. 0127C] that the Christians were then exposed to the greatest calamities? And yet there is not a single ancient writer who relates that Christians were persecuted under Caracalla. Why is this? Clearly because they then enjoyed a peace disturbed by no imperial edict, although they were not everywhere free from the cruelty of the governors. That matters stood in precisely this way when Tertullian committed these opinions *On the Crown* to writing can best be seen if what we ourselves think about the date of this book is admitted. For this deed of the soldier occurred in the East, perhaps even in Parthia, where we nowhere read that Christians were harassed before Severus’ edict; but the events narrated by Tertullian in the *Apologeticum* and his other books of that kind occurred chiefly in Africa, and therefore other Christians in the East could certainly fear that they would be deprived of peace because of the soldier’s deed.

But perhaps Orsi’s arguments will make a more powerful impression upon people’s minds; let us therefore see what they are and how strong they are. There are three passages in Orsi’s excursus in which he described this matter [Note: [Col. 0127D] In the historical dissertation demonstrating that the Catholic Church in the first three centuries by no means denied peace and absolution to persons guilty of capital crimes, sect. II, chap. 3, digression 2, pp. 104, 113. It appeared at Milan in 1730, quarto.]. First he observes that this book must be assigned to A.D. 198 and discovers traces of Severus’ generosity among the ancient writers; next he replies to Pagi, who had thought that this book was written in A.D. 202; then he shows that all the circumstances adduced by Tertullian concerning this act of generosity [Col. 0128A] agree excellently with the events of A.D. 198 but conflict with every other year. We too, therefore, shall follow in the footsteps of this most learned man, since we fear that we may otherwise confuse matters which he has already confused sufficiently; and, passage by passage, we shall indicate what he said correctly and what incorrectly, adding a briefer examination of Pagi’s arguments.

In that first passage, which concerns the number of the emperor Severus’ largesses, he says—I do not know whether quite correctly—that the soldiers were given donations by him five times, since some coins commemorate this seven times (see Vaillant, *Coins of the Roman Emperors*, vol. II, p. 234); but this may be conceded without prejudice to the point in dispute. Yet Orsi is not even consistent with himself in judging that Tertullian spoke of the donative of the year 198 (p. 104), while afterward saying nevertheless [Col. 0128B] that he spoke of the fourth largesse listed by Orsi himself (p. 106). For, as he himself observed, this fourth largesse is precisely the one that followed the capture of Ctesiphon.

The second passage, which he devoted to examining Pagi’s opinion, we do not reject in itself; yet there are some things in that discussion which must by no means be admitted. For, first, he absurdly says that Tertullian is speaking of the soldiers stationed in the city, whose praetorian camp was at the Viminal Gate for the protection of Rome. There is not even the slightest indication of this; and who could doubt that, outside Rome during wartime, Severus had military camps? Next—and this we see as especially contrary to our opinion—he says that every largesse was issued in time of peace and after a return from war; but Spanheim, whom he cites in passing on this matter, says absolutely nothing of the kind. [Col. 0128C] Even if I were to concede that this could be said of distributions to the people, I certainly doubt very strongly that it can be said of military donatives, as this undoubtedly was. Indeed, can the very largesse issued after the capture of Ctesiphon, which Orsi admits, be understood from Spartianus’ words in any other way than as one issued outside Rome during the war?

Finally, in replying to Pagi’s second argument, Orsi reasons badly that Pagi’s opinion cannot be granted because the Christians would have feared that their peace was to be disturbed either by the governors or by the emperor. Of these alternatives, he says, the former cannot be defended because the Christians had already been persecuted in A.D. 201, nor the latter because Severus was absent and extremely occupied in that year. The latter point is utterly trivial. For donatives were given to soldiers outside Rome as well, and in that same year 201 Severus brought the eastern war to an end. [Col. 0128D] But I admit that no weight should be assigned to Pagi’s arguments [Note: [Col. 0128D] He advanced them in his *Critique of Baronius’ Annals*, at A.D. 199, no. 3, in my edition pp. 91–92.]. For Caracalla’s quinquennalia, on account of which Pagi wishes the largesse mentioned by Tertullian to have been issued, belong to A.D. 203, when, as everyone knows, the Christians had already been torn apart by every form of outrage because of Severus’ edict. Furthermore, in the second argument it is also wrongly said that these hostilities were begun by the soldiers, as in the time of Diocletian; and that entire position is defended on the basis of the opinion that Christian affairs had not yet been disturbed before Severus’ tenth year—an opinion sufficiently refuted above.

Finally, the third passage of Orsi’s discussion, [Col. 0129A] in which he maintains that Tertullian’s words cannot refer to any year except 198, was not handled under a more favorable Jupiter. For his belief that this largesse of Severus occurred before the Parthian expedition does indeed contain some ambiguity. If it was issued twice—first in A.D. 197, after Severus’ return, as Herodian recorded, and then in A.D. 198, before the war against the Parthians was undertaken, as Spartianus relates—the latter, as we have said, cannot possibly be so understood, nor does it appear to have been so understood by Orsi, who cited Herodian to confirm the matter; while the former has absolutely no bearing upon the issue, because in that year Caracalla had not yet received the dignity of Augustus. But there is no need to linger at length over the passages he brought forward from Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, chap. 35, and the book *To Scapula*, chap. 4. [Col. 0129B] For we have assigned these to that same year 198 and to the following year, without any injury to our case; and all the rest collapses of its own accord once we know that donatives were distributed outside Rome.

### § 3.—Baronius’ Arguments for A.D. 199 Are Overthrown

There remain the conclusions of Baronius and Pamelius concerning the year in which this little work was written, and they are so constituted that they can be overthrown without difficulty. For Baronius (see his *Ecclesiastical Annals*, at A.D. 201, vol. II, Cologne ed., 1609, p. 299), in order to prove that this book was composed in A.D. 201 before Severus departed for the East—that is, as we more correctly customarily speak, in A.D. 199—[Col. 0129C] adduces a coin, one side of which shows Severus with *Tr. P. XI*, and the other *Liberalitas V*; and he adds, even more emphatically: “Know for certain that this coin pertains to the matters which we are about to discuss.” This seems to me to have been said in precisely the manner in which the Pythagoreans of old, when asked why something was so, used to interpose their formula, “The master himself has said it”; for nothing is further removed from the truth than this assertion of Baronius. For although he clearly relates that the seventh year of Severus’ tribunician power began in that year, he nevertheless adduces a coin bearing *Tr. P. XI*, which must plainly be referred to A.D. 204—or, if one prefers to follow Baronius himself, to A.D. 206. Does he not therefore contradict himself?

And what shall I say of his second argument, which he derived from the claim [Col. 0129D] that no calamities befell the Christians before this Parthian expedition, while many befell them after it? Here he erred twice: first, because he denied that any had occurred before Severus set out against the Parthians; and second, because he did not consider that the book *On the Crown* was later than the book *On the Spectacles*. We refuted the former claim in its proper place. As for the latter, no one can deny it unless it is pitch-dark for him at high noon—for this too has been sufficiently discussed. Consequently, there can be no mention in *On the Crown* of a persecution of Christians preceding Severus’ law, since there Christians are said to fear these calamities as events yet to come, whereas by the time Tertullian committed the book *On the Spectacles* to paper, the worshippers of the gods had already turned their assault against the Christians. Therefore, according to Baronius’ own reasoning, [Col. 0130A] the book *On the Crown* will have to be assigned to Severus’ tenth year.

### § 4.—Finally, the Conclusions of Pamelius and Allix, Who Had Placed This Book in A.D. 209, Are Rejected

I come to Allix, a most learned man, whom I am surprised to find so compliant toward the opinion of Pamelius—a scholar than whom, I doubt, anyone approached the task of determining the dates of Tertullian’s writings under less favorable auspices. Since Tertullian introduces mention of a largesse, Pamelius thinks that the little book must plainly be assigned to A.D. 209 and to Antoninus’ decennalia (*Life of Tertullian*, prefixed to Tertullian’s works published by Pamelius, Cologne, 1617, p. 22 E). But Pamelius does not prove [Col. 0130B] that a largesse was issued at those decennalia; he simply fabricates it at his pleasure. Yet elsewhere (*Notes on This Book*, p. 210, no. 1), merely for form’s sake, he appeals to a coin bearing this legend: *Imp. Caes. L. Sept. Sev. Pert. Aug. . . . Liberalitas Augg. SC.* Here let someone first tell me where the marks of the decennalia are. Next, although I am well aware that Severus and Caracalla jointly gave something to the soldiers and the people at the decennalia, were no other distributions to the people or military donatives issued besides these? No one, surely, could say so without rashness or ignorance. Indeed, since Pamelius ventured to foist the year 208 upon others without any adequate reason, it cannot in turn be held against us if we understand the year to be 201; for in that year too a largesse was issued, and issued by both Augusti.

Yet although Pamelius has exposed himself altogether disgracefully, [Col. 0130C] Allix nevertheless (*On the Life and Writings of Tertullian*, a very rare dissertation, chap. VI, p. 49) went over to his opinion. “For in the book *On the Crown*,” he says, “the little book *On the Spectacles*, which was written in A.D. 204, is cited.” Anyone who reflects upon what we stated above concerning that little work will readily understand that Pamelius, whom alone Allix follows, spoke absurdly. Nor, even if this claim concerning *On the Spectacles* were especially true, do we perceive any reason why the book *On the Crown* would have to be assigned to A.D. 208 rather than to some other year. Nor did he more correctly employ, in defense of that conclusion, the Montanist errors occurring in the book *On the Crown*. I do not deny that such errors are present in this book; but that this argument can establish the required conclusion will never be perceived except by [Col. 0130D] those who think that Tertullian went over to the Montanists only in that year.

### § 5.—Both Books *On the Dress of Women* Are Shown, against Pamelius and Tillemont, to Have Been Published Together

But our inquiry into the book *On the Crown* has detained us long enough. We must therefore hasten on to certain other books, namely those which Tertullian composed *On the Dress of Women*. Here it does not seem foreign to the discussion we are undertaking to explain briefly at the outset what the title of these books once was, and to consider whether the latter little work was published by Tertullian at the same time as the other. Those who published Tertullian’s books before Nicolas Rigault called the one *On Women’s Apparel* and the other [Col. 0131A] *On the Dress of Women*. But Rigault, after obtaining that very ancient manuscript of Agobard, was the first to venture to entitle both *On the Dress of Women*. We therefore have no doubt that these books were never separate, but constituted, as it were, a single volume. We are moved not only by the authority of the Agobard manuscript, but also because the very plan and subject of these books suggest that the latter was published by Tertullian not very long after the former.

Pamelius, who followed the title otherwise received, could scarcely digest the fact that although Tertullian had carefully distinguished *cultus* from *ornatus* in the first book, he nevertheless treated women’s *ornatus* in the second, which was entitled *On the Cultus of Women*. But once the different titles of the two books are removed and their subject carefully understood, that difficulty vanishes in a remarkable way. For Tertullian had said that a woman’s appearance consists of *cultus* and *ornatus*. As examples of the former category he gives women’s finery, gold, silver, precious stones, garments, and things of the same kind; as examples of the latter, [Col. 0131B] the care of the hair, the skin, and those parts of the body which, as he says, attract the eyes. Now in the first book he treats gold and silver (chap. 4), precious stones (chap. 5), and garments (chap. 7); in the second he deals generally with the observance of modesty in the regulation of *cultus* and *ornatus* (chaps. 1–5), then with the coloring and adornment of the hair, and concludes the whole book with certain admonitions concerning adornment contrary to modesty. It is therefore clear that in the former he dealt with women’s *cultus*, in the latter with their *ornatus*, and that both, as parts, constituted a single book.

For Tillemont’s statement (*Mémoires pour servir à l’Histoire ecclésiastique*, vol. III, p. 662, note 12) that it is manifest that these two books were separate and cannot in any way be related to one another [Col. 0131C] is easier asserted than proved and does not even deserve refutation; it is the statement of an ignorant man. There is also another proof of our position. For in the latter book Tertullian makes unmistakable mention of evils impending over the Christians, while in the former there is no indication of them. Now if they had then been afflicted by hardship, how could Tertullian have refrained from mentioning it in order to dissuade Christian women from luxury, when elsewhere he frequently employs this argument? But since that first little work is later than *On the Spectacles*, as will presently appear, let someone tell me whether the Christians enjoyed peace in any year that elapsed from the year in which we said that *On the Spectacles* was written down to A.D. 202, when hostilities against the Christians began—unless perhaps in A.D. 201 or 202. [Col. 0131D] For both the order of the books established by us in the earlier part of this discussion and the history of the Scillitan Martyrs (see above, § 6) show that Christians were severely persecuted from Severus’ return from Gaul until A.D. 201. Accordingly, since Severus’ first edict was issued at approximately some point after the beginning of A.D. 202, as we shall demonstrate in its proper place, it can scarcely be said that the first little work *On the Dress of Women* was published at any other time than approximately that at which the later book on the same subject appeared.

### § 6.—Specifically, in A.D. 201 or 202

[Col. 0132A]

What is the point of all this? Clearly, that if the date at which either of the two books was written is established, the date of both is discovered. Therefore, to follow the surest indication of that date in the books themselves, we shall make use of the calamities to which Tertullian says the Christians were then exposed, and which his words show had approximately begun at the time when he was composing the second little book *On the Dress of Women* (book II, chap. 13): “The times for Christians are always, and now most especially, passed not in gold but in iron. The robes of martyrdom are being prepared.”

These words cannot be understood of the earlier persecution of the Christians by the populace, because in the first book on this subject he refers to the book [Col. 0132B] *On the Spectacles* (chap. 7). For I cannot persuade myself that Tertullian, in citing it, intended to indicate the Greek book on a similar subject, since we have shown (§ 14) that the Latin book is earlier than the Greek, and since this admonition of Tertullian—in which he states that something may come from God even though God is not the author of its abuse—certainly occurs in the Latin book *On the Spectacles* (chap. 2). Indeed, when he wrote *On the Spectacles*, the Christians had long been afflicted by the greatest evils, of which he does not speak even a word in that first book *On the Dress of Women*; and at the time of the later little work those evils were again impending. Therefore, these books can in no way be referred to the earlier persecution under Severus, but rather to the year after the birth of Christ 201 or 202, according as one prefers to believe that the Christians’ sufferings had then arisen or were still impending. [Col. 0132C]

Nor should we listen to Tillemont, who thinks (*loc. cit.*, p. 210, art. VI) that the first book was published only after A.D. 203, but the second much earlier, specifically in A.D. 197. For in the passage he cited [Note: [Col. 0132D] Book I, chap. 6: “We have also seen at Rome the nobility of precious stones blushing before matrons at the disdain of the Parthians and Medes and the rest of their countrymen.”], Tertullian may equally well have had in mind Severus’ earlier return from Parthia or some embassy of the Parthians as that later victory over the Parthians. As for his assertion that there is some reason for believing that the book which we call the latter was written long before he published the other: because Tillemont did not see fit to provide the basis for his opinion, we in turn see fit to remain doubtful until we see something of that kind produced by someone else.

### § 7.—The Book *On Flight in Persecution* Is Assigned to A.D. 202

[Col. 0132D]

The book *On Flight in Persecution* likewise owes its origin to the same period. Tertullian had already promised elsewhere, in *On the Crown*, to discuss this subject (chap. 1). Although some have thought that the words of that passage ought rather to be referred to the *Scorpiace*, others have already observed that they cannot be so understood, because he opposed the *Scorpiace* to heretics, but the treatise *On Flight* to the timidity of those who were [Col. 0133A] members of the orthodox Church. It is therefore evident that the little book *On Flight* was published by Tertullian only after A.D. 201. And I have no doubt about assigning that book to the following year.

I shall not, indeed, make use of that mildness and moderation of Tertullian which deserves notice in a book where he raises a question that separated the Montanists from the purer Church, although no one will doubt that he was then attached to them [Note: [Col. 0133D] The mention of the Montanists’ Paraclete may serve as evidence: he says that the Paraclete was not received by those to whom belonged the brother to whom he dedicated the book (chap. 1). Furthermore, he cites as testimony an utterance of the Spirit (chap. 9) which, since it does not occur in the sacred text, undoubtedly belongs to the Montanist spirit; of this spirit he also speaks in chaps. 1 and 14.]. For one might say that, in writing these things to a man who stood with the orthodox party, Tertullian restrained himself from harsher language. Certainly a weightier argument lies in the fact that, when he published our little work, he said that certain outrages against the Christians had already begun.

For he opens as follows: “You recently asked, brother Fabius, whether one should flee during a persecution, because something or other was being announced.” [Col. 0133B] And a little afterward: “Both your inquiry has commended this subject, and the condition of the times has now imposed it under its own name. For the more frequent the persecutions that threaten, the more carefully we must examine how faith ought to receive them.” Finally: “This is that chaff which even now cleanses the Lord’s threshing floor, namely the Church, by winnowing the mixed heap of the faithful—the grain of the martyrs and the chaff of the deniers.”

But no one who has observed that the earlier persecution befell the Christian community in A.D. 197 and the later one in A.D. 202, and that our little work was written after Tertullian had adopted Montanist opinions, can fail to see which persecution of the Christian body must be understood here. We therefore say that this discussion *On Flight* was published approximately toward the end of A.D. 202, [Col. 0133C] chiefly because Severus was still staying in Syria at the beginning of the year; hence it can scarcely be said that he enacted his law against the Christians before the middle part of that year had passed.

### § 8.—The Common Opinion That the Book *Scorpiace* Is Later Than Tertullian’s Books against Marcion Is Examined, and His Lost Books against Marcion Are Discussed

We now come to Tertullian’s *Scorpiace*, which we have resolved to assign to A.D. 204 and to explain the basis of that judgment. As we undertake this task, we do not doubt that there will be very many who say that our opinion does not deserve the least consideration. For nearly all those whom we know to have discussed this matter hold that Tertullian’s *Scorpiace* was written [Col. 0133D] only after the publication of the books against Marcion which survive to our own time. They seem to regard themselves as bound, as though by prescription or command, to defend this position because in the fifth section of the *Scorpiace* Tertullian says that the Marcionites learned from him that their God is good—a matter which he certainly discussed systematically in the first and second books against Marcion.

I am well aware that Tertullian published the books against Marcion—or at least the first—in A.D. 207 or 208, and that it therefore seems consistent [Col. 0134A] with the truth that he published the *Scorpiace* only in A.D. 208 or later. But since I always inquire what is most probable in any matter, I should like in some measure to weigh how much force this argument possesses. When Tertullian was composing the first book against Marcion which we possess, he had already opposed Marcion twice in writing; but one of these treatises he afterward suppressed when he undertook a fuller discussion of the subject, while the other he lost through the fraud of a certain man who had copied it and shared it with others. To make good this loss, he composed the books against Marcion which now survive, the first specifically in the fifteenth year of the emperor Severus, as he himself acknowledges in that book (chap. 15), where he also explained the matters we have just mentioned (chap. 1).

When I reflect upon these facts, [Col. 0134B] it certainly appears doubtful whether, in the book which he called *Scorpiace*, Tertullian referred to those books against Marcion which have not reached our age, or rather to those which we know to survive, since the lost books dealt with the same subject. I am not now arguing which of these alternatives must be chosen; that will become evident in its proper place. But everyone sees that the mode of argument employed by those who think otherwise is doubtful and finds little support in the argument that, from that mention, the book could be assigned to A.D. 208 or later.

Nor does Hoffmann’s reply to Tillemont, who prudently hesitated here, prevent us from doubting the matter. [Col. 0134C] He says (*dissertation cited*, § 14, p. 44):

> “The first little work, being slight, he suppressed himself; the second was published through another man’s fraud against Tertullian’s will, while it was still rough and insufficiently polished, and was not acknowledged by Septimius; the third does not survive. Now, since it is scarcely probable that Tertullian referred readers to a treatment of the Marcionite controversy which he himself had suppressed or never acknowledged, it follows that the books now in our hands must be understood.”

I have no desire here to dwell at length upon Hoffmann’s insufficiently accurate discussion of the second treatment of that subject, as if, when it was published, it had neither been sufficiently worked out nor acknowledged by Septimius. I certainly do not understand how this can be inferred from Tertullian’s words:

> “This one too,” he says, “not yet supplied with copies”—that is, not yet transcribed into several manuscripts—“but existing in only one, which he took away, I afterward lost through the fraud of a man who was then a brother but later an apostate; he had happened to copy certain passages most incorrectly and presented them to the public.”

[Col. 0134D] Yet even if I grant that everything Hoffmann has said here is correct, no one will deny that his entire argument accomplishes nothing if it assumes what first had to be proved. Thus, if we wish to be held bound by the opinion that the surviving books against Marcion are earlier than the *Scorpiace* before we have judged what is most correct in this matter, I admit that it is hardly probable that Tertullian commended in the *Scorpiace* a book which had been suppressed or never acknowledged. But suppose that this has not yet been established—as in truth it has not—and that the *Scorpiace* was perhaps written before those surviving books. In that case he could quite suitably cite the first and second volumes against Marcion, although they are now lost; for the first little work, as Tertullian himself says, he suppressed only later, when he produced a fuller composition, and it could therefore be cited before he had treated these matters in a more exact discourse.

Accordingly, Hoffmann’s argument either assumes as already true the very things that are plainly in controversy, [Col. 0135A] or else leaves them undecided. If the former, his argument clearly moves in a circle; if the latter, our opinion is no weaker than Hoffmann’s. But now that this difficulty, which might have worked to our disadvantage, has been removed, come, let us determine the date at which the *Scorpiace* was written.

### § 9.—It Is Established That the *Scorpiace* Was Written in A.D. 204

When Tertullian committed to writing the points concerning the dignity of martyrdom which he wished to discuss in this *Scorpiace*, the worshippers of the gods were occupied in utterly destroying the Christians. This is shown by the entire argument of this little work, directed against those heretics who, when faith was in the heat of trial and the Church was being burned in the figure of the bush, regarded it as disgraceful to suffer evils on account of religion (chap. 1). [Col. 0135B] Such evils must already have been extremely atrocious, as is shown by Tertullian’s words immediately added in the same section:

> “And now the present state of affairs is the very midpoint of the heat, the very dog days of persecution—proceeding, plainly, from the dog-headed one himself. Fire has tested some Christians, the sword others, wild beasts others,” etc.

There is no doubt here that the persecution which blazed forth after Severus’ edict must be understood, since he composed this book after *On Flight in Persecution*. For nowhere do we read Tertullian maintaining that God is acknowledged as the author of such calamities suffered on account of religion except in that little book *On Flight*, and especially in its third section; yet in the fourth section of the *Scorpiace* he says that he had already discussed this matter previously.

But to speak more precisely about the date of this book, I consider it to have appeared definitely in A.D. 204. [Col. 0135C] My first argument is this: when Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* VI.2, Cambridge ed., p. 257) had said that calamities were inflicted upon the Christians by Severus with the greatest violence, so much so that many already thought the advent of Antichrist was approaching, neither Eusebius himself nor any other ancient writer recorded any evils other than those which befell the Christians in Severus’ tenth year under Laetus, governor of Africa, and in the following year at the instigation of Aquila, governor of the same province (Eusebius, *loc. cit.*, chap. 3, p. 261).

Eusebius himself teaches that these latter events occurred in that year. He confirms that Origen was then placed in charge of instructing catechumens while Aquila opposed the growth of the Christian religion by injuring its adherents, and that Origen was eighteen years old, whereas he had said that he was in his seventeenth year when his father Leonides was put to death (chap. 2, p. 258), which occurred in Severus’ tenth year. [Col. 0135D] Now if some nevertheless persist in thinking that our little work was written in A.D. 208, let them tell me by what right they can assign to that year the Christians’ greatest calamities, of which no memory survives among the ancient writers.

At this point one may well marvel at the frivolity of so many extremely learned men. Once they had imbibed the opinion that the *Scorpiace* was later than our surviving books against Marcion, they forgot that nothing is more disgraceful in history than to believe rashly. For if I see two opinions advanced, one plausible but incapable of being established by the testimony of any ancient writer, the other so constituted that it displays this support of truth, why should I not abandon the former and follow the latter?

And this argument is at hand and must be pressed again and again, provided that we later show that Tertullian’s book *On the Pallium* must be placed in A.D. 208. For in that book (chap. 1) he says: [Col. 0136A]

> “I am glad, Carthaginians, that you enjoy such prosperous times, since you have sufficient leisure and inclination to find fault with clothing. These are the idle pursuits of peace and plenty; all is well from both the government and the heavens.”

How could Tertullian there have commemorated the prosperity of the times, or descended because of the leisure afforded by peace to matters of no importance at all, such as these trifles about the pallium, if the Christians were then suffering the extremely grave calamities which he depicts in the *Scorpiace*? Consequently, although others have thought that the *Scorpiace* was written nearly five years later, I certainly judge that it appeared before the surviving disputations against Marcion, specifically in A.D. 203 or 204. For this conclusion we have in Eusebius a most reliable witness. Although he nowhere states it expressly, what he does say, as we have seen, shows approximately the same thing.

For my part, I prefer the latter year because of the games which Tertullian says had been celebrated very recently [Note: [Col. 0136D] Chap. 6: “How greatly the world celebrates those contests, the contentious solemnities and superstitious competitions of the Greeks, both in religion and in pleasure, Africa too has had the opportunity to see. Even now the individual cities keep Carthage in a state of agitation with their congratulations, because, after the old age of its stadium, it has been presented with a Pythian contest.”]. [Col. 0136B] If anyone prefers to believe that they were celebrated during Severus’ decennalia and Caracalla’s quinquennalia, I shall not strongly object, since Spanheim—a man assuredly respected and highly distinguished in this field of scholarship—has adequately demonstrated [Note: [Col. 0136D] In *Letter I to Andreas Morell*, appended to his *Specimen of a Universal Account of Ancient Coinage*, Leipzig ed., p. 12 f.] that Pythian games were held elsewhere than at Delphi, and not only in honor of Pythian Apollo but also in honor of the emperors.

Nevertheless, I would prefer the other alternative because the Pythian games in Greece were celebrated in every third year of an Olympiad, and the third year of the 245th Olympiad itself falls in the year after the birth of Christ 204. [Col. 0136C] In this way no violence is done to Tertullian’s words; for if they are taken to mean that those games were held at Carthage—where Carthage itself could not be presented with the prize—they do not appear to cohere very well.
