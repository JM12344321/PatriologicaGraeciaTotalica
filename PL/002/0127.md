# DISSERTATION ON THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN BAPTISM AND PENANCE, THE AUTHOR OF THE FOLLOWING BOOK, AND THE DATE OF ITS COMPOSITION

**I.** Having therefore completed Tertullian’s *Book on Baptism*, we are now brought—not, indeed, according to the proper and customary order of our Sacraments, yet not by a leap [Col. 1205B] either—to his *Book on Penance*, which likewise is to be elucidated with certain notes. For the Author himself did not wholly pass over the intermediate Sacraments, Confirmation and the Eucharist. He treated the former, in Chapters VI and VII of his preceding book, by no means cursorily, as a kind of completion of Baptism; while the latter he at least touched upon briefly in Chapter XVI, as we noted there.

**II.** There is, moreover, a certain special connection, founded even upon necessity itself, between Baptism and Penance. For when GOD, the Creator of all things, had launched the First Man upon this universe as upon a vast open sea, and thereafter had appointed him, as it were, admiral of the sea for the whole human race, that man, swept away by the waves of his harmful desire, struck his ship upon the most unyielding rock of Original Sin [Col. 1205C] and caused the most wretched shipwreck not only of himself but of us all. The boundless GOODNESS of our SAVIOR then extended to us a first plank, BAPTISM, by grasping which we might rise again and reach the harbor of salvation. But because he foresaw that, even in this very harbor, there would be many shipwrecks among men who would again be engulfed by new waves, Christ, the same Institutor of the Sacraments, cast out to our enfeebled and excessively unstable will a second plank after shipwreck, PENANCE, by whose aid we might swim back once more to a safe shore. This, indeed, is that BAPTISM which the Council of Trent, Session XIV, Chapter 2, calls TOILSOME; for in it one must labor mightily through the arduous works of Penance, so that offenses may be washed away no longer in another’s font of water, but in [Col. 1205D] our own tears.

**III.** Accordingly, the following Book is joined to the preceding one all the more suitably because that earlier book ended with Penance itself as a preparation necessary for Baptism in adults, whereas this book, like a fitting joint, pursues the same subject more fully and also treats in greater detail that Penance which remains available to those who relapse after Baptism.

**IV.** There is, however, more than one difference between Baptism and Penance.

First, with respect to their end: Baptism was instituted chiefly to destroy original sin and, in adults, the other sins adhering to it; and then so that through it a person might be incorporated into the People of GOD and become a member of the Church. For no other entrance into that Church lies open than through this gate of Baptism, the first of all the Sacraments and one of the most necessary. [Col. 1206A] Penance, by contrast, must be employed to destroy actual sins, and especially those [Col. 1206B] committed subsequently, after Baptism.

Second, with respect to their effect: in Baptism, together with guilt, all liability to punishment is also removed, both eternal and temporal. Penance, however, does not have the same efficacy with regard to the latter; for temporal punishment remains to be discharged in purgatory unless full satisfaction for sins has been rendered in this life through works of penance.

Third, with respect to their intrinsic nature: Baptism is regeneration—spiritual, namely, and according to the soul—which, like carnal generation, can take place only once. Baptism therefore cannot be repeated in one who has once validly received it, since it is sealed with a kind of impressed seal, that is, a character. Penance, however, takes place in the form of a judgment in which a person, as the accused and at the same time his own accuser, [Col. 1206C] is absolved of his offenses. Just as an accused person may be brought before a human tribunal after each new offense and relapse, so one who falls after Baptism, once he has performed penance, must repeat it whenever he relapses again. Penance is therefore never sealed by any character, since the mercy of GOD is never afterward closed to him.

Fourth, with respect to necessity: Baptism is necessary for salvation for all human beings, even infants, who nevertheless are stained only by original sin. Penance, however, is necessary only for adults who are liable for sins committed by their own will, whether before or after Baptism. Nor can there be true and properly so-called penance for original sin, but only penance in the broader sense, namely any displeasure with [Col. 1206D] and detestation of a past evil. I deliberately pass over other differences, since my intention at present is to abstract from the Sacrament of Penance.

**V.** We must now come to the AUTHOR of this Book. No one either formerly doubted or now doubts that he was Tertullian. Erasmus of Rotterdam alone attempted to take it away from Tertullian, and only Beatus Rhenanus followed him; both relied upon this single and certainly rather weak argument: difference of style. I do not deny that the style in this book is somewhat more florid, but it does not differ greatly from that of the other works, even those which Tertullian wrote nearly at the end of his life, and the difference consists only in certain rather more brilliant allegories and metaphors. But if there were leisure to pluck similar blossoms from the other little plots of this same author, [Col. 1207A] I could easily weave whole garlands.

**VI.** Besides, critics of the highest reputation long ago perceived that from any mere discrepancy of style—unless supported by other and weightier arguments—an author can scarcely be rendered suspect, much less can any judgment be pronounced against him. Of these critics I cite even one, William Cave, who in the Prolegomena to his *Historia Literaria*, Section IV, no. 64, says that an argument for νοθεύσεως (*notheuseōs*), or spurious attribution, is supplied only when the style and the whole texture of the discourse are FAR DIFFERENT from the manner of expression employed by an author in his genuine works. Yet any reader will discover that it is so far from being greatly different that, apart from a few somewhat more animated phrases which scarcely occur in Tertullian’s other writings, that African and [Col. 1207B] concise mode of expression almost peculiar to Tertullian, that peculiar majesty of style, that abundance of meaning in almost every word, and that lofty eloquence—which, however harsh it may be, can nevertheless somehow draw even an unwilling reader toward itself—triumph no less in this book. I say nothing of other phrases so carefully reproduced from his other books, which I shall intentionally note in their proper places.

**VII.** Beatus Rhenanus himself then admits that the author of this book uses the same words and figures, that whoever he was undoubtedly belonged to Tertullian’s age, and that he was a most devoted student of Tertullian. Why, then, should he not be TERTULLIAN himself? For what other person could have reproduced his assuredly singular style, scarcely capable of imitation by anyone, so skillfully that he deceived St. Pacian, a writer of the fourth century, and all others for more than [Col. 1207C] eleven centuries down to Erasmus, into accepting him as Tertullian himself, while no one ever suspected from a difference of style that this Book had another author?

**VIII.** We therefore judge, finally, that nothing more can be inferred from that discrepancy of style, whatever its degree, than that Tertullian undertook this composition while he was still of more vigorous age and livelier talent. For minds of this fervent and agile kind are accustomed, like bees, to fly around among various flowers, to draw allegories and metaphors even from matters foreign to their subject, and carry them into their own hive, and so forth. Likewise, as age advances little by little, the style is accustomed to settle down and become more restrained and robust. Thus TULLY himself, the Prince of Orators, delivered his speeches for Publius Quinctius and Roscius of Ameria—the former his very first speech, delivered when he was twenty-six years old, [Col. 1207D] as Aulus Gellius reports in *Attic Nights*, Book XV, Chapter 28, and the latter in court in the following year. Some think that he sprinkled them, especially the latter, somewhat too freely with such florid ornaments—Malon, his last teacher, having vainly attempted to restrain from this verbal exuberance so vivid and flourishing a talent—although others even praise him for them. For as he gradually reached maturer age and this floral adornment fell away of its own accord, he ripened into fruits all the more enduring, and the more manly gravity and majesty of his eloquence was thereafter, as it were, consolidated.

**IX.** Finally, one will not easily find any writer who remains perpetually consistent with himself, especially if he has not carried his pen through one continuous work but across diverse and [Col. 1208A] unconnected subjects, at different times and with intervals of years. For one subject is by its very nature rather somber or humble and more barren, enough to blunt and depress even the vigor of a youthful talent, and before which the otherwise rich eloquence of men withers. Another subject, however, is more agreeable and pleasant and better suited to the author’s own disposition, and can sufficiently rouse and stimulate a talent otherwise rather languid. A final kind is so lively and diffuses something so radiant that it can kindle and inflame the writer completely, and even sometimes revive sparks long dormant beneath the gray ashes of old age. There are also other things that come to a writer from elsewhere and, as it were, obliquely, yet can sometimes do much to alter his disposition, [Col. 1208B] so to speak. Why, then, is it surprising if Tertullian too is not everywhere consistent with himself, but when he first turned to writing, though already of more mature age, used a somewhat more spirited style, which afterward settled down somewhat in his other books and compensated, as it were, by being adorned with a greater apparatus of learning? But let this have been said ὡς ἐν παρόδῳ (*hōs en parodōi*, “by way of digression”), although at somewhat excessive length.

**X.** But if the actual DATE of composition must now be designated somewhat more closely—although that cannot be done precisely—I think that this treatise was certainly committed to writing before A.D. 200, and probably in the year 197 or perhaps earlier. Certainly the dates of both this work and the other, *On Baptism*, were not far apart.

[Col. 1208C] **XI.** Whether, however, in the order of composition he placed the book *On Baptism* after this book *On Penance*, or this book after that one, can scarcely be determined. Yet it seems more probable to me that he placed this one afterward. For if he had written *On Penance* first, I believe that in the final chapter of *On Baptism*, where he discusses Penance as a preparation to be placed before Baptism, he would have made some mention of it as a subject already treated more fully elsewhere. Certainly in the book *On Baptism* itself, at the end of Chapter 15, he carefully refers his readers to another treatise on the same subject, previously composed in Greek. Who, then, will appear to judge wrongly if he says that Tertullian afterward adopted a new plan and undertook a fuller treatment, in a separate book, of this subject of Penance which he had treated somewhat briefly at the end of the book *On Baptism*? [Col. 1208D] He did so partly for the sake of catechumens, so that they might conceive a greater appreciation of the value of Baptism, which they would understand had to be obtained through such toilsome works of penance. Why, then, should they not afterward exercise themselves in these works with all the more ardent zeal, so that they might the more certainly obtain the so lavishly proposed reward of Baptism and its grace, and afterward preserve it all the more safely?

**XII.** Finally, we should hear the splendid judgment on this book delivered by Beatus Rhenanus himself, a man of great discernment, in the prefatory summary attached to it: namely, that this Commentary is such that theologians ought to learn it perfectly, “to the fingernail.” “For,” he continues, “it is an outstanding monument of antiquity: so holily [Col. 1209A] does it teach, so devoutly persuade, so insistently urge a matter supremely necessary to ecclesiastical discipline—*exomologesis*, that is, the act and ministry of Penance.” Certainly, just as we everywhere learn from Tertullian the traditions and discipline of the ancient Church, so here too, with signal [Col. 1210A] usefulness against our innovators, we are beautifully and solidly taught the manner of performing penance as it was customarily performed from the earliest centuries, and assuredly by apostolic institution. But let us now proceed to the subject itself.

# BOOK ON PENANCE

**Chapter I.** Penance ought to concern only evil deeds, not good works.

**II.** True Penance is that which concerns evils, or offenses against GOD.

**III.** Penance must be performed for all sins, whether external or merely internal. [Col. 1209B]

**IV.** An exhortation to embrace Penance, which is as necessary to offenders as it is beneficial.

**V.** Penance must not be effaced by new sins, nor performed only in the mind, but also in actual deed.

**VI.** Catechumens are not therefore without need of penance merely because their sins are ultimately to be destroyed through Baptism itself. [Col. 1210A]

**VII.** Those who fall after Baptism must again perform penance.

**VIII.** That Penance must be performed as often as a person has sinned is confirmed by examples from Holy Scripture.

[Col. 1210B] **IX.** What *Exomologesis* is, and what its principal works are.

**X.** Public confession of sins must neither be a cause of shame nor be postponed.

**XI.** Nor should bodily hardships deter us from performing penance.

**XII.** The exhortation to penance is confirmed by examples set forth.

# Notes on Chapter I

[Col. 1209B]

**Penance.** According to St. Isidore, Bishop of Seville, *Origins*, Book IV, Chapter 19, *poenitentia* (“penance”) was so called as though it were *punientia* (“punishment”), because by repenting a person punishes in himself what he has done wrong. The author of the book *On True and False Penance*—formerly ascribed to St. Augustine—says almost the same in Chapter 19: [Col. 1209C] “To repent is the same as to maintain punishment, so that a person may continually punish in himself by taking vengeance upon what he committed by sinning.” In Greek it is called μετάνοια (*metanoia*), “a change of mind,” or of one’s former judgment, from μετανοεῖν (*metanoein*), which means “to understand afterward,” or “to become wise after the event.” In this same sense it is also called μετάγνωσις (*metagnōsis*), μετάγνοια (*metagnoia*), and μεταμέλεια (*metameleia*).

**This kind of men.** Here he censures the Gentiles, and especially their philosophers.

**We formerly were.** For Tertullian, born of Gentile parents and raised in paganism, did not turn to the Christian rites until he was already fairly advanced in age, as we indicated in the epitome of his Life prefixed to the book *On Prescription*, drawing upon his own testimony in the *Apology*, Chapter 18.

**By the light of God.** By faith, namely, which is the supernatural light of the soul.

**So far as nature extends**, that is, solely according to the guidance of natural reason, or so far as the light innate in our intellect itself extends.

[Col. 1209D] **Of the former judgment.** This is a general definition drawn from moral philosophy. He therefore says that penance is a movement, alteration, or certain affection of the mind, joined with a certain distress and arising from aversion to the former judgment or opinion from which something was done. For when someone now understands that he formerly acted less than rightly and thus incurred some loss or disadvantage, a certain movement afterward arises in his mind from the sense of that distress or offense. He therefore now changes and retracts his former judgment, having conceived displeasure with it and a certain hatred of it; and those philosophers suppose that the true nature of penance consists in this. Thus, some writers cited by Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights*, Book XVII, Chapter 1, say: “We customarily say that we repent when things we ourselves have done, or which were done by our will and counsel, afterward begin to displease us, and we change our judgment concerning them.”

**Nature**, or true conception and definition.

**Its Author.** Supply: “by rightly knowing him.” And how could those know him whose understanding was so blinded that they located Divinity in the stars, human beings, beasts, and every sort of basest creature? [Col. 1210B]

**Ordered.** For he made all these things according to the counsels of his infinite WISDOM.

**Strangers**, that is, those placed outside and alienated from knowledge of the true God.

**Storm.** We have already spoken of this florid allegory in the intervening Dissertation. [Col. 1210C]

**They dwell.** He also uses this verb in the book *Against the Valentinians*, Chapter 30: “He who dwelt in the world,” etc. But other manuscripts, and certainly better ones, have *diversentur*. Whichever form we believe Tertullian used, the expression will always be rendered more idiomatically in Latin by *conversentur*, “they dwell” or “associate.”

**To resolve.** In the sense of “to refute” or “to place beyond doubt.”

**Unto displeasure**, or it happens as something unwelcome, troublesome, or unpleasant. *Ingratia*, therefore—an obsolete word—is used here for displeasure at another’s deed. He also uses this word shortly afterward in Chapter 2. A parallel passage occurs in Book II *Against Marcion*, Chapter 24: “For it does not follow that, because a person repents from recollection of a great offense, and sometimes even from displeasure at some good work, God therefore does likewise.”

**Is inflicted.** In the sense of “is applied,” “expended,” “imposed,” “brought upon,” and so forth, as Plautus says in the *Captives*:

[Col. 1210D] “I shall appoint a day against them and inflict punishment.”

And Cicero says in *On His House*: “The Twelve Tables forbid laws to be imposed upon private persons.” Tacitus too, *Annals*, Book IV, Chapter 10, says of Drusus that, “when the poison had been drained, he increased suspicion, as though he were inflicting upon himself the death he had contrived for his father.” Again, Cicero in *For Rabirius* spoke of “the imposition of a fine.” Others understand *irrogare* in the sense of *erogare*, “to pay out,” whose meaning is almost the same as the former. We have already spoken of the word *erogare* in the book *On Prescription*, Chapter II, note “In the act,” though in a different sense. But let these matters be left to grammarians.

**On the contrary.** The word *contra* must certainly be marked with an accent, so as to mean “contrariwise” or “on the opposing side.”

# Notes on Chapter II

**Reason.** For reason is a thing of God and was given to us by divine gift, as our Author said above.

**Merits**, that is, considerations or grounds, in the sense in which jurists customarily speak of the “merits of a case.” It may also be understood here as utility [Col. 1211A] or benefit.

**They would weigh.** That is, weigh as though in a balance.

**They would maintain.** By not preparing for themselves material for repenting so often and so long.

**For salvation.** “For sorrow according to God produces a steadfast PENANCE UNTO SALVATION.” 2 Cor. 7:10.

**Inaugurated.** Elsewhere too our Author uses the verb *auspicor* passively. Thus in the book *On Patience*, Chapter 5, he says: “Since it is manifest that impatience was inaugurated together with malice, or malice by impatience.” And in the book *On the Soldier’s Crown*, Chapter 1: “There he laid aside his very heavy cloak, beginning to be relieved.” Properly, however, *auspicari* means “to watch birds”—for the ancients said *spicere* in place of *aspicere*, “to behold”—when from their flight, song, or feeding people took auspices. For they customarily undertook nothing without first consulting the birds, and only then believed that they had begun their business auspiciously and successfully, with favorable auspices and birds.

Among the ancients the active form *auspico* is also found, as [Col. 1211B] in Plautus’s *The Persian*: “I took the auspices for this day”; and in the work *Against Cato on His Consulship*: “After I had taken the auspices, and the army,” etc. But I am certainly excessive in these grammatical trifles.

**With a dowry.** He means Paradise and the other temporal gifts bestowed upon Adam.

**Had hastened**, that is, God had betaken himself promptly, or at a timely and appropriate moment. There appears to be a difference between “to mature” and “to hasten” or “accelerate”: the latter signifies mere speed, while the former signifies speed joined with deliberation. Thus delay is entirely absent from speed, but is mingled with maturation. Speed also sometimes does harm, whereas maturation never does. Hence the Greek maxim of Caesar Augustus was ΣΠΕΥΔΕ ΒΡΑΔΕΩΣ (*speude bradeōs*), “MAKE HASTE SLOWLY.” That is, he thought maturation coalesced, as it were, from two things otherwise opposed to one another: the speed of industriousness and the slowness of diligence. If these are duly proportioned, we call a business mature and say that it is now being done at the proper time. Thus also we call fruits that develop too quickly, as though nature were hurrying them forth, premature: they are rarely flavorful or durable. But those which grow by slower increments than is fitting mature more rarely and generally remain unripe, suitable neither for use nor for food. [Col. 1211C]

I do not, however, deny that *maturare* is sometimes used for “to hasten” or “act quickly.” That elegant statement found in St. Cyprian’s *Letter to Donatus* deserves to be cited here: “Receive what is perceived before it is learned and is not gathered through delays of time by prolonged cognition, but is imbibed through the short course of hastening grace.”

**Inaugurated.** On this word see the book *On Baptism*, Chapter XIX, note “Inaugurated.” Our Author generally uses this verb when he wishes to signify something new, as in the *Apology*, Chapter 12: “The body of your god is first inaugurated upon a gibbet”; and in the book *On the Soul*, Chapter 19, concerning a newborn infant: “Inaugurating all the senses at once,” he says, “sight by light, hearing by sound,” etc. Likewise, in the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, Chapter 11, he says that “the universe”—this world—“was inaugurated by God.” In these and similar passages the word therefore seems to be understood as “to initiate”; in this sense he says that God inaugurated penance in himself as an example for us. [Col. 1211D]

Furthermore, repentance is predicated of God, like other properties of human affections of this kind, ἀνθρωποπαθῶς (*anthrōpopathōs*), “in an anthropopathic manner.” “In human beings,” says St. Augustine in Book II *To Simplicianus*, Question 2, “there is an act of repenting when a will is found that must be changed; but in a human being it is accompanied by mental pain, for he reproves in himself what he did rashly. Let us therefore remove,” he continues, “those things which arise from human weakness and ignorance, and let only the WILL that something should not be as it was remain. In this way there can be intimated to our mind the rule according to which it may be understood that GOD repents. For when he is said to repent, he wills that something should not be as he had made it to be. Yet while it existed, it was right that it should exist in that way; and when it is no longer permitted to exist in that way, it ought no longer so to exist. This is done by a perpetual and tranquil judgment of equity, by which God disposes all mutable things with an IMMUTABLE WILL.” Again, on Psalm 131: “When he changes his works through his immutable counsel, he is said to repent because of the change not in his counsel, [Col. 1212A] but in his work.” You will find similar statements in the same author’s *Eighty-Three Questions*, Question 52, and in the author of the book *On the Essence of Divinity* among the works of St. Augustine.

**Of acts of anger.** God indeed also becomes angry, but by no means as a human being does. “Human anger,” St. Augustine again says in Book II *To Simplicianus*, in the question cited, “is turbulent; but the anger of God, while he himself continually remains in TRANQUILLITY, executes vengeance upon the subjected creature with wondrous equity. . . . From human anger I remove the turbulent movement, so that the vigor of vengeance may remain; and thus, in some measure, I rise to a knowledge of what is called the ANGER OF GOD.” Shall I also add what he says in Book I *Against the Adversary of the Law and the Prophets*, Chapter 20? “When God repents, HE IS NOT CHANGED, yet he changes things; just as when he becomes angry, HE IS NOT CHANGED, yet he takes vengeance.”

**To his image**, that is, to the human being created in his image. Gen. 1:26 ff. Recall, if you wish, what we said fairly fully in Chapter V of *On Baptism*, note “To the image of God,” 1159A.

**Gathered.** As God said in Exod. 6:7: “I shall take you to myself as a people, and I shall be your God,” etc. So too [Col. 1212B] in very many other passages.

**He exhorted.** The books of Sacred History are full of these things; the books of Exodus and Judges in particular abound with examples of this kind.

**For prophesying.** Rhenanus says that this is a dative and means “in order to prophesy.”

**Sent forth.** For our Author, *emittere* means either simply “to send,” or, with greater emphasis, “to send forth from oneself,” so that they might prophesy in his own name, namely God’s.

**About to illuminate.** This was fulfilled on the day of Pentecost through those fiery tongues, when Peter shortly afterward declared in his address, Acts 2:17–18: “This is what was foretold through the prophet Joel: ‘And it shall be in the last days,’ says the Lord, ‘I shall pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh, and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy,’” etc. This is the very thing previously foretold in Isa. 44:3, where God says: “I shall pour out my Spirit upon your offspring,” etc. Furthermore, “to illuminate for the whole world” seems to have been taken from the Greek phrase in the Canticle [Col. 1212C] of Zechariah, Luke 1:[79]: ἐπιφᾶναι τοῖς ἐν σκότει καὶ σκιᾷ θανάτου καθημένοις (*epiphanai tois en skotei kai skiai thanatou kathēmenois*), that is, “TO ILLUMINATE—to bring light to—THOSE who sit in darkness and in the shadow of death.”

**Immersion**, or the baptism of penance, which John preached for the remission of sins. Mark 1:4.

**Appointed.** Namely, Gen. 12:3, where God says to Abraham: “In you all the kindreds of the earth shall be blessed.” And Isa. 41:8: “The offspring of Abraham, my friend, in whom I took hold of you from the ends of the earth . . . and said to you, ‘You are my servant; I have chosen you and have not rejected you,’” etc. And so very often elsewhere.

**Countersignature**, that is, the signature attached to that promise in the manner of a condition, so that the people of Israel would likewise bind themselves to keep that covenant.

**Might prepare**, in the sense of “might arrange” or “adapt,” as St. John admonishes: “Prepare the way of the Lord,” etc. Mark 1:3.

**Will draw near.** For thus John says to the people in the wilderness, Matt. 3:2: “Perform penance, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near.” [Col. 1212D]

**Ancient error.** Original Sin.

**Ignorance.** Sins committed through ignorance—that is, vincible and culpable ignorance.

**Sweeping.** Cleansing as if with brooms, for these are properly what we sweep with; thus Matt. 12:[44] says: “He finds it swept clean.”

**Scraping.** Those stains, or other things, which have settled somewhat more deeply are scraped off; hence we also speak of shaving a beard, etc.

**Lies open.** For receiving him in Baptism, but more fully through the subsequent imposition of Hands.

**Gladly.** Finding nothing now unseemly in this dwelling of his, but rather everything clean and worthy of himself.

**Cause.** The final cause, namely.

**This work.** *Opera* is used here for labor, as our Author likewise says in the book *On Patience*, Chapter 16: “This is the principle of patience, this its discipline, this its heavenly and true labor,” etc. And in the book *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, Chapter 15: “Let them deny the fellowship of labors, so that they may deservedly deny the reward also.” [Col. 1213A]

**Benefits a human being**, that is, contributes to his advancement, as in the book *On Baptism*, Chapter VII.

**Its nature**, namely Penance.

**We learn.** For if this is unknown, the true nature of penance must necessarily be unknown. See above, Chapter I: “Therefore all who are ignorant of God,” etc.

**Be thrust upon.** By violently twisting penance, contrary to its own nature, so as to apply even to good works, which would thereafter be hindered by it.

**And**, in the sense of “also.”

**Let him see to it.** A form of expression frequently used by our Author. Thus in the *Apology*, Chapter 16, he says: “Let the appearance see to itself, provided the quality of the material is the same; let the form see to itself, provided it is the very body of God,” etc.; and in the book *On Patience*, Chapter 16: “Let his own people see to their own affairs”; and in the book *On the Shows*, Chapter 15: “Let the principal title of idolatry see to itself,” etc. We therefore generally use this phrase when we warn another who has been brought into some danger to take care concerning it and look after his own affair. [Col. 1213B] Thus Cicero says in the *Brutus*: “You will see to this,” that is, “I leave the care of this matter to you, lest, if anything happens otherwise, you impute it to me.” And Terence says in the *Brothers*: “I shall see to that”—as though he said, “That will be my concern.”

**Unto displeasure.** See Chapter I, note “Displeasure.” But what if I explain the sentence in this way: “Let the perverse opinion of men, certainly displeasing to others, see to itself,” etc.? Or: “If those whom we have benefited begin to displease us and to be ungrateful—that is, hateful—to us.”

**Forces penance.** The sense appears to be: “If it compels, drives, and twists penance, as though reluctant and contrary to its nature, even toward evil deeds.”

**Its pursuit.** The pursuit of favor is the desire to conciliate another’s favor and goodwill toward oneself, so that we may please the other and be pleasing—that is, acceptable—to him. For a person who confers a benefit upon another is rarely found either not to desire to please him or not to hope for and seek some advantage that will return to himself. Thus we love ourselves everywhere [Col. 1213C] and excessively.

**Mortal**, that is, temporal and destined at some point to end—both favor and displeasure, namely.

**Of gain.** Or profit and advantage, as the best Authors everywhere use the word.

**A debtor.** Of reward, namely.

**Evil.** Punishment, certainly.

**Rewarder**, that is, one who renders recompense.

**May decree**, in the sense of “may establish.” To decree is to establish something which cannot be violated with impunity.

**Must be rendered.** By repairing through satisfaction his supreme Right, which has been violated by sins.

**Private.** Latinius deletes *privatum* as otherwise introducing remarkable obscurity into the passage. I think, however, that it can be retained without difficulty, and understand “private” here as “one’s own,” as our Author says more explicitly in the book *On the Shows*, Chapter 10: “things which are private and proper to the stage,” etc. And Cicero says in Book III *On Ends*: “In speaking, nevertheless, they employ words that are, as it were, private and their own.”

**Duty.** What duty, however, can belong to malice except among those doctrines of which Tully says near the beginning of Book I *On Duties*: “There are certain schools which, by establishing ends of good and evil, throw every duty into confusion”? Here, however, our Author appears instead to have followed the etymology of the word, concerning which St. Ambrose says in Book I *On Duties*, Chapter 8: “We think that *officium* (‘duty’) is so called from *efficiendo* (‘performing’), as though it were *efficium*,” etc. In this sense even malice has its own “duty,” though improperly and only according to the name, being empty of the reality. Yet, as the words immediately following suggest, I rather think it should be understood thus: “It even expends upon goodness—or upon good works and benefits conferred upon another—the duty proper toward malice, namely that we should repent of evil.” [Col. 1213D]

**May be neglected.** See what was said at the end of Chapter I.

# Notes on Chapter III

**Spirit.** The soul, or its intellect.

[Col. 1214A] **Regard.** As though by rays emitted into it from God, the source of all Light.

**Precepts**, namely, precepts to be known.

**Must be reckoned.** Must be numbered among sins.

**May be a human being.** As consisting of body and soul.

**The two make one.** For the soul joined to the body makes one human being.

**Materials.** Or constituent parts, which are, as it were, the matter of the whole.

**A thing of God.** As he said above in Chapter I: “Reason is a thing of God.”

**Fashioned.** For God formed man—that is, his body—from the clay of the earth. Gen. 2:7.

**By his breath.** “And he breathed into his face the breath of life, and MAN became a living soul.” Ibid.

**Offends.** Tertullian did not mean by this that all sins are equal, as the Stoics thought. He meant only that because some sins are carnal and others spiritual—or some are committed through the body, while others subsist in the soul and its will alone—the greater gravity [Col. 1214B] of one sin over another must not be measured from this fact.

**Into judgment.** Namely, the fire of eternity; for “judgment” is here opposed to life—that is, eternal life. See what we said in the book *On Baptism*, Chapter X, note “Into judgment.”

**For that reason.**

**To expend.** Thus the body too must necessarily participate in penance, namely through perceptible and external acts and bodily afflictions, while the soul participates through inward sorrow, etc.

**Belongs to both.** For just as actions are said to belong to subjects, so neither the body alone nor the soul alone sins, but the Human Being, who is composed of body and soul and constitutes one subject or person. To this person actions, both good and evil, are imputed.

**Is performed.** Namely, by some external and perceptible work.

**Is thought.** And is retained merely within the mind.

**Is capable of being perceived.** “To be perceived” is not used here solely of the sense of touch, but also of perception by the other senses. [Col. 1214C] Furthermore, *contingi habet* (“has to be perceived”) in the sense of “can be perceived” is a Hellenism; for the Greeks customarily say, for example, οὐδεὶς εἰπεῖν ἔχει (*oudeis eipein echei*), “no one can speak.”

**Nor is apprehended.** That is, it is reached by no sense.

**Sins of the will.** Merely internal sins, namely.

**Mediocrity.** Human limitation, which, as though placed at the boundaries, cannot penetrate farther or reach the very heart of the matter. Human limitation, therefore, is such that it perceives only external acts—and not even these always or with sufficient certainty—while it is unable to look upon internal acts.

**Is not competent.** For even the Praetor himself does not judge internal things.

**Under God.** Namely, with no regard for God.

**Whence**, that is, through which thing and in whatever manner.

**May decide unto judgment.** For he first decides that one is to go to judgment before, in the judgment itself, he decides and pronounces sentence. Or perhaps he understands “judgment” here as condemnation itself and the punishment of eternal fire, as an example occurred above. Or, finally, this agrees with what appears at the beginning of the following Chapter: [Col. 1214D] “He appointed punishment THROUGH JUDGMENT.”

**Is the origin.** For otherwise it cannot be considered ours or imputed to us.

**When therefore.** Supply: “the will.”

**Is released.** This can be understood of the will, which is released or absolved from guilt; or of guilt itself, which is released, that is, removed. Thus we are also said “to release promises” when we remove and cancel their obligation by fulfilling them.

**Intercepts.** So that it cannot issue forth into the work itself and be consummated. For, as Festus says, a person *perpetrates* when he carries out and completes the work itself. The word is used especially of evil deeds, although we sometimes find it said even of good ones.

**It had done its own part.** Insofar as it lay within the will itself, efficaciously willing something, it had already accomplished it. Hence, as our Theologians commonly say, the external act does not superinduce a new species of sin upon the internal act; rather, [Col. 1215A] the former is the mere completion of the latter.
