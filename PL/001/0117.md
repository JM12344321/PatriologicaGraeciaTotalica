Since, therefore, Socrates, by denying the gods of the pagans, had aroused the hatred of the Athenians [Col. 1111D] against himself, Tertullian immediately added: “Clearly, of old”—that is, “always”—“truth has been hated.” Yet someone may, not wholly without reason, call into question whether these words, “that is, always,” were subsequently inserted into this text. For why, he will ask, would Tertullian have explained the adverb *olim* by these words? Could he not have said more simply: “Truth is always hated”? Moreover, the adverb *olim* is used by Virgil to signify any time whatever, where he sings: “Now for you, ever exceedingly strong” (Virgil, *Georgics*, bk. II, v. 100; and *Aeneid*, bk. VIII, v. 391); that is, Servius says, “at all times.” Later the same poet writes: “No otherwise than thunder is wont,” that is, as Servius again observes: “As it is accustomed to occur. For it is not an adverb of time” (Servius, [Col. 1112A] p. 511). But could Tertullian not have said that truth was hated by men of old—namely, in the time of Socrates, of whom he was speaking—and in his own time also, and thus always? Whatever judgment you may reach, Tertullian concludes from this that hatred of truth is so great that the Athenians hated it in Socrates when he overthrew their fictitious gods. We discussed his condemnation and death above, in the earlier dissertations cited there (above, ch. 25, art. 3).

Tertullian joins two other philosophers to Socrates (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 14, p. 355), one Greek, Diogenes, and the other Roman, Varro. But many may perhaps wonder why, in his testimony against the pagans, both here and in the first book *To the Nations*, he argues as follows: “Diogenes,” he says, “makes some jest or other at Hercules”; [Col. 1112B] in the other book, “he jested” (bk. I, *To the Nations*, ch. 10, p. 576). For, they will object, is this argument of such importance that it deserved to be repeated? If Tertullian did not know what jest Diogenes made at Hercules, what could he infer from it against the false gods of the pagans? And who is this Diogenes? Since that is difficult to say, some suspect that *Diagoras* should be read. He, because he lacked a fire on which to cook his dinner, is said to have asked a statue of Hercules to cook it for him as his thirteenth labor. But absolutely all the manuscripts oppose this emendation. Yielding, therefore, to their authority, we are readily persuaded that Tertullian calls him Diogenes the Cynic, just as in the same passage he calls Varro a Cynic. For who does not know how greatly Hercules is mocked by this very Cynic Diogenes in Lucian’s *Dialogues* [Col. 1112C] (Lucian, *Dialogues of the Dead*, 56, p. 137; and *Old Authors*, p. 190)? There is, moreover, that epigram of Ausonius in which Diogenes is said, not without humor, to have made Hercules the founder of his school:

> I am the first founder of the Cynic school. What reasoning is this?  
> Alcides is said to be much earlier.  
> Once, with Alcides as my teacher, I was second;  
> Now I am the foremost Cynic, and he a god.

(Ausonius, *Epigram* 26.)

Is that not the “some jest or other” by which Tertullian attests that the divinity of Hercules was mocked by Diogenes? If, however, you show that he was referring to some other mockery recorded by Lucian or another author, we shall not oppose you. Moreover, although he called it “some jest or other,” since it was nevertheless not unknown [Col. 1112D] to the pagans, he properly drew an argument from it against their gods (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 24, art. 4).

Someone may perhaps doubt whether the other philosopher, whom he calls “Varro the Cynic,” is the man whose learning the pagans, as we have seen, heaped with such great praise, and who had composed so many books on religions. For did he too dare to jest about the gods? Why not? Do we not read in our author’s books *To the Nations*: “Varro, a Diogenes of the Roman pen, introduces three hundred Joves, or Jupiters” (our manuscripts have *Jupiteros*), “without heads” (Tertullian, bk. I, *To the Nations*, ch. 10, p. 576)? But how could he introduce so many Joves without ridicule? Did not Minucius Felix, undoubtedly following our Tertullian, say that there are “as many monstrous forms of Jove [Col. 1113A] as there are names”? See what we have observed elsewhere on these words (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 15, art. 2). Some, moreover, think that Varro said these things in his *Menippean Satires*; why not in his other books on the religion of the pagans, of which there were so many? But wherever he said them, the false divinity both of Jove and of the others is thereby most clearly demonstrated. This was indeed so thoroughly accepted by those in whom some spark of uprightness and fairness still remained that, as we saw above (above, ch. 18, art. 9), they utterly rejected Father Liber, Serapis, and Harpocrates together with his Cynocephalus. Yet, after setting aside all the other false divinities, they assuredly ought to have worshiped the one almighty God.

# CHAPTER XXVII

## Other arguments of Tertullian by which he demonstrates that the gods of the pagans are false and nonexistent are examined

[Col. 1113B]

### ARTICLE I

#### How impious was the license of provinces and cities, and especially of the Egyptians, to adopt particular gods for themselves; how improbable are the things reported by Tertullian concerning the patriarch Joseph; whether the Egyptians called and worshiped him as a god under the name Serapis; and by what warrant Tertullian calls Astarte the god of Syria, Dusares that of Arabia, Belenus that of the Norici, Caelestis that of Africa, and their own petty kings the gods of Mauretania

Another very evident proof of false religion is surely the license granted to cities and peoples [Col. 1113C] to adopt and worship any god whatever, often unknown to the others. But, our Tertullian says, many provinces and cities of the pagans worshiped gods adopted at whim, not from a knowledge of the truth, whether gods rejected by others or unknown to them (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 417; and bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8). There he particularly reproaches the Egyptians, who, as we have shown in our other dissertations (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 34, art. 2, and the passages cited there), in a superstition as impious as it was absurd, not only consecrated sheep and other beasts, but even condemned to death those who killed any such god. Having received this freedom to adopt them, the Egyptians, Tertullian says, so abused it that they made the patriarch Joseph a god under the name Serapis (Tertullian, [Col. 1113D] passage cited). But there he confuses many things, and indeed to such a degree that it seems astonishing how a man exceptionally well versed in the sacred Scriptures should offer us several assertions plainly opposed to them.

First, Joseph, he says (Tertullian, bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8), was younger than his other brothers—he ought to have excepted Benjamin—and, after being sold in Egypt, served in the household of Pharaoh, king of the Egyptians. But in the book of Genesis (Gen. 37:18, 36; 39:1 ff.) we read that he was sold to the Midianites, and by them to Potiphar, Pharaoh’s eunuch and commander of the soldiers, whom he actually served until he was cast into prison. Unless, therefore, you prove that this Potiphar, whom Josephus calls πετεφρὴς (Petephres) (Josephus, bk. II, *Jewish Antiquities*, ch. 3, [Col. 1114A] p. 39 ff.), belonged to Pharaoh’s royal household, this opinion of Tertullian appears false. Nor is what he adds any truer: that Joseph was desired by the less than chaste queen and was for that reason thrust into prison at the king’s command. For it was not the queen, but Potiphar’s wife, as is related in the same sacred book, who solicited him to adultery; and it was the deceived Potiphar who cast him into bonds. Tertullian further asserts that Joseph received in marriage the daughter of King Pharaoh, whose dreams he had explained. But sacred Scripture (Gen. 41:50) and Josephus the historian (Josephus, passage cited) give us the more certain information that the patriarch Joseph married Asenath, daughter of Potiphera, priest of Heliopolis.

Finally, some judge the following addition by our author to conflict with the truth handed down in those same books of Genesis and [Col. 1114B] Josephus: “Joseph taught the ways of release from prison; he disclosed the dream to the king” (Tertullian, bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8). For he had been released from prison when he interpreted the king’s dreams. Yet could Tertullian not have indicated by these words the dreams of Pharaoh’s two eunuchs, which Joseph actually explained in prison and which afterward furnished the occasion for his interpretation of Pharaoh’s own dreams? Moreover, the things contrary to sacred Scripture that Tertullian reports were written only in the book *To the Nations* and were wholly omitted from the *Apologeticum*. Who would dare determine the reason for this omission—whether it was because he recognized that they were not altogether true? Besides, he had perhaps drawn them from corrupt sources, unless you can show that copyists, as so often elsewhere, erred in transcribing the proper names in this [Col. 1114C] book.

Vossius therefore considers more probable (*On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, bk. I, ch. 29) what Tertullian further relates: that after Joseph supplied them with grain in a time of famine, the Egyptians ordered a bull to be reared by the priests as a memorial and symbol of so great a benefit; this bull they honored first with civil and then with divine worship. It is, indeed, agreed that the same Egyptians made the bull into the god Apis, and thence Serapis, concerning whom see Rufinus (Rufinus, *Ecclesiastical History*, bk. II, ch. 23) and our discussion elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1090). A very celebrated statue of this Serapis was also fashioned at Alexandria, concerning which the same Rufinus says: “A grain measure was placed upon his head, and he is said to have been fashioned in honor of our Joseph, because of the measurement of the grain [Col. 1114D] with which he relieved the Egyptians in the time of famine.” The Suda supports Rufinus (Suda, s.v. Σαράπις (Sarapis)), although neither could deny that others held different opinions.

From the Egyptians our author passes to other peoples, who with no less license had adopted for themselves particular gods unknown to others, and especially to the Romans: “For how many are there,” he says, “who know by sight or hearing which god belongs to each province and city—such as Astarte of Syria?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 418; our manuscripts have *Adstartes*); in the book *To the Nations*, “Atagartis of the Syrians” (bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8). This reading can be confirmed by these words of Pliny: “But for the Syrians, Magog. There is the marvelous Atagartis, called Derceto by the Greeks” (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. V, ch. 23, [Col. 1115A] p. 577). Strabo, moreover, says of Hierapolis: “ἐν ᾗ τιμῶσι τὴν Συρίαν θεὸν τὴν Ἀταργατίν, in which they worship Atargatis, the Syrian goddess” (Strabo, *Geography*, bk. XVI, p. 748). But later he wrote: “Ἀταργατὴν δὲ τὴν Ἀθάραν, Δερκέτω δ` αὐτὴν Κτησίας καλεῖ, they called Atargatis Athara, whom Ctesias called Derceto” (ibid., p. 785). By others, however, she was called Astarte, as by Minucius Felix, who had read this name in our Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, as he had many other things. See Vossius (*On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, bk. I, ch. 28) and, if you please, what we have annotated on that passage of Minucius (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 14, art. 1).

Just as this was the goddess of Syria, so, Tertullian says, Dusares was that of Arabia (Tertullian, passage cited); but in the second book *To the Nations* the name is given differently, *Obodanedussiren*. They think that this [Col. 1115B] name was corrupted by an unskilled copyist who made one name out of the two, Obodas and Dusares. They attempt to confirm their opinion by the authority of Eusebius, by whom, they say, these Arabian gods were mentioned, although they indicate neither his words nor the book. Vossius has more about both those gods and is readily accessible to anyone (Vossius, *On the Origin and Progress of Idolatry*, bk. II, ch. 8, p. 178). Belenus was also the native god of the Norici, our author says (same author, ibid., ch. 24, p. 419); but these names were also corrupted in our manuscripts as *Nori Cibelenus*, and in some printed editions as *Tibelenus*. Vossius suspects that he is the god whom they called Tybelinus, whom the ancient Saxons called Tebilenus, and the Slavs Zerneboch. But is it not far more probable that Tertullian [Col. 1115C] is speaking of the god Belenus, whom the people of Aquileia, as Julius Capitolinus (Capitolinus, *Life of Maximinus*, p. 361, col. 2) and Herodian (Herodian, *History*, bk. II, p. 612) have recorded, held in the highest honor and believed to be the sun? Gruter, moreover, presents various inscriptions dedicated “To the God Belenus,” set up by Augustus Caesar (Gruter, vol. I, *Inscriptions*, pp. 26–27). Indeed, he is proclaimed by Ausonius as the god of the people of Bordeaux in more than one poem (Ausonius, *On the Professors of Bordeaux*, poem 4, §145; and poem 10, §154). Belenus was thus worshiped not only by the Norici, peoples of Germany, but also by others. Tertullian perhaps named them specifically because he knew that they honored this god above the rest (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 420). Nevertheless, if you can show that he meant some more obscure god of the Norici or of another nation, we shall readily agree with you.

Nor did he omit his native land: “Caelestis of Africa”; and in the book *To the Nations*, “Caelestis of the Africans” (bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8), concerning whom we discussed above (above, ch. 18, art. 6). [Col. 1115D] Moreover, Mauretania was a region of the same Africa, concerning which he immediately adds: “Mauretania, its own petty kings.” But Minucius Felix and Lactantius name Juba, whom the inhabitants of this province honored specifically with divine worship. Consult, if you wish, what was discussed in our dissertations on the books of both authors (*Dissertation [Col. 1116A] on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 16, art. 2; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 30, art. 2).

### ARTICLE II

#### On other gods unknown to the Romans who were worshiped in the provinces and cities of Italy, such as Delventinus of the people of Casinum or Crustumerium, Visidianus of the people of Narnia, Ancaria or Ancharia of the people of Asculum, Nursia or Nortia of the people of Volsinii, Valentia of the people of Ocriculum, and Juno, who among the Faliscans received her surname in honor of Father Curis

Tertullian continues his argument and passes from provinces more distant from Rome to Italy: “Throughout Italy itself,” he says, “gods are also registered by municipal consecration” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, [Col. 1116B] p. 420), though the Romans did not worship them. So great, indeed, was the blindness and license of the inhabitants of each city that they not only possessed gods unknown to others, but gods whose very names, as he says, “do not differ in dignity from human surnames” (same author, bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 2). But just as they bore the name of a human being, so it is manifest that they lived a human life and therefore were not gods. He enumerates several of them (ibid.); and so that no opportunity might exist for anyone to detract from his credibility, he cites Varro as a witness, undoubtedly in those books which, according to Augustine (*City of God*, bk. VI, ch. 3, p. 148), he published *On Divine Matters*.

At the head of this troop of obscure gods is placed “Delventinus of the *Casianienses*” (Tertullian, passage cited), or, as our older handwritten manuscripts and others have it, “of the *Casianenses*”; the older printed editions have “of the Crustuminians,” [Col. 1116C] and certain later editions, *Belventinus*. Varro says that Casinum, a town of Campania, was first held by the Samnites, who sprang from the Sabines, and afterward by the Romans (Varro, *On the Latin Language*, bk. VI, p. 77). Pliny (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. III, ch. 4, p. 229) and Silius (Silius Italicus, bk. XII, v. 525) mention that town, and its name occurs in various inscriptions published by Gruter (Gruter, pp. 136, 151). If, however, someone should prefer to read “of the Crustuminians” there, he may support his opinion with these verses of Virgil:

> . . . Nor does the same shoot  
> Belong to Crustumian and Syrian pears.

(Virgil, *Georgics*, bk. II, v. 88.)

For Servius observes on this passage: “Crustumian pears are red on one side, taking their name from the town Crustumium.” [Col. 1116D] Pliny describes the territory of Crustumerium and its location in these words: “Within thirteen miles of the city, separating the territory of Veii from that of Crustumerium, then the territory of Fidenae, and the Latin territory from the Vatican” (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. III, ch. 5, p. 323). But shortly before this he had mentioned another Crustumerium (ibid., p. 322), whose location is now unknown. If we must yield to the authority of the better and older manuscripts, Tertullian meant the town of the Casinians (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 420; and bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8), which they believe to be what is now Monte Cassino.

Next he says: “Visidianus of the Narnians.” Pliny, when enumerating the towns and peoples of Umbria, says: “The Narnians, whose town was formerly called Nequinum” [Col. 1117A] (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. III, ch. 14, p. 365). And indeed we read in Livy: “The other consul, Apuleius, laid siege to the town of Nequinum in Umbria. The place was difficult of access and precipitous on one side, where Narnia now stands” (Livy, bk. X, §9). It took its name from the river Nar, which flows past it, as you will understand from these words of Strabo: “Νάρνα δι` ἧς ῥεῖ ὁ Νάρ ποταμὸς, Narnia, through which the river Nar flows” (Strabo, *Geography*, bk. V, p. 227). The Narnians are also commemorated in various inscriptions in Gruter (Gruter, p. 174, inscr. 4; p. 388, inscr. 2; p. 460, inscr. 4).

Furthermore, “Ancharia of the people of Asculum”; in the book *To the Nations* and our manuscript copies: *Ancharia* (Tertullian, passage cited). Pliny, however, says: “The colony Asculum, the most renowned city of Picenum” (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. III, [Col. 1117B] ch. 13, p. 362), in whose name many inscriptions survive in Gruter (Gruter, p. 465, inscr. 1 and 10; p. 371, inscr. 1; p. 427, inscr. 1). Strabo, however: “Ἀσκλον τὸ πικηνὸν (Asculum of Picenum).” But there is another Asculum, a town of Apulia, very famous for the defeat of Pyrrhus. Our author undoubtedly speaks of one of the two, unless you prove that the same goddess was worshiped in both. Someone may perhaps think that it can be inferred from these verses of Lucan about Lentulus that Tertullian is speaking of the former:

> . . . Lentulus is driven  
> From the Asculan citadel.

(Lucan, *Pharsalia*, bk. II, v. 150.)

For some observe that *Esculea* and *Asculea* were used interchangeably; why not also *Aesculea*, whence the Aesculani of whom we are now speaking?

[Col. 1117C] Turnebus, moreover (Turnebus, *Adversaria*, bk. XVII, ch. 24), suspects that Ancharia is the goddess from whose madness men possessed by frenzy were called *ancarii*. He attempts to establish this by two verses of Lucilius cited by Nonius (Nonius, s.v. *Quiritare*):

> This same man will roar from among our ranks and raise a howl,  
> Rushing about like an *ancarius* and loudly shrieking.

In our edition of these verses we indeed read: “This man, I say . . . like an arena-fighter.” But this is undoubtedly corrupt and contrary to the rules of meter. Nevertheless, this is a mere conjecture of Turnebus and still requires some other proof. In the book *To the Nations* the name *Anchariam* appears, followed by *et quam praeverint*. Considering this corrupt, editors wished to substitute *et quam praeverim*; but they gave no reason for the emendation, which seems no better than the adulterated text. We therefore prefer to await the aid of another manuscript rather than indulge in various conjectures.

It will thus be easier to prove what Tertullian adds: “Nursia of the people of Volsinii” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 421); but in the book *To the Nations* (bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8, p. 71) and in our unpublished manuscripts, *Norcia* and *Nortia*, or, as Lipsius notes on the passage of Tacitus soon to be cited, *Neurscia*. Livy more than once mentions the people of Volsinii (Livy, bk. V, §31; bk. IX, §21), who are also so called in an inscription published by Gruter (Gruter, inscr. 1, p. 385). But Tertullian himself elsewhere, as we [Col. 1118A] observed (above, ch. 23, art. 3), calls them *Volsinii*, as do Propertius (Propertius, bk. IV, elegy 2, v. 2) and Pliny (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. II, ch. 52, p. 287; bk. III, ch. 4, p. 322), who testify that this was a very wealthy city of the Etruscans, now under the dominion of the supreme pontiffs.

That Nortia was their goddess we learn from these words of Livy already cited: “Cincius, a careful authority on monuments of this kind, affirms that at Volsinii nails, marking the number of years, can be seen driven into the temple of Nortia, an Etruscan goddess” (Livy, bk. VII, §3). Juvenal too sang the following concerning her:

> . . . The same people, if Nortia [Note: [Col. 1118D] In some editions, *Nursia*.] had favored the Etruscan,  
> If the aged prince, feeling secure, had been overwhelmed,  
> Would at this very hour proclaim Sejanus  
> [Col. 1118B] Augustus.

(Juvenal, *Satires*, X, v. 70 ff.)

Furthermore, “Valentia of the people of Ocriculum,” Tertullian says (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 421). Ocriculum, or Ocriculi—Strabo’s Ὀκρίκλου (Ocriculum) (Strabo, *Geography*, bk. V, p. 227)—is a city on the borders of Umbria, not far from Rome, and is now called Otricoli. It is mentioned by Pliny (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. III, ch. 4, p. 323; ch. 14, p. 365), Livy (Livy, bk. IX, §41), and others, especially geographers. That Valentia actually was the goddess of the people of Ocriculum you will recognize from this ancient inscription in Gruter (Gruter, p. 2, inscr. 12):

> FOR THE SAFETY OF THE DEPARTURE AND RETURN OF OUR MOST SACRED LORD . . . TI. AUG., A SHRINE OF THE ASSEMBLY OF THE GODS AND GODDESSES, AUG. N., FOLLOWING A VISION OF THE GODDESS VALENTIA, S. P. E. C. [The unresolved source abbreviations are retained.]

[Col. 1118C]

What more? “Nortia of the people of Sutrium,” our author says (Tertullian, passage cited). But Sutrium, Livy says, “a city allied with the Romans, was like the gateway of Etruria” (Livy, bk. IX, §32; bk. VI, §3). He had already mentioned it earlier, and it also occurs in various inscriptions presented by Gruter (Gruter, pp. 254, 302, 305, 481). It lies twenty-four miles from the city of Rome, as Marsus noted on these words of Silius Italicus: “the roofs of Sutrium” (Silius Italicus, bk. VIII, v. 408). See Erasmus on the proverb “As though they were going to Sutrium” (Erasmus, *Adages*, chiliad 4, century 4, adage 52, p. 841). The goddess of this city’s inhabitants, moreover, was, as Tertullian says, Nortia. But previously, as we have seen, he had said “Nortia of the people of Volsinii.” Was [Col. 1118D] the same goddess, then, goddess of both peoples? Is he not enumerating particular gods proper to each city? Yet it was undoubtedly for this reason that, as we have noted, some wished the former name to be written a little differently. Against this emendation, however, stand not only all our manuscripts, but also the passage of Livy cited by us. But because Volsinii and Sutrium were cities of Etruria, and this goddess is called a goddess of Etruria by Livy, she could have been the goddess of both cities. If anyone should show that there were two goddesses called by different names, we shall willingly subscribe to his opinion.

Tertullian closes the troop of these municipal and local gods [Col. 1119A] with these words: “Among the Faliscans, in honor of Father Curis, Juno also received her surname” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24, p. 421; and bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8). Falisca, moreover, was a city of Etruria situated between Mount Ciminus and the Tiber. The colony of the Faliscans is mentioned in Pliny’s books (Pliny, *Natural History*, bk. III, ch. 4, p. 320) and in Gruter’s inscriptions (Gruter, p. 288, inscr. 1), but more frequently in the histories of Titus Livy (Livy, bk. V, §§17, 18, 26; bk. VII, §17, etc.) and in these verses of Virgil:

> These hold the Fescennine ranks and the Aequian Faliscans;  
> These hold Soracte’s heights and the Flavinian fields,  
> Lake Ciminus with its mountain, and the groves of Capena.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, bk. VII, vv. 695 ff.)

You will find similar things celebrated by Silius Italicus (Silius Italicus, [Col. 1119B] bk. VIII, vv. 406 ff.). Juno, moreover, as no one fails to know, had many surnames, of which one, according to Festus, was *Curis*. If anyone asks why she was so called, let him hear Servius: “Juno has many names: she is *Curetis*, because she uses a chariot and spear” (Servius, on *Aeneid*, bk. I, v. 6). If he wishes, let him also hear Festus Pompeius: “*Curis* is the Sabine word for spear. . . . They called Juno *Curitis* because they believed her to carry a spear” (Festus, s.v. *Curis*). But just as these writers favor our Tertullian when he asserts that Juno was surnamed *Curris* or *Curetis*, so they oppose him when they openly state that this surname was not given to her in honor of Father Curis, but was derived from the spear, which the Sabines called *curis*, or from the chariot, as Servius implies. Servius, however, is criticized by some who consider the former [Col. 1119C] etymology of this name the truer.

What then? Can it be answered that Juno derived this surname from the spear, but that the Faliscans afterward conferred it upon her in honor of Father Curis? But who, you will ask, is that Father Curis? Ovid teaches us that Romulus was so called when he sings:

> The next day is vacant, but the third is named for Quirinus;  
> He who holds this name was Romulus before:  
> Whether because a spear was called *curis* by the ancient Sabines,  
> And the warlike god rose from his weapon to the stars;  
> Or because the Quirites bestowed the name upon their king;  
> Or because he had joined Cures to the Romans.

(Ovid, *Fasti*, bk. II, §13.)

But not only Romulus: the name had previously been conferred upon Janus, as Macrobius attested in these words [Col. 1119D]: “In sacred rites we invoke Janus Junonius, Janus Quirinus . . . ‘Father,’ as though father of the gods; ‘Junonius,’ as though presiding not only over the entrance of January but over that of every month. All the Kalends are under Juno’s dominion. . . . ‘Quirinus,’ as though parent of wars, from the spear that the Sabines call *curis*” (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, bk. I, ch. 9). Janus thus had various names, three of which bear upon our purpose. The first is *Father*; the second, *Quirinus*, derived from *curis*, or “spear”; and the third, *Junonius*, as though he were the beginning and entrance both of January and of all the months. Can this not confirm Tertullian’s statement that Juno received her surname *Curis*, or *Curitis*, from Father Curis, who was also called Junonius? [Col. 1120A] Let others, however, judge this conjecture.

Moreover, according to the variant readings of our manuscripts, Tertullian’s words can bear two meanings. For if you read there, as in our handwritten copies and Rigault’s edition, “Among the Faliscans, in honor” (MSS: “in the honor”) “of Father Curis, Juno also received her name,” the meaning will be: “The goddess of the Faliscans is Juno, who obtained her designation *Curitis* in honor of Father Curis.” But if we read with the other editions, “Among the Faliscans, Father Curis is held in honor, from whom Juno received her surname,” these words will signify that divine honors were rendered by the Faliscans to Father Curis, from whom Juno was surnamed. But if he is speaking of the honor of Father Curis, why, I ask, is Juno—who has plainly nothing to do [Col. 1120B] with that matter—mentioned there? Do not those approach Tertullian’s meaning more closely who prove that he must be understood as referring to Juno Curitis, whom the Faliscans worshiped as the particular goddess of their city? You, however, weigh the matter and decide which reading and interpretation is the truer.

Someone may perhaps confront us and object that Tertullian here suffered a human lapse, since he so expressly affirms that the Romans rendered no divine honor to these gods of the provinces and cities whom he has named thus far (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24). For had not the Capitol previously been called by him “the senate-house of demons”? Are these not also his words in another book: “The amphitheater is consecrated by more numerous and harsher names than” (*quam* is absent from the older printed editions) “the Capitol is the senate-house of all the gods” (same author, *On the Spectacles*, ch. 12)? Did not Rome, as Leo I says, serve the errors of all nations and adopt their gods (Leo, sermon on the feast of Saints Peter and Paul)? The Romans therefore worshiped the gods of every nation. Yet although the matter is so, they worshiped all of them only collectively, not individually by name. For they could not even enumerate them, much less honor and invoke them! Tertullian, however, is discussing local and particular gods whom the inhabitants of cities and provinces were accustomed to worship as their own fellow citizens and to invoke by their own names.

What wonder, then, if most of them are now unknown to everyone, such as Delventinus and Visidianus, named in [Col. 1120D] the *Apologeticum*, and Numentinus or Numertinus of the Athenians—or rather of the Aternians—in the book *To the Nations* (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 24; and bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 8)? For knowledge of these and other local gods, whose number was infinite, was taken from us long ago. Yet our author’s argument is no less forceful on that account. From what has thus far been said, he most justly concludes that the pagans were exceedingly unjust: although they had the power to worship, according to their own pleasure and whim, any false god unknown to other nations, they did not permit Christians to adore the true God.

# CHAPTER XXVIII

## With what great folly the Romans boasted that by their piety toward the gods they had merited dominion over the whole world

[Col. 1121A]

### ARTICLE I

#### How false it is that the Romans merited dominion over the whole world by their piety toward the gods Sterculus, Mutinus, Larentia, Cybele, Jupiter, Juno, and others, who ought to have defended their own native lands rather than the Romans, and who were all subject to fate

We have seen the false religion of the pagans, who worshiped wholly fictitious gods, so overthrown by Tertullian with so many arguments of such great weight that no one, if he is of sound mind, seems capable of resisting them. But [Col. 1121B] because such is the obstinacy of most men in error that they never wish to confess themselves defeated, the pagans still rose up in opposition and objected to us, as Tertullian says, that “the Romans, in proportion to the merit of their most scrupulous religious observance, were raised so high that they took possession of the world” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 25, p. 422; and bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 17); that is, by their great piety and religious devotion toward their gods they merited and obtained dominion over the whole terrestrial globe. “These gods therefore exist,” they said, “and ought to be honored by all men.” This entire argument, which we have explained more fully elsewhere (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 16, arts. 2–3), our Tertullian not unjustly considers entirely frivolous and devoid of weight. Nevertheless, so as not to provide the pagans even the slightest occasion for muttering, he refutes and demolishes this argument, such as it is, with various answers.

For the gods of the Romans, he says, were either native and peculiar to them or foreign and adopted. Can it be said that the boundaries of the Roman Empire were extended by the former? “To be sure, this recompense”—these are the words of Tertullian mocking this opinion—“was entered to the Romans’ credit by the gods. Sterculus, Mutinus, and Larentina advanced the empire” (Tertullian, passage cited). In the older printed books, *si* is read in place of “to be sure,” but with entirely the same meaning, though in a less elegant expression. He names the vilest and plainly most infamous of the Roman gods there, so that he might sting the pagans, who most foolishly vaunted the power of their gods, with the sharper barbs of irony. Sterculus, as Tertullian teaches elsewhere, [Col. 1121D] was diligent in assisting the fields with manure (same author, bk. II, *To the Nations*, ch. 9). But we have previously discussed him, as well as the most shameful Mutinus and Larentina, whom Tertullian calls “a public prostitute,” both above and in our earlier dissertations (above, ch. 25, art. 3; *Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 17, art. 2; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 32, arts. 1–2). No one in possession of his faculties will ever dare seriously to affirm that the boundaries of the Roman Empire were extended by such infamous native gods. Nor were they extended by Rome’s other domestic and particular gods. For the power, excellence, and holiness of the true God, just like his nature, cannot exist in diverse forms in a plurality of beings. Yet no one has ever said that the aforementioned infamous gods, or the other domestic gods of the Romans, possessed the same nature and omnipotence, excellence and holiness, as [Col. 1122A] almighty God. They are therefore not gods and could not have brought any increase whatever to the Roman Empire.

It remains, then, that the greatness of the Roman Empire increased through the aid and power of foreign gods. Yet this opinion is no less wholly devoid of every appearance of probability than the preceding one. For, besides the argument just advanced: “I would not suppose,” as Tertullian rightly says, “that foreign gods wished to favor an alien nation more and to surrender to men from across the sea the native soil in which they were born, raised, ennobled, and buried” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 23); that is, that they favored their own native and nurturing land less than the Romans, who came from overseas and were foreigners. By what vice of an ungrateful disposition could they have delivered their native soil to be laid waste by the Romans [Col. 1122B] and subjected to their rule?
