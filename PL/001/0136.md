1. Most recently, my dearest fellow servant in the Lord, I discussed as well as I could what course a holy woman [Col. 1288D] ought to follow when, by whatever circumstance, she has been deprived of marriage.

> **Note [Col. 1287D]: “A holy woman.”** Rhenanus very properly observed [Col. 1288D] that by “holy women” Christians are meant here; for you will find this word used several times [Col. 1289C] in that sense by this author. The earliest Christians were also called “saints,” in accordance with the Apostle’s words in 1 Cor. 7: “Otherwise your children would be unclean, but as it is they are holy.” Saint Luke has the same usage (Acts 9:32): “As Peter passed through all the regions, he came down to the saints who lived at Lydda”; and blessed Paul again, in 2 Thessalonians, chapter 1: “To all who are in Rome, called to be saints.” In former times bishops too were indiscriminately called “Most Holy”; but from antiquity the Supreme Pontiff alone has retained this title for himself. LE PR.

Now let us turn to secondary counsels, out of regard for human weakness, since we are warned by the examples of certain women who, when divorce or the death of a husband offered them an opportunity for continence, not only cast away the opportunity for so great a good, but even in marrying again refused to remember the rule that they should marry preferably “in the Lord” (1 Cor. 7:39).

> **Note [Col. 1289C]: “Divorce.”** Thus Christian wives too, when their marriage had been dissolved by divorce, sometimes married another man without waiting for the former husband’s death. Consequently, they were not wives of one husband. Nor were those men husbands of one wife who, after repudiating one wife, married another. In the Apostle’s judgment, persons of this kind were considered less qualified for ecclesiastical orders or honors, since their example was not sufficiently blameless or temperate. For although such marriages were not forbidden by the laws, they were nevertheless not free from reproach; nor did a man seem likely to govern the Church successfully when his own marriage had turned out so unsuccessfully, or to preserve faithfully his goodwill toward the Christian people when he had by no means preserved it toward his wife. These are the words of Chrysostom in his interpretation of Paul’s Epistle to Titus: Ὁ γὰρ πρὸς τὴν ἀπελθοῦσαν μηδεμίαν φυλάξας εὔνοιαν, πῶς ἂν οὗτος γένοιτο προστάτης καλός; τίνα δὲ οὐκ ἂν ὑποσταίη κατηγορίαν; Ἴστε γὰρ ἅπαντες, ἴστε ὅτι εἰ μὴ κεκώλυται παρὰ τῶν νόμων τὸ δευτέροις ὁμιλεῖν γάμοις, ἀλλ’ ὅμως πολλὰς ἔχει τὸ πρᾶγμα κατηγορίας: “For how could a man who preserved no goodwill toward the wife who departed become a good leader? And what accusation would he not incur? For you all know—yes, you know—that even if association in a second marriage is not prohibited by the laws, the matter nevertheless remains open to many accusations.” LE PR.

> **Note [Col. 1289D]: “But even in marrying again they refused to remember the rule.”** He does not accuse them of violating the rule because they had married another while the husband who had dismissed them was still alive, but because they had married a pagan and had taken their marriages outside the Church. LE PR.

And so my mind is troubled lest I, who recently exhorted you to perseverance in single marriage and widowhood, should now, by mentioning the ready availability of marriage, cause you to slip down from higher things.

> **Note [Col. 1290C]: “Lest now, by mentioning marriage, I remove your safeguard against a downward slope.”** In the preceding book he had advised his wife to abstain from a second marriage. Now, when about to discuss that same marriage—should necessity for it arise—as something to be contracted only with a Christian, he says that he fears the indulgence of this secondary exhortation may make it seem that he has abandoned the austerity of the first, or greatly relaxed it, although he still earnestly desires it to be observed. “And so my mind is troubled,” he says, “lest I, who recently exhorted you to perseverance in single marriage and widowhood, should now, by mentioning marriage, cause you to slip down from higher things.” Thus the editions of Rhenanus and Pamelius. But Agobard’s manuscript gives the final clause in words better suited to Tertullian’s meaning: “lest I remove your safeguard against a downward slope”; that is, lest the removal of caution become, as it were, a downward slope for you. [Col. 1290D] “I fear,” he says, “lest this mention of second marriage amount to removal of the prohibition and thereby make the road to second marriage slope downward.” By *proclivium* he means the danger of slipping, which is certainly more immediate on a slope. A downward slope, or *proclivium*, appears to be created by the removal of caution whenever the barriers are taken away or the handles by which one might have held fast and taken care are cut off. LE PR.

But if your judgment is sound and complete, you certainly know that you must observe what is more advantageous.

> **Note [Col. 1290D]: “But if your judgment is sound and complete, certainly this must be observed there: what is more advantageous” (according to the variant reading).** We have corrected many things here at the suggestion of Agobard’s manuscript. Tertullian says to his wife: If your judgment is sound and perfect, then in this mention of marriage you must pursue what is more advantageous—namely, abstinence from a second marriage. But because I know that this undertaking in life is arduous and difficult, I have decided to sit down and pause, and to make some interval in my writing while I gather myself for the counsel that follows. I would not, indeed, have reported even on this matter to you had I not discovered a difficulty [Col. 1291C] greater than I had foreseen in my preceding discourse. He then shows that a woman sins more seriously by marrying a pagan than by entering a second or third marriage. Immediately afterward he makes assertions about Christians who seek marriage outside the Church and expresses grave indignation. LE PR.

But because this is difficult, not without compelling circumstances, and this highest purpose in life [requires?] supreme assistance—the text is corrupt here.

> **Note [Col. 1289B]:** Rigaltius thought that, on the basis of Agobard’s codex, he had restored the corrupt passage as follows: “Now, by mentioning marriage, I may remove your safeguard against a downward slope. But if your judgment is sound and complete, certainly this must be observed there: what is more advantageous. Because—compelling circumstances—this highest purpose in life, I paused.”

Nor would I have had reason to write to you about this matter also, had I not perceived that my anxiety was the more serious.

> **Note [Col. 1289B]:** Rigaltius reads: “in that matter.”

For the greater the continence of the flesh that sustains widowhood, the more pardonable its failure may appear; difficult things readily obtain pardon. But the more practicable it is to marry in the Lord, since this lies within our power, the more blameworthy it is not to observe what one can.

Furthermore, with regard to widows and unmarried women the Apostle advises that they remain as they are, when he says, “I desire all to persevere after my example” [Col. 1290A] (1 Cor. 7:7). But concerning marriage in the Lord, when he says “only in the Lord,” he no longer advises but expressly commands. Therefore it is especially in this matter that, unless we obey, we are in danger; because one may neglect advice with impunity more readily than a command, since the former comes as counsel and is proposed to the will, whereas the latter descends from authority and binds by necessity: there it is liberty, here contumacy, that appears to commit the offense.

> **Note [Col. 1289B]:** Wouwer’s codex reads: “because advice too,” etc. Rigaltius: “because his own things—someone may neglect with impunity,” etc. Perhaps one should write: “because you may indeed neglect advice with impunity, but by no means a command.”

## CHAPTER II

Accordingly, when in these days a certain woman took her marriage away from the Church—that is, joined herself to a pagan—and I recalled that others had done this previously, I was amazed either at the women’s own wantonness or at the dereliction of their advisers, since no Scripture grants permission for such an act.

Do they perhaps, I asked, flatter themselves with that passage in the First Epistle to the Corinthians (1 Cor. 7:12–14), where it is written: “If any brother has an unbelieving wife, and she consents to the marriage, let him not dismiss her; likewise, if a believing woman is married to an unbeliever and finds her husband agreeable, let her not dismiss him; for the unbelieving husband is sanctified by the believing wife, and the unbelieving wife by the believing husband; otherwise your children would be unclean”?

> **Note [Col. 1290B]:** Rigaltius reads “of the brethren.”

> **Note [Col. 1290B]:** Rigaltius and Pamelius read *consentaneum*; Semler, *consentanea*.

Perhaps, by understanding this admonition simply as referring to believers already joined in marriage, they think that it is also lawful to marry unbelievers.

> **Note [Col. 1290B]:** The first edition of Rhenanus reads “movement” rather than “admonition.”

> **Note [Col. 1290B]:** Rhenanus reads “found”; Franeker and Paris, “enjoined.”

> **Note [Col. 1290B]:** Semler reads “to be understood.”

> **Note [Col. 1290B]:** The Franeker and Paris editions read “they think it is to be understood.”

> **Note [Col. 1291C]:** Rigaltius emended the passage here in this way. Others read: “perhaps they think it is to be understood simply of believers bound” (variant: “enjoined”; variant: “found”), “and that it is also lawful to marry unbelievers. Those who interpret it thus—God forbid,” etc.

God forbid that anyone who interprets it in this way should knowingly deceive himself. It is, rather, manifest that this passage of Scripture designates believers whom God’s grace finds already in a pagan marriage, as the words themselves show: “If any believer,” it says, “has an unbelieving wife.” It does not say, “takes an unbelieving wife.” It shows that the man is already living in marriage with an unbelieving woman and is afterward converted by God’s grace, and that he ought to persevere with his wife—clearly so that no one who has attained faith should think that he must separate from a woman who has now, in a certain sense, become alien and foreign to him.

Indeed, the Apostle also adds the reason: that we are called by the Lord in peace, and that an unbeliever may be won by a believer through the practice of marriage.

> **Note [Col. 1291C]:** Rigaltius reads “to the Lord God.”

The conclusion itself also confirms that this is how the passage must be understood: “As each person,” he says, “is called by the Lord, so let him persevere” (1 Cor. 7:15–17). But it is pagans, I think, who are called—not those who are already believers.

For if he had made an absolute pronouncement concerning a believer before marriage, he would have permitted the saints to marry indiscriminately. Yet if he had permitted that, he would never have appended a pronouncement so different from and contrary to his own permission, saying: “A woman is free when her husband has died: let her marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord.”

> **Note [Col. 1291D]: “A woman is free when her husband has died.”** Here he paraphrastically renders “falls asleep” as “dies.” In both Greek and Latin the Apostle has: “But if her husband sleeps,” a phrase most familiar in Scripture. PAM.

Nothing here is open to reconsideration; for the Apostle forestalled what might have been reconsidered: lest we misuse his statement, “let her marry whom she wishes,” he added, “only in the Lord”—that is, undoubtedly, to a Christian.

> **Note [Col. 1291C]:** Apart from Rigaltius, the others read: “concerning that which might have been discussed.”

> **Note [Col. 1292C]:** In both places Rhenanus reads “Christ” in the manuscripts; Rigaltius, “the Spirit.”

Thus that holy Apostle, who prefers widows and unmarried women to persevere in chastity, and who exhorts us by his own example, prescribes no other form for entering marriage again except “in the Lord.” Only on this condition does he permit the loss of continence: “Only,” he says, “in the Lord.”

> **Note [Col. 1291D]: “Unmarried women in chastity.”** *Integritati* is ablative, not dative. Accordingly, this case takes both endings in all nouns. For *caput* indisputably forms the ablative *capite*, and yet Virgil said:
>
> *Capiti tremit aerea cassis*  
> “The bronze helmet trembles upon his head.”
>
> And *latus* forms the ablative *latere*, yet the same poet said:
>
> *Includunt caeco lateri*  
> “They enclose it within the hidden flank.”
>
> There he used *capiti* and *lateri* for *capite* and *latere*. So too *ruri* and *vesperi* are commonly used for *rure* and *vespere*. LAC.

> **Note [Col. 1291D]: “Only, he says, in the Lord.”** Our Septimius was so rigorous an observer of Christian discipline that in some matters he may appear, if it is permissible to say so, to have sinned through excessive holiness. Evidence of that fault will be found in his explanation of Paul’s admonition, which teaches that a widow possesses the freedom to marry whom she wishes, provided that she marry in the Lord: ἐλευθέρα ἐστίν, ᾧ θέλει γαμηθῆναι, μόνον ἐν Κυρίῳ, “she is free; let her marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord.” By that qualification, “only in the Lord,” he says that “to a Christian” is undoubtedly meant. [Col. 1292D] Chrysostom interpreted this more gently and indulgently, taking “in the Lord” to mean μετὰ σωφροσύνης καὶ κοσμιότητος, ἐπὶ παιδοποιΐᾳ καὶ προστασίᾳ, μὴ διὰ πάθους ἐπιθυμίας: “with chastity and propriety, for the begetting and upbringing of children, not through the passion of desire.” All these things may occur even with a pagan, provided his life outside the sphere of religion is blameless. But our Tertullian insists inflexibly: “Only to a Christian.” RIG.

The word “only” added weight to his law. Whatever tone and manner you use to pronounce this declaration, it is burdensome: it both commands and advises, enjoins and exhorts, entreats and threatens.

> **Note [Col. 1292D]: “And entreats and threatens.”** Tertullian presses the point and weighs the Apostle’s language carefully. Clement of Alexandria’s *Paedagogus*, book III, chapter 8, agrees with him: τὰ μὲν παρήνεσε, τὰ δὲ ὠνείδισεν, “in part he advises, in part he reproaches.” LE PR.

It is a strict, unencumbered judgment, eloquent in its very brevity. Such is the custom of the divine voice: that you understand at once and obey at once.

> **Note [Col. 1292D]: “A strict, unencumbered judgment.”** Pamelius read with Rhenanus “an unqualified and explicit judgment”; Rigaltius’ reading is better and clearer. LE PR.

> **Note [Col. 1292C]:** Rhenanus and Pamelius read: “It is an unqualified and explicit judgment, fruitful in its very brevity. Such is its custom. Divine wife, obey at once. For who else could—the Apostle have foreseen?”

For who could fail to understand that the Apostle foresaw the many dangers and wounds to faith involved in marriages of the kind he prohibits, and that he sought, first of all, to guard against the pollution of holy flesh by pagan flesh?

Someone will say at this point: “What difference, then, is there between a man who, while living in a pagan marriage, is chosen by the Lord, and one who was already a believer—that is, before his marriage—such that they are not equally required to guard their flesh, when the latter is barred from marriage with an unbeliever while the former is commanded to persevere in it? If we are polluted by a pagan, why is the former not separated, just as the latter is not bound?”

I shall answer, if the Spirit grants it, by alleging before all else that the Lord regards it as preferable for a marriage not to be contracted than for it to be entirely dissolved. Thus he prohibits divorce except on account of fornication, but commends continence. The former man, therefore, is under a necessity to persevere; the latter, moreover, has the power not to marry at all.

Then, if according to Scripture those whom faith finds already in a pagan marriage are not polluted precisely because the others are also sanctified along with them, undoubtedly those who were sanctified before marriage cannot sanctify foreign flesh if they mingle with it, since faith did not find them in that flesh. God’s grace sanctifies what it finds. Thus what could not be sanctified is unclean; and what is unclean has no share with what is holy, except to pollute it from its own uncleanness and kill it.

## CHAPTER III

Since these things are so, it is established that believers who enter marriages with pagans are guilty of fornication and must be barred from every communion of the brotherhood, on the authority of the Apostle’s words that one must not even take food with a person of that kind (1 Cor. 5:11).

Or shall we produce the marriage documents concerning that union before the Lord’s tribunal and plead that the marriage was duly contracted? Is what he himself forbade not adultery? Is what has been prohibited not fornication? Does the admission of an alien man violate God’s temple less, or mingle Christ’s members with an adulteress’s members less?

> **Note [Col. 1292D]: “Shall we produce the marriage documents on that day before the Lord’s tribunal and plead what he himself forbade?”** There were evidently women who married non-Christian husbands and nevertheless believed that they were marrying “in the Lord”—that is, in just and lawful marriages, undertaken for the sake of seeking children rather than lust. But Tertullian strips away these pretexts and turns to his wife: “It is not enough,” he says, “to have married properly and lawfully and to have preserved the fidelity of marriage in holiness. Nor, when you plead your case before the Lord’s tribunal, will it benefit you at all to produce the marriage documents; for by pleading the documents you will be pleading a marriage with an unbeliever, and by your own plea you will condemn yourself for the sin that the Lord commanded you through the Apostle to avoid. What, then, do you think is forbidden by the words ‘only in the Lord’? Fornication? Adultery? Those are forbidden under another provision of the law, not this one. A Christian, whether man or woman, is a temple of God. A Christian woman who marries a non-Christian man allows God’s temple to be contaminated and polluted—indeed, more shamefully than an adulteress does. For admitting an alien man violates that temple less, does it? It mingles Christ’s members with an adulteress’s members less, does it? I want you to know this and weigh it diligently again and again. We are not our own, but were bought at a price—and indeed with God’s blood. Therefore, in injuring this flesh, we injure God directly, because we injure his flesh, flesh redeemed by his blood.” This was certainly Tertullian’s meaning. RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1293]:** These words, “and plead that the marriage was duly contracted,” found in Wouwer’s manuscript, are absent from Rigaltius’ edition.

As far as I know, we are not our own, but were bought at a price. And at what price? With God’s blood. Therefore, in injuring this flesh, we injure him.

> **Note [Col. 1293D]: “As far as I know, we are not our own.”** The old copy reads, “as he may know.” One should write either “as you know” or “as far as I know,” a formula very commonly used by Tertullian. RIG.

What did that man recently mean who said that marrying an outsider was indeed an offense, but a very small one, when, quite apart from injury to the flesh that belongs to the Lord, every voluntary offense against the Lord is serious? The greater one’s power to avoid it, the more heavily one is burdened with the guilt of contumacy.

Let us now review the other dangers and wounds to faith which, as I said, the Apostle foresaw—things exceedingly troublesome not only to the flesh but also to the spirit itself. For who can doubt that faith is daily worn away by intercourse with an unbeliever? “Bad conversations corrupt good morals”; how much more do life together and inseparable familiarity do so?

Every believing woman must necessarily attend to the Lord. And how can she serve two masters—the Lord and, in addition, a pagan husband? For by attending to the pagan she will furnish pagan things: appearance, elaborate coiffure, worldly adornments, more shameful endearments, and even the stained secrets of marriage. Among the saints, by contrast, the functions of sex are discharged modestly and temperately, with the honor due to necessity itself, as though beneath God’s eyes.

> **Note [Col. 1293D]: “Appearance, elaborate coiffure.”** By “elaborate coiffure” understand a structure of piled-up hair, an edifice by which, in Juvenal 6, a woman “builds her head high.” RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1293D]: “Among the saints, the functions of sex,” etc.** We have already discussed this word; here, however, by “the functions of sex” he means ἁγνὴν ἀναστροφήν, “a most chaste manner of life,” as in blessed Peter’s First Epistle, chapter 3. LE PR.

> **Note [Col. 1293D]: “As though beneath God’s eyes, modestly and temperately.”** Jerome expresses the same view in his commentary on Titus, chapter 2: “So that with modesty and shame, and as though under the necessity of sex, she may render the debt to her husband rather than herself demand it from him, and may believe that she performs the works of childbearing before the eyes of God and the angels.” RIG.

## CHAPTER IV

But let her see how she renders her duties to her husband. She certainly cannot satisfy the Lord in accordance with the rule when she has at her side a servant of the devil, an agent of his own master for obstructing the pursuits and duties of believers.

If a station-fast is to be observed, her husband arranges a visit to the baths on that very day. If fasts must be kept, her husband holds a banquet on that same day. If she must go out in procession, never is some household occupation more certain to arise.

> **Note [Col. 1294B]: “The husband arranges a visit to the baths on that very day.”** The old copy reads “permits.” RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1294B]: “If she must go out in procession.”** He notes the ancient rite of going forth, or public supplication, in the Church; and this passage is important for confirming many rites. One cannot but marvel at the negligence of the Magdeburg writers, who relate the rite of going forth, or processions, to the Montanist heresy, although elsewhere they admit that Tertullian wrote this book before he gave his allegiance to Montanus’ heresy. I do not know whether Albaspinaeus mocks them quite safely when they understand these words as referring to the rite of going forth; he has, I think, pierced the eyes of crows. LE PR.

For who would allow his wife, for the sake of visiting the brethren, to go from street to street around other people’s huts, especially the poorer ones? Who would gladly endure her being taken from his side for nocturnal gatherings, should that be necessary? Who, finally, would without anxiety tolerate her spending the night away during the solemnities of Easter? Who would let her go, without suspicion of his own, to that Lord’s banquet which they defame? Who would allow her to crawl into prison to kiss a martyr’s chains? Or indeed to meet one of the brethren for the kiss? To offer water for the feet of the saints? To seize upon, long for, and keep in mind something from their food or drink?

> **Note [Col. 1294C]: “From street to street.”** The old copy reads “along the roads.” RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1294C]: “To that Lord’s banquet.”** Toward the end of this book he will say “God’s banquet.” In both places he appears to mean what Paul in 1 Corinthians 11 called Κυριακὸν δεῖπνον, “the Lord’s Supper,” when he rebuked the Corinthians for approaching the Lord’s meal as though it were their own—τὸ κυριακὸν ἰδιωτικῶν ποιήσαντες, “making the Lord’s meal a private affair”—because the more powerful or wealthy alone seized and consumed the eucharistic offerings, without waiting for poorer people or admitting them to a share. This was certainly not to eat the Lord’s Supper, which, because it is the Lord’s body, ought to have been treated worthily. But those who did not regard the other believers, however poor, as brethren treated it most unworthily, being devoid of charity as well as humility, and consequently guilty concerning the Lord’s body and blood because they so shamelessly seized for themselves what belonged to all in common. Jerome appears to interpret the matter in this way.
>
> Theodoret, however, preferred to refer it to the *agapae*, which were celebrated on solemn days after the Lord’s Supper. On that interpretation, the Apostle introduced mention of the Lord’s Supper to rebuke the pride or gluttony of the wealthy Corinthians, so that he might strike down all the more forcefully the shamelessness of those who, immediately after the Lord’s Supper—in which they themselves had shared equally with all the poorest as their brethren—at once assumed such arrogance at the *agapae* that they seized the food first, without calling the poorer people to share or waiting for them. This was evidence that the Lord’s Supper had been celebrated most unworthily—that is, faithlessly and wrongly.
>
> But if the weight of this interpretation perhaps impels us so far [Col. 1295B] that we judge that here too our Septimius calls the *agapae* “God’s banquet” and “the Lord’s banquet,” scarcely any reason for this designation will be more probable than that the *agapae* were called “the Lord’s” because Christians assembled for them on the Lord’s days.
>
> “That Lord’s banquet which they defame”—that is, the pagans. Tertullian himself says in the *Apology*: “Besides defaming our little suppers as criminal, you also denounce them as extravagant.” And on the following page of this very book, where he speaks of the pagan husband of a Christian woman: “Will the husband not know what you secretly taste before all other food? And if he learns that it is bread, will he not believe it to be that bread of which the story is told?”—that is, bread saturated with the gore and blood of a slaughtered infant. Tertullian himself says in the *Apology*: “We are said to be utterly criminal because of the sacrament of infanticide and the food derived from it, followed by incest after the banquet.”
>
> That man of Cirta in Minucius spun out a tale from both slanders, with all the greater shamelessness because the letters of Pliny the Younger to the emperor Trajan had established that it was false. For after conducting an investigation, Pliny states that he discovered from deserters of the Christian religion, who had even cursed Christ, that Christians did indeed customarily assemble to take food, but food of an ordinary and innocent kind.
>
> It should also be observed that, amid all the charges and insults brought by those who accused Christians of impiety because they possessed no altars and offered no sacrifices, and amid so many betrayals by faithless brethren, no one appeared who charged Christians with eating the flesh and drinking the blood of their God and Lord, although in their eucharistic rites they testified that they did so. RIG.

If a brother should arrive from abroad, what hospitality is there for him in another man’s house? If anything must be given to someone, the granary and store cupboard are locked.

## CHAPTER V

But some husbands tolerate our practices and do not object. This, then, is the offense: that pagans know our practices; that we are under their knowing observation; and that whatever we do is done by their indulgence.

> **Note [Col. 1295B]:** Rigaltius reads: “But someone tolerates our practices and does not object. This—under the knowing observation of the unjust,” etc. Variant: “of the just.”

A man who tolerates them cannot say that he does not know. Or, if the matter is concealed from him because he does not tolerate it, he is feared. But since Scripture commands both that we act for the Lord without another’s knowing observation and that we act without constraint upon ourselves, it makes no difference on which side you offend: either under your husband’s knowing observation, if he knows and tolerates it, or in conflict with yourself, while you avoid him because he will not tolerate it.

“Do not,” he says, “cast your pearls before swine, lest they trample them and then turn and overthrow you as well” (Matt. 7:6). Your pearls are the distinguishing marks of your daily conduct. The more carefully you endeavor to conceal them, the more suspicious you make them and the more objects of wariness for pagan curiosity.

> **Note [Col. 1296B]:** Rigaltius reads “liable to be caught.”

Will you escape notice when you sign your bed and your little body with the cross, when you reject something unclean by blowing and spitting, and when you rise even during the night to pray? Will you not appear to be practicing some kind of magic?

> **Note [Col. 1295C]: “Something unclean while blowing.”** Thus, very properly, the old copy. By “something unclean while blowing,” he means some offensive smoke or stench by which the nostrils of a passing Christian woman might happen to be struck and offended by the fumes from a smoking altar. RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1296B]:** Rigaltius reads: “you drive away what is unclean by blowing.”

Will the husband not know what you secretly taste before all other food? And if he learns that it is bread, will he not believe it to be that bread of which the story is told? How will anyone who knows nothing of these things tolerate their meaning without concern, without suspicion that the bread is poison?

Some do tolerate them—but in order to trample upon and mock women of this kind, whose secrets they preserve for the danger they believe may arise, should they themselves happen to be offended.

> **Note [Col. 1295D]: “Some tolerate them, but in order to trample upon and mock women of this kind,” etc.** He shows what dangers threaten and what wounds are inflicted on faith by the unequal marriage of a Christian woman to a pagan. Through their very daily life and intimate cohabitation, the Church’s sacraments—which would better remain concealed between Christian spouses—come before profane eyes, thereby giving advantage-seekers opportunities for harassment and slander.
>
> For how could a woman not sometimes be detected by her husband when, at every forward movement and advance, every entrance and departure, when dressing and putting on her shoes, at the baths, at meals, at the lighting of lamps, on going to bed, and on taking a seat, she wears down her forehead with the sign of the cross? Or when, abhorring the smell or stench of a smoking altar, she blows it away and spits? Or, finally, when she rises during the night to attend gatherings before dawn and sing hymns to Christ as God? Will he not interpret these things as curses, spells, and magical incantations?
>
> Will he not know when you consume the Eucharist preserved at home? Will he not inquire more curiously what you secretly taste before all other food? And if he learns that it is bread, will he not immediately reflect that it is the bread said to be stained with the blood of infanticide? This was the slander that especially afflicted Christians at that time.
>
> Let us grant that someone may be found who suspects nothing concerning magical arts, the slaughter of infants, the consumption of raw flesh, or bloodstained bread, but simply does not know the reasons for the practices mentioned above—the sign, the blowing, the gatherings before dawn, and the bread preserved with special care. Do you think there is such a man who would tolerate these unknown things without concern? Even if he suspects nothing about that frenzied and barbarous bread, he will still suspect something about poisoned bread and begin to fear that, instead of a matron, he has married a poisoner.
>
> Yet some men tolerate all these things without concern or suspicion of poison. But they tolerate them in order to crush their wives and hold them up to ridicule, preserving their secrets for this use: to have some means of creating danger for them if they should happen to incur suspicion of adultery, often falsely entertained.
>
> They tolerate them and, once they have investigated them, disclose them themselves, either to shake the Christian faith from their less steadfast wives or, in the case of the more steadfast, to extort their dowry. For there were women who gave extortioners and informers considerable sums of money to avoid denunciation. Nor were there lacking husbands who employed advantage-seekers and suborned informers, with whom they bargained, under fictitious claims, for a share of the money. By that “subjection of a claim”—that is, by putting forward a creditor—they often plundered the dowries of Christian wives through a most disgraceful bargain as the price of silence.
>
> For if a wife brought an action over a defrauded dowry, she was immediately placed in danger of her life for being a Christian by the informers whom her husband had put forward and suborned. Thus the husband himself, the spy who had cunningly investigated Christian secrets through the opportunity afforded by marriage, became arbiter of the dispute. Unless the wife was entirely prepared to die, she was compelled either to betray the faith she had pledged to Christ or to lose her dowry. This was certainly what Tertullian meant.
>
> Those who think that no question mark should be placed after the words, “And if he learns that it is bread, will he not believe it to be that bread of which the story is told?” are mistaken; for the clauses that precede and follow are interrogative in the clearest possible sense. Tertullian is therefore asking a question there also. Does it really seem possible for a Christian woman who has married a pagan to conceal the Christian mysteries so successfully that, when she keeps the Eucharist at home, he will not inquire what she religiously preserves? “And if he learns that it is bread,” Tertullian says, “will he not believe it to be that bread of which the story is told”—that is, the bread defamed by the common people? RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1297B]: “Whose secrets they preserve for the danger they believe may arise, should they happen to be offended.”** By “the wives’ secrets” he means that pagan husbands do not betray their wives when they are engaged in Christian sacred rites. Thus pagan husbands preserve their wives’ secrets for the danger they believe may arise—that is, for the danger they believe threatens the Christian name when, as they hope, persecution flares up—so that they may betray them at that time if, through some hurt to their feelings arising from any source, they should desire their wives’ destruction. RIG.

> **Note [Col. 1297B]: “Should they happen to be offended.”** That is, if some hurt to their feelings happens to arise; if there is some quarrel, injury, complaint, offense, harm, lawsuit, or danger. Hence those expressions of affection in tomb inscriptions: “FROM WHOM HE SUFFERED NO GRIEF”; “FROM WHOM HE HAD RECEIVED NO PAIN”; “WITH WHOM HE LIVED WITHOUT ANY DISPUTE”; “WITHOUT ANY QUARREL”; “WITHOUT ANY HURT TO THE FEELINGS”; “WITHOUT RANCOR”; and other expressions of this kind. RIG.

They tolerate women whose dowries they make the price of their silence by bringing an accusation based on the Christian name—women who, of course, must litigate before an arbiter who is himself their spy. Most women, failing to foresee this, have departed either after their property was extorted from them or after losing their faith.
