**34. The sixteenth year of St Zephyrinus, Roman pontiff; the fourth of Emperor Antoninus; consuls Silius Messala and Q. Aquilius Sabinus.** The emperor returned to Rome from the Gallic expedition mentioned by Spartianus and was called Caracalla from the ankle-length garment brought from there and distributed to the people, so that he might be greeted in that attire. We judge that about this year TERTULLIAN wrote against the Church his book *On Fasting against the Psychics* [Col. 0080D] (*On Fasting* 1, 13, 16). From it especially, the likelihood arises that after the aforementioned debate Pope Zephyrinus excommunicated Tertullian together with Proclus and the other Montanists, from these words: “While from every side we hear anathema because we announce otherwise.” He also testifies that various councils were then celebrated throughout Greece: “Moreover, in fixed places throughout Greece councils are held from all the churches, in which all the more profound matters are treated in common, and the representation of the whole Christian name itself is celebrated with great reverence. Those assemblies first performed stations and fasts.” I think these met chiefly against the Montanists, since that heresy seems to have begun creeping like a cancer even among certain bishops, from the author’s words [Col. 0081A]: “These will be examples both for the people and even for spiritual bishops”; for he opposes the “spiritual,” Montanus’s followers, to the “psychics,” as he slanderously calls Catholics (*On Fasting* 15). Furthermore, if the reading in Blessed Jerome’s Letter 54 to Marcella concerning the Montanists is true—that they kept three Lents a year, as though three Saviors had suffered—the author begins to differ somewhat from them by observing only two, as is clear from these words, also according to the manuscript: “You say that we offer God two weeks a year of dry fasting, and not even whole weeks, Saturdays and Sundays excepted, abstaining from things we do not reject but postpone.” More on this in our annotations.

The seventeenth year of St Zephyrinus, Roman pontiff; the fifth of Antoninus; consuls Aemilius Laetus** (whom Spartianus says Antoninus killed shortly afterward) **and Anicius Cerealis.** In the same year in which Antoninus, on a German expedition, killed many barbarians near Raetia (Spartianus), and was chiefly for this called GERMANICUS, TERTULLIAN is thought to have written *On Modesty* (*On Modesty* 7, 10). In it the following words confirm St Damasus’s statement in the *Pontifical* about glass vessels appointed for the sacrifice by St Zephyrinus: “If perhaps that Shepherd whom you depict on the chalice will patronize him”; especially what follows: “But I drink in that Shepherd’s scripture, which cannot be broken.” Glass chalices can very easily be broken; [Col. 0081C] hence it is also plain that they were then customarily painted. Here too he differs from the Montanists. According to Blessed Jerome’s letter cited above, Montanus held that the fallen could not be restored through repentance and Jerome therefore joins him with Novatian; the author denies the Church’s peace only to adulterers and fornicators—wrongly, and contrary to what he had earlier written in *On Repentance*—deliberately opposing this decree of Zephyrinus (whom, even as a heretic, he calls Pontifex Maximus and bishop of bishops): “I remit the sins of adultery and fornication to those who have performed repentance” (*On Modesty* 1). This, in my judgment, is what Blessed Augustine writes about him in *On Heresies*: that, afterward divided from the Cataphrygians, he propagated his own conventicles [Col. 0081D], perhaps because, when those called followers after Proclus (whose follower he was) had died, the followers of Aeschines gained the palm. Against them he tacitly wrote *Against Praxeas*, on the Father and Son not being the same. We have also noted his disagreement concerning only two Lents. Those therefore err who infer from that passage that he returned to the Catholic Church, especially since Blessed Augustine assigns a twofold cause of his heresy: both because, passing to the Cataphrygians, he began to condemn second marriages, and because he propagated his own conventicles.

**36. The eighteenth year of St Zephyrinus, pope of Rome; the sixth of Antoninus; consuls Q. Aquilius Sabinus II** (to whom Ulpian wrote the fifty-one books of the *Digest*) **and Sextus Aurelius [Col. 0082A] Anullinus, son of Publius.** We judge that TERTULLIAN’s six books *On Ecstasy* and a seventh *Against Apollonius* were written then (among the fragments). Blessed Jerome, Sophronius, and Nicephorus testify that these too were written against the Church. It is no objection that Blessed Jerome in his catalogue of ecclesiastical writers places Apollonius before our author, since, as he elsewhere commonly does, he assigns him to no emperor’s reign. Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* V.17), and Nicephorus after him, numbers Apollonius among those who wrote against Montanus but sufficiently indicates by these words that the work was composed some time later: “Moreover, the ecclesiastical writer Apollonius, when the heresy called Cataphrygian was still seen to flourish in Phrygia, separately composed a book refuting its patrons.” What follows proves it [Col. 0082B]: “This same Apollonius in the same book records that it was now the fortieth year since Montanus began his fabricated and counterfeit prophecy when he applied himself to writing this work.” Yet from the beginning of St Eleutherius’s pontificate, when Montanus spread his heresy as stated above, to this year are forty-two years. Eleutherius sat from after the consulship of Cethegus and Clarus (having been created on 14 May, while a decretal letter of St Soter dated 23 May survives under those consuls) until the consuls Maternus and Bradua; he died 26 May—that is, from AD 172 to 186, fourteen years and thirteen days. St Victor, as stated above, sat twelve years, one month, twenty-eight days; with the eighteen years of St Zephyrinus [Col. 0082C], these make forty-two years.

### § XVII. The latest time at which he flourished

**37. The nineteenth and last year of St Zephyrinus, pope of Rome, and the seventh and last of Antoninus; consuls Bruttius Praesens and Messius Extricatus.** Here we end our history of Tertullian. We do not assert with certainty that TERTULLIAN did not survive longer—Trithemius writes that he lived into old and extreme age—but only that he ceased writing. When composing our annotations we once thought *On Fasting* was published under Pope St Callistus, because it mentions fasts apart from the days on which the bridegroom was taken away (*On Fasting* 13 §78; 4), that is [Col. 0082D], outside Lent, thinking this alluded to the Ember fasts instituted by Callistus. But on closer inspection it can refer to what follows, “interposing the half-fasts of stations” (Wednesdays and Fridays until the ninth hour), and “sometimes living on bread and water”; or to the Saturday fast observed by decree at Rome, though not in Africa (which he mentions shortly afterward); or to the fasts of penitents and even catechumens, of whom before his heresy he had said: “Those about to enter baptism must pray with frequent prayers, fasts, kneelings, and vigils” (*On Baptism* 20); or, finally, to fasts on vigils of ecclesiastical feasts. We therefore accept the opinion of Blessed Jerome, Sophronius, and Nicephorus, who testify that our author flourished and wrote not under Macrinus or Heliogabalus but under Severus [Col. 0083A] and Antoninus Caracalla; especially since all write that he was renowned under St Zephyrinus, not Callistus, except one modern writer who had already made him contemporary under Hadrian with Terty[l]ianus the jurist. That this was Antoninus Caracalla’s final year, not the following (as Cassiodorus has it), is clear from the more correct consular fasti: “This year, on 8 April, IMPERATOR CAESAR MARCUS Aurelius Antoninus Augustus Britannicus Germanicus, son of the divine Severus, was killed” (*Apology* 15). For, says Spartianus, when he wished again to make war on the Parthians and winter at Edessa, and had gone thence to Carrhae out of devotion to the Moon-god (what he adds aids understanding of our author, who somewhere mocks the Moon as masculine), he was killed on his birthday during the Megalensian games through snares set by Macrinus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard, who seized the empire after him; yet Macrinus enrolled him among the gods, built him a temple, and added Salian priests. My view is confirmed because no laws survive under the consuls Antoninus and Adventus, but two bearing the title Antoninus Augustus under Praesens and Extricatus: one in book IX of the *Code*, dated 22 February, the other in book VIII, title 38, law 3, from the Kalends of March shortly before his death. He therefore reigned from 4 February AD 212 until the said day, seven years, two months, four days. No more than nineteen years and sixteen days can be assigned to Pope St Zephyrinus, whom St Damasus and all others say ended his life under these consuls, since precisely that interval is counted from 10 August AD 189 to 26 August, when he died. Because the Church honors him among the martyrs, persecution of Christians must have been stirred again at Rome by the pagan priests when Antoninus was already absent, and perhaps continued by Macrinus (who accordingly was himself punished by death after a year, a month, and some days); but because it was merely local, historians did not count it.

### And how he ended

**38.** For historical truth it must also be added that some vainly think he returned to the Catholic faith. We showed two years above that this cannot be gathered from St Augustine. We also noted at the very beginning that not TERTULLIAN but Tertullinus should be numbered among the martyrs (*On [Col. 0083D] Repentance*, end of the Argument, and at the relevant places). Finally, St Pacian’s words to Sympronianus against the Novatians do nothing for them: “Tertullian,” he says, “after his heresy” (which we judged should be added), “is yours, for you have taken much from him. You will hear him confessing in his own epistle, and precisely one he published as a Catholic, that the Church can forgive sins.” He does not speak of an epistle [Col. 0084A] published after his heresy, but of one issued earlier while he was still Catholic, namely *On Repentance*, from which we have noted elsewhere that Pacian copied much. Surely, had his return been believed, St Vincent of Lérins would not have lamented this good man’s heresy so greatly. We thought Vincent’s words should meanwhile be added as an antidote, lest anyone be scandalized by him: “He too was a great trial in the Church” (alluding to Origen, of whom he had written similarly, who survived from the end of Severus’s reign to Decius). “But I do not wish to say more of him; I shall recall only this: contrary to Moses’ command, by asserting that Montanus’s new frenzies arising in the Church and the mad dreams of mad women concerning a novel dogma were true prophecies, he deserved that it should be said of him [Col. 0084B] and his writings: ‘If a prophet rises in your midst,’ etc., ‘you shall not hear the words of that prophet.’ Why? ‘Because,’ he says, ‘the Lord your God tests you, whether you love him or not.’ From these many and weighty masses of ecclesiastical examples, and others like them, we must plainly perceive and understand more clearly than light, according to Deuteronomy’s laws, that if any ecclesiastical teacher ever strays, divine providence permits it as our trial—whether we love God or not with all our heart and all our soul.”

### § XVIII. On the Tertullianists

**39.** Finally, as a crown, we must not pass over what we learned from Blessed Augustine: the heretical TERTULLIANISTS survived to his own times and only then passed into the Catholic Church. We judge that to them, more than to Tertullian himself, must be imputed the utterly absurd heresy that the worst souls of men turn into demons after death, especially since Tertullianists who held this are first placed in Augustine’s age after Donatists and Jovinianists, both by Augustine and by St Isidore, Honorius of Autun, Rabanus, and Gratian in their catalogues of heretics. Augustine says to Quodvultdeus in *On Heresies*: “The Tertullianists, named from Tertullian, whose many most eloquently written works are read, gradually dwindled to our time and could not survive (for so we emend the text) in their final remnants in the city of Carthage. When I was stationed there some years ago, as I think you too remember, they were wholly consumed. Even the very few who remained passed into the Catholic Church [Col. 0084D] and handed their basilica, still very well known, to the Catholic Church.” Would that, by their example, the failing heretics of our own time in Belgium might soon do likewise, with God’s help, to whom be honor, power, and glory forever and ever. Amen. [Note: [Col. 0084D] The pious prelate says this of his contemporaries and fellow citizens.]

## Opinions of Tertylianus, the Roman Jurist, Extant in the Books of the Digest

### From the Eight Books of Questions

[Col. 0083D] *Digest*, book I, title 3, *On Laws and Decrees of the Senate*, law 27.

Since it has always been customary to extend earlier laws to later cases, it must be believed to be implicit in laws that they also apply to those persons and things which at any time will be similar.

[Col. 0085A] *Digest*, book 29, title 3, *On Acquiring or Losing an Inheritance*, law 82, according to Baldus’s manuscript.

If the slave of one who cannot take an inheritance is appointed heir, and before entering upon it at his master’s command is manumitted and transferred to another, and nothing was done to evade the law, he himself is admitted to the inheritance. If his master can take only a share, the same must be said of the share he cannot take. It makes no difference whether the inquiry concerns the whole that he cannot take or a portion.

*Digest*, book 41, title 2, *On Acquiring or Losing Possession*, law 28.

If I possess something and afterward lease that same thing, do I lose possession? In such matters the intention makes a great difference. First it matters whether I know or do not know that I possess it, and whether I lease it as though it were not mine or as mine; and, knowing it mine, whether with regard to ownership or possession only. For even if you possess my thing and I purchase from you possession of it, or stipulate for it, both purchase and stipulation will be valid. It follows that a *precarium* and a lease also are valid, provided there is a specific intention to lease possession alone or request it as a *precarium*.

### From the Single Book on Military Peculium

*Digest*, book 29, title 1, *On Soldiers’ Wills*, law 23.

The same applies if a soldier who is head of a household, having made a will only concerning his military property, gives himself in arrogation; but if he does so after discharge, the will is invalid.

Ibid., law 33.

If a son under paternal power serving as a soldier made a will under military law and then, after his father’s death, a posthumous child was born to him, his will is certainly broken. But if he persisted in wishing that will still to be valid, it will be valid as though made anew, provided he was still serving when the posthumous child was born. Yet if a soldier under paternal power made a will and afterward, while he and the grandfather were still alive, a posthumous child was born to him, his will is not broken, because, since the child would not come under his power, he is not regarded as a direct heir; nor, when born during the son’s lifetime, does this posthumous grandson immediately become his grandfather’s direct heir. Therefore the grandfather’s will is not broken either, since although the child immediately begins to be under the grandfather’s power, the son precedes him. Accordingly, if a soldier under paternal power makes a will and mistakenly omits a posthumous child whom he did not wish disinherited, and the child is then born after the grandfather’s death while the son—his father—is still alive, he will altogether break that will. [Col. 0086B] If born after the soldier has become a civilian, the broken will does not recover validity. If born while he is still serving, it is broken; but if the father then wishes it ratified, it recovers validity as though made anew. If the posthumous child is born during the grandfather’s life, he does not immediately break the father’s will. If he survives after the grandfather’s death while his father still lives, he breaks it, because only then is a new direct heir born to him. Thus he can never break the wills of two persons at once, grandfather and father.

*Digest*, book 49, title 17, *On Military Peculium*, law 4.

A soldier ought to have as his own the things he took with him into camp with his father’s permission. A son always has an action and right of pursuit regarding military property even against his father’s will. If a head of household, while serving or after discharge, gives himself in arrogation, it must be considered whether administration of things acquired in camp before arrogation is also understood to be permitted to him. Although imperial constitutions speak of those who served from the beginning as sons under paternal power, this conclusion must be admitted.

## Article I. On Tertullian’s Genius, Style, and Reputation

[Col. 0089A]

[Col. 0089B] Tertullian’s writings clearly declare his great power in letters and learning. In Dupin’s judgment, “His genius was vigorous and acute, though not sufficiently exact and sound. His reasonings often have more brilliance than solidity. He affects and strikes by novel and unusual expressions rather than convinces by force of argument. His matter is studied and sometimes rather inflated. He expresses it with great but not wholly natural charm. He often speaks beyond measure, and in almost everything seethes and is carried away. He abounds in figures and hyperboles. He was filled with great knowledge and learning, which he used very aptly. He excelled in satire; his wit was urbane and biting. He attacked adversaries skillfully and overwhelmed them with a multitude of connected arguments. Finally, if he does not persuade by reasoning [Col. 0089C], he at least extorts assent by his brilliant manner of speaking. His discourse is Laconic, his expressions powerful; in his writings there are nearly as many thoughts as words. Yet Lactantius rightly criticizes three considerable faults: ‘Tertullian,’ he says, ‘was highly skilled in every kind of literature, but in speaking he was insufficiently fluent, insufficiently polished, and very obscure’ (*Divine Institutes* V.1). These three faults occur in most African authors, except Minucius, Cyprian, and Lactantius, who departed from the African style, although something remains even in Cyprian. In Tertullian, however, they exist in the highest degree; no writer’s style is harsher, less polished, or more obscure. Each work suffers from them, some more and some less. He is clearer and more fluid, though less elegant, in polemical books; obscurer and harsher in commonplaces, as in *On the Pallium*, one of antiquity’s most obscure works. *On Repentance* is the most elegant of all. His finest and most useful works are the *Apology*, *Prescriptions*, the books *On Repentance, Baptism,* and *Prayer*, and the exhortations to patience and martyrdom.” Thus Dupin (*Bibliothèque des auteurs*, I, p. 165, Latin trans., Cologne, 1703). But John Cave must also be heard. After quoting Lactantius’s words, he adds: “Yet Tertullian’s style has a certain majesty proper to itself, and lofty eloquence highly seasoned with wit and point, which at once exercises the reader’s intellect and sweetly delights his mind” (Cave, *Literary History*, I, p. 92, Basel ed.).

“From the foregoing,” Dupin continues, “it is not difficult to judge Tertullian’s character. Yet it is no easier to assert whether he deserves more praise or blame. First, if he is judged according to his service to the Church, it is difficult to say whether he harmed her more than he benefited her. On one side he vigorously defended her teaching against many heretics, maintained in certain works important heads of her discipline, and everywhere taught morals beautifully; on the other, besides always being infected by many errors, he openly attacked the Church’s discipline after deserting her. If he is next judged from his genius, it is so powerful for good and evil that it is hard to say which prevails. Finally, if from his style, it is uncertain whether he should be praised and admired for sublimity or blamed for faults. Opinions have therefore always differed, and men still vary considerably in their judgments of this author. I am thus led to believe [Col. 0090C] that it is better to praise what is good in him and blame what was evil than to issue an absolute judgment that would always be exposed to a thousand disputes.”
