But if this entire line of argument seems altogether weak to you, take from him another, which you will doubtless [Col. 1133B] acknowledge to carry greater weight. It is established, he says, that all the images of the pagans—or, what amounts to the same thing, all their gods—were either domestic and private or common and public. The first were called Lares and Penates, which each person used to keep at home and worship according to a particular rite (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 344, and book I *Ad Nationes*, ch. 10). Tibullus has these verses about the Penates:

> But may it be my lot to honor my ancestral Penates  
> And render monthly incense to the ancient Lar.

(Tibullus, book I, elegy 3, v. 33.)

We may add, if one wishes, these lines published by Virgil:

> Within are fifty maidservants, whose task in long succession  
> Is to lay out the provisions and honor the Penates with flames.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, book I, v. 707.)

[Col. 1133C] Juvenal, for his part, sang of the Lares:

> O humble Lares who are ours, whom I am accustomed to adorn  
> With a little incense, or meal, or a slender garland.

(Juvenal, satire 9, near the end.)

There is no need to gather here the many other things repeatedly celebrated by the pagans about both, in prose and verse. Yet such was those same pagans’ fickleness of mind and such their impious contempt for their gods that, against those whom they had long worshiped, as Tertullian says, they exercised “domestic authority by pawning, selling, and altering them—sometimes making a cooking pot out of Saturn, a drinking vessel out of Minerva” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 344). Thus, just as the pagans fashioned images, or gods, out of vessels intended for the basest uses, as we saw above, so afterward [Col. 1133D] they fashioned other vessels out of these images, or pawned or sold them. From Saturn, or an image of Saturn, they would sometimes make a *cacabŭlum*, that is, a small *cacabum*, which, according to Varro, was a vessel in which they cooked food; from it they derived the name *cacabum* (Varro, *On the Latin Language*, book IV, § 27). A *trulla*, however, was a drinking vessel, as is clear from these verses of Horace:

> He who on feast days was accustomed to drink Veientan wine  
> From a Campanian *trulla*.

(Horace, book II, satire 3, v. 143.)

This is also confirmed by Juvenal (Juvenal, satire 3, v. 112). Sometimes, however, others seem to use the word for a vessel into which the burden of the bowels was discharged, as we showed in another dissertation (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, final chapter, final article), [Col. 1134A] but we shall be more readily persuaded that Tertullian took it in the former sense, since in the same place he spoke of a vessel used for preparing food. We have said something about the *trulla* and *cacabŭlum* in the dissertation cited.

Furthermore, there were two reasons why these gods, or images, were altered, pawned, and sold by the pagans. The first, says Tertullian, was “as each god has been worn and battered through long worship” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 344). Thus they rejected a god eaten away or disfigured by age, or exchanged it for another. The second reason was: “As each of the gods found domestic necessity more sacred.” So indeed Rigault; but in the Froben edition: “And each one the master.” Better in the other editions [Col. 1134B] and in all our unpublished copies: “As each master”—that is, the master of that god and image—“has found a more sacred domestic necessity” (p. 345). But what, someone will ask, does the word “more sacred” mean there? Some reply that Tertullian meant nothing other than that the poor owner sold this god because his own need and poverty seemed more sacred to him than his god. For they think an extreme and most urgent necessity is called “more sacred,” just as Livy calls the treasury “more sacred,” because, he says, it “was reserved for the final emergencies” (Livy, book XXVII, § 10). Cicero might also have been cited, who wrote to Atticus that it had been decreed that they should withdraw money from the “more sacred treasury” (Cicero, book VIII, letter 20 to Atticus, p. 284, line 5).

Yet would the man stray entirely from the point [Col. 1134C] who said that the adjective “more sacred” refers not to “necessity,” but to “god”? For the more sacred the pagans supposed a god to be, the more dearly they sold it. Indeed Tertullian himself says in that same place and in the book *Ad Nationes*: “The more sacred the gods, the more heavily taxed” (Tertullian, *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10). But whether the god—or rather the pagans’ necessity—is designated by this term “more sacred,” Tertullian rightly reproaches them in these words: “The private gods whom you declare to be Lares and Penates through domestic consecration, you trample underfoot by domestic license, selling and pawning them according to need and inclination” (*ibid.*, p. 55).

They valued their public gods no more highly; as he continues, they “dishonored them equally under public law” (the same author, [Col. 1134D] *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 345, and *Ad Nationes*, book I, passage cited)—indeed, they treated them still more disgracefully and insultingly. For each person sold his private gods at his own discretion, certainly, but did so privately; their public gods, however, they sold publicly, since they held them in the *hastarium* as taxable property (*ibid.*). According to Turnebus (*Adversaria*, book XXVIII, ch. 5), the *hastarium* was the place where the property of proscribed persons was sold. Rigault, however, thinks the term signifies the *liber hastarius*, a record book or register of taxes, which were customarily auctioned to contractors every five years beneath a spear and at the herald’s proclamation. And indeed our author says: “Those whom you have entered in the *hastarium*, you auction to tax-farmers under . . . every five years, proscribed among your revenues” (Tertullian, [Col. 1135A] *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10; text materially defective).

But Tertullian undoubtedly appears to signify both of these. For he says that public gods were transferred to that place where, like the other possessions of proscribed persons, after first being recorded in the public register, they were kept for five years until sold publicly; and thus “under the same spear,” as he says, “under the same notation of the quaestor, their divinity is auctioned off.” Could not *hastarium* have taken its name both from that spear and from the quaestor’s notation or register?

Whatever the case, these gods were considered not only saleable but also taxable, says Tertullian, because some tax or tribute was paid for their five-year stay in the *hastarium* (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 344). With how much disgrace and reproach, then, I ask you, [Col. 1135B] were those wretched gods afflicted? If you ask how people went there to sell and buy them, Tertullian will answer: “Thus one goes to the Capitol; thus to the vegetable market” (the same author, *ibid.*). Some printed editions less correctly have “if . . . if” in place of “thus . . . thus.” For in the book *Ad Nationes* we read: “Thus one goes to the Serapeum; thus to the Capitol” (*Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10, p. 55). That is, the *hastarium* was leased for storing and selling gods in the same manner and subject to the same tax as the Capitol, the Serapeum, and the vegetable market. No one could enter those temples, or sell anything in the vegetable market, without paying the tax. Therefore, he concludes, “the more heavily taxed, the more sacred—indeed, the more sacred, the more heavily taxed” (*ibid.*); that is, the more sacred a god was judged in the pagans’ false opinion, [Col. 1135C] the greater the price and tax demanded for its stay in the *hastarium* and its sale.

If someone should now ask us who those public gods were, we shall reply that they were chiefly of three kinds. Some were placed in temples; others were either manufactured or bought and imported; others, finally, were openly carried around through streets and squares, as we noted above (above, ch. 24, art. 4). The first were customarily taxed and sold merely because of their age; the second and third at the pagans’ discretion. Did not those who branded their gods with such infamy openly confess, even against their will, that neither they nor their images were gods or worthy of any divine honor?

### ARTICLE III. How perversely the pagans consecrated Simon Magus, like their other gods, by means of a statue of a holy god; how they represented them all in the same dress and with the same insignia as belonged to their age, craft, and occupation before death; and how they erected equivalent statues to their emperors, whether dead or alive—and why nothing can be inferred from this against the sacred images of Christians

[Col. 1135D]

Tertullian’s final argument against the insane worshipers of images is not altogether free of difficulty. Let us therefore listen to him a little longer as he rebukes the pagans in these words: “With what effrontery,” he says, “do you worship Junos, Cereses, and Dianas”—that is, images of those and of your other goddesses and gods—[Col. 1136A] “when you consecrate Simon Magus with a statue and an inscription as a holy god?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 347).

We observed that Justin Martyr reported that, in the reign of Claudius, a statue was erected to this Simon Magus on the Tiber Island between the two bridges, bearing the inscription: “To Simon, the holy god” (*Simoni sancto deo*) (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 6). Cyril of Jerusalem supports the most holy martyr (Cyril, catechesis 6, p. 157), saying that it was erected by the same emperor Claudius; and Eusebius (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book II, p. 52) unhesitatingly affirms that it was erected by the Romans. To cite other witnesses here would be as easy as it would be superfluous. Everyone indeed concedes that this was the common opinion of the ancient writers.

Recent critics, however, boldly assert that this opinion [Col. 1136B] rests upon a gross error which, originating with Justin himself, who was utterly ignorant of Latin, subsequently spread through the minds of the rest. For when, they say, he had persuaded himself that the statue of the god Semo Sancus Fidius was the same as that of Simon Magus, all the others incautiously agreed with him, since he himself had been deceived by a false resemblance of names.

What then? Did everyone do so, including not only those who knew Greek but those who knew Latin, and even those who came to Rome after Justin Martyr? Did none of them detect this error, or warn anyone of it once he had recognized it? Because in recent times a statue of Semo Sancus was found, does it necessarily follow that in Justin’s time there was no statue of Simon Magus? What of the fact that Augustine bears explicit witness that Simon Magus himself had images made of himself and of Helen the prostitute, [Col. 1136C] which, he says, “he offered to his disciples for worship and had erected at Rome as images of gods by public authority” (Augustine, *On Heresies*, ch. 1, p. 6)? But if he erected these images at Rome, could not one of them still have existed in Justin’s time? What, then, prevents the conclusion that that holy martyr and others after him either saw one of them or were informed that others had seen it?

But suppose, if you please, that Justin, Augustine, and very many others were mistaken; Tertullian’s argument against the statues of the gods nevertheless remains no less valid. Everyone at least admits that an image of Semo Sancus was erected at Rome. But it was no less impious to worship the image [Col. 1136D] of this false god than the image of Simon Magus. Moreover, Tertullian says that in statues of the gods “the dress is the same, the insignia are the same; and as were the age, craft, and occupation of the dead man, so is the god” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 347). What could be more absurd than that, as he wrote in another book, “an old man is fashioned in Saturn, a beardless youth in Apollo, a virgin in Diana, a soldier is consecrated in Mars, and an ironworker in Vulcan” (the same author, *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10)? Who, on seeing them, would not immediately perceive that they had been human beings, formerly engaged even in lowly crafts, and thus could never have been gods? It was therefore an act of the highest impiety to worship their statues and images as those of gods.

Finally, these same pagans erected temples not only to these and their other ancient [Col. 1137A] gods, but also to their dead emperors, and sometimes to emperors still living, as we shall say shortly; in those temples their images were placed and worshiped with the same honor as the rest of their gods. What greater impiety could there be than to adore and venerate as a true god the image of a living or dead man, who while alive had often been defiled by every kind of crime? Yet this detestable superstition belonged not only to an insane and ignorant rabble, but also to others who possessed the greatest ability and authority. For Pliny wrote the following to the emperor Trajan concerning himself and certain persons who denied that they were Christians: “When, with me leading them, they invoked the gods and offered incense and wine in supplication to your image—which for this purpose I had ordered to be brought in with the images of the divinities—[Col. 1137B] and moreover cursed Christ, none of which, it is said, those who are truly Christians can be compelled to do” (Pliny, book X, letter 101 to Trajan). It is certainly no wonder that true Christians utterly and absolutely refused an act that could not be performed without incurring the gravest guilt of idolatry.

The pagans, therefore, must no longer be heard when they objected to us that help was given them by such statues and images of the gods, especially the ancient gods, that oracles were delivered from them, or that some supposed miracles were performed. For do not the things we have said about these gods or images demonstrate more clearly than the noonday sun that nothing—much less miracles—could be performed by them? Moreover, Tertullian, as we saw above, plainly shows that if anything appeared to have been done by them outside the customary order of nature, it [Col. 1137C] was to be attributed to the evil arts, deceptions, and frauds of demons, who, lying concealed, as he puts it, “beneath the prostitution of images and the consecration of human names” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 27), deceived the unwary minds of the pagans. But we have discussed these matters sufficiently above (above, ch. 10, art. 3 and following).

Nor is there any reason for some heretic to snarl in our ear or shout openly that from what has thus far been elucidated after Tertullian one may conclude that he condemned absolutely all sacred images of Christians. For he discussed none other than images of false gods, which, as has been sufficiently proved, cannot be worshiped without the vice of sacrilegious impiety (Tertullian, *On Idolatry*, ch. 5). Nor will another person have any stronger grounds [Col. 1137D] for insisting that elsewhere he firmly declared that every kind of statue and image was forbidden by the divine law of Moses. For in the same place he asserts no less clearly that by God’s command Moses himself made images or statues of the bronze serpent, the Cherubim, and others, which for that reason entailed no corrupt worship or idolatry. He therefore judged that images which have no affinity with the impious worship of idols are not to be condemned. These matters, however, have been explained more fully in the first volume of our *Apparatus* and in other earlier dissertations of ours (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 748; *Dissertation on Minucius’s Octavius*, ch. 29, art. 1 and following; *Dissertation on Arnobius*, ch. 22, art. 1 and following; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 34, art. 1 and following). If those discussions are compared with what we have related here, [Col. 1138A] they will cast and receive fresh, reciprocal light upon one another.

## CHAPTER XXX. On the Temples and Sacrifices of the Pagans

### ARTICLE I. With what great impiety the pagans built temples and altars to their gods and to dead and living emperors; how they exacted payment for the temple site, the entrance, the doors, and the doorposts; and with what unspeakable crimes they polluted those same temples, which Christians did not frequent even by day

By the same arguments with which Tertullian demonstrates that images and statues of false gods are opposed to true religion, he demonstrates that their temples also must not be built. [Col. 1138B] For it has never been permissible to construct temples to a false god, much less to a dead or living human being as though he were the true God. Who, then, could calmly endure those pagans who had erected temples—or, as Tertullian calls them, shrines and altars—not only to fictitious ancient gods but also to their dead and living emperors? For this reason he addresses and mocks them with these words: “You worthily assign the honor of divinity to deceased emperors, since you also award it to them while living”; or, as he added in another book, “you establish priesthoods, sacred apparatus, sacred carriages, chariots, *solisternia*”—that is, seats and thrones—“and even *lectisternia*” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13, p. 344; *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10). Lest anyone doubt that those temples were consecrated to these new gods, the pagans themselves took care to inscribe upon the thresholds and altars conspicuous [Col. 1138C] titles naming the gods to whom they were dedicated.

If anyone should wish examples of temples dedicated to emperors to be produced, here are Suetonius’s words about Julius Caesar: “He allowed honors surpassing the elevation of a human being to be decreed to him: temples, altars, images, a sacred couch beside the gods, flamens, and Luperci” (Suetonius, book I, § 76). Indeed, this insane impiety of the pagans went so far that Valerius Maximus dared to pray to this same Julius Caesar in these words: “Having venerated your altars and your most sacred temples, divine Julius, I pray that, with your divine power propitious and favorable, you will allow the misfortunes of such great men to find concealment under the shelter and protection of your example” (Valerius Maximus, book I, ch. 6, § 13).

The emperor Augustus was indeed more restrained. According to Suetonius, although he knew that temples were customarily decreed even to proconsuls, [Col. 1138D] he accepted them in no province unless they were dedicated jointly in his own name and that of Rome (Suetonius, book II, § 52). Yet he nevertheless accepted temples that could not be accepted without injury to the true and supreme God. Much more blameworthy, therefore, were others who founded these and other temples in the name either of an emperor or even of the commonwealth. What, then, must be said of Domitian and other most criminal emperors who, as the writers of Roman history relate, ordered themselves to be called gods and temples to be built to them? No one of sound mind will deny that this was the supreme crime of impiety.

But observe, I ask you, how blind and unbridled [Col. 1139A] were the obstinacy, impiety, and avarice of the pagans. A temple or sacred shrine is certainly built to any god for no other reason than that it may be frequented by all; its entrance should therefore be easy, voluntary, and free for everyone. Nevertheless, just as the pagans, with supreme injustice, strove to compel Christians by every kind of punishment to enter them, so, with the grossest avarice, they barred their fellow pagans from them unless a price was paid. Through them, indeed, absolutely no one was permitted to enter any temple for free; rather, from every entrant, says Tertullian, they exacted “payment for the ground of the temple and for entrance into the sacred place” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 13), that is, for entry into the sacred building. The term “ground” there designates the place upon which the sacred building had been constructed. Cicero clearly explains this in the following words: [Col. 1139B] “They change their ground”—that is, “they change their residence and place” (Cicero, speech 12 *For Caecina*, p. 241). Elsewhere he says: “They could not previously have changed this citizenship without changing the ground of this city” (speech 36 *For Cornelius Balbus*, p. 519, line 38).

Tertullian therefore spoke somewhat more obscurely in the first book *Ad Nationes* when he said that the pagans extorted payment “for the doorposts, for the doors of the temples to be opened to each person” (Tertullian, *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10). Gothofredus thinks that the word *stipitibus* (“doorposts”) is corrupt and that *stipibus* (“contributions”) should be read, because Tertullian himself wrote in the *Apologeticum*: “How few now cast in a contribution?” (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 42). But the former words concern people entering a temple, while the latter concern those who have already entered. Rigault therefore did not do badly to retain the word *stipitibus*, [Col. 1139C] although he ought to have offered some reason for his opinion. We, for our part, may readily be persuaded that by the word *stipitibus* Tertullian meant either stones or perhaps the gods Terminus placed before the doors of temples, which really were called *stipites*, as we demonstrated from verses of the ancient poets in the other dissertations cited (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 19, arts. 2 and 17; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, p. 32, art. 2). For there he appears to distinguish the doorpost, the door, and the entrance of the temple, so that, by distinguishing them, he might attack the pagans’ avarice and impiety more sharply. Yet what can one dare to determine with certainty if the word is corrupt and no better manuscript is found?

Moreover, besides these sordid practices of intolerable avarice, the pagans polluted the same temples of their gods with procuring [Col. 1139D] and adulteries, as was observed above (ch. 26, art. 4) with our Tertullian (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 15), as shamelessly as they did with impunity. How, then, could they believe that their gods inhabited temples that were so very often disgraced by such outrages and crimes?

It is therefore no wonder if Christians, says Tertullian, “do not know these temples even by day” (Tertullian, passage cited); that is, they did not visit them during the day, much less by night, which was privy to such great crimes. Yet if they ever entered, perhaps “they too would despoil them, if they worshiped them,” as he says; that is, if Christians could ever have entered these temples as the pagans did, they undoubtedly would have stripped them bare, like the tyrant Dionysius [Col. 1140A] and others whom we have mentioned elsewhere—not, as those men did, from avarice, but because of the crimes and sacrileges perpetrated in them. We have discussed the temples of the pagans and also of the Christians more fully in our cited dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 12, art. 2; *On Arnobius*, ch. 21, art. 1; and *On Lactantius*, ch. 33, art. 1).

### ARTICLE II. With what guilty conscience and how much avarice the pagans sacrificed to their gods, offering grains of incense worth one as, two drops of undiluted wine, the blood of a rejected ox longing to die, and victims exhausted, consumptive, and diseased, along with hooves, little heads, and scraps plucked from feathers and bristles; and how they withheld the tithe owed to Hercules

[Col. 1140B]

The piety and religious devotion of the pagans in their sacrifices was certainly no greater than in their temples; nor was their avarice and greed for gain any less. For when sacrificing to their gods, after all their other defilements they also offered, says Tertullian, “a filthy conscience” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 30), that is, one contaminated by all the crimes noted shortly before and throughout this dissertation. If you wish to know what kind and quantity of incense they burned, our author will answer you: “grains of incense worth one *as*, and tears of the Arabian tree” (*ibid.*)—that is, grains of incense not indeed from Saba, which fetched a higher price, but Arabian incense, which was bought at the lowest price. Indeed, these avaricious men took no more grains of this incense, however common it might be, [Col. 1140C] but only three, as we observed above (above, ch. 24, art. 3; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 35, art. 2), grasped with fingers that could certainly hold very few.

But what, someone will object: did not very many people burn whole heaps, even of costly incense, with a full hand, as Tibullus sang in these verses?

> Juno of the birthday, receive the sacred heaps of incense  
> Which the learned young woman gives you with tender hand.

(Tibullus, book IV, elegy 6.)

Did not Martial also sing:

> I consumed the false sacrificial meal and heaps of incense;  
> The flocks diminished while ewe after ewe fell.

(Martial, book VII, epigram 53.)

But these are undoubtedly poetic exaggerations; and Martial is speaking there of the interpretation of dreams. [Col. 1140D] Who does not know that those who wish to learn the future and, above all, obtain what they desire, spare no expense? It is no less certain that only the rich—and very few of them—burned a great quantity of incense or more costly incense to their gods, and then only when pressed by some necessity or seeking to catch at whispers of empty renown. Almost all the rest gave three or four grains of incense and, as Tertullian adds, “two drops of undiluted wine” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 30)—that is, as little incense and wine as they could. With what frame of mind they did so you will learn from these verses of Ovid about an old woman sacrificing:

> She also drips in wine—whatever wine is left;  
> She herself or her companions drink it, though she herself drinks more.
>
> [Col. 1141A] “We have bound hostile tongues and unfriendly mouths,”  
> She said as she departed, and the old woman went out drunk.

(Ovid, *Fasti*, book III, § 15.)

With precisely the same vice of avarice, the pagans sacrificed victims that were certainly neither fat, nor choice, nor sound, but, as Tertullian further says, “the blood of a rejected ox longing to die” (Tertullian, passage cited). Some more fastidious critics would have the word written there as not “ox” but “man,” because only a man can wish for death. But this is mere captiousness against the common agreement of all the manuscripts. Who does not see that our Tertullian said this metaphorically of an ox so wasted by old age, emaciation, and consumption that, if it were possible, it would prefer death to life? Hence Tertullian himself had already addressed the pagans in these words: “You slaughter every victim that is exhausted, consumptive, and mangy . . . [Col. 1141B] things which at home you would have assigned even to boys, or slaves, or dogs” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 14, and *Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10).

Yet by law they were obliged to sacrifice whole, excellent, and becoming victims to their gods, a law which Cicero reported in these very words: “Let them take care that the victims offered to each divinity are becoming and pleasing to that divinity” (Cicero, *On the Laws*, book II, p. 334, line 46). According to Vitruvius, as quoted by Macrobius, these were called *eximiae* (“choice victims”), either because they were “taken out” (*eximuntur*) from the herd or because, being of exceptional appearance, they were selected as offerings to the divine powers (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, book III, ch. 5). Hence, according to Pliny, there was a custom of inspecting and selecting victims. Virgil recalls this custom in the verse:

> [Col. 1141C] . . . They sacrifice, according to custom, selected two-year-old sheep.

(Virgil, *Aeneid*, book IV, v. 57.)

For, as Servius perceptively annotates there: “It was customary for sheep to be selected for sacrifice which lacked nothing,” as in the sixth book of the same poet, where he sings again:

> Now it would be best to sacrifice from an untouched herd  
> Seven bullocks and as many selected two-year-old sheep, according to custom.

(The same author, book VI, v. 38 and following.)

And just as Virgil repeats the same thing, Servius again observes upon these verses: “‘According to ancient custom,’ which he omitted as though known to everyone: that is, they must have neither a pointed tail, nor a split tongue, nor a black ear. By this he teaches that ‘untouched’ is one thing and ‘selected’ another.”

The pagans, therefore, contrary to the law given and the custom received, offered all the worst things and the blood, [Col. 1141D] as was said, “of a rejected ox”—that is, one disapproved and rejected by their ancient law and custom. If some of them did in fact sometimes select the best victims as commanded, they certainly did not present them whole and untouched, but only certain and inferior parts of them, such as “little heads,” says Tertullian, that is, heads. For in another book he calls the chameleon’s head a *capitulum* immediately adjoining its back, “for it lacks a neck” (Tertullian, *On the Pallium*, ch. 3), the neck being the posterior part of the throat. Thus *capitulum* is there used by him for the chameleon’s entire head.

After keeping the better portions of the victims, therefore, they sacrificed the head and “the hooves and scraps of feathers” (the same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 14)—or, as he says elsewhere, [Col. 1142A] things “plucked off beforehand” (*Ad Nationes*, book I, ch. 10)—along with other parts which masters and heads of households would have assigned to their slaves, or even to their dogs. But if anyone should clearly prove that Tertullian has said something there “hyperbolically” (ὑπερβολικῶς), we shall not resist him greatly. Nevertheless, no one can deny that he rightly reproached the pagans for sacrificing inferior victims to the gods and for taking away the better part of fat and whole victims and reserving it for their own uses.

Nor did the avaricious impiety of the pagans stop here. For they did not shrink from withholding the tenth part of their property owed to Hercules. Tertullian therefore addresses and rebukes them in these words: “Of Hercules’ tithe, you do not place even a third part upon his altar” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 14). But Cicero openly testifies [Col. 1142B] that the whole of this tithe had to be paid by them (Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, book III, p. 253, line 22). It was surely for this reason that Plautus introduces a certain Gelasinus speaking thus:

> I must come here as quickly as possible,  
> So that I may offer a tenth part to Hercules.

(Plautus, *Truculentus*, act II, scene 2.)

Do not, I ask you, doubt that this poet means the full tenth of one’s property. For elsewhere he said that from it a slave could easily determine how much gold his master possessed. Here are his very verses:

> But I should like to know how much gold the master deducted for himself  
> And how much he gave back to his father.
>
> If he is thrifty, he made Hercules a participant in the share:  
> He gave him the tenth part and took nine for himself.

(The same author, *Bacchides*, act IV, scene 4.)

[Col. 1142C] Plutarch gave various explanations of this custom and institution, which anyone may easily read in his works (Plutarch, *Roman Questions*, question 13, p. 267, vol. II). But the pagans, little concerned about this duty of their religion, subtracted whatever they could with impunity from this tithe and did not pay even a third of it. How true this was you will further learn from these verses of the same Plautus:

> Just now, from this provision money, from a single mina, I reduced  
> Five coins; I withheld Hercules’ share for myself.

(Plautus, *Truculentus*, act II, scene 7.)

Tertullian therefore mocks the pagans as wittily as he does justly and deservedly: “I praise your wisdom all the more, because you rescue something from what was lost” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. [Col. 1142D] 14). He rightly called “lost” what was given to false gods and divine powers.

With what reason, then, with what effrontery, did the pagans strive, with a cruelty greater indeed than that of beasts, to compel Christians to worship and sacrifice to their gods? Even if they had believed them to be true gods; even if they had honored them with worship as sincere as it was false; even if they had sacrificed to them whole, selected, and altogether excellent victims—nevertheless nothing was more unjust or perverse, nothing more contrary to human and divine laws and to the laws of nature itself, than to condemn Christians without allowing them a defense or hearing them, solely on account of their name, and to kill them with the most exquisite tortures that could be devised by the ingenuity of a man [Col. 1143A]—or rather, of a demon. But these most cruel and unjust men feared that, if Christians were heard, they would demonstrate so clearly the truth of their own religion and the falsity of the others that no one could ever contradict them. How easy and readily available this was to all of them has been most clearly proved throughout this dissertation.
