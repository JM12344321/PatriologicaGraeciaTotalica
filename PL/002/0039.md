Certainly he proclaims peace and every good thing to Zion and Jerusalem; certainly he ascends the mountain and spends the night there [Col. 0386C] in prayer, and surely he is heard by the Father. Unroll the prophets, then, and examine the whole sequence. “Ascend a high mountain,” says Isaiah (Isa. 40:9), “you who proclaim good news to Zion; raise your voice with strength, you who proclaim good news to Jerusalem.”

[Note: [Col. 0386D] “Ascend a high mountain,” says Isaiah, etc. St. Ambrose, *On Luke*, book V, chapter 6, interprets this prophecy of Isaiah 40 in the same way, as referring to Christ praying on the mountain. PAM.]

Still “with strength”: “They were astonished at his teaching, for he taught as one possessing power” (Luke 4:32). And again (Isa. 52:6): “Therefore my people shall know my name on that day.” What name, if not Christ’s? “For I am he who was speaking.”

[Note: [Col. 0386D] “I who speak,” Oberth.]

For at that time he himself was the one who spoke in the Prophets—the Word, the Son of the Creator: “I am present, when the hour has come, upon the mountains, as one proclaiming the report of peace, proclaiming good things.”

Likewise Nahum (Nah. 1:15), [Col. 0387A] one of the Twelve: “For behold, the swift feet upon the mountain of one proclaiming peace.”

[Note: [Col. 0386D] “Likewise Nahum, one of the Twelve,” etc.—namely, of the prophets. [Col. 0387D] For in Nahum 1 there is found the passage he cites: “For behold, the swift feet upon the mountain of those proclaiming peace,” except that the Septuagint reads ἐπὶ τὰ ὄρη, that is, “upon the mountains.” This is not surprising, since St. Jerome himself writes that here he differs from the Septuagint translators because even the chapters themselves are confused among them through variations of translation. PAM.]

Concerning the voice of nocturnal prayer to the Father, the Psalm is explicit (Ps. 21:2): “My God, I shall cry by day, and you will hear; and by night, and it shall not be in vain for me.” Elsewhere, concerning the same place and voice, the Psalm says (Ps. 3:4): “With my voice I cried aloud to the Lord, and he heard me from his holy mountain.”

You have the representation of the name; you have the act—

[Note: [Col. 0387D] “Deed,” Ciaccon.]

—of the one proclaiming good news; you have the location, the mountain; the time, night; the sound of the voice; and the Father’s hearing.

[Note: [Col. 0387D] “Peace,” Rig., Venet.]

You have the Christ of the Prophets.

But why did he choose twelve apostles, and not some other number? Indeed, from this too I could interpret him as my Christ, proclaimed not only by the words of the Prophets but also by the symbolic evidence of events. For I discover prefigurations of this number in the Creator’s [Col. 0387B] Scriptures: the twelve springs of Elim (Num. 33); the twelve gems on Aaron’s priestly vestment (Exod. 28); and the twelve stones chosen by Joshua from the Jordan (Josh. 4) and deposited in the ark of the covenant. For the same number of apostles was being foreshadowed: like springs and rivers, they would water the world of the nations, formerly dry and barren of [the knowledge of God];

[Note: [Col. 0387D] The editors conjecture that “of God” has been omitted.]

as also through Isaiah (Isa. 43:20): “I shall set rivers in a waterless land”; like gems, they would illuminate the sacred vestment of the Church, which Christ, the Father’s high priest, puts on; and like stones, they would be solid in faith—those whom the true Jesus chose from the washing of the Jordan and received into the sanctuary of his covenant.

What comparable defense of the number is applicable to Marcion’s Christ? Nothing done by him can be regarded as merely incidental when it can be regarded [Col. 0387C] as having been done by my Christ with a definite purpose. The event will belong to the one in whose Scriptures its preparation is found.

He also changes Peter’s name from Simon, because the Creator likewise altered the names of Abraham, Sarah, and Hoshea, calling the latter Jesus—

[Note: [Col. 0387D] “Calling the latter Jesus.” This is the same as Joshua. EDD.]

—and adding syllables to the names of the former. But why “Peter”? If it was because of the strength of his faith, many names drawn from strong and solid substances would have been available to him. Or was it because Christ too is both rock and stone? For we also read (Isa. 8:14; 1 Pet. 2:8) that he was appointed “as a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense.” I omit the rest. Thus he desired to share particularly with the dearest of his disciples a name drawn from his own figures—more properly, I think,

[Note: [Col. 0387D] “More nearly,” Rig., Venet., Seml.]

than from figures that were not his own.

A multitude comes together from Tyre and from other regions, even from across the sea. This—

[Note: [Col. 0387D] “These,” Ciaccon.]

—the Psalm had in view (Ps. 86:4): “And behold, the Philistines and Tyre, [Col. 0387D] and the people of the Ethiopians—these were there. [Col. 0388A] ‘Mother Zion,’ a man shall say; ‘and a man was made in her’” (because God was born as man), “and he built her by the Father’s will.” This is so that you may know that the Gentiles then assembled to him because God had been born as man and was going to build the Church by the Father’s will, even from foreigners.

Isaiah likewise says (Isa. 49:12): “Behold, these come from afar; these come from the north and the sea; and others from the land of the Persians.” Concerning them he says again (ibid. 18): “Lift up your eyes round about and see: they have all been gathered together.” And further below concerning them (ibid. 21): “You see—

[Note: [Col. 0387D] “Seeing,” Lat., Jun.]

—the unknown and the foreign; and you will say in your heart, ‘Who has begotten these for me? Moreover, who has brought these up for me? And where were these people of mine?’”

Is this not the Christ of the Prophets? And who will be the Christ of the Marcionites—if perversity is preferred—if he is not the Christ of the Prophets?

## CHAPTER XIV

[Col. 0388B]

I now come to his customary pronouncements, through which he presents the distinctive character of his teaching in what I might call Christ’s edict: “Blessed are the beggars” (for this is what the proper rendering of the Greek word requires), “because theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

[Note: [Col. 0387D] “Blessed are the beggars, for this is what the translation requires,” etc. The Greek at Luke 6 is πτωχοί, which properly means “beggars,” in accordance with the expression from Aristophanes’ *Plutus*, πτωχοῦ vita vivere, “to live a beggar’s life,” that is, the life of one who possesses nothing; whereas πένητος, that is, “of a poor person,” means to live sparingly and devote oneself to labor. Above, however, in *On Idolatry*, chapter 12, he translated it “happy are the needy.” PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0388D] “Of God,” Seml., Oberth.]

The very fact that he began with blessings belongs to the Creator, who, when he brought the whole universe forth, consecrated it with no utterance other than one of blessing.

[Note: [Col. 0388D] “With blessings,” Rig., Venet.]

“For my heart,” he says (Ps. 44:1), “has poured forth an excellent word.” This will be the excellent word—the word of blessing, namely—which is also recognized as inaugurating the New Testament after the example of the Old.

[Note: [Col. 0388D] “Of the Old,” Jun.]

What wonder is it, then,

[Note: [Col. 0388D] “Is” omitted by Seml.]

if he also began from the Creator’s own affections through such a pronouncement—the Creator who has always loved, consoled, defended, and vindicated beggars, the poor, the humble, widows, [Col. 0388C] and orphans? Thus you may believe that this kindness, supposedly Christ’s private possession, is a stream flowing from the Savior’s fountains.

In truth, amid so great an abundance of statements of this kind, I do not know where to turn. As though in a forest, a meadow, or a grove of fruit trees, the desired object must be gathered wherever one happens upon it.

The Psalm cries out (Ps. 81:3): “Judge for the orphan and the beggar, and treat the humble and the poor justly. Deliver the poor man, and rescue the beggar from the sinner’s hand.”

[Note: [Col. 0388D] “The Psalm cries out: Judge,” etc. This passage from Psalm 81 differs from the published Vulgate in reading “treat justly,” where all the others, following the Greek δικαιάσατε, have “justify.” PAM.]

Likewise Psalm 71: “He shall judge the beggars of the people in righteousness and shall save the children of the poor.” And in the following words, concerning Christ: “All nations shall serve him.” David, however, ruled over the single Jewish nation, lest anyone suppose that this was said of David merely because he too took in the humble and those afflicted by need.

[Col. 0388D] “For he shall deliver—

[Note: [Col. 0388D] “He delivered,” others.]

—the beggar from the ruler,” he says; [Col. 0389A] “he shall spare the beggar and the poor, and shall save the souls of the poor. From usury and injustice he shall redeem their souls, and their name shall be held in honor in his sight.”

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “Before him,” Fran.]

Likewise (Ps. 9:18): “Let sinners be turned back into hell—all nations that forget God—because the beggar shall not be forgotten forever; the endurance of the poor shall not perish forever.”

Likewise (Ps. 112:5): “Who is like our God, who dwells in the heights and looks upon the lowly in heaven and on earth? He raises the beggar from the earth and lifts the poor man from the dunghill, so that he may make him sit with the princes of the people”—surely in his kingdom.

So too, earlier in the *Books of Kingdoms*,

[Note: [Col. 0389D] *Basiliis*, that is, the Books of Kings, among which our author also numbers the first two, called Samuel by the Hebrews and consequently by Protestants. EDD.]

Hannah, Samuel’s mother, gives glory to God in the Spirit: “He,” she says (1 Sam. 2:8), “raises the poor man from the earth and the beggar, so that he may make him sit with the rulers of the people”—[Col. 0389B] surely in his kingdom—

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “Surely in his kingdom” omitted by Seml.]

“and upon thrones of glory”—surely royal thrones.

Through Isaiah, moreover (Isa. 3:13), how he inveighs against the oppressors of the needy! “But why do you set fire to my vineyard, and why is the plunder of the beggar in your houses? Why do you injure my people and shame the faces of beggars?” And again (Isa. 10:1): “Woe to those who write wickedness; for when they write, they write evil, evading the judgments of beggars and plundering the judgments of the poor among my people.”

He also demands those judgments for orphans and widows, so that they too, being needy, may receive consolation.

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “Of consolation,” Rig., Pamel., Venet.]

“Judge for the orphan and treat the widow justly” (Isa. 1:17),

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “And come, let us be reconciled, says the Lord.” He seems to have read διαλλάχθωμεν, for others translate “let us dispute,” [Col. 0390D] reading διαλεχθῶμεν. LE PR.]

“and come, let us be reconciled, says the Lord.”

To the one whose affection for every condition of lowliness is so great—that is, to the Creator—will also belong the kingdom promised by Christ; for those [Col. 0389C] to whom it is promised have long belonged to his affection. For even if you suppose that the Creator’s promises were earthly, whereas Christ’s were heavenly, it is well that, up to the present, heaven appears to belong to no other God than the one to whom the earth also belongs. It is also well that the Creator promised the lesser things, so that I may more easily trust him concerning the greater, rather than one who had never first established confidence in his generosity by lesser gifts.

“Blessed are those who hunger, because they shall be filled.”

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “They” omitted by Seml.]

I could have transferred this title to the preceding discussion, since those who hunger are none other than the poor and beggars, had not the Creator also specifically appointed this promise as a preparation for his own Gospel. For through Isaiah, concerning those whom he was going to summon from the end of the earth—surely the nations—he says: “Behold, they shall come swiftly and [Col. 0389D] lightly” (Isa. 5:26). “Swiftly,” because

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “Because,” Seml.]

they hasten at the end of the ages; “lightly,” because

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “Because,” Seml.]

they are without the burdens [Col. 0390A] of the former law. “They shall not hunger, nor shall they thirst”: therefore they shall be filled, something surely not promised except to those who hunger and thirst.

And again (Isa. 65:15): “Behold,” he says, “those who serve me shall be filled, but you shall hunger; behold, those who serve me shall drink, but you shall thirst.” We shall also see whether these antitheses are prepared in advance for Christ. For the present, the fact that he promises fullness to the hungry belongs to God the Creator.

“Blessed are those who weep, because they shall laugh.”

[Note: [Col. 0389D] “Who,” Venet.]

Run through Isaiah’s pronouncement (Isa. 65:13): “Behold, those who serve me shall exult in gladness—

[Note: [Col. 0390D] “Shall rejoice,” Oberth.]

—but you shall be confounded. Behold, those who serve me shall rejoice—

[Note: [Col. 0390D] “Shall exult in gladness,” Oberth.]

—but you shall cry out because of the pain of your heart.” Recognize these same antitheses in Christ. Certainly joy and exultation in gladness are promised to those who are in the opposite condition: [Col. 0390B] the sorrowful, the sad, and the distressed. Psalm 125 likewise says: “Those who sow,” it says, “in tears shall reap in exultation.”

[Note: [Col. 0390D] “In joy,” others.]

Laughter, moreover, accompanies those who exult and experience gladness, just as weeping accompanies those who mourn and suffer pain. Thus the Creator, when he foretold the occasions for laughter and weeping, had already said that those who weep would laugh.

Therefore, the one who began by consoling the poor, the humble, the hungry, and those who weep immediately sought to show himself to be the one whom he had revealed through Isaiah (Isa. 61:1): “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to proclaim good news to the poor.” “Blessed” (Luke 6) “are the beggars, because theirs is the kingdom of heaven.” “He sent me” (Isa. 61) “to heal those broken in heart.” “Blessed” (Luke) “are those who hunger, because they—

[Note: [Col. 0390D] “They” omitted by Seml.]

—shall be filled.” “To comfort” (Isaiah) “the afflicted.”

[Note: [Col. 0390D] “To comfort the afflicted.” *Advocare* means “to console” and “to act as advocate for the afflicted,” whom he calls below an *advocator*, “advocate.” RHEN.]

“Blessed” [Col. 0390C] (Luke) “are those who weep, because they shall laugh.” “To give” (Isaiah) “glory to the mourners of Zion, and—

[Note: [Col. 0390D] “And” omitted by Fran.]

—the joy of ointment in place of ashes, and the garment of glory in place of the spirit of weariness.”

If Christ, immediately upon his admission, carried out these things, then either he is himself the one who foretold that he would come for them; or, if the one who foretold them has not yet come, I must say—ridiculously, but necessarily—that perhaps he entrusted them to Marcion’s Christ.

“Blessed shall you be when men hate you, reproach you, and cast out your name as evil—

[Note: [Col. 0390D] “Bad,” Pamel.]

—because of the Son of Man.” By this declaration he undoubtedly exhorts them to endurance.

What less does the Creator say through Isaiah (Isa. 51:7)? “Do not fear disgrace from men, and do not be diminished by their contempt.” What disgrace? What contempt? That which was to come because of the Son of Man. Which Son of Man? The one who exists according to [Col. 0390D] the Creator. How shall we prove it? From the destruction foretold even against him, [Col. 0391A] as through Isaiah (Isa. 52:7) to the Jews, the authors of the hatred:

[Note: [Col. 0391D] “Of the hatred” omitted by Seml.]

“Because of you my name is blasphemed among the nations.” And elsewhere: “Sanctify—

[Note: [Col. 0391D] “Sanctify,” Latin codex.]

—the one who restricts his own soul, who is held in contempt by the nations, servants, and magistrates.”

For if hatred was foretold against that Son of Man who belongs to the Creator, while the Gospel testifies that the name “Christian,” which is of course derived from Christ, would be hated because of the Son of Man—that is, Christ—then it identifies as the cause of the hatred that Son of Man who belongs to the Creator and against whom hatred was foretold.

And surely, if those things had not yet come to pass, the hatred of the name that is occurring today could not have preceded the person who bears the name. For among us he is both sanctified and restricts his own soul, laying it down [Col. 0391B] for us; and he is held in contempt by the nations. The one who was born will himself be the Son of Man because of whom our name also is rejected.

## CHAPTER XV

“Accordingly,” he says (Luke 6), “their fathers used to do these things to the Prophets.” O shape-shifting Christ, now the destroyer and now the defender of the Prophets! He destroyed them as a rival by drawing away their disciples; he claimed them for himself as a friend by branding their persecutors.

Now, just as the defense of the Prophets would have been inappropriate—

[Note: [Col. 0391D] “Not” is absent in others.]

—for Marcion’s Christ, since he had come to destroy them, so the branding of their persecutors is appropriate for the Creator’s Christ, since he was fulfilling them in every respect. Or again, it belongs more properly to the Creator to reproach sons with their fathers’ offenses than to that god who does not punish even the personal offenses of each individual.

“But,” you say, “he was not immediately [Col. 0391C] defending the Prophets merely because he wished the iniquity of the Jews to be affirmed, since they had not treated even their own Prophets piously.” On the contrary, there was no iniquity here for which the Jews should have been reproached. They should rather have been praised and approved, if they prefigured the treatment of those whom the supremely good god was moved to destroy after so great a span of time.

But I think he is already no longer supremely good: he has already acquired some character along with the Creator,

[Note: [Col. 0391D] “He has already acquired some character along with the Creator.” He calls something *moratum* when it is endowed with certain characteristics such as those of the Creator—namely hatred, benevolence, anger, mercy, justice, and clemency. RIG.]

and is no longer entirely the god of Epicurus.

[Note: [Col. 0391D] “Nor entirely the god of Epicurus”: stupefied, inert, ἀλειτούργητος, “performing no service.” RIG.—The Epicureans maintained that God lives free from care, without any business or concern for human affairs; for this reason they rejected dreams, lest they impose business upon God. “The Epicurean,” says Tertullian in *On the Soul*, chapter 46, “judged dreams altogether vain, freeing the divinity from business.” Plutarch says in *On Tranquillity of Mind* that the Stoics were for a time more moderate: ἀπράγμονας εἴναι τοὺς σοφοὺς, ἐκκλίνειν γὰρ τὸ καθῆκον, “The wise are free from business, for they even avoid duty.” Seneca mentions both schools in *On the Happy Life*. LE PR.]

For behold, he changes over to a curse and reveals himself as one who knows how to take offense and become angry: for he says, “Woe.”

But a question arises for us concerning the character of this word, on the supposition that it is not so much a curse as an admonition. Yet what difference does the reason make, when even an admonition is not without the sting of a threat, [Col. 0392A] especially when it is made more bitter by “Woe”? Both admonition and threat belong to one who knows how to become angry. For no one will admonish, and no one will threaten someone not to do a thing, unless he will punish it when done; and no one will punish unless he knows how to become angry.

Others do acknowledge that the word is a curse, but they maintain that Christ pronounced “Woe” in this fashion not properly as an expression of his own judgment, but because “Woe” belongs to the Creator and he wished to set the Creator’s harshness over against them, so that he might commend his own forbearance in the preceding blessings.

[Note: [Col. 0392D] “Might commend his forbearance.” *Sufferentia* is a substantive used for patience, which he presently also interprets as kindness. RHEN.]

As though this did not properly belong to the Creator, who supplies both functions, both as good God and as judge! Since he had set kindness first in the blessings, he could also append severity in the curses, establishing the full range of both forms of discipline: both the blessing that must be pursued [Col. 0392B] and the curse that must be avoided. For he had also set them forth in this way (Deut. 30:19): “Behold, I have set before you blessing and curse.” This also foreshadowed the present arrangement of the Gospel.

Otherwise, what sort of god is he who, in order to suggest his own goodness, set against it the harshness of the Creator—

[Note: [Col. 0392D] “Of God” added by Seml.; “of the other God,” Rhen.]

? A weak commendation—

[Note: [Col. 0392D] “A weak commendation.” This passage, corrupted through the transposition of words, could be restored from the Gorze collation: “a weak commendation,” etc. RHEN.]

—is one that is supported by the destruction of another.

[Note: [Col. 0392D] “Of another” omitted by others.]

Yet—

[Note: [Col. 0392D] “Yet indeed,” Seml.]

by opposing the Creator’s harshness, he confirmed that the Creator must be feared. If he must be feared, surely he must be obeyed rather than disregarded; and now Marcion’s Christ begins to teach obedience to the Creator.

Then, if the “Woe” directed toward the rich belongs to the Creator, Christ therefore is not offended by the rich, but the Creator is; and Christ approves the causes of the rich—I mean pride, glory, worldly pursuits, and neglect of God—by which they deserve a “Woe” from [Col. 0392C] the Creator.

But how can it fail to belong to the same one to condemn the rich who above approved the beggars? Everyone condemns the opposite of what he has approved. Thus, if the curse against the rich is assigned to the Creator, the blessing of the beggars will be defended as belonging to the same; and now Christ’s entire work belongs to the Creator. If the blessing of the beggars is ascribed to Marcion’s god, the curse against the rich will likewise be imposed upon him; and he will now be the Creator’s equal, both good and judge. There will no longer be room for the distinction by which two gods are made; and once the distinction has been removed, it will remain to proclaim that there is one God, the Creator.

Therefore, if “Woe” is an utterance of cursing, or at least some sterner exclamation, and is directed by Christ against the rich, I must prove that the Creator likewise despises the rich, just as [Col. 0393A] I proved him the advocate of beggars, so that in this pronouncement too I may demonstrate that Christ belongs to the Creator.

The Creator did indeed—

[Note: [Col. 0393C] “Indeed” omitted by Pamel.]

—enrich Solomon (1 Kings 3), but this was because—

[Note: [Col. 0393C] “Who,” Jun.]

—when a choice was granted to him, he preferred to request the things that he knew were pleasing to God—wisdom—and had consequently deserved to obtain even the riches that he had not especially desired. And yet the granting of riches is not inconsistent with God, since by means of riches even the rich are assisted with consolation, and many works of righteousness and love are thereby administered.

But it is the vices that attach themselves to riches which are assigned to the Gospel’s “Woe to the rich”: “Because,” he says, “you have received your consolation,” surely from riches, from their glory and worldly fruits.

[Note: [Col. 0393D] “Because,” he says, “you have received your consolation”—that is, comfort. He is continuing the words of Luke 6, differing in that he translates the Greek word ἀπέχετε as “you have received,” both here and shortly afterward, whereas others have “you have had.” He apparently wished to explain the sense in which it means to receive something from another source, like revenue and income from fields. This is especially appropriate here, and the Vulgate translator renders it in the same way at Matthew 6: “They have received their reward.” The Syriac translator also renders it thus. The translator of Irenaeus, book III, chapter 14, renders it not very differently, though more suitably to the Greek word: “you receive.” PAM.]

Thus Moses says in Deuteronomy (Deut. 8:12): “Take care lest, when you have eaten and been filled, and [Col. 0393B] have built great houses, and your flocks and cattle have multiplied, and your silver and gold have increased, your heart be exalted and you forget the Lord your God.”

[Note: [Col. 0394D] “You have built great houses,” etc. The Greek manuscript requires “beautiful.” He has used “flocks” here for “sheep,” although the Greek reads προβάτων, and he ought to have translated it “sheep,” so that he could afterward add the multiplied cattle, or cloven-hoofed animals. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0393C] “Have been multiplied,” Pamel.]

Likewise, when King Hezekiah was puffed up by his treasures and gloried in them rather than in God before those who had come from Persia, the Creator attacks him through Isaiah (Isa. 39:6): “Behold, the days are coming, and all the things in your house shall be taken away; and the things your fathers amassed shall be carried off to Babylon.”

So too, through Jeremiah (Jer. 9:22), he declares:

[Note: [Col. 0393C] “Declared,” Seml.]

“Let not the rich man glory in his riches”; and he who glories should, of course, glory in God. So too, through Isaiah (Isa. 3:15), he inveighs against the daughters of Zion, puffed up by adornment and abundance of riches. Elsewhere also (Isa. 5:14), he threatens the nobles and the proud: “Hell has enlarged its soul and opened its mouth, and the renowned and the great and the rich shall descend”—this will be Christ’s [Col. 0393C] “Woe” upon the rich—“and man shall be humbled,” surely the man exalted by riches, “and the man shall be dishonored,”

[Note: [Col. 0393C] “Shall be honored,” some.]

surely the man who was honorable on account of his possessions.

Concerning them he again says (Isa. 10:23): “Behold, the Lord of hosts shall confound the glorious with might, and the exalted shall be crushed, and the lofty shall fall by the sword.” Who are these more than the rich? For they have received—

[Note: [Col. 0393D] “Shall have received,” Pamel.]

—their consolation—that is, their glory, honor, and eminence arising from riches.

[Note: [Col. 0394D] “They have received their consolation,” namely. I think he has nothing else in view here than the words of the Gospel rebuking the rich and powerful: “Amen I say to you, they have received their reward.” LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0394C] “And riches,” Jun.]

Turning us away from these things, he also says in Psalm 48: “Do not be afraid when a man has become rich and when his glory abounds, because when he dies he shall not take everything with him, nor shall his glory descend with him.” Likewise in Psalm 61: “Do not desire riches; and if they shine, do not set [Col. 0394A] your heart upon them.”

[Note: [Col. 0394C] “You shall not desire … you shall not set,” Seml.]

Finally, the very word “Woe” was long ago directed through Amos against the rich who overflow with pleasures: “Woe—

[Note: [Col. 0394C] “For” added by Oberth.]

,” he says (Amos 6:4), “to those who sleep on ivory beds and luxuriate upon their couches; who eat kids from the flocks of goats and suckling calves from the herds of cattle, clapping their hands to the sound of instruments. They regarded these things as permanent and not as passing away; they drink strained wine and anoint themselves with the finest ointments.”

Therefore, even if I were to show merely that the Creator discouraged riches, without also showing that he condemned the rich beforehand by the very word Christ used, no one would doubt that Christ’s threat against the rich by means of “Woe” was added by the same one whose discouragement of its very subject matter—that is, riches—had preceded it. For a threat is an addition to discouragement.

[Note: [Col. 0394C] Venet. corruptly reads: “the communication chosen against the rich.”]

He presses the point: [Col. 0394B] “Woe also to those who are filled, because they shall hunger; and to those who laugh now, because they shall mourn.” These will correspond to the statements in the Creator’s Scriptures that were set above in opposition to the blessings: “Behold, those who serve me shall be filled, but you shall hunger” (Isa. 65:12)—surely because you have been filled. And: “Behold, those who serve me shall rejoice, but you shall be confounded”—surely because you who now laugh are going to weep.

For just as in the Psalm (Ps. 125:6), “Those who sow in tears shall reap in gladness,” so in the Gospel those who sow in laughter—that is, in gladness—

[Note: [Col. 0394C] “From gladness,” Rig., Venet.; “and gladness,” Rhen.]

—shall reap in tears. The Creator long ago set these things together; Christ renewed them merely by distinguishing them, not by changing them.

“Woe when men bless you: their fathers used to do the same things to the false prophets.” The Creator likewise denounces through Isaiah (Isa. 3:12) those who pursue human blessing and praise: “My people, those who call you blessed [Col. 0394C] deceive you and disturb the paths of your feet.” Elsewhere he also forbids trust in man altogether, and therefore trust in human praise, as through Jeremiah (Jer. 17:5): “Cursed is the man who places his hope in man.”

And in Psalm 117:

[Note: [Col. 0394C] “You shall not desire … you shall not set,” Seml.]

[Note: [Col. 0394C] “For” added by Oberth.]

“It is good to trust in the Lord rather than to trust in man.” And: “It is good to hope in the Lord rather than to hope in princes.” Thus the Creator rejected everything sought from man, much more a blessing.

For the Creator to reproach their fathers with having praised or blessed false prophets belongs to him just as much as to reproach them for having harassed and rejected the Prophets. Just as injuries to the Prophets would not have concerned [Col. 0395A] the God of the Prophets, so favors shown to false prophets would not have displeased him, unless he were the God of the Prophets.

## CHAPTER XVI

“But I say to you,” he says (Luke 6), “who hear”—showing that this had long ago been commanded by the Creator (4 Esdras 15:1): “Speak into the ears of those who hear.”

“Love your enemies, and bless those who hate you, and pray for those who slander you.”

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “And bless those who hate you,” etc. He seems to combine the Gospel’s words according to their sense; they occur in Luke 6: those who are enemies and hate, curse, and slander. LE PR.]

The Creator included all these things in a single declaration through Isaiah (Isa. 66:5): “Say, ‘You are our brothers,’ to those who hate you.” For if those who are enemies and who hate, curse, and slander must be called brothers, then surely the one who commanded that they be regarded as brothers also commanded that those who hate be blessed and that prayers be offered for slanderers.

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “Three,” Venet., corruptly.]

Plainly, Christ teaches a new patience—

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “Plainly, he teaches a new patience,” etc. This is written according to Marcion’s view, for I do not think these are Marcion’s own words. LE PR.]

—even restraining the repayment of injury permitted [Col. 0395B] by the Creator (Exod. 21:24), who required “an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.” Christ, by contrast, commands that the other cheek be offered in addition and that, over and above the tunic, the cloak also be surrendered.

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “An eye for an eye,” etc. The same words occur in *On the Pallium*, chapter 6. LE PR.]

Plainly, Christ must have added these things as supplements consistent with the Creator’s discipline. Accordingly, the question that must immediately be settled is whether the discipline of patience is proclaimed—

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “Proclaims,” Jun.]

—in the Creator’s Scriptures.

If through Zechariah he commanded (Zech. 7:10) that no one should remember his brother’s malice—and not even his neighbor’s, for he says again, “Let no one—

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “Let each,” Seml.]

—ponder the malice of his neighbor” (ibid. 8:17)—then the one who commanded forgetfulness much more emphatically commanded patience under injury. Moreover, when he says (Deut. 32:35), “Vengeance is mine, and I shall avenge,” he likewise teaches the patience that waits for vengeance.

Therefore,

[Note: [Col. 0395D] Oberth., following Seml., inserts “faith.”]

insofar as it is inconceivable that the same one should appear to require tooth for tooth and eye for eye in repayment of injury who prohibits not only repayment but also vengeance and even the remembering and reconsidering of injury, [Col. 0395C] to that same extent it is disclosed to us how he ordained eye for eye and tooth for tooth: not to permit a second injury by way of retaliation—which he had prohibited by forbidding vengeance—but to restrain the first injury, which he had prohibited by setting retaliation before it,

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “By retaliation set before it,” Seml.; “by an opposing retaliation,” Pamel.]

so that each person, considering the possibility of a second injury, might restrain himself from the first. For he knows that violence is more easily checked by the presentation of retaliation than by the promise of vengeance.

Both measures had to be established in view of the nature and faith of human beings: so that the one who believed God would await vengeance from God, while the one who trusted—

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “Who had confidence,” others.]

less [Col. 0396A] would fear the laws of retaliation.

The Lord of the Sabbath, the Law, and all the Father’s arrangements—Christ—both revealed this intention of the Law, which had suffered from lack of understanding, and made it intelligible by commanding that the other cheek also be offered. His purpose was all the more fully to extinguish the repayment of injury, which the Law too had sought to prevent through retaliation and which prophecy had certainly restrained explicitly, both by forbidding the remembrance of injury and by referring vengeance to God.

Thus, if Christ introduced anything, he did not destroy the Creator’s disciplines, since his command was not opposed to them but assisted them.

Finally, if we consider the very rationale for commanding patience, and indeed such full and perfect patience,

[Note: [Col. 0395D] “If I consider,” Pamel.]

it will not stand unless it belongs to the Creator, who promises vengeance and presents himself as judge. Otherwise, if the one who imposes upon me so great a burden of patience—not merely refraining from striking back but also offering the other cheek; not merely refraining from cursing in return but even blessing; and not merely refraining from withholding my tunic but conceding my cloak as well—is not going to defend me, [Col. 0396B] then he has commanded patience in vain, since he does not provide me with the reward of the command. By this I mean the fruit of patience, which is vengeance. He ought to have permitted it to me if he does not himself provide it; or, if he did not permit it to me, he ought himself to provide it—

[Note: [Col. 0396D] “Should provide it,” Pamel.]

—because it is also relevant to discipline that injury be avenged. For through fear of vengeance all iniquity is restrained. Otherwise, once unrestricted liberty is let loose everywhere, it will rule supreme, gouging out both eyes and knocking every tooth loose.

[Note: [Col. 0396D] “And knocking every tooth loose.” What is “roused” is virtually moved from its place. Thus he elegantly says that when a fist is driven into the jaw, every tooth is knocked loose.
