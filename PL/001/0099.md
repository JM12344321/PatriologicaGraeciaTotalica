Our author continues: “The Christian, though his eyes are intact, does not see women” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500). For thus we read this passage in the manuscript codices and in the editions of Rigault and later editors; but in the older printed editions: “The Christian, though his eyes are intact, sees a woman” [Col. 0922B]. Yet Tertullian’s meaning is readily understood to be this: although the Christian possesses sound and healthy bodily eyes, he nevertheless does not see women in such a way as to desire them in his mind. For he immediately added: “In his mind he is blind to lust” (*Ibid.*). He knew, of course, the law laid down by Christ: “Everyone who looks at a woman in order to desire her has already committed adultery with her in his heart” (Matt., ch. 5, v. 28). Therefore, in order that a true disciple of Christ may fulfill this precept, he sees women with his bodily eyes intact, but in the mind with which he might desire them he is blind and unmoved.

How great, by contrast, was the incontinence even of pagan philosophers Tertullian shows by the example of Democritus: “Democritus,” he says, “by blinding himself, because he could not look upon a woman without desire [Col. 0922C] and would suffer pain if he did not possess her, acknowledges his incontinence by the very act of correcting it” (Tertullian, *ibid.*). But there may be no slight reason to doubt both this voluntary blindness of Democritus and its cause. For Plutarch plainly and utterly denies that he extinguished his own eyes of his own accord so that they might not distract his mind or prevent it from dwelling upon things discernible by the mind alone (Plutarch, book *On Curiosity*, p. 521, vol. II). Cicero, moreover, appears to pronounce nothing certain about his blindness: “Democritus,” he says, “who—whether truly or falsely we might inquire—is said to have deprived himself of his eyes; certainly so that his mind might be distracted as little as possible from its reflections” (Cicero, book V, *On Ends*, p. 113, line 43). For if, as some think, the word “we might inquire” should there be deleted, and the reading should be “whether falsely,” he plainly signifies that he himself was [Col. 0922D] doubtful and undecided about that blindness of Democritus. But even if the word “we might inquire” proceeded from Cicero himself, nevertheless by the other expression, “is said,” he declares clearly enough that the matter could not be affirmed by him with certainty. Aulus Gellius, however, testifies that in the records of Greek history mention was made of Democritus, who voluntarily deprived himself of the light of his eyes so that the reflections of his mind in contemplating the principles of nature might be more vigorous and exact (Gellius, book X, *Attic Nights*, ch. 17, p. 541). He then immediately cites verses of Laberius describing another reason for this blindness which Democritus had brought upon himself, expressed by the poet in this line:

> That he might not see wicked citizens prosper.

[Col. 0923A] Jerome, without naming anyone individually, relates that certain philosophers tore out their own eyes so that they might not be distracted from the contemplation of philosophy, and might direct their entire thought toward purity of mind (Jerome, book II *Against Jovinian*, p. 203, and Epistle 55 to Abigaus, p. 589). Who, then, would not readily allow himself to be persuaded that Democritus was one of these philosophers, and perhaps the foremost?

Although, therefore, some appear to have doubted his voluntary blindness, we have nevertheless cited several witnesses to it. But certainly far fewer are found who report that this same Democritus deprived himself of his eyes so that he would not desire women. Yet I would have you consider whether the sophist Himerius, as preserved by Photius, did not indicate this in the following words: [non-Latin text omitted] [Col. 0923B] [non-Latin text omitted]. “Democritus afflicted his body with a voluntary infirmity so that the better part might be sound” (Photius, *Bibliotheca*, cod. 243, p. 1087). For did he not, by this general manner of speaking, designate Democritus’s voluntary blindness, by which he wished to heal his mind, the better part of himself? However that may be, Christians certainly had no need of voluntary blindness of this kind, since, being blind in mind to desire, they not only cultivated with the greatest zeal the chastity prescribed by divine law, but very many also, as we said shortly before (*Supra*, this chapter, art. 4), were accustomed to persevere in the state of virginity throughout the whole course of their lives.

Finally, by a still recent example Tertullian proves that Christians were all the more solicitous to preserve chastity, the more shamelessly this virtue was violated by the pagans [Col. 0923C]. Hear, I ask you, the words with which he addresses and assails these enemies of chastity: “Most recently,” he says, “by condemning a Christian woman to a procurer rather than to a lion, you confessed that among us the violation of chastity is regarded as more dreadful than every punishment and every death” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 50, p. 530). Although that Christian woman is unknown to us, no one can nevertheless call into doubt that shortly before she had been condemned, by a most unjust and shameful judgment, to a brothel, where her chastity was to be overcome. Yet the vilest men made a mockery not merely of the chastity of one woman, but of that of many others. We think there is no one who has not read numerous examples of them in the *Acts of the Martyrs*.

## CHAPTER XIII

### How absurdly the pagans boasted that the other virtues of their philosophers were equal to those of the Christians, and how Tertullian demonstrates that the virtues of the latter were genuine and those of the former false

[Col. 0923D]

## ARTICLE I

### With what undeserved praises, statues, and salaries the pagans rewarded philosophers who voluntarily destroyed the worship of the gods, railed against rulers, and did not cast out demons—which they regarded as second only to the gods—although, contrary to right and justice, they killed Christians by every kind of torture merely because of their name

Our Tertullian compares not only the chastity of Christians and pagans, but all the virtues of both [Col. 0924A], and demonstrates that the virtues of the latter were corrupt and those of the former perfect. He conducts this comparison all the more carefully because the pagans used to cry out all the more insistently that the philosophers professed innocence, justice, patience, and sobriety equal to those of the Christians (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500). For, to omit many others, Celsus argued in the same way against the Christians (Origen, book I *Against Celsus*, p. 6). The Christian discipline, he said, introduced nothing new and nothing great in morals that had not been shared with the other ancient philosophers and handed down by them.

Tertullian therefore first refutes and demolishes the pagans’ objection by a general argument. If, he says, the morals of philosophers and Christians are the same, and their virtues the same [Col. 0924B], both must surely possess the same freedom to profess their religion. Why, then, are Christians, but not philosophers, compelled—as will be explained below (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*)—to sacrifice or swear by the gods, or “bring forth useless lamps at midday” (Rigault, contrary to the authority of the manuscript and printed codices, reads “prostitute”)? “Why,” our author continues, “do the philosophers openly destroy your gods and assail your superstitions even in their commentaries, while you applaud them?” Such, indeed, were Diagoras, Theodorus, and Nicagoras, whom we have discussed elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius*, ch. 23, art. 2, and *Dissertation above*, ch. 14, art. 1). But because he added “while you applaud them” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 14, p. 348), he perhaps appears more particularly to indicate Socrates, whom he had named both immediately and previously [Col. 0924C], as well as Epicurus, his follower and admirer Lucretius, and Cicero, by whom the pagan gods are utterly abolished in his books concerning their nature.

When our author added (*Ibid.*, ch. 46, p. 500), “They accuse your superstitions,” he undoubtedly had Seneca in view, whom he had cited earlier. “For you,” he says there (*Ibid.*, ch. 12, p. 337), “are the same men who approve a certain Seneca when he declaims against your superstition at greater length and with greater bitterness.” We have cited these words of Seneca elsewhere, and they must still be presented below in their proper place.

Furthermore, Tertullian continues (*Ibid.*, ch. 46, p. 500), “Most philosophers bark against rulers.” In one Vatican codex scholars have noted [Col. 0924D] the reading “tear to pieces”; but this is less satisfactory. For he appears deliberately to have said “bark,” and by this word to have censured the Cynics. According to Suetonius, the emperor Vespasian, when Demetrius the Cynic met him on the road after his condemnation and did not deign either to rise or to greet him, but even barked out something or other at him, contented himself with calling him a dog (Suetonius, book VIII, *Life of Vespasian*, §13). Xiphilinus relates that this same Vespasian, at Marcianus’s urging, sent the philosophers into exile, because not only that Cynic Demetrius but other philosophers as well publicly uttered many disgraceful things (Xiphilinus, *Epitome*, book X, p. 220 ff.). In private, however, he ordered the following to be written to Demetrius when the latter would not desist: “Although you do many things deserving of death, and I could therefore order you to be killed: [Col. 0925A] [non-Latin text omitted]. Yet I do not kill a barking dog.”

Nor were the pagans content merely to tolerate such philosophers patiently and to praise them; rather, our author says, “they were very readily rewarded with statues and salaries” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 45, p. 497; *ibid.*, ch. 14, p. 348). Does he not there, without naming Socrates, indicate his golden image, which, as he noted above, the Athenians themselves—by whom Socrates had been put to death—later placed in a temple? If you ask to be shown statues of other philosophers, hear Juvenal castigating ignorant men who thought that they had sufficiently demonstrated a false learning by placing statues and images of philosophers in each of their libraries. Here are his verses:

> [Col. 0925B] First, the ignorant—although everything is full of plaster—  
> You will find a Chrysippus. For the most accomplished of these  
> Is he who buys a likeness of Aristotle or Pittacus  
> And orders an original Cleanthes to guard his shelf.[^1]

(Juvenal, *Satire* 2, v. 4 ff.)

[^1]: [Col. 0925D] That is, his library.

Ménage, moreover, reproduces several images of philosophers in his edition of Laertius.

A salary was also given to philosophers—that is, an allowance and wage, so called from salt, as Pliny observes (Pliny, book XXXI, *Natural History*, ch. 7, p. 809). Capitolinus indeed testifies that the emperor Antoninus ordered salaries and honors to be bestowed upon rhetoricians and philosophers throughout all the provinces (Capitolinus, *Life of Antoninus*, near the end). Lucian, however, recorded for posterity (Lucian, *The Eunuch, or Pamphilus*, [Col. 0925C] p. 535) that the emperor established [non-Latin text omitted] a considerable salary, [non-Latin text omitted] ten thousand coins, to be distributed among Stoics, Platonists, Epicureans, Peripatetics, and other philosophers, so that they might teach the young. Does not Augustine also say of these same philosophers, as well as of others, the following words, which contribute no little to our purpose (Augustine, book I, *City of God*, ch. 3, p. 4)? “The pagans are angry with us when we say such things about their gods, yet they are not angry with their own authors, whom they paid to learn by heart; and, in addition, they regarded the teachers themselves as most worthy both of a public salary and of honors.”

With what effrontery, then, or with what madness did they torture and slaughter Christians by every kind of punishment, while they tolerated their own philosophers with impunity, praised them [Col. 0925D], and rewarded them with great benefactions and prizes, although they advertised the morals and virtues of both groups as similar? Yet Christians were not merely equal to the pagans in virtue but far holier and more excellent, as we have demonstrated with irrefutable force.

Whence, then, so great a discrepancy in their judgments? Because the philosophers, as Tertullian rightly observes, “are not called Christians” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500). For it was with the greatest injustice that the pagans cried out that Christians deserved public hatred merely on account of their name, as has been said, and that they should be punished [Col. 0926A] with every kind of torture. Indeed, on account of their most devout manner of life they were all the more worthy of the same rewards and salaries with which philosophers were showered, since they very frequently cast demons out of the bodies of possessed persons solely by the name of Christ, as we have shown, but never by the name of any philosopher.

And indeed, how could demons be put to flight in the name of philosophers who thought them, as Tertullian continues, to be “second after the gods”—that is, as some understand it, nearest to the gods, or, as others prefer, who regarded them as gods? For Cicero wrote: “to judge concerning an absent person as concerning one who is present.” Suetonius, moreover, wrote of the emperor Claudius: “He gave to those absent as to those present” (Suetonius, book V, *Life of Claudius*, §15), that is, just as if they were present. But both explanations come down to almost the same meaning [Col. 0926B], although the latter appears preferable.

Whatever the case, Tertullian openly declares (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*) that the philosophers believed demons to be superior to them and to rule over them. They were therefore so far from being able to put them to flight that they actually obeyed them. For Socrates, our author continues, although he had been declared the wisest of all by the oracle of Apollo, as we have observed elsewhere (*Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, ch. 3, arts. 2 and 3; *ibid.*, and vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 1115), was accustomed to say: “If the daemonic power”—supply “my”—“permits me to do this or that” (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*, and *Supra*, ch. 22). In this and other dissertations we have shown how he asserted that a demon had attended him from childhood, and that he never did anything without consulting it and receiving its approval (the same author, *Apologeticus*, [Col. 0926C] ch. 46, p. 500). Nevertheless, this same Socrates, when dying, ordered that a cock be sacrificed or slaughtered to Aesculapius. But because he had some taste of the truth, he denied that the gods existed. These matters have been explained by us in another dissertation (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 39, art. 1, and *Dissertation on the Octavius of Minucius*, *loc. cit.*).

Furthermore, whether Socrates acknowledged the gods or denied them, he could certainly not put demons to flight or expel them from the bodies of the possessed, since, like the other pagans, he supposed them to be gods or beings nearest to the gods. But if Christians had been granted the power to confer so great a benefit upon the pagans, and if their morals and virtues were better than those of the pagans, then those same pagans could not, except in violation of all right and justice, kill the former [Col. 0926D] by every penalty and torture, while bestowing great praises and benefits upon the latter. But let us continue to follow Tertullian as he compares the virtues of the two groups individually.

## ARTICLE II

### How absurdly pagan philosophers—lustful for glory, pretending to teach the truth which they had found in Holy Scripture but corrupted in order to win popular favor, and driven into exile by the Spartans, Thebans, and Argives—were equated with Christians in knowledge and discipline

It is remarkable with what vain and foolish ostentation [Col. 0927A] the pagans objected that the knowledge, discipline, and wisdom of their philosophers were equal to those of the Christians (Tertullian, book II *To the Nations*, p. 585). For, as Solomon, cited by Tertullian, most rightly teaches concerning wisdom: “The beginning of wisdom is fear of God” (Ecclesiasticus, ch. 1, v. 16). These words are taken from the book of Ecclesiasticus, which our author and others, as we have shown elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 11, art. 3), expressly attribute to Solomon. Yet even if the work were denied to him, the statement would be no less true. But how could pagan philosophers possess the beginning of wisdom through fearing a God whom they never acknowledged? Christians, however, truly knew and feared him, and were therefore endowed with this beginning of wisdom.

[Col. 0927B] Moreover, Christians, not only from this book but also from other books of Holy Scripture, as from divine and most pure fountains, necessarily pursue the truth from which this wisdom is drawn, says Tertullian, in order to provide for their eternal salvation (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500). Pagan philosophers, however, he says, “affect the truth, and by affecting it they corrupt it, since they seek glory” (*Ibid.*, ch. 47, p. 515). For nothing was more important to them than increasing by any means the renown of their name and reputation and of empty glory. For this reason he later calls them “men lustful only for glory and eloquence” (the same author, *On the Soul*, ch. 7), that is, exceedingly eager for them. In another book, however, he says: “The philosopher is an animal of glory.” Jerome undoubtedly borrowed these words from our author and subscribed to his judgment [Col. 0927C]: “The philosopher is an animal of glory and a base slave of popular favor and rumor” (Jerome, Epistle 54 to Pammachius, p. 585). Lucian, although himself a pagan, attacks these philosophers far more bitterly. For he openly asserts that they are a class of men [non-Latin text omitted]: “idle, contentious, eager for glory, irascible, devoted to gluttony, foolish, ostentatious, insulting, and—to speak in Homer’s words—‘a useless burden upon the earth’” (Lucian, *Icaromenippus*, p. 739 ff.). He says still more in his dialogue *On the Sects* (the same Jerome, p. 284), where he testifies that he had received it as certain from everyone that the Epicureans were corrupted by delicacies and pleasures; that the Peripatetics devoted themselves to acquiring [Col. 0927D] and accumulating riches, and that some of them were excessively contentious; and that the Platonists were arrogant, swollen with excessive pride, and exceedingly desirous of glory: [non-Latin text omitted], he says, [non-Latin text omitted]. We have assembled these statements here in anticipation of what our author is about to say. But we have already touched upon something of these matters in the preceding dissertation.

The philosophers, therefore, adulterated the truth with depraved opinions through a deceitful display of it, lest, if revealed, it offend the mad populace, and so that they themselves might obtain and pursue the false glory of empty learning. Indeed, Tertullian says: “In proportion as hatred burns against the truth, so he who presents it faithfully gives offense. But he who adulterates it wins favor under this very name [Col. 0928A] among the persecutors of the truth” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500). This, and no more, appears in the editions of Rigault and the other editions following it. In the older editions, however, as well as in our manuscript codices, after the word “adulterates” there is added “and affects”; and after the word “truth” one reads: “which”—the printed editions read “who”—“hostilely”—the printed editions read “mimically”—“the philosophers, mockers and corrupters, affect.” If you ask why Rigault excised all this, you will find no reason given by him. What then? Did he perhaps think the words too obscure and therefore to be removed? He would have been right if they had been his own words and not those of an ancient author—words which no editor ever had the right to suppress without giving a reason. But do they not yield this excellent sense? To the degree that truth is hated, the person who teaches it sincerely [Col. 0928B] offends others. But the person who adulterates it by affecting it thereby wins favor among its persecutors—the truth which philosophers affect as its mockers and corrupters. Accordingly, the pagan philosophers, like mimes imitating the words, deeds, and characters of others, affect and corrupt the truth they have recognized. Tertullian, however, appears to have said that philosophers affected the truth not “hostilely,” as is written in the manuscript codices through the carelessness of scribes, who turned the single initial letter *m* into the two letters *in*, but rather “mimically,” just as in another book he said that the soldier of Mithras is, as it were, a mime of martyrdom (the same author, *On the Crown*, near the end, p. 131).

He therefore admits that these same philosophers often recognized the truth: “For who among the poets,” he says, “who among the sophists, has not drunk from the fountain of the prophets? From there, therefore, the philosophers too watered the thirst of their intellect” [Col. 0928C] (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47, p. 515). Thus far the edition of Rigault agrees with the others and also with our manuscript codices; but in the latter the following words, removed by Rigault, are added: “So that they might compare what they possess from our writings with their own.” If these words escaped Rigault or erring printers, who would not wish that he had exercised greater diligence? But if he alone removed them on his own authority, who could excuse his audacity? For they contribute greatly to Tertullian’s purpose. He had undertaken to prove that pagan philosophers were compared with Christians in knowledge in an utterly perverse manner. He rightly demonstrates this by the fact that, in order to compare their own doctrine with ours, they had watered their little gardens, to use Cicero’s words [Col. 0928D], from the sacred fountain of the prophets (the same author, passage cited above, ch. 4, art. 4). Nor was this very difficult for them. For, as he himself says and as we have shown more than once, the sacred Scripture of the Old Testament is far more ancient than all the writings of the pagans. They therefore stole many teachings from our sacred books, as we have proved elsewhere, so that they might be believed equal to Christians in knowledge (*Supra*, ch. 6, art. 4 ff.; vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 384, 531, 909, and 1112 ff.).

But those philosophers, “lustful only for glory and eloquence,” says Tertullian, “if they encountered anything in the Holy Scriptures, once these had been arranged, diverted it, in accordance with the purpose of their curiosity, to their own works” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47, p. 515). But Rigault, despite the opposition of the manuscript and printed [Col. 0929A] codices, deleted the word “Scriptures,” and suspected that “arranged” was a substantive and signified the same thing as “books.” But this is wrong. For it is an adjective, meaning the same as “disposed and ordered,” or “explained and interpreted.” In the former sense Virgil said of the Sibyl:

> Whatever verses the maiden has written upon the leaves,  
> She arranges in order.

(Virgil, book III, *Aeneid*, v. 445.)

That is, Servius says, she orders and disposes them (Servius, *ibid.*, p. 295). Elsewhere Virgil uses it in the second meaning as well:

> . . . Thus Calchas expounds everything.

(Virgil, book II, *Aeneid*, v. 182.)

That is, as Servius again interprets it (Servius, *ibid.*, p. 233), he discloses the movement of the divine will or the future—that is, the order of the oracles—or orders and disposes it [Col. 0929B].

Rigault then wished the words “in accordance with the purpose” to be corrected and written “then transferred back for the purpose”; and finally, after adding the verb “transferred back,” he entirely excised the word “works,” without citing any codex. All the unpublished codices, however, and those printed before Rigault agree with us, except that in the older ones we read “ex improstituto” [a corrupt reading]. In later editions the preposition “from” was removed, and simply “for the purpose” was written. This is indeed better, but we do not venture to define what Tertullian’s true reading may be until some uncorrupted codex is found.

Nevertheless, his meaning appears without doubt to be this: if the philosophers drew anything true from the Holy Scriptures when rightly ordered and [Col. 0929C] explained, they diverted it, in accordance with the purpose of their curiosity—or their excessive zeal to explain those Scriptures badly—to their own works, that is, to the false opinions and popular errors of the common people. For since they did not believe the Scriptures to be divine, they thought that they had been granted the liberty to interpolate them and interpret them at will. Nor, since they failed to understand that the teachings of Scripture were veiled in obscurity and Jewish shadows, did they refrain from scorning its simplicity (Tertullian, *ibid.*, p. 515). Accordingly, what they found certain in them they rendered uncertain; and by rejecting truths or mixing falsehoods with them, they perverted them. But Clement of Alexandria says more about these matters, as we have noted in the proper place (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 871 and 113 ff.).

Tertullian further proves this point (Tertullian, [Col. 0929D] *Apologeticus*, ch. 47, p. 515) by the philosophers’ various opinions and errors concerning the nature, quality, and providence of God; the creation and duration of the world; the nature of the human soul, which some considered incorporeal, divine, and eternal, while others thought it dissoluble and corporeal; and its condition, punishments, and rewards after the destruction of the body. We have elsewhere analyzed and discussed all these various opinions of theirs (*Supra*, ch. 5, art. 4, and ch. 9, art. 1 ff.; *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 12, art. 1 ff.; ch. 14, arts. 1 and 2 ff.; ch. 23, art. 1 f.; and ch. 28, arts. 4 and 6).

Nor does our author argue in this way against the pagan philosophers only in this *Apologeticus*, but also in the second [Col. 0930A] book *To the Nations*, where he further proves that the vanity and weakness of philosophical wisdom are most plainly demonstrated by the diversity of opinions, which arose from nothing other than ignorance of the truth: “For although,” he says, “through curiosity to examine every kind of literature they may appear to have made incursions even into the divine Scriptures, as being more ancient, and to have taken certain things from them” (Tertullian, book II *To the Nations*, ch. 2), nevertheless, scorning their simplicity and being driven besides by a lust for glory, they altered and adulterated the truth found in our sacred Scriptures.

“And hence,” as he says in the *Apologeticus*, “philosophy was also expelled by certain peoples—from the Thebans, Spartans, and Argives—when they are defiled by approaching our teachings” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 47, p. 515). So indeed [Col. 0930B] in Rigault’s and later editions. But in our manuscript codices and the older printed ones: “Hence, I think, also by certain peoples . . . the Spartans . . . while they attempt [to corrupt] our teachings”—wrongly, in some editions, “away from yours”—where “by imitation” must be understood, just as he said, “the fruits strive” (*Ibid.*, ch. 40, p. 478), supply “to become ripe.” Why, by whom, and through whose effort or negligence the word “I think” was omitted, and what harm it does there, no one has told us; just as you will readily persuade others that it is Tertullian’s. For elsewhere he uses this expression in that sense when discussing a matter about which he has no doubt at all.

As for philosophy’s expulsion by the pagans, Athenaeus relates (Athenaeus, book XI, ch. 12, p. 542, and book XIII, ch. 9, p. 160) that sophists and two Epicurean philosophers, Alcaeus and Philiscus, were expelled from their city by the Romans; all members of the same sect from their town by the Messenians [Col. 0930C]; all philosophers whatsoever from their respective kingdoms by Antiochus and Lysimachus; and from Attica by the Athenians under a public edict of Sophocles; and, finally, that philosophy and rhetoric were not admitted by the Lacedaemonians. Julius Pollux also mentions this decree of the Athenians, which he calls [non-Latin text omitted] (Pollux, book XI, p. 427). He does not, however, say that it was issued by Sophocles, but merely mentions it. Laertius, however, openly supports Athenaeus, similarly asserting that Sophocles truly enacted a law forbidding anyone to preside over a philosophical school unless approved by the senate and people, on pain of death (Laertius, book V, *Life of Theophrastus*, §38). But who enacted that law and how it was enacted contributes either nothing or little to our purpose [Col. 0930D]; it is enough that philosophy was rejected by its command.

Aulus Gellius presents to us that decree of the senate and another as well, and adds a third enacted during the reign of Domitian, by which philosophers were banned not only from Rome but from Italy also, and for that reason Epictetus withdrew from there to Nicomedia (Gellius, book XV, *Attic Nights*, ch. 14). Suetonius likewise mentions this edict (Suetonius, book VIII, *Life of Domitian*, §10). Philostratus, however, added that such terror was thereby struck into the philosophers that, changing their dress, some fled to Spain, others to the deserts of Libya and Scythia, while others attached themselves to Domitian as advisers in his crimes (Philostratus, book VII, *Life of Apollonius*, p. 877). Finally [Col. 0931A], to omit more, we have noted elsewhere that Clement of Alexandria committed to writing that Greek philosophy perished as often as it was prohibited by the magistrates (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 823). Moreover, the philosophers deservedly suffered these punishments because they adulterated the truth and, as Tertullian says, “disputed about truth against the truth” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500). But blind desire and lust for human glory and popular favor hurled them headlong into so great an abyss of errors.

Christians, however, scorning the praises of men, “necessarily seek,” says Tertullian, “and preserve inviolate” the truth handed down in the Holy Scriptures, “since they care for their salvation” (*Ibid.*). Hence he properly concludes against the pagans whom he addresses: “We are not equated with you either in knowledge [Col. 0931B] or in discipline, as you suppose” (*Ibid.*). No comparison, therefore, could have been more unequal or perverse than that made by the pagans in equating both groups in knowledge and discipline. Nor did they advertise with any better right that their other virtues were equal and alike. This must now be examined together with our Tertullian.

## ARTICLE III

### How perversely the pagans contended that the moral integrity of their philosophers was equal to that of the Christians, although Diogenes trampled upon Plato’s pride with another kind of pride; how supreme was the modesty of the Christians, whereas Pythagoras and Zeno aspired to tyranny; how great was the Christians’ equanimity, whereas Lycurgus chose death by starvation; how faithfully Christians kept their word, whereas Anaxagoras denied a deposit entrusted to him by guests; and how remarkable was the Christians’ simplicity, whereas Aristotle displaced Hermias from his position and flattered Alexander the Great, Aristippus reveled in purple, and Hippias plotted against the state

[Col. 0931C]

If, together with our Tertullian, we pass to the other moral virtues of Christians and pagans, no less a difference can certainly be discovered between them than between those mentioned above. For if the contest concerns “moral integrity,” as he likes to call it—that is, uprightness of conduct, or, as it is commonly called, the virtue of humility—then behold, he says, “Diogenes, with muddy feet,” or, as he wrote elsewhere, “with filthy feet, tramples upon the proud couches of Plato with another kind of pride” [Col. 0931D] (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500, and *On the Pallium*, ch. 4). Does not Jerome appear to have had this passage of Tertullian in view when he said that Diogenes, with mud-covered feet, trampled upon the couches of the wealthy Plato (Jerome, book II *Against Jovinian*, p. 203; Laertius, book VI, *Life of Diogenes*, §26)? But before them both, Laertius relates that, while Diogenes was trampling upon Plato’s [non-Latin text omitted], he said [non-Latin text omitted], “I trample upon Plato’s empty ostentation,” and that Plato replied to him: [non-Latin text omitted], “How much arrogance you display, you who wish to appear swollen with no arrogance!” Or, as others report, Diogenes said: [non-Latin text omitted], “I trample upon Plato’s pride,” to which Plato replied: [non-Latin text omitted], “But with another pride.” As for why [Col. 0932A] our author said “with muddy” or “filthy” feet—or, as Jerome says, “mud-covered,” that is, stained with mud—you will learn from the same Laertius that the disciples led by this Diogenes went without shoes [non-Latin text omitted], while he [non-Latin text omitted] trod upon the snow with bare feet. Although, therefore, he openly professed such great poverty, he was nevertheless no less inflated with the vice of pride and arrogance than the wealthy Plato.

Tertullian therefore properly opposes to those proud philosophers the Christian, “who is proud neither toward the poor, much less toward the rich” (Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, ch. 46, p. 500; *ibid.*, ch. 25, p. 422). For among Christians, who were united, as we observed above, by the bond of brotherhood and the spirit of holiness, everything except wives was held in common [Col. 0932B], and there was no rivalry over superior rank or dignity.
