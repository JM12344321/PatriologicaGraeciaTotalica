Nor was our Thomas content with these achievements; for, observing, as he himself says, “that this reading [of the holy Fathers] would be supremely profitable and delightful, especially for clerics, throughout every stage of life,” he undertook Tertullian and published the books *On the Prescriptions*, *On Baptism*, and *On Repentance* (Salzburg, 1752, 1755). But “just as [Note: [Col. 1137B] Preface to the books *On Baptism* and *On Repentance*.] the African style of that same author [Tertullian] is sometimes covered and obscured, as it were, by the ashes of that scorched Carthage—although even those who criticize him admit that they themselves are sometimes wonderfully captivated by him—so most of his statements rightly demand that light be shed upon them. This is all the more true because [Col. 1137B] this author is extremely ancient, and is one of the Latin writers whom posterity has reclaimed for itself almost entire from that most remote age. From him the discipline of those times, especially in the African churches—which owed their original foundation to the Roman Church and had therefore first received that discipline from it—has come down to our knowledge; and this matter must certainly be regarded as being of no small importance in dogmatic theology.”

Hence there are the very numerous annotations with which Dom Thomas adorned these three books of Tertullian. We were obliged to relegate them to the end of this second volume, since [Col. 1138A] they reached our hands too late for each to be inserted in its proper place. Should anyone perhaps complain that these notes “seek out every minute detail as though with a little probe,” Dom Thomas replies that he “wrote these things not for the learned, but for those who are to be taught”; and he adds that “even much slighter matters can sometimes cause sufficient difficulty.” We have nevertheless omitted a few minor notes here and there for the sake of brevity; but, consulting both the reader’s profit and his pleasure, we have retained the interpolated dissertation concerning Baptism and Repentance. We have also arranged, as best we could, for the two other dissertations, on the use and authority of Tertullian—which are merely shorter and scantier and sometimes stray beyond their subject—to be recast into the preface.

Why, moreover, our Thomas chose specifically to publish and annotate the books *On Baptism* and *On Repentance*, we learn from Thomas himself in the aforementioned preface:

[Col. 1138B] “I wished, therefore,” he says, “to place this author in the hands of students, both so that they might perceive somewhat more accurately what the practice of the sacraments of Baptism and Repentance—sacraments supremely necessary for salvation—was in the primitive Church and then in its second century, when the traditions were still recent and could be least open to suspicion; and so that they might learn not to pass hurriedly and overhastily over the writings of the ancient Fathers, but to dwell upon them a little longer, though without utterly dying over one and the same author. In this way due esteem will accrue to those writings, and immense profit and pleasure to the students themselves.” —EDS.

## Notes on Chapter I

[Col. 1137D] **Our water.** Ordinary and elemental water, of course—the sort encountered everywhere in our daily use and most easily procured for Baptism, which is so necessary for our salvation.

**Because.** I am almost inclined to prefer *which*, referring, that is, to the water.

**From transgressions.** Not only from original sin—for this is one and the same in everyone [Col. 1138D]—but also from personal sins, as below, Chapter V.

**Of blindness.** For just as blindness is a kind of death of the bodily eye, depriving it of all its vigor and, as it were, its life, so, conversely, through Baptism sight is restored to the eye of the soul, which had been blinded through sin, [Col. 1139A] and it is flooded with the new light of grace. Thus the Apostle’s statement in Ephesians 5:8 can truly be said of the baptized: “You were once darkness, but now you are LIGHT in the Lord.” From this one may readily understand the saying of Clement of Alexandria, Book I of the *Paedagogue*, chapter 6:

> Βαπτιζόμενοι φωτιζόμεθα, φωτιζόμενοι υἱοποιούμεθα, υἱοποιούμενοι τελειούμεθα, τελειούμενοι ἀπαθανατιζόμεθα,

that is:

> Being baptized, we are illuminated; being illuminated, we are adopted as children; being adopted, we are perfected; being perfected, we are made immortal.

And shortly afterward he calls Baptism φώτισμα, “illumination,” through which we behold that holy and saving light—that is, through which we perceive what is divine. But perhaps more concerning this elsewhere.

**We are delivered.** And he therefore called this Sacrament “happy,” because it makes us happy in this way.

**A digest.** It is used substantivally here, as the jurists speak of the *Old Digest* and the *New Digest*. So too our Author, in Book IV *Against Marcion*, chapter 3, calls the Scriptures *Digesta*, because in them the doctrine of our faith is distributed and arranged in due order.

[Col. 1139B] **Especially.** Or “to the highest degree,” as in Cicero’s *For Cluentius* and elsewhere.

**Are formed.** In the sense of “are informed,” or imbued with a fuller knowledge of the mysteries of the faith.

**They carry.** He speaks of those who are still untutored in the faith and cannot give reasons why they believe, and who are ignorant of the sources from which faith itself flows and of the channels of tradition through which the truths to be believed have come down to us. Thus, having been nourished in that faith from earliest childhood, they adhere to it simply through inexperience, or through lack of fuller knowledge: it is an untested faith—not yet sufficiently examined—yet a probable one, that is, approved by others, namely their teachers, and in reality worthy of approval by all.

**There.** At Carthage in Africa, of course, where Tertullian was residing when he wrote these things.

**The Cainite heresy.** Concerning this utterly shameful heresy, see our Tertullian, *On the Prescriptions*, chapters XXXIII and XLVII. St. Augustine, [Col. 1139C] in his book *On Heresies*, addressed to Quodvultdeus, heresy 15, reports that these Cainites, or Caians, among their other errors, abolished all efficacy of baptism.

**Most venomous.** The most virulent reptile is called *vipera* in Latin, as though *vivipara*, “bearing living young,” because, unlike other serpents, it brings forth not eggs but living offspring; or, as Martinius says in his *Lexicon*, because it loves thorn-brakes, *vepreta*—for the ancients are said to have used *vipre* for *vepre*; or because it gives birth violently, its offspring, as though weary of so laborious a delivery, gnawing through its mother’s womb with what seems the height of impiety. Thus Pierius, Book XIII, under the heading *On the Viper*, testifies that the Egyptian priests represented sons conspiring against their mother by the hieroglyph of a viper. Some among the Greeks likewise maintain that the viper is called ἔχιδνα for this reason: παρὰ τὸ ἔχειν ἐν ἑαυτῇ τὴν γονὴν ἄχρι θανάτου, “because it retains its offspring within itself until its own death.” Experience, however, as Theophrastus already observed long ago, contradicts this excessive credulity of the ancients. Moreover, some—not without reason—punctuate that passage as follows: “the viper, most venomous through her doctrine,” etc. Finally, whom he designates by the name “viper” he reveals plainly and by her own [Col. 1139D] name shortly below.

**Snatched away.** Seized, or snatched over to herself and to her most pestilential heresy.

**Nature.** The nature of vipers, that is.

**Generally.** Others read “wild,” which Junius rightly dislikes. Accordingly, *generally* must be retained here in the sense of “for the most part.”

**Asps.** Ἀσπίς, ἴδος properly means “shield” in Greek. From this the scholiast on Aristophanes thinks that a certain species of serpent was called “asp” διὰ τὸ εἰς κύκλους πολλοὺς ἑλισσόμενον, “because it is accustomed to coil itself into many circles,” and by this coiling assumes a circular shape such as shields commonly had at that time. How great the power of an asp’s venom is may be demonstrated by this fact alone, related by Kircher in *Mundus Subterraneus*, Book IX, section I, chapter 3, concerning one of his companions:

> When he struck an asp sitting in a tree, the creature poisoned the man who struck it by its breath alone, rendering him [Col. 1140A] immobile for three full years by a kind of paralysis; and afterward he died from it, since no remedy could match such venom.

Thus Kircher. Suetonius, in *Augustus*, chapter 17, likewise records that Cleopatra died from the bite of an asp which she herself placed upon her breast, when the remedies of the Psylli were no longer of any avail. It is therefore unsurprising that Deuteronomy 32:33 speaks of “the incurable venom of asps,” and that Psalm 13:3 says generally of the impious: “They dealt deceitfully with their tongues; the venom of asps is beneath their lips.” Concerning the insuperable potency of this venom, see what our most reverend Calmet writes on Psalm 90:13, and Bochart in the *Hierozoicon*, Part II, Book III, chapters 5 and 14.

**Basilisks.** The basilisk-serpent, called Βασιλίσκος in Greek, is said to have received that name because it gleams with a white spot upon its forehead, like a royal diadem; or because, although extremely small, it nevertheless surpasses even the largest of the other serpents in the fierceness of its venom, and is accustomed to kill a person—unless he flees—by its breath alone, or even by its gaze if it sees the person first. This was the common [Col. 1140B] belief of the ancients, as may be seen in Aelian, *On the Nature of Animals*, Book II, chapters 5 and 7, and likewise in Pliny, *Natural History*, Book VIII, chapter 21. Through Jeremiah 8:17, certainly, God threatens the Jews with what amounts to the greatest possible plague: “Behold, I shall send among you”—what the Vulgate renders as “basilisk-serpents,” but the Septuagint as ὄφεις θανατοῦντας, “death-dealing serpents”—“against which there is no enchantment,” or against which no magical incantation is effective.

They say that it is hatched from the egg of a cock already one year old. Basilisks are in fact exhibited in certain museums, as I myself have seen more than once: two-footed and winged, distinguished by a serpentine tail, and not very unlike the form of a cock, except that they are featherless. Others, however, affirm that they are rays belonging to one of the smaller species and are customarily fashioned into that shape by artful fraud. It is certain, at any rate, that the ancient writers—Pliny, Aelian, Solinus, and others—class them not among birds but among true serpents. Finally, the more distinguished physicians [Col. 1140C] and philosophers of our own age do not hesitate to consign basilisks—and especially accounts of their birth—to the realm of fables.

**They frequent.** For they are said to make their lairs in sandy deserts, rocky mountains, and especially in the ruins of ancient buildings.

**We are born.** Among the Sibylline Oracles there circulates a certain acrostic poem, consisting of twenty-seven verses, attributed to the Erythraean Sibyl—or, as others prefer, the Cumaean Sibyl—whose beginning is this: ΙΧΘΥΣ.

Since ἰχθύς means “fish” in Greek, certain ancient Fathers called Christ the Ἰχθύς, “Fish.” “In this name,” says St. Augustine in Book XVIII of *The City of God*, chapter 23, “Christ is mystically understood, because in the abyss of this mortality, as in the depth of the waters, he was able to remain alive—that is, without sin.”

Although these oracles were highly esteemed by the ancient Fathers—Justin Martyr, Theophilus of Antioch, Athenagoras, Clement of Alexandria, Eusebius, Lactantius, and others—and were hurled against the pagans like mighty weapons, [Col. 1140D] nevertheless even the pagans themselves were persuaded that those Sibylline acrostics were wholly spurious. This was true not only of Celsus, as cited by Origen in Book V *Against Celsus*, but also of Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities*, Book IV, chapter 70, who relied in this matter upon the authority of Terentius Varro. Indeed, the far more common opinion among critics now holds that they were composed long after the Sibyls, around A.D. 130 under the reign of Hadrian, by some Christian wishing to commend our Religion to the pagans, and that they were issued under the falsely assumed name of the Sibyl. That even the apostolic age was not without pious frauds of this sort is evident from Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, Book III, chapter 7, and other sources. St. Augustine, in the same Book XVIII, chapter 45, appeared to express some doubt concerning them, saying: “Unless perhaps someone should say that Christians invented concerning Christ those prophecies which are produced under the name of the Sibyl or of others,” etc. He has similar remarks in chapter 47.

But you will object: could this acrostic, already praised by Cicero in Book II *On Divination* before the birth of Christ, be attributed [Col. 1141A] to a Christian? I reply: Tullius was indeed acquainted with a poem of this kind under the name of the Sibyl, but certainly not with the poem which so clearly refers to Christ. It was another poem, obscurely predicting that some king or other would rule the Romans—a prediction which, in the opinion of many, was to be fulfilled in Julius Caesar, who was secretly seeking the title of King. “A report had already spread,” says Suetonius in *Julius Caesar*, chapter 79, “that Lucius Cotta, one of the Board of Fifteen, intended to propose that, since it was contained in the books of fate”—namely the Sibylline books, whose custody and consultation are known to have been entrusted to a single college of fifteen men—“that the Parthians could not be defeated except by a king, Caesar should be called King.” Suetonius confirms the same point still more clearly immediately afterward, in chapter 80. Yet Tullius found it incredible that this very poem had been composed by some Sibyl under divine inspiration: “This,” he says, “is the work of a writer, not of one in a frenzy; of one employing diligence, not madness.” For my own part, I would more readily believe it to have been the work of some flatterer seeking to ingratiate himself with Caesar. Nor was the acrostic form of poetry unknown at that time, since it had already been frequently employed by Ennius, who was more than sixty years older than Cicero, [Col. 1141B] and was praised by Tullius himself in the same passage.

“But what then?” you ask. “Are we to believe that the ancient Fathers brought forward those spurious oracles against the Jews and pagans to persuade them of the true Messiah’s advent—or, more accurately, mocked them by means of those oracles?” I answer that this was done in good faith and not ineptly, especially against the pagans: not, of course, in order to confer authority upon oracles of this sort, but to employ, as what we call an *argumentum ad hominem*, the authority which those oracles already possessed among the pagans. Finally, those earliest Fathers of the Church, intent solely upon propagating the true Religion by whatever means they could, had no leisure to evaluate writings of this sort by critical examination. Hence they are known frequently to have used the books of Hystaspes and Hermes Trismegistus, the *Acts of Pilate*, and certain Gospels now judged apocryphal by everyone. Why, then, should we be surprised that Tertullian also alludes to that acrostic of some supposed Sibyl and calls us “little fishes,” imitators of our Ἰχθύς, “Fish,” [Col. 1141C] as it were of Christ the great Fish? Following his example, we receive Baptism and are born in its waters into the spiritual life of the soul. Moreover, anyone wishing to learn more about the Sibyls will find it in Dupin, *Bibliothèque nouvelle*, vol. I, p. 28 of my edition, etc.; Cave, *De Scriptoribus Ecclesiasticis*, under “Sibylla”; Basnage, at the fourth year before Christ; Isaac Vossius; Blondel; Marckius; Crasset; and many others.

**We are saved.** By persevering in the faith which we received and professed through Baptism; for “he who perseveres to the end shall be saved” (Matthew 24:13).

**Quintilla.** An otherwise obscure woman, had not men permitted themselves to be smeared with her venom and with her very name—being called, for shame, “Quintillians.” Moreover, St. Epiphanius, *Heresy* 49, enrolls this Quintilla in the company of Priscilla and Maximilla, the prophetesses of Montanus. Basnage, however, in the *Politico-Ecclesiastical Annals* at A.D. 200, no. 5, carefully distinguishes two women of the same name. He places the former, a follower of Montanus and teacher of the Pepuzians, in Phrygia in Asia, [Col. 1141D] but the Quintilla of our Tertullian in Africa alone, as an adherent of the Cainite heresy, as he had already noted above.

**Most monstrous.** For she was the leader among a company of men and had already been compared above to a viper, an asp, and a basilisk.

**She had the right.** St. Paul in 1 Corinthians 14:34ff. takes this right away from women universally, strictly imposing silence upon this talkative sex, which was likely to cause excessive disturbance in the sacred assemblies of the faithful: “Let women keep silence in the church; for they are not permitted to speak, but are to be subject to their husbands,” etc. Thus he does not permit them the office of teaching even sound doctrine—that is, doctrine otherwise free from corruption. See below, Chapter XVII, near the end. It is clear from this passage, moreover, that when Tertullian wrote these things he was not yet among the Montanists, among whom women were permitted, even with public applause, to prophesy openly and to teach men themselves—an indignity!—as we demonstrated in Critical Question [Col. 1142A] V, prefixed by us to Tertullian’s book *On the Prescriptions*.

**Taking away.** Withdrawing Baptism, without which no one can become a Christian.

## Notes on Chapter II

**It is evident.** This statement expresses astonishment at the perversity of the heretics and must therefore be taken θαυμαστικῶς, “as an exclamation of wonder”: namely, that they labor to fashion weapons against us from the very elements of our Faith.

**May harden**, that is, render obstinate and refractory.

**In the act**, or in the action itself and the outward work.

**In the effect.** The interior and spiritual effect, namely Sacramental Grace, which exceeds every power and comprehension of nature as a whole.

**Is promised again.** Simply for “is promised,” just as our Author commonly uses *retractare* for *tractare* and other such compounds in place of their simple forms.

**In water.** Others think it should read “into water”; but what prevents us from retaining “in water”? Tertullian is speaking of baptism by immersion, commonly practiced at that time not only in the African [Col. 1142C] Churches but almost everywhere. In it those being baptized were not merely placed into the water, but were customarily lowered into it with their whole body. But we shall discuss this baptism elsewhere.

**Dipped.** Our Author consistently uses “to dip” for the Greek “to baptize.”

**Rises again.** For the sudden immersion and emergence from the waters of Baptism brings the body not the cleanliness ordinarily produced by baths, but brings the soul a far greater cleanliness.

**For that reason**, in the sense of “therefore.”

**Of eternity.** Or of eternal salvation, into which we are initiated through Baptism.

**Solemn rites.** According to Festus, an ancient grammarian, a “sacred solemnity” was something customarily performed at fixed times and years—hence also “anniversaries,” as things especially to be celebrated whenever the year comes around—principally on behalf of the people and at public expense, such as the Agonalia, Carmentalia, Quirinalia, and so forth.

[Col. 1142C] **Mysteries.** The ancients called secluded sacred rites *sacra seclusa*, and the Greeks called them Μυστήρια, “mysteries” or “hidden things.” An *arcanum*, moreover, was a particular kind of sacred rite which, according once again to Festus, was performed by the Augurs and was so far removed from common knowledge that it was not even divulged in writing, but was celebrated from the memory of their successors. Such were the rites celebrated with the common people excluded, such as the Nyctelia mentioned by St. Augustine in Book VI of *The City of God*, chapter 9; likewise the Samothracian rites of the Cabeiri, or “powerful gods,” concerning whom our Author writes in the *Apology*, chapter 7; the Eleusinian rites in Attica; and others, which because of their obscenity were called ἄῤῥητα, “unspeakable.” Among these deserve to be numbered the rites performed among the Romans for the Good Goddess. Among the mysteries were also the Mithraic rites, concerning which our Author writes in the final chapter of *The Soldier’s Crown*, and we in *On the Prescriptions*, chapter XI, note h, and below, Chapter V. Here, however, Tertullian chiefly means those sacred rites into which the celebrants had to be initiated by certain ablutions, such as those of the god Aesymnetes, concerning which Pausanias writes in the *Achaica*, chapter 226 [probably 26; reading uncertain], and others mentioned here and there.

**Display.** A *suggestus* is properly a somewhat elevated place constructed from a heap of many things, or by placing some things on top of others. Hence the Latins eventually called any elevated place a *suggestum*, [Col. 1142D] such as the one in the Campus Martius from which the magistrates addressed public assemblies. Indeed, a speech of that kind itself was also called a *suggestio*, concerning which Vopiscus, chapter 19, writes: “We refer to you, Conscript Fathers, the recommendation of the pontiffs.”

It is also used generally for “equipment” or “display,” as in our Author’s *On the Spectacles*, chapter 7: “The display of the circus games is somewhat more pompous, since what properly bears the name ‘procession’ precedes them,” etc. And in the same work, chapter 12: “We shall interpret the same concerning ceremonial equipment, which must be accounted part of the display of the honors themselves,” etc. Likewise, in *On Idolatry*, chapter 18: “Now, indeed, we must examine the mere display and ceremonial equipment of office,” etc. Thus the gold and silver images of the Caesars, affixed one above another upon a spear and carried before the legions, were also called *suggestus*, as again in our Author’s *Apology Against the Pagans*, chapter 16: “All those conspicuous displays of images are the pendants of crosses.”

It is also used for women’s adornment in clothing, as in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 12: “Only those who openly parade their finery carry their whole womanhood about with them [Col. 1143A] . . . denying by a single garment what they profess by their entire display.” And in *On the Apparel of Women*, chapter 2: “You should know that you must reject not only the display of contrived and elaborated lust,” etc. In a more particular sense, however, it is applied to an adornment of the head and to hair arranged in curling locks, as in Statius, *Silvae*, Book I:

> . . . Behold from afar the glory of her lofty brow,  
> And the structure of her hair.

It is sometimes also taken to mean “provision” or “supply,” as again in Tertullian, *Against Hermogenes*, chapter 16: “If he who made it is himself the author of evil, matter is plainly his accomplice through the supply of its substance.”

**They build up.** He indicates here especially the enormous luxury of games, garments, banquets, and so forth, which was customarily employed in certain pagan rites.

**Power.** These are indeed attributes of God which especially commend one another, since so great a simplicity of divine works shines forth within infinite Power, and infinite Power within so great a simplicity. [Col. 1143B] The Author of nature takes the shortest route and does not use a great apparatus of instruments; he does nothing in vain, does not multiply entities without necessity, and so forth, but confines his Power to the fewest possible means. Indeed, those very things which seem to have been created merely for the adornment of the universe nevertheless possess their own simplicity; nor does God employ more ornament even in his own works than is necessary to attain the end established by Supreme Wisdom.

Yet nearly everything happens in the opposite manner in human affairs; for the very absence of simplicity and the great apparatus of instruments betray the weakness of human power. The larger the house that is to be built, the greater the accumulation of material and the greater the number of workmen required. Yet this entire fabric of the world was created from nothing by its Architect, without another’s assistance, without tumultuous exertion, through a single “Let it be.” Nature perfects gold by silent and extremely simple processes; Art, amid all its attempts, has perhaps been able to produce genuine gold [Col. 1143C]—to say nothing of diamonds and the like—only with difficulty and rarely, or rather never. Mechanical art provides remarkable assistance to what are called motive powers, but only through multiplied pulleys and other laborious machines, and with correspondingly greater slowness in achieving success. How many medicines are required to overcome the obstinacy of a more serious disease? How many diseases are there which no medical skill can conquer?

Yet Christ—to mention only these few examples—restored to health the leper with two little words, “I will; be cleansed” (Matthew 8:3); the daughter of the Canaanite woman with a single “Let it be” (Matthew 9:25 [reference apparently corrupt]); the man blind from birth merely by anointing him with clay and washing him in the pool of Siloam (John 9); Peter’s mother-in-law suffering from fever (Matthew 8:15) and two blind men (Matthew 20:34) by touch alone; and the centurion’s paralytic servant even while absent (Matthew 8:13). Indeed, he restored to life itself the ruler of the synagogue’s daughter, who was completely dead, merely by taking the girl’s hand (Mark 5:41ff.): all through means, that is, which were utterly disproportionate to their effects.

**Death.** For who is ever recorded as having raised the dead by human power and by pouring [Col. 1143D] a bath over them? Yet to raise the soul, through Baptism in ordinary, simple water encountered everywhere, from the death of sin to the life of grace is a much simpler act and one requiring power greater than all human weakness.

**It is not believed.** For we human beings are accustomed to admire things which surpass our comprehension; therefore, we ought to believe these things all the more, the more Divinity shines forth in them and the more remote they are from human power and the human mode of acting.

**He does not believe.** There is therefore a twofold wonder. One arises from faith, when we do not in fact doubt the existence or properties of a thing, and so forth, but nevertheless wish inwardly to know and understand how that thing is constituted or its causes, though they surpass our comprehension, and are unable to do so; hence we can only admire it. In this sense wonder—as Aristotle says somewhere—is the daughter of ignorance.

The other kind is without faith, when we are astonished that someone else believes or proclaims something which surpasses our own faith, [Col. 1144A] because we refuse to conform that faith to the authority of another—indeed, to the authority of GOD himself.

**Empty**, that is, devoid of content; for unbelief is accustomed to measure everything by the senses. Accordingly, things which appear outwardly simple it supposes to be simple inwardly as well.

**Impossible.** The unbeliever does not believe that things which are said to be inwardly magnificent, or to produce great effects, can occur, since they cannot be apprehended by the senses. For, as the Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 2:14, “the natural man does not perceive the things that are of the Spirit of GOD, for they are foolishness to him, and he cannot understand them.” Such people therefore judge from the senses alone, but with little sense.

**With God.** This plainly alludes to Christ’s saying in Matthew 19:26:

> παρὰ ἀνθρώποις τοῦτο ἀδύνατόν ἐστι, παρὰ δὲ Θεῷ πάντα δυνατά ἐστι,

which our Vulgate renders word for word:

> With men—that is, in human opinion—this, namely to be saved or to attain eternal salvation, is impossible; but with GOD all things are possible.

Perhaps, however, Tertullian read *extremely difficult* in his manuscript, in the sense in which Cicero translated the passage from Plato’s *Timaeus*, ἀδύνατον Θεῶν παισὶν ἀπιστεῖν, [Col. 1144B] as: “It is difficult not to place faith in those born of the gods”—that is, in the children of the gods. And certainly we are accustomed to call things that are extremely difficult, or very difficult, “impossible” morally, or in human estimation. What immediately follows makes this meaning of Tertullian clear.

**Passing over it**, that is, considering it incidentally and, as it were, in passing.

**By opposites**, or contrary things.

**Cause**, that is, causal being, or, if we speak in the manner of the Scholastics, causative power. It may also be taken as the cause or reason why something is or exists—that is, as its principle or origin. The same mode of expression occurs in our Author’s *On the Spectacles*, chapter 12: “Whatever is administered under the name of an office must necessarily share also in the stains of that from which it has its causes.” Yet what prevents us from taking “cause” here as the state and condition of a thing? Cicero also used the word in this sense in *On Ends*: “Those who have been put to death are in the same condition as [Col. 1144C] before they were born.” Likewise, in *On Duties*, he says: “When he was being killed by being kept awake, his condition was better than if he had remained at home as an aged captive,” etc.

**Is called forth**, or is brought into effect and, as it were, summoned from a state of possibility into actual existence. The meaning, therefore, is that every work receives its state and condition from that which produces it, and consequently that the efficacy of Baptism must be measured not from its outward apparatus but from the Wisdom and Power of GOD operating inwardly and spiritually.

## Notes on Chapter III

**To be re-formed.** We are said to be re-formed through water when in Baptism we recover the form of divine grace which had previously been lost through sin.

**Why.** In place of “for what reason,” as in Ovid’s epistle of Oenone to Paris:

> . . . Why do you entrust seed to sand?

**Authority.** A certain excellence and, as it were, inherent dignity.

**Must be demanded.** To some this entire sentence seems tangled, and they therefore judge that, with Junius, it should be read: “. . . we discuss being re-formed by water; what office, then, etc., has it deserved? Its authority, I suppose,” etc. [Col. 1144D] But what need is there to punctuate it thus? Or why should we not instead understand the words “I suppose” ironically, in the sense of “naturally,” so that the meaning is: as though GOD could do nothing which exceeded the powers and claims of water or of any other creature?

**From the beginning**, namely, of the world itself.

**Display**, that is, adornment, or its unfolding into distinct species. On *suggestus*, see the preceding chapter, col. 1142D.

**Unfinished.** Or still crude and formless.

**With God.** In the sense of “in GOD’s presence,” or as resting in GOD’s power and will—namely, whatever he wished to make from each thing. In almost the same sense our same Author says in *On Patience*, chapter 1: “What is supremely good belongs supremely to GOD, nor does anyone distribute it except the one who possesses it, as he deems each person worthy.”

**They rested.** As though still inert and endowed with no faculty of action.

**He says.** Moses, namely, in Genesis 1:1.

**Invisible.** The Hebrew [word omitted in the supplied text] is [Col. 1145A] rendered *empty* in the Vulgate, κένωμα, “emptiness,” by Aquila, ἀργόν, “idle and inert,” by Symmachus, and κενόν, “empty,” by Theodotion. The Septuagint, however, translates it as ἀόρατος, “unseen” or “invisible,” because it was still enveloped in utter darkness. In the same way our Vulgate renders a certain passage of Wisdom 11:18: “He created the world from INVISIBLE matter,” where the Greek has ἐξ ἀμόρφου ὕλης, “from FORMLESS matter.”

**Uncomposed.** The Hebrew word [omitted in the supplied text] is rendered *void* by our Vulgate, οὐθέν, “nothing,” by Aquila and Theodotion, ἀδιάκριτον, “undifferentiated” or “confused,” by Symmachus, and ἀκατασκεύαστος, “unprepared, unworked, UNCOMPOSED,” or “confused,” by the Septuagint.

**The abyss.** The Hebrew [word omitted in the supplied text] is rendered ἄβυσσος, “abyss,” by all the Greek translators. As Eustathius interprets this name, it means an immeasurable body of water possessing infinite depth, or lacking a bottom; or, as Cornelius a Lapide explains it, the mass of waters which at that time covered the whole earth; and finally, according to our most reverend Calmet, it is the watery matter still commingled with earth, or mud.

**The Spirit of God.** But what is this [Hebrew term omitted in the supplied text], the SPIRIT OF GOD? [Col. 1145B] This considerable difficulty has chiefly produced three opinions.

The first holds that it must here be understood as wind, or air moving more violently over the waters. This is the opinion of Severianus, Bishop of Gabala, a writer of the fourth century, whom Theodoret explains more fully in Question VIII on Genesis:

> I should consider it more correct that he here calls the air “spirit”; for, after saying that GOD created HEAVEN—namely the Empyrean, named from FIRE, which in Greek is πῦρ—and EARTH, and after mentioning the Waters under the name ABYSS, he necessarily made mention of the AIR, etc.

Nor is this opinion rejected by Saints Basil, Ambrose, Augustine, and others. It is well established that the Hebrews, whose language lacks superlatives, often employ, among other devices for expressing them, a noun qualified by the proper name of GOD. Thus Genesis 35:5 says: “The TERROR OF GOD”—that is, the greatest terror, sent, as it were, by GOD himself—“fell upon them all.” [Col. 1145C] Similarly, Psalm 35:7 has: “Your righteousness is LIKE THE MOUNTAINS OF GOD,” that is, the highest mountains. And Psalm 79:11: “Its shadow covered the mountains, and its branches the CEDARS OF GOD,” namely, the loftiest and tallest cedars. In addition, Isaiah 40:7 uses the same words as Moses in Genesis 1:2: “The grass has withered because the SPIRIT OF THE LORD blew upon it”; and this plainly must be understood of the wind.

The second opinion is held by many who, according to St. Augustine in the unfinished *Literal Commentary on Genesis*, chapter 4, understand by that spirit “the LIVING CREATURE by which the whole visible world and all corporeal things are contained and moved,” etc.; or, as St. John Chrysostom affirms in Homily III on Genesis, that “a certain efficacious and LIFE-GIVING OPERATION was present in the waters”—that is, a fecundity secretly impressed upon them for generating living creatures, namely fish and birds, Genesis 1:21—“and that it was not simply still and motionless water, but water that moved and possessed a certain LIFE-GIVING POWER.”
