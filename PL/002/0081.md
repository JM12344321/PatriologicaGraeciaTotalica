## CHAPTER X

I turn to others who are equally wise in their own estimation [Note: [Col. 0773D] Codex Agobardinus, Rhen., Seml., Oberth. read *custom*.]. They affirm that Christ’s flesh was animate, because the soul became flesh and consequently the flesh became soul; and just as the flesh was ensouled, so the soul was fleshly.

Here too, therefore, I ask for their reasons. If Christ took a soul into himself in order to save the soul, because it could not be saved except through him while it was in him, I do not see why he made it into animate flesh by putting on flesh, as though he could not otherwise save the soul unless it were made fleshly. For since he renders our souls saved not merely when they are not fleshly but even when they are separated from [Note: [Col. 0773D] Rhen., Seml., Oberth. read *in*.] the flesh, how much more could he have brought to salvation the soul he himself assumed even without making it fleshly!

Likewise, since they assume [Note: [Col. 0773D] Rhen., Seml., Oberth. read *he assumes*.] that Christ came forth for the sake of liberating not our flesh but our soul alone [Note: [Col. 0773D] Not our flesh but our soul alone. The Agobardine manuscript extends this far. Henceforth the discussion will proceed only with the manuscripts of Ursinus and Pithoeus. RIG.], first, how absurd it is that, when he was going to liberate the soul alone, he should have made it into the very kind of body he was not going to liberate! Next, if he had undertaken to liberate our souls through the soul he bore, he ought also to have borne our kind of soul—that is, one of the same form as ours, whatever the form of our soul may be in its hidden state, yet certainly not fleshly. Otherwise, he did not liberate our soul if the one he possessed was fleshly; for ours is not fleshly.

Moreover, if he did not liberate ours because he liberated a fleshly soul [Note: [Col. 0773D] “Because he liberated a fleshly soul” absent from Rhen.], this has no relevance to us [Note: [Col. 0773D] Rhen. adds *It is not fleshly*.], because he did not liberate our soul. Nor was that soul in need of liberation, since it was not ours—namely, because it was fleshly; for it was not in danger if it was not ours—that is, not fleshly. Yet it is established that that soul was liberated. Therefore it was not fleshly; and it was ours, if it [Note: [Col. 0773D] Others insert *not*.] was the soul that required liberation, because [Note: [Col. 0773D] Others read *in what manner*.] it was in danger.

Therefore, if the soul in Christ was not fleshly, neither could the flesh have been animate in the sense they allege.

## CHAPTER XI

But let us confront another of their arguments, asking why Christ, by assuming animate flesh, is thought to have possessed a fleshly soul. For God, they say [Note: [Col. 0773D] Rhen. reads *he says*.], desired [Note: [Col. 0773D] Rig. reads *bore*.] to display the soul visibly to human beings by making it a body. Previously it had been invisible [Col. 0774A], by nature a nothing, unable even to see itself because of the obstruction of this flesh, so that it was even debated whether the soul was born or not and whether it was mortal or not [Note: [Col. 0774D] “Whether it was mortal or not” is absent from the Vatican manuscripts and the first edition of Rhen.; Jun. also rejects it.]. Accordingly, the soul was made a body in Christ so that we might see it being born, dying, and—what is more—rising again.

But what kind of procedure was this: that through flesh the soul should be demonstrated either to itself or to us, when through flesh it could not be recognized; that it should be revealed in this way, by becoming the very thing to which it was hidden—that is, flesh? It received darkness, evidently, so that it might be able to shine [Note: [Col. 0774D] Pam., Rhen., Seml. read *may be able*.].

In any case, let us first reconsider [Note: [Col. 0774D] Rhen., Seml., Oberth. read *for this purpose*.] whether the soul had to be revealed in this manner, or whether they allege that it was previously invisible altogether: whether because it was incorporeal or because it possessed some kind of body of its own. And yet, when they call it invisible, they establish that it is corporeal, possessing something that is invisible. For if it possesses nothing invisible, how can it be called invisible? Indeed, it cannot even exist unless [Note: [Col. 0774D] Others read *nothing*.] it possesses that by which it exists. Since it exists, however, it must necessarily possess something by which it exists. If it possesses [Note: [Col. 0774D] *Possesses* absent from Rhen., Seml.] something by which it exists, this will be its body. Everything that exists is a body of its own kind; nothing is incorporeal except what does not exist.

Since the soul therefore possesses an invisible body, the one who had undertaken to make it visible would surely more fittingly have made visible that body of it which was regarded as invisible. For neither falsehood nor weakness is compatible with God: falsehood, if he showed the soul as something other than what it was; weakness, if he was unable to show what it was. No one wishing to display a man puts a helmet or mask on him. Yet this is what was done to the soul if, after being transformed [Note: [Col. 0774D] Pam., Rhen., Seml. read *having been transformed*.] in flesh [Note: [Col. 0774D] Others read *into flesh*.], it put on [Note: [Col. 0774D] Pam. reads *you put on*.] a foreign exterior.

But even if the soul is judged incorporeal, so that by some abstruse force of reasoning the soul is indeed something, yet whatever the soul is, it is not a body, it was no more impossible for God—and would have accorded more suitably with his purpose—to display it in some new bodily form than in this common form belonging to everyone and already known as something different [Note: [Col. 0774D] “Different.” He used *alterius* for *alius*, that is, “of another kind.” RHENAN.]. Otherwise, he would have desired without cause to make the soul visible from invisible, merely exposing it to those same questions through the interposition of human flesh over it [Note: [Col. 0774D] *Over it* absent from Codex Pithoeanus.].

But Christ could not be seen among human beings except as a human being. Restore to Christ, then, his good faith: since he wished to walk as a human being, he also displayed a soul of human condition—not by making it fleshly, but by clothing it with flesh.
