Let Marcion’s Christ stand forth and cry out: “O unbelieving generation, how long shall I be with you? How long shall I endure you?” [Note: [Col. 0416D] “How long shall I endure you?” omitted by Semler.] He should immediately hear from me: “Whoever you are, you newcomer (ἐπερχόμενε, *eperchomene*), first declare who you are, whence you come, and what right you have over us. Up to this point, everything about you belongs to the Creator. [Note: [Col. 0416D] “You are,” Oberthür.] Certainly, if you come from him and act on his behalf, we accept the rebuke. But if you come from another, I should like you to say what of your own you have ever entrusted to us, which we ought to have believed, so that you may reproach us for unbelief—you who have never even revealed yourself to us. How long ago did you begin to deal with us, that you should complain about the length of time? In what circumstances have you endured us, that you should charge us against your patience? You have only just come like Aesop’s ass from the well, and already [Col. 0416C] you are shouting.”

I still assume the person of the disciples against whom he sprang: “O unbelieving nation, how long shall I be with you? How long shall I endure you?” I would certainly, and with perfect justice, have beaten back his outburst in this manner: “Whoever you are, you newcomer (ἐπερχόμενε, *eperchomene*), first declare who you are, whence you come, and what right you have over us. Up to this point, I think, you belong to the Creator, and therefore we have followed you, recognizing in you everything that is his. But if you come from him, we accept the rebuke. If, however, you act on behalf of another, I beg you, say what you have ever entrusted to us—at least anything of your own—which we ought already to have believed, so that you may reproach us for unbelief, when even now you do not reveal your author. And how long ago did you begin to deal with us, that you should also hold the length of time against us? In what circumstances have you endured us, that you should boast of your patience as well? Aesop’s ass has only just appeared here from the [Col. 0416D] well, and already it is shouting.”

Who would not [Col. 0417A] thus have beaten back the injustice of the rebuke, if he had believed Christ to belong to one who did not yet have the right to complain? Except that neither would Christ have assailed them if he had not long dwelt among them in the Law, in the prophets, in mighty works, and in acts of kindness, and had always found them unbelieving.

But look: Christ loves little children, teaching that those who always wish to be greater ought to be like them. The Creator, however, sent bears against children, avenging the prophet Elisha for the insults he had suffered from them (2 Kings 2). A thoroughly shameless antithesis, since it sets together such different things: little children and older children—an age still innocent, and one already capable of judgment, which could insult, not to say blaspheme. [Note: [Col. 0417C] “Since,” Paris and Franeker editions.] Insofar, then, as God is just, he did not spare even impious children, requiring honor to be shown to the greater age, and surely still more by the lesser. Insofar as he is good, he so loves little children [Col. 0417B] that in Egypt he showed favor to the midwives who protected the Hebrew children endangered by Pharaoh’s edict. Thus this affection of Christ, too, is shared with the Creator.

Now Marcion’s God, who abhors marriage [Note: [Col. 0417C] “Opposes,” Rigault, Venetian edition.], how can he be regarded as a lover of little children, [Note: [Col. 0417D] Marriage is the entire cause of these children. The author uses “entire cause” for “the principal cause”; for the blessed Augustine noted in more than one place the three goods of marriage—fidelity, sacrament, and offspring—while most people in fact contract marriage chiefly for the sake of children. For, as Nonius says, “the whole,” which is regarded as the greater part, may sometimes be taken as not meaning the entirety. The author indicates this in express words when, shortly afterward, he adds: “He also promised by his blessing the very fruit of marriage, the first of which is infancy.” PAM.] when marriage is the entire cause of their existence? He who hates the seed must necessarily execrate its fruit as well. Indeed, he must be judged crueler than the Egyptian king: for Pharaoh did not allow infants to be brought up; this god does not even allow them to be born, taking life away from them even during their ten months in the womb. How much more credible it is that affection for little children should be attributed to him who, by blessing marriage for the propagation of the human race, also promised by his blessing the very fruit of marriage, the first of which is infancy.

At Elijah’s request the Creator reproduces the plague of fire in the case of that “false prophet” (2 Kings 1). [Col. 0417C] I recognize the severity of the Judge; on the other side, I recognize the gentleness of Christ rebuking [Note: [Col. 0417C] “Reproving,” Wouwer manuscript.] the disciples when they intended the same punishment against that Samaritan village. Let the heretic recognize that this gentleness of Christ was promised by that same most severe Judge: “He will not contend,” he says (Isa. 42:2), “nor will his voice be heard in the street. A bruised reed he will not break, and smoking flax he will not extinguish.” Such a one, surely, was much less likely to burn human beings. [Note: [Col. 0417D] “For even then, in his approach to Elijah, he was not in the fire.” This alludes to the history of Elijah in 1 Kings 19. His words, “For even then, in his approach to Elijah,” mean that even in the Old Testament God was gentle, merciful, and placable. Procopius: ὅτι δὲ οὐ πῦρ πῦρ ὁ τῆς παλαίας θεὸς, Ἡλίας διδάσκεται, καὶ δι’ αὐτοῦ πάντες ἡμεῖς· ἀλλ’ οὐδὲ αὐρὰ λεπτή, δι’ αὐρᾶς δὲ λέγεται παρεῖναι λεπτῆς. “That the God of the Old Testament is not fire, Elijah is taught, and through him all of us; but neither is he a gentle breeze, though he is said to be present by means of a gentle breeze.” [The repeated Greek πῦρ is an evident textual duplication.] See [Col. 0418D] the remarks on the book *On Patience*. RIG.] For even then, in his approach to Elijah (1 Kings 19:12), the Lord was “not [Col. 0418A] in the fire,” but in a gentle spirit.

But why does this most humane God reject the man who offers himself to him as so inseparable a companion? If it was because he had said proudly or hypocritically, “I will follow you wherever you go,” then, in judging pride or hypocrisy worthy of rejection, he acted as a judge. And surely he condemned the man whom he rejected, since he would not attain [Note: [Col. 0418C] “Follow,” Semler.] salvation. For just as he calls to salvation one whom he does not reject, or even one whom he calls of his own accord, so he condemns to destruction the man whom he rejects.

But when he answers the one who pleaded the need to bury his father, “Let the dead bury their dead; but you, go and proclaim the kingdom of God,” he plainly confirmed both laws of the Creator: the law concerning the priesthood in Leviticus, which forbids priests to attend even the final rites of their parents: “The priest,” he says (Lev. [Col. 0418B] 21:1), “shall not go in to any dead person, and he shall not defile himself for his parent” [Note: [Col. 0418C] “Father,” Oberthür.]; and the law concerning a vow in Numbers (Num. 6:6–7). For there too, among other things, he commands that one who has vowed himself to God shall not go in to any dead person—not even to his father, mother, or brother. I think that he intended for both a vow and the priesthood the man whom he had instructed to preach the kingdom of God. Or, if this is not so, he must be pronounced thoroughly impious for commanding sons to despise their parents’ burial when no provision of the law intervened.

Moreover, when he forbids that third man, who was preparing first to bid farewell to his own people, to look back, he follows the rule of the Creator. The Creator had also refused to allow those whom he delivered from Sodom to do this.

## CHAPTER XXIV

[Col. 0418C] He also chose another seventy apostles in addition to the twelve (Luke 10). For why were there twelve, corresponding to the same number of springs at Elim, unless there were also seventy, corresponding to the same number of palm trees? Difference of circumstances has produced most of the *Antitheses*, not difference of powers. But one who failed to consider the difference of circumstances readily supposed it to be a difference of power.

The Creator brought the children of Israel out of [Note: [Col. 0418C] “From,” Semler.] Egypt, loading them not only with the spoils of gold and silver vessels and clothing, [Note: [Col. 0418D] “In addition to burdens of dough.” So it reads in the copy. Here, therefore, *praeter* functions adverbially and means “in addition.” By “burdens of dough” he means a mass of flour bound together by the sprinkling of water. Exodus 12:34: ἀνέλαβε δὲ ὁ λαὸς τὸ σταῖς αὐτῶν, “and the people took up their dough.” Hesychius: Σταῖς, φύραμα ἀλεύρου πυροῦ, “*stais*: a kneaded mass of wheat flour.” An old glossary: σταῖς, “fat.” Likewise, φύραμα, “mass, dough”; so too, against the Valentinians: “he hid it in the kneading-troughs of the dough until the whole was leavened.” The Vulgate version of Paul’s First Epistle to the Corinthians 5: “that you may be a new dough, as you are unleavened.” RIG.] but also with burdens of dough packed upon them. [Note: [Col. 0418D] “Packed upon them.” Exodus: τὰ φυράματα αὐτῶν ἐνδεδεμένα ἐν τοῖς ἱματίοις αὐτῶν, “their kneaded masses bound up in their garments.” RIG.] Christ, [Note: [Col. 0418D] “Christ, however, prescribed that the disciples should not carry even a staff,” etc. I would read with Pamelius, “not even a bag,” for this reading agrees exactly with the Gospel of the blessed Luke. LE PR.] [Col. 0419A] however, prescribed that the disciples should not carry even a staff [Note: [Col. 0419D] “Bag,” Franeker edition.] on their journey. For the former were advancing into the wilderness, while the latter were being sent into cities. Consider the difference of circumstances, and you will understand that the power is one and the same: it arranged its people’s equipment according to scarcity or abundance, curtailing what would be plentiful throughout the cities just as it had furnished [Note: [Col. 0419D] “Restricted,” notes Rhenanus.] what would be needed throughout the wilderness.

[Note: [Col. 0419D] “He also forbade them to carry sandals.” Do not understand this to mean that Christ the Lord wanted his disciples to walk with bare feet, but only that they should not provide themselves with sandals to use when those already on their feet had worn out. For the same one who, in accordance with the Old Testament, preserved the Israelites so safe and unharmed for forty years that neither their clothes nor their sandals wore out, could in the New Testament give his own people the same confidence and security. The context itself requires this meaning. LE PR.] He also forbade them to carry sandals. For he himself was the one under whom the people did not wear out their sandals even during all those years in the wilderness.

“You shall greet no one,” he says, “along the way.” [Note: [Col. 0419D] “On the way,” Oberthür.] O Christ, destroyer of the Prophets, from whom he received this command also! When Elisha sent his servant Gehazi along the road, I think, to raise the Shunammite woman’s son from death, he instructed him as follows (2 Kings 4:29): “Gird your loins, take my staff in your hand, and go. Whoever meets you on the road, do not bless him”—that is, do not greet him—“and whoever blesses you, do not answer him”—that is, do not return his greeting. For what blessing occurs between people on the road except the mutual greeting exchanged when they meet?

So too, the Lord’s command that they should speak peace to whatever house they entered [Note: [Col. 0419D] Others read: “that they should speak peace to whatever house.”] follows the same example. For Elisha also gave this command: when he came in to the Shunammite woman, he was to say to her, “Peace to your husband; peace to your son.” [Note: [Col. 0419D] “Your” omitted by Semler.] These will rather be our *Antitheses*: those which compare Christ, not those which separate him.

“The laborer is worthy [Note: [Col. 0419D] “Is” omitted by Oberthür.] of his wages”: who is more likely to have pronounced this than God the Judge? For the very act of declaring the laborer worthy of his wages is an act of judgment. No recompense has ever been determined without judgment.

And now here too the Creator’s law is ratified, which judges even [Col. 0419C] working oxen worthy laborers of their wages: “You shall not muzzle,” he says (Deut. 25:4), “the ox that treads out the grain.” Who is so generous toward human beings except one who is generous even toward cattle? But if Christ also pronounces laborers worthy of wages, he has vindicated that commandment of the Creator concerning the removal of the Egyptians’ gold and silver vessels. For those who had labored in the Egyptians’ country estates and cities were surely laborers worthy of their wages: they were instructed [Note: [Col. 0420D] “Prompted,” Pamelius.] not to commit fraud, but to obtain compensation for wages which they could not otherwise exact from their masters.

He also confirmed that the kingdom of God was neither new nor unheard-of [Col. 0420A] when he commanded them to announce that it had drawn near. For something that was once far away can be said to have drawn near. But if it had never existed previously, before it drew near, neither could something that had never been far away be said to have drawn near. Everything new and unknown is sudden. Everything sudden, when it is announced, then assumes appearance for the first time and first acquires a point in time. Otherwise, it could not have been delayed in the past, as long as it was not being announced, nor could it have drawn near from the point when its announcement began.

He further adds [Note: [Col. 0420D] Others read “says”; another reading, “decrees.”] that they should say to those who had not received them: “Nevertheless, know that the kingdom of God has drawn near.” If he does not command this also by way of threat, his command is utterly pointless. For what would it matter to them that the kingdom was drawing near, unless it was drawing near with [Col. 0420B] judgment—namely, for the salvation of those who had received its announcement? How so? If a threat cannot exist without its execution, you have God as executioner in the threatener, and as judge in both capacities.

Thus he also commands them to shake off the dust against them as testimony, [Note: [Col. 0419D] “And the clinging soil of their land.” The copy reads: “Thus he also commands the dust both to be shaken off against them as a testimony to him; and also the land’s heart.” He seems to have said “the clinging soil of their land” to explain what Luke intended to convey by the word “dust.” RIG.] and even the soil of their land that clung to them, to say nothing of any remaining fellowship. [Note: [Col. 0420D] “Adhering soil,” Paris edition; “abhorrent soil,” Franeker edition; “rough soil,” Junius.] For if inhumanity and inhospitality are to receive no vengeance from him, why does he send testimony in advance, which surely portends threats?

Furthermore, since the Creator also forbids the Ammonites and Moabites to be received into the assembly in Deuteronomy (Deut. 23:3), [Note: [Col. 0420D] “He forbids the Ammonites and Moabites to be received into the Church.” Septimius adapts the term “Church” to Jewish times. RIG.] because they had inhumanely and inhospitably withheld provisions from the people departing Egypt, it will therefore be evident that the prohibition of fellowship passed from there into Christ; [Note: [Col. 0420D] “Prohibition of fellowship.” Thus in the book *On Monogamy*: “But they exclude that woman from fellowship.” RIG.—“Prohibition of fellowship.” This can be explained with reference to the Church’s interdicts. Tertullian, a diligent observer of forensic formulas and legal terminology, employed this phrase. Among the jurists an interdict is something pronounced temporarily by a judge; and the glosses say, διαλεγόμενον δύο ἀμφισβητούντων περὶ νομῆς, “a determination between two persons disputing over possession.” Thus an interdict of the Church is temporary separation from the common assembly of the faithful until one who is ἀκοινώνητος, “excluded from communion,” has made satisfaction for his offenses. *Interdictum* means, as it were, *interim dictum*, “something pronounced for the meantime,” as in Cicero, *Letters to Friends* VII.32: “Let us, [Col. 0421D] I beg you, defend our possession of urbanity by every kind of interdict.” The emperor Justinian, however, maintains that it is so called as though “pronounced between two parties.” LE PR.] here he has [Col. 0420C] the formula: “He who despises you despises me.” The Creator said the same thing to Moses (Num. 14:27): “They have not despised you, but me.” For Moses was as much an apostle as the Apostles were prophets. The authority of the two offices must be made equal, since there is one and the same Lord of Apostles and Prophets.

Who, now, will give power to tread upon serpents and scorpions? The Lord of all animals, or a god who does not own even one lizard? [Note: [Col. 0420D] “Lizard” omitted by Semler.] It is fortunate that through Isaiah (Isa. 11:8) the Creator promised this power to little children [Note: [Col. 0420D] “Very young children,” Oberthür.]: to put their hands into the holes of asps and into the den of the offspring of asps, and yet [Col. 0421A] suffer no injury at all.

And we surely know—without prejudice to the literal sense of Scripture, for even the beasts themselves will be unable to harm where there is faith—that scorpions and serpents figuratively portend spiritual forces of wickedness. Their prince himself, in the Creator’s writings, is designated by the name of the serpent, the dragon, and every most monstrous beast. The Creator granted this power first to his Christ, as Psalm 90 says to him (Ps. 90:13): “You shall walk upon the asp and the basilisk, and you shall trample the lion and the dragon”; and as Isaiah also says (Isa. 27:1): “On that day the Lord God will bring his holy, great, and mighty sword”—that is, his Christ—“against that dragon, the great and twisting serpent, and on that day he will kill him.”

But the same Isaiah also says (Isa. 35:8): “It shall be called a clean way and a holy way, and nothing unclean shall pass along it, nor shall there be an unclean way there; but those who have been scattered shall walk upon it, and they shall not go astray. There shall no longer be a lion there, nor shall any of the worst beasts come up upon it or be found there.” [Col. 0421B] Since the way signifies the faith by which we shall come to God, he promised even then to that same way—that is, to the faith—this dispensation [Note: [Col. 0421D] “Removal,” Pamelius; “summoning,” Paris edition.] and subjection of the beasts.

Finally, you will find that the times of the promise also agree, if you read what precedes (Isa. 35:3): “Be strengthened, hands that hang down and knees that are feeble. Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall hear. Then the lame man shall leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute shall be clear.” Therefore, when he manifested the benefits of healing, he also subjected scorpions and serpents to his holy ones: he, namely, who had first received this power from the Father so that he might confer it upon others also, and who bestowed it in accordance with the order of the proclamation.

## CHAPTER XXV

[Col. 0421C]

Who will be invoked as Lord of heaven if he has not first been shown to be its Maker? For he says: “I give thanks and make confession, Lord of heaven, because you have revealed to little children the things that were hidden from the wise and prudent.” What are these things? Whose are they? By whom were they hidden, and by whom revealed?

If they were hidden and revealed by Marcion’s god, who had introduced absolutely nothing beforehand in which anything could have been hidden—no prophecies, no parables, no visions, no indications in things, words, or names, obscured by allegories and figures or by the clouds of riddles—but had hidden his own greatness itself, which he was just then revealing through Christ, he had acted quite unjustly. For what offense had the wise and prudent committed, that God should be hidden from them? Their wisdom and prudence had not sufficed for knowing him, [Col. 0421D] because he himself had provided no path through any proclamation of his works, or any footprints by which the wise and prudent might be led.

And even if they had in some respect [Col. 0422A] sinned against the unknown God—suppose him then [Note: [Col. 0422D] “Now,” Semler.] to have been known—they still ought not to have found him jealous, since he is presented as unlike the Creator. Therefore, if he had neither provided materials from which he might conceal something, nor had guilty persons from whom he might conceal it, nor ought to have concealed it even if he had had such persons, then he will not be its revealer either, since he was not its concealer. Thus neither is he Lord of heaven nor Christ’s Father; rather, he is the one to whom all these things properly belong.

For the Creator both concealed, having first supplied the instrument of prophetic obscurity, whose understanding faith would deserve—“For unless you believe,” he says (Isa. 7:9), “you will not understand”—and had as guilty persons [Col. 0422B] the wise and prudent, who did not seek the intelligible God from so many and such great works of his, or who philosophized wrongly about him and furnished their cleverness to heretics. Finally, he is jealous. Indeed, long ago he proclaimed through Isaiah the very thing over which Christ rejoices (Isa. 29:14): “I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and I will hide the prudence of the prudent.”

Likewise elsewhere (Isa. 45:3) he signifies both that he has concealed and that he has revealed: “I will give them hidden treasures; I will open invisible things to them.” And again (Isa. 44:25): “Who else will scatter the signs of ventriloquists [Note: [Col. 0421D] “The signs of ventriloquists.” The author translates the Greek ἐγγαστριμύθων, and does so not only here, but wherever the blessed Jerome translates it as “Pythons.” According to the lexicons of Cyril and Hesychius, they were so called because they pretended to have gods in their bellies and therefore spoke from the belly; hence women possessed by a Python were more common than men. PAM.], and divinations from the heart, turning the wise backward and making foolish their thoughts?”

Moreover, if he also designated his Christ as the illuminator of the nations (Isa. 42:6), “I have appointed you as a light of the nations”—whom we understand under the name of “little children,” since formerly they were small in understanding and infants in lack of prudence, and now are also lowly through the humility of faith—then we shall surely find it easier to believe that the same one also revealed to little children through Christ who had formerly concealed and had promised revelation through Christ.

[Col. 0422C; the supplied source is textually corrupt at this transition, and the translation follows its apparent sense.] Or if Marcion’s god disclosed things that had formerly been hidden by the Creator from the wise and prudent, then he acted on the Creator’s business, explaining the Creator’s affairs. “But,” you say, “he did so to destroy them, by exposing them.” Then he ought to have exposed them to those from whom the Creator had hidden them. For if he acted out of kindness, recognition ought to have been granted to those from whom it had been denied, not to little children, from whom the Creator had withheld nothing. And yet, even now, I think, we prove that Christ is found to build up rather than destroy the Law and the Prophets.

[Note: [Col. 0422D] “He says that all things were delivered to him by the Father.” He proceeds again with the words of the Gospel. Yet what the author adds here—that the Creator delivered all things to the Son as to one who was not inferior to himself—also argues against the Arians and certain modern Arianizers. The blessed Hilary and Augustine likewise interpret this passage in the same way in various places. PAM.] He says that all things were delivered to him by the Father. You may believe it if Christ belongs to the Creator, to whom all things belong: the Creator delivered all things, which he made through him—that is, through his Word [Col. 0422D]—to the Son, who was not inferior to himself.

But if he is that newcomer, ἐπερχόμενος (*eperchomenos*), what are the “all things” that were delivered to him by the Father? Are they the Creator’s things? Then the things that the Father delivered to the Son are good, and the Creator, all of whose things are good, is now good; [Col. 0423A] and the other is now not good, for he invaded another’s goods in order to deliver them to his Son, while teaching people to abstain from what belongs to another. Certainly he is utterly destitute, since he had nothing with which to enrich his Son except another’s property.

Or, if nothing belonging to the Creator was delivered to him by the Father, how does he claim for himself the Creator’s human being? Or, if the human being alone was delivered to him, a human being is not “all things.” Scripture, however, declares that all things were delivered to the Son.

But even if you interpret “all things” as the races of humanity—that is, all nations—it is the Creator who delivered these also to the Son: “I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession” (Ps. 2:8). Or, if Marcion’s god himself has some things of his own, all of which he might deliver to the Son together with the Creator’s human being, show me any one thing [Note: [Col. 0423D] “Anyone,” Pamelius.] out of them all, as proof and example. Otherwise I have as much reason [Col. 0423B] not to believe that all things belong to one of whose property I see nothing, as I have reason to believe that even the things I do not see belong to the one to whom belong all the things I do see.

“But no one knows who the Father is except the Son, or who the Son is except the Father, and anyone to whom the Son has revealed him” (Matt. 11:27). [Note: [Col. 0423D] “And anyone to whom the Son has revealed him” omitted by Semler, who then writes by repetition: “For no one knows the Father except the Son, or the Son except the Father, and anyone to whom the Son has revealed him.”] And thus Christ proclaimed an unknown God.

[Note: [Col. 0423D] “For other heretics too are supported by this.” These were evidently those who, because of the frequent revelations expressed in Scripture, confused the Father with the Son and asserted that they were the same; Praxeas fell into impious delusions of this kind. LE PR.] For other heretics too are supported by this, arguing that the Creator was known to everyone: to Israel through familiarity, and to the nations through nature. Yet how does he himself testify that he was not known even to Israel: “But Israel did not know me, and my people did not understand me” (Isa. 1:3)? Nor to the nations: “For behold,” he says, “there is no one even among the nations” (Isa. 65:5). For this reason he also reckoned them as a drop from a bucket and left Sion like a watchtower in a vineyard (Isa. 1:8; 40:15).

Consider, therefore, whether this is not a confirmation [Col. 0423C] of the prophetic utterance that reproaches humanity’s ignorance of God, which continued until the Son. For this reason he also added that the Father is known by the one to whom the Son reveals him, since he was the one announced as appointed by the Father to be the illumination of the nations (Isa. 42), which certainly had to be enlightened concerning God, and of Israel also, surely through a fuller recognition of God.

Thus arguments that can properly apply to the Creator will not advance belief in another god; only those that cannot properly apply [Note: [Col. 0423D] “Do not properly apply,” Pamelius.] to the Creator will be able to advance belief in another god.

If you also examine what follows—“Blessed are the eyes that see what you see; for I tell you that the prophets did not see what you [Note: [Col. 0423D] “You” absent from the Franeker edition.] see”—these words descend from the preceding sense: to such a degree had no one known God as was fitting, since not even the prophets had seen the things that were being seen under Christ. For if Christ were not mine, [Col. 0423D] he would not have introduced mention of the prophets in this passage. For what wonder would it be if they had not seen the affairs of an unknown god who was revealed so many ages later?

And what blessedness would have belonged to those who then saw things which others [Col. 0424A] had rightly been unable to see, if they had not obtained the realization of things that those others had never proclaimed? The point is rather that [Note: [Col. 0424D] Pamelius adds “who.”] those who might have seen the works of their own God, which they had also proclaimed, nevertheless had not seen them. [Note: [Col. 0424D] Junius proposes “these” in place of “who.”] But this will be the blessedness of the others: they saw the things that the former had only proclaimed.

Finally, we shall show—and have already shown—that the things seen in Christ were the things that had been proclaimed, yet had remained hidden even from the prophets themselves, so that they might also be hidden from the wise and prudent of the age.

In the Gospel of truth, a teacher of the Law approached the Lord and said: “What must I do to obtain eternal life?” In the heretic’s Gospel merely “life” is written, without mention of “eternal,” so that the teacher may appear to have inquired about that long life [Note: [Col. 0424D] Oberthür adds “eternal.”] which is promised in the Law by the Creator, and so that the Lord may therefore appear to have given him an answer according to the Law: [Col. 0424B] [Note: [Col. 0424D] “Gave,” Semler.] “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength,” because he was asking about the life promised by the Law.

But surely the teacher of the Law knew how he could obtain the life prescribed by the Law, and so would not have asked about something whose rules he himself taught. Rather, since even the dead were now being raised by Christ, and since the examples of restored life had awakened him to hope for eternal life, he inquired about obtaining eternal life in order to learn whether that higher hope required some additional observance. [Note: [Col. 0424D] “Of hope,” Rhenanus.]

Accordingly, the Lord, showing both that he himself was not another and that he was introducing no new commandment other than what is fundamentally effective for all salvation and for both kinds of life, confronts him with the very head of the Law: the command to love the Lord his God in every respect.

Finally, if the man inquired about long life, and Christ answered concerning that life which is with the Creator, [Col. 0424C] not concerning the eternal life which is with Marcion’s god, how does he obtain eternal life? Surely not in the same way as long life. For corresponding to the difference in rewards, a difference in works must also be believed to exist. Therefore a Marcionite will not obtain eternal life through love of your god, in the way that a lover of the Creator obtains long life.

But what sense does it make that the one who promises eternal life should not be loved still more, if the one who promises long life must be loved? Therefore both kinds of life will belong to the same God, since the discipline by which each life must be sought is the same. What the Creator teaches is also what Christ requires in order that he may grant the reward: namely, the love of God. [Note: [Col. 0424D] The Wouwer manuscript reads “the command to love God.”] Here too there intervenes that rule by which greater things ought more readily to be believed of the one with whom lesser things precede than of one for whom no lesser things have prepared any confidence concerning the greater.

Let him now consider whether our people added the word “eternal.” [Col. 0424D] This is enough for me: that this Christ, the inviter to eternal rather than merely long life, when consulted about the long life which he was destroying, did not exhort the man instead to the eternal life which he was introducing.

[Col. 0425A] What, I ask you, would the Creator’s Christ have done if one who had built the man up to love the Creator did not belong to the Creator? I suppose he would have denied that the Creator ought to be loved.
