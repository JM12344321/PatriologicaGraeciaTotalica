How highly, then, Tertullian argues, do you pagans value—that is, at what price do you estimate—this surely very great benefit which Christians confer upon you? [Col. 1090C] By that fact alone, were they not assuredly of the greatest benefit to you, as we said above (supra, chap. 22, art. 1)?

But he presses the matter further and, turning back upon the pagans the weapon they had aimed at Christians, plainly demonstrates that they themselves inflict the greatest harm upon the commonwealth. For since, as we have said more than once, the number of Christians was far greater than theirs, did not the commonwealth assuredly suffer the greatest loss from those very men through whose cruelty so many righteous Christians, as our Author says, “we are expended,” that is, consumed and killed? For it was in this sense that the Latin translator of Paul’s epistles wrote: “But I will most gladly expend, and myself be expended besides, for your souls” (Paul, Second Epistle to the Corinthians, chap. XII, 15). Tertullian further adds: [Col. 1090D] “When we innocents are expended” (Tertullian, *On Prescription*, chap. 2). Pamelius, indeed, would prefer *erodimur*, “we are consumed”; but our Author uses the verb *erogare*, “to expend,” too frequently for it to be believed, against the evidence of all the manuscripts, that the word is corrupt there. It is therefore used there with the same meaning as when he said elsewhere that fever had been appointed “for expending a man.” And indeed a man is expended by a fever whose outcome is death. Elsewhere he also wrote that gladiators who had learned how to be killed were expended on the appointed day of the funeral rites (*On the Shows*, chap. 12). Finally, concerning the fire of hell, as we have observed (supra, chap. 11, art. 4), he said: “While it expends, it restores.”

Moreover, what, I ask, could have resulted in greater harm to the commonwealth than, contrary to human and divine [Col. 1091A] laws, to persecute countless Christians—not only useful to the commonwealth by their very numbers, but also excellent citizens, venerable for the integrity of their conduct and life, enemies of no one, guilty of no crime—merely, as we demonstrated above (supra, chap. 14, art. 3), on account of their name, and to torture and kill them with every kind of exquisitely devised torment? The pagans, therefore, for this reason alone, were certainly inflicting a great evil upon the entire commonwealth. Christians, by contrast, assuredly brought no evil upon it; or, if anything ever done by them could be pointed out, it was certainly repaired by them from other sources, in various ways, and with ample interest.

Thus far, then, we have expounded and elucidated each and every argument by which Tertullian demonstrates the truth and holiness of the Christian religion, its sincere [Col. 1091B] teachings, and the utterly unjust persecution of Christians. It therefore remains now to examine how he proved the religion of the pagans to be false.

## CHAPTER XXV. The Arguments by Which Tertullian Demonstrates That the Religion of the Pagans Is False, and That All Their Gods Are False

### ARTICLE I. How Clearly the Falsity of the Pagans’ Religion Is Demonstrated from the Truth of the Christian Religion; from Demons Cast Out of the Bodies of the Possessed; from the Certain Testimony of Human Conscience; from the Superior and Better-Known, but Entirely False, Divinity of Saturn, Who Was a Man and, After Being Received by Janus in Italy, Gave His Name to the City He Had Staked Out and to All Italy, Which Had Previously Been Called Oenotria; and Likewise from the Testimony of Thallus, Cassius, and Nepos; and How It Therefore Follows That the Divinity of Jupiter and the Other Gods, Vaunted by the Pagans, Is Also False

[Col. 1091C]

The very arguments by which, as we have seen, Tertullian invincibly asserted and vindicated the truth of the Christian religion utterly overthrow all the gods and superstitious religions of the pagans. For if Christ’s divinity is true, as our Tertullian acutely observes, it follows that every other belief found to be contrary to it must be pronounced false (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 404). Moreover, we have demonstrated most clearly with him (supra, chap. 7, art. 1) that there can be only one God, and consequently that all the others, however many the pagans could invent, are false.

[Col. 1091D] Nor have we demonstrated any less clearly (supra, chap. 8, art. 1) that demons were cast out by Christians from the bodies they possessed, and that the demons were then compelled openly to confess that they were not gods, as the pagans believed, but unclean spirits; that the one proclaimed by the Christians was the only God; and that Christ was His Power, Wisdom, Reason, and Son, by whose name and power they were put to flight. There was therefore no need for Tertullian (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 23) to accumulate many further arguments by which he might prove more fully that the pagans’ gods were wholly imaginary and fictitious, and consequently that their religion was false. But so that those same pagans might at long last, if possible, feel utter shame for the impious worship of their gods and for their perverse religion, [Col. 1092A] he employs various further arguments to make it perfectly evident to everyone that such manifest marks of that false religion can be detected that whoever fails to perceive them must wish to blind himself completely.

First, therefore, he calls their very conscience as a witness against the pagans. Constrained by it, they were compelled to confess, as we saw above, that there is one God, and that all those whom they called and worshipped as gods had in reality been men. But if any wicked man had dared to resist the most certain testimony of his own conscience, he was plainly refuted by the voiceless indeed, but most eloquent, outcry of all the cities in which these gods, as we have observed more than once (supra, chap. 7, art. 1), were born, lived, died, and were buried. Since, however, their multitude [Col. 1092B] was infinite, and it would take too long to enumerate them individually or even call them by their names, Tertullian attacks their very chief and the origin best known to all (*ibid.*, chap. 11, p. 12 ff.; chap. 29, p. 438; and book II *To the Nations*, chap. 1).

He therefore presses and addresses the pagans as follows: “Before Saturn there is no god among you: from him derives the census of the whole divinity, or at least of its superior or better-known part” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 332; and book I *To the Nations*, chap. 12, p. 578). He said “the superior or better-known” (*ibid.*), or, as in our handwritten copies, “and better-known,” which is not at all amiss. For he indicates that he derives the beginnings of the gods not from the first origin of idolatry—which, as we have seen elsewhere, is obscure and is not assigned identically by everyone (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 717 ff.; and dissertation [Col. 1092C] above, chap. 30 ff.)—but from the very divinity of Saturn, who was held to be superior to and better known than all the other gods. What wonder is it that he should begin with Saturn, when Varro, who had composed so many books about his gods, vaunts Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva as the most ancient of all the gods (Tertullian, book II *To the Nations*, chap. 12)? Yet Jupiter was said to have been begotten by Saturn, who was therefore more ancient than he. Tertullian did not think that he should begin with Saturn’s parents, Heaven, or Chronus, and Earth, both because Saturn, their only son, was better known than they and all the other gods and was believed to be superior, and because these accounts and fables of the poets concerning his origin from Heaven and Earth are ridiculous inventions. He was indeed imagined to have been born from them because his parents were unknown. For, as Tertullian says, in ordinary human usage those whose origins are unknown, or who appear unexpectedly, are said to have descended from heaven (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 10, p. 331; and book II *To the Nations*, chap. 12), [Col. 1092D] just as the common people call those whose lineage is uncertain “sons of the earth.” But we have explained all this in our preceding dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 15, art. 1; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 30, art. 1, and chap. 31, art. 2).

Moreover, in the same place we made it clear to everyone how rightly Tertullian asserted that Saturn had been a man, who, after many expeditions, reached Italy and, after being received there by Janus, “the mountain,” he says, “which he inhabited was called Saturnian; the city which he had staked out remains Saturnia to this day.” In the old edition and another [Col. 1093A] more recent one we read *debellaverat*, “he had conquered”; but *depalaverat*, “he had staked out,” is Tertullian’s word (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*). For when disputing against Hermogenes concerning the creation of the world, he says: “God completed all His works in due order, first staking out, as it were, the world with its elements still uncultivated” (the same, *Against Hermogenes*, chap. 29). Again, against Marcion: “As for his affirmation that he is a wise architect, we find this to mean the one who marks out the divine discipline received from the Creator” (the same, book V *Against Marcion*, chap. 6). Accordingly, he said that the city had been “staked out” by Saturn, that is, founded or established by setting up stakes, or, as others prefer, fortified with stakes. They attempt to support their explanation with these words of Hermas: “When he had staked out the vineyard” (Hermas, book III, similitude 5, § 2, p. 61).

[Col. 1093B] Tertullian then continues: “Saturn settled there, received by Janus,” or *Jane* (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 10); our manuscripts have *Janae*, “as the Salii maintain.” In two of our copies written in a more recent hand, however, and in the first book *To the Nations*, where the same material is repeated, we read: “Even if others call him” (book I *To the Nations*, chap. 12, p. 578); perhaps better, “as others call him.” For what purpose would the Salii be mentioned there? Was it either by them alone or by them first that Saturn was said to have been named after Janus, rather than received by Janus? But we leave this to the judgment of others.

After Saturn had established the dwelling given him by Janus, “all Italy,” says our Author, “was called Saturnia after Oenotria” (Tertullian, *ibid.*); and in the book *To the Nations*: “All Italy was named after Saturn.” Virgil plainly [Col. 1093C] teaches in the following verses that Italy was first named Oenotria:

> There is a place the Greeks call by the name Hesperia,  
> An ancient land, mighty in arms and in the richness of its soil.  
> Oenotrian men inhabited it; now report has it that their descendants  
> Called the nation Italy after the name of their leader.  
> (Virgil, book I of the *Aeneid*, v. 534 ff.)

Not content to have sung this only once, he repeats it in the same words in another book (book III, v. 163 ff.). See Servius’ annotations on these verses, Antiochus as cited by Strabo (Strabo, book III of the *Geography*, p. 254), and others. Italy, therefore, named after Saturn, was previously called Saturnia, as is evident from these verses of Ovid:

> I remember that Saturn was received in this land;  
> He had been driven by Jupiter from the celestial realms.  
> Hence for a long time the name Saturnia remained with the nation;  
> The land was also called Latium because the god lay hidden there.  
> (Ovid, book II of the *Fasti*, § 3, v. 235.)

[Col. 1093D] For this reason it is called by the poets, as we have seen (*Dissertation on Minucius*, chap. 16, art. 1), the Saturnian soil and land, and by Virgil, where he sang:

> Hail, great mother of crops, Saturnian land!  
> (Virgil, book II of the *Georgics*, v. 174.)

Do not all these things prove clearly enough that Saturn was a man? Tertullian further confirms this, both in the *Apologeticum* and in the second book *To the Nations*, by the testimony of Diodorus, Thallus, Cassius Severus, and Cornelius Nepos, whom we have discussed elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius*, final chapter, art. 2; and *On Lactantius*, chap. 31, art. 3). Yet we may first offer an observation of some relevance: in the former book the name is written Cassius [Col. 1094A] Severus. There were two distinguished men of that name. One, as we read in Eusebius’ *Chronicle*, was an outstanding orator (*Chronicle of Eusebius*, at the year 2047), who was sent into exile during the reign of the emperor Augustus. The other was the poet of Parma, one of those who, together with Brutus, killed Julius Caesar; concerning him Lilius Giraldus (Giraldus, Dialogue IV, *On the History of Poets*, p. 160), Vossius (Vossius, *On Latin Poets*, chap. 2), Scaliger (Scaliger, on the cited *Chronicle of Eusebius*), and others (Fabius, book X; and Seneca, epistle 79) provide more information. Both, moreover, are called Cornelius Severus by some. But in Tertullian’s text the cognomen *Severus* was perhaps added; it is omitted, as we have seen elsewhere, by Minucius Felix and Lactantius. For the things he relates about Saturn seem to have been transmitted in writing by Cassius [Col. 1094B] Hemina rather than by Cassius Severus, as we have also noted. In Tertullian’s book *To the Nations*, too, a careless copyist wrote: “The Cornelii Nepotes and Tacitus.” For what purpose would Tacitus be mentioned there, when no mention of him is made in the *Apologeticum*, or by Minucius and Lactantius (*Dissertation on Minucius*, final chapter, art. 2)? Should we not read there, as in their books, “the Greek Thallus”?

Whatever judgment those more expert in the matter may pronounce—and we shall always abide by their judgment—Tertullian affirms without hesitation that no author can be found or cited by any pagan who ever denied that Saturn was a man, who received both life and a kingdom from another man, and who therefore cannot be God (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 10, p. 331; and book II *To the Nations*, chap. 12, p. 578). For the true God, as we have often said [Col. 1094C] (*Dissertation on Minucius*, final chapter, art. 2), exists from Himself alone and is therefore almighty: He created men and all other things, rules over all, and dispenses all kingdoms.

After this Tertullian says: “Enough now concerning Saturn” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 10, p. 331), to demonstrate, even to someone rather dull and obstinate, that his divinity is altogether false. He deliberately added there, “though in few words.” For in the second book *To the Nations* he demonstrates its falsity by various arguments (book II *To the Nations*, chaps. 7 and 16), which have been explained in our earlier dissertations and need not be repeated here.

Furthermore, if Saturn neither was nor could have been a god, because he was a man like other men, much [Col. 1094D] more are Jupiter, his son, and almost the whole host of countless others devoid of divinity, since it is established that they originated from the same Saturn, or at least after him (the same, *Apologeticum*, chap. 10, p. 333). For who does not know that Jupiter, whom the pagans called the best and greatest god, was said to have been born from the union—or rather the incest—of that same Saturn with his sister Ops, or Rhea? When grown, however, after driving out his father, he seized the latter’s kingdom and, assuming, as Tertullian says, the forms of a scaly creature, a horned creature, a feathered creature, and a shower of gold, begot children through infamous debaucheries and adulteries (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 21, p. 396; and book II *To the Nations*, chap. 13).

Who, then, after weighing all these matters, which we have elucidated in our other dissertations (*Dissertation on [Col. 1095A] Minucius*, chap. 15, art. 1; *Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 11, art. 4; and *On Lactantius*, chap. 3, art. 1 ff.), could ever deny that he was not a god, but a wicked man who had cast off every sense of humanity and shame?

### ARTICLE II. How Preposterously the Pagans Vaunted That Their Gods Had Been Made Gods After This Mortal Life by Another, Superior Dispenser of Divinity; That There Is Only One God, Who Had Created the World Before All the Gods of the Pagans, and the Crops Before Ceres, Bacchus, and Minerva; and That the Discoverers of Those Crops Could No More Be Called Gods Than Lucullus, Who First Brought Cherries into Italy, and Cato, Who First Brought in the Green Fig

[Col. 1095B] The pagans indeed admitted—what they could never deny—that Saturn and the others whom they had dreamed to be gods had in reality been men; but with Varro they maintained that divinity was bestowed upon them after they had duly completed the service of this mortal life (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 335). Yet in vain they attempted to defend their absurd opinion with another, still more absurd invention. For men could not become gods after their death unless either by their own power or through some other god more powerful than they. But both alternatives are false. They themselves certainly did not possess the power to confer divinity upon themselves when dead. For if they had ever possessed such power, they would never have been men subject to the weaknesses and vices of mortals. Our Author gives this reason for it: “They would never have been men, since they possessed, that is, a power superior [Col. 1095C] to the frail human condition” (*ibid.*). For only the Son of God, God from eternity, lived a mortal life after assuming human nature, though in a wholly wonderful manner and without any stain of vice or sin.

Nor could the fictitious gods of the pagans receive divinity from anyone else unless he were greater and more powerful than they; or, to use Tertullian’s words to the pagans: “You must concede that there is some higher God and a certain dispenser of divinity” (*ibid.*). The term *manceps* is used by writers of more polished Latin for one who publicly contracts for, sells, or purchases something. But here it seems rather to be used by our Author for a sort of guardian, administrator, and superintendent of divinity (the same, *On Idolatry*, chap. 1; and [Col. 1095D] *On Modesty*, chap. 14); in this sense the superintendents of baths and other things are called *mancipes* in the Theodosian Code (Theodosian Code, sole law *On Mancipes*, vol. V, p. 183). But whoever says that Saturn and other dead men received their divinity from some other dispenser of it thereby openly confesses that they are not gods. For it is altogether necessary that the true God exist from Himself alone and be eternal.

Moreover, no one, unless he is plainly foolish, would ever say that anything was done by the almighty and supremely wise God without reflection, rashly, and without cause. Yet absolutely no cause can be assigned why He should share His divinity with dead men (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 333). Only a twofold [Col. 1096A] cause could be—or rather could have been imagined—for this. First, because God needed their services and assistance; second, because through their outstanding deeds and virtues they merited divinity. But both are proved more false than falsehood itself.

For what assistance from either dead or living men has almighty God ever needed? Was it perhaps for creating the world? But any pagans who assert this will find Pythagoras opposed to them in the matter (*ibid.*, p. 13), for, as we saw above (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 21, art. 2), he proclaimed that the world was uncreated. Yet although the world was in reality created, as Plato and other philosophers rightly taught, as has also been noted, the true God, whose power is infinite, nevertheless needed no one’s assistance whatsoever [Col. 1096B] in making it.

What of the fact that it is established that, before Saturn and any of the other pagan gods, rain, as Tertullian says, had fallen from heaven, the heavenly bodies (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*)—namely, the sun and moon—had shone, and the lights—that is, the stars—had flourished? For in another book he wrote: “Heaven had flourished with lights; the sun had exulted all the more with its rays” (book IV *Against Marcion*, chap. 42). What assistance, then, could Saturn or anyone else have given God in creating the world, when they arose long after the sun, moon, and stars? Tertullian adds that before all the gods of the pagans “thunder had bellowed, and Jupiter himself had feared those lightning bolts which you place in his hand” (or, as in the older printed and unprinted copies, “impose upon him”) (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 334). And indeed, since [Col. 1096C] it is certain that thunder had bellowed before Jupiter existed, no one should doubt that, through the fear innate in all human beings, he was afraid, at least the first time, of thunderbolts he had never heard before. Tertullian therefore rightly and deservedly ridicules the pagans, who babbled that flashes of lightning and thunderbolts were in Jupiter’s hand and were hurled by him, although they had not been made by him.

But he presses still more forcefully: nothing imperfect is made by the Almighty, and therefore the world was created by Him complete in every respect. He had therefore created everything necessary to sustain human life before any of the pagan gods and before all men. Accordingly, says Tertullian himself, after man had been created, nothing could be introduced as having been provided and given for “containing” man—that is, preserving him and preventing him from dying—or for supporting, that is, sustaining, nourishing, [Col. 1096D] and restoring him (*ibid.*). But these words of his must not be pressed so literally that he is believed to have said that after man was established nothing could be created by God by which human life might be preserved and sustained. For he meant nothing other than that everything without which human life cannot be preserved had been created by God before any human beings were established, and much more before Saturn and Jupiter and all the other gods of the pagans. He therefore concludes from this as certainly as he does clearly that the true God needed no one’s assistance in making the world. No gods, therefore, were made for this reason.

He further insists that every crop had abounded from the earth before [Col. 1097A] Liber, Ceres, and Minerva, indeed “before that first man” (*ibid.*), namely Saturn, whom the pagans babbled was the superior and first of all the gods, as we said a little above. Here he chastises the pagans, who believed that Liber was enrolled among the gods because of the discovery of wine, Ceres because of grain, and Minerva because of the olive tree. For these crops necessarily had to be created before they could be discovered. But only He by whom they were created and established is God. Nor should one press the objection that Lucretius, speaking of these matters, used the verb “establish,” when he sang:

> For Ceres is said to have established crops for mortals,  
> And Liber the liquid of the vine-born juice,  
> Although life could remain without these things,  
> As some peoples are reportedly living even now.  
> (Lucretius, book V, v. 14 ff.)

[Col. 1097B] For the poet is speaking there of things discovered by the skill and ingenuity of certain persons. He therefore used “establish” in no other sense than “discover.” If anyone maintains that he said “establish” because Bacchus and Ceres were said to have taught the method of making bread from crops and wine from grapes, we shall not object very strongly. But he will gain not the slightest advantage from this. For it is wholly sufficient for us that it is more certain than certainty itself that no crops were created by Bacchus, Ceres, and Minerva. No one can either become or be a god because crops were pointed out or discovered by him.

Nor should anyone thereafter be heard who dares to object that the discovery of crops was of such great benefit to human beings that, on account of this benefit alone, Ceres, Bacchus, and Minerva deserved to be numbered among the company of the gods. [Col. 1097C] For this reasoning has already been sufficiently refuted. Then again, if it were of any importance, Lucullus too ought to have become a god, since he was the first, as Tertullian says, to introduce cherries (the manuscripts read *cerasia*) from Pontus into Italy (Tertullian, *ibid.*). From Pliny, moreover, we learn that in the year 680 from the founding of the city, after the victory over Mithridates, cherries were brought by this Lucullus from Pontus into Italy (Pliny, book XV of the *Natural History*, chap. 25, p. 207), or, as Jerome says, from Cerasus in Pontus (Jerome, epistle 23 to Eustochium, p. 60), from which they derived their name. On the authority of these distinguished men, therefore, another passage of Tertullian in the second book *To the Nations* must be corrected, where we read: “Gnaeus Pompey introduced from Pontus . . . . . into Italy” (Tertullian, book II *To the Nations*, chap. 16).

[Col. 1097D] Tertullian further insists that the green fig was not known at Rome before Cato brought it before the senate (*ibid.*). This whole history, moreover, was related by the already cited Pliny (Pliny, book XV of the *Natural History*, chap. 18, p. 198). From it, as from the preceding account, Tertullian concludes that the pagans are unjust: they placed Ceres, Bacchus, and Minerva among the number of their gods because of crops discovered by them, yet left among the dead Lucullus and Cato, who had brought into Italy and Rome other fruits previously unknown there. But we pursued these matters more fully in our other dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 14, art. 2; *On Arnobius*, chap. 9, art. 2; and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, chap. 30, art. 5), where we refute those same pagans who babbled that human beings had been made [Col. 1098A] gods after their deaths because of benefits conferred upon other human beings.

### ARTICLE III. With What Great Folly the Pagans Vaunted That Saturn, Jupiter, and Others Had Been Made Gods on Account of Their Merits and Illustrious Deeds, While Others More Excellent Than They Were Left among the Dead—Namely Socrates, Aristides, Themistocles, Alexander the Great, Pompey, Polycrates, Sulla, Croesus, Crassus, Demosthenes, and Cicero; and How It Is Shown That, by the Same Reasoning by Which the Pagans Ascribed Divinity to Their Gods, They Ought to Ascribe It to Lais, Phryne, Larentia, and to the Caesars Themselves, Whether Dead or Living

The second reason the pagans gave for bestowing divinity upon dead men was drawn from their merits and [Col. 1098B] outstanding deeds, by which, they boldly vaunted, those men had attained it. Yet whoever seriously affirmed this was compelled, as Tertullian rightly observes, to admit that there is some “deifying God” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 333), that is, one who makes others gods because of certain illustrious deeds. That preeminent God must therefore weigh the merits of others in the most equitable balance of justice and reward them with the due recompense through His omnipotence. But divinity belongs to this supremely just and omnipotent being alone, and He cannot give it to anyone distinct from Himself, whether by nature or through any merits whatsoever. For supreme divinity cannot be divided, and, as we have often said, either God is one or there is none.

But come, tell us, I pray, what virtues and outstanding [Col. 1098C] merits Saturn, Jupiter, Juno, and the others possessed by which they could have merited divinity. Did not the pagans openly proclaim and insolently boast that those gods had lived lives infamous and defiled by debaucheries, adulteries, and every kind of most grievous crime and outrage, as we have demonstrated elsewhere (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 12, art. 1 ff.)? Grant, nevertheless, that they were adorned with as many and as great virtues as the vices by which they were stained; why, then, Tertullian asks, when these were raised to heaven and endowed with divinity, were others equal to them in virtue, and even more excellent, left among the dead (Tertullian, *ibid.*)? For no pagan, however reckless in daring, could deny what is most clearly proved by the examples of illustrious men whose exceptional and singular virtues the pagans themselves extolled with the highest praises.

[Col. 1098D] For, to review the examples proposed by Tertullian (*ibid.*), what pagan god was wiser than Socrates, who was pronounced the wisest of all mortals by the oracle of Apollo cited by us elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius’ Octavius*, chap. 3, art. 2)? Yet so far was he from being called a god that he was actually condemned by the judgment of the Athenians. “Whenever I read Plato,” Cicero used to say, “I am accustomed to weep over his death” (Cicero, book III *On the Nature of the Gods*, p. 252, line 29). We discussed that death of Socrates in our preceding dissertations (*Dissertation on Minucius*, chap. 3, art. 2; and *On Lactantius*, chap. 14, art. 1), and something further must be said a little later (infra, chap. 26, art. 4).

What pagan god was more just than Aristides (Tertullian, [Col. 1099A] *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 336), “by whom,” as Valerius Maximus says, “the justice of all Greece is measured” (Valerius Maximus, book V, chap. 3, § 3, foreign examples)? For this reason he is called by Cicero, antonomastically, “Aristides the Just” (Cicero, book III *On Duties*, p. 339, line 7), and in Greek by Plutarch Ἀριστείδης ὁ δίκαιος (“Aristides the Just”) (Plutarch, vol. I, p. 318; and vol. II, p. 186). Nevertheless, the pagans not only denied him the divinity granted to other, utterly unjust men, but, through a faction of wicked men, he was ordered to leave his homeland, as the same Valerius Maximus testifies. He immediately adds: “Happy Athens, which after his exile was able to find anyone who was either a good man or a lover of herself, since holiness itself then departed with him” (Valerius Maximus, *loc. cit.*).

What warlike god was more distinguished in military glory than Themistocles (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, [Col. 1099B] p. 336)? Yet although he performed brilliantly in the many military exploits that Thucydides (Thucydides, book I of the *Histories*, p. 62 ff.) and Plutarch (Plutarch, *Life of Themistocles*, p. 113 ff.) recorded for posterity, and although he had rendered his homeland “illustrious, secure, wealthy, and foremost in Greece,” as Valerius Maximus again says (Valerius Maximus, book V, chap. 3, foreign examples, § 3), he found it utterly ungrateful. For, falsely accused of treason, he fled first to Argos, thence to Corcyra, afterward to Admetus, king of the Molossians, and then to Artaxerxes, son of Xerxes. At last, in exile, having drunk bull’s blood, as some think—or poison, as more numerous authorities relate—he flew from the bonds of the body as though from a prison.

What pagan god, our Author continues, was more exalted [Col. 1099C] than Alexander and Pompey? For each man’s achievements in arms and war earned him the surname “Great.” Yet no one who has even lightly handled the books written by Quintus Curtius and Plutarch about their lives can possibly be ignorant of the unhappy end by which both departed this life. Nevertheless, no less than Mars, Romulus, and others, they had merited the reward of divinity by their outstanding deeds.

Moreover, what pagan god was more fortunate than the Greek Polycrates and the Roman Sulla (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*)? For many, as Sallust says, doubted whether Sulla was braver or more fortunate (Sallust, *The Jugurthine War*, chap. 17). Plutarch further attests that after celebrating his triumph, when he had recounted before the assembly the deeds he had performed as bravely as successfully, Sulla ordered that he be surnamed [Col. 1099D] “the Fortunate” (Plutarch, *Life of Sulla*, vol. I, p. 453 ff.). But these achievements did not lead him to divinity. Corrupted by indulgence, he ended his life through a disease of lice. The good fortune of Polycrates, however, is described in these words of Valerius Maximus: “All his undertakings were received upon a smooth course; his hopes grasped the certain fruit of the thing desired; his vows were made and at the same time fulfilled; to will and to be able stood on equal terms” (Valerius Maximus, book VI, chap. 8, § 5, foreign examples). And indeed he enjoyed such success that when he threw a ring of enormous value into the sea to test his good fortune, it was later found in a fish that had been caught. As witnesses of this and his other successes you have Strabo (Strabo, book XIV of the *Geography*, p. 657), Herodotus (Herodotus, book III, [Col. 1100A] § 39 ff.), and a great many others. Yet he completed the course of so many and such great successes not by receiving the title of divinity, but, as Valerius Maximus again records, by suffering the punishment of the cross.

Why say more? No pagan god was richer than Croesus and Crassus (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 337). Concerning the first, Ovid sang:

> Who has not heard of the wealthy Croesus’ opulence?  
> (Ovid, book IV, *From Pontus*.)

Concerning Crassus, however, Cicero says: “He maintained that no amount of money was great enough for one who wished to be foremost in the commonwealth, unless he could support an army from his own revenues” (Cicero, book I *On Duties*, p. 354, line 3). Hence arose the proverb, “Richer than Croesus and Crassus,” concerning which you may read more in Erasmus (Erasmus, [Col. 1100B] *Adages*, chiliad 1, century 6, adage 74). Yet Croesus, defeated and captured by Cyrus, was compelled to endure slavery, “the last,” Cicero says, “of all evils,” and to serve him (Cicero, *Philippic II*, p. 606, line 12). See Herodotus (Herodotus, book I, § 86 ff.), Thucydides, Justin the historian, and others. Who likewise does not know of the unhappy slaughter of Crassus, after he had been routed and defeated by the Parthians together with the greater part of the Roman army, and whose severed head was subjected to mockery? Plutarch relates much concerning him in his life (Plutarch, *Life of Crassus*, vol. I, p. 564 ff.); you may consult him, as also Florus, Velleius Paterculus, Justin the historian, and other writers of Roman history.

What pagan god, moreover, Tertullian says, ever surpassed the Greek Demosthenes or the Latin Cicero in eloquence? [Col. 1100C] But what was the reward of such great Greek and Latin eloquence, utterly unknown to no one? Certainly not divinity, but a most unhappy end by cruel death. Since these facts are sufficiently known to everyone, let it now suffice to prove them with the following five verses of Juvenal, who, after singing of Crassus and Pompey, wrote:

> He begins to desire the eloquence and fame of Demosthenes or Cicero,  
> And desires them throughout the whole festival of Minerva,  
> Whoever still reveres Minerva, purchased for a single coin,  
> . . . . . . . . .  
> But each orator perished through his eloquence; in each case  
> The generous and overflowing fountain of genius delivered him to death.  
> (Juvenal, *Satire* 10, v. 113 ff.)

Who, then, Tertullian argues, would not be indignant with the pagans? For they left so many men who excelled so greatly in virtue and merit among the dead, yet babbled that so many other wicked and thoroughly depraved men had been proclaimed gods after their deaths because of certain [Col. 1100D] illustrious deeds (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, chap. 11, p. 337).

He presses them still further (*ibid.*, chap. 13, p. 347), saying: “What were the virtues and merits of the goddess Larentina?” Was she not a public prostitute? We have certainly proved this sufficiently elsewhere (*Dissertation on Minucius*, chap. 18, art. 1; and *On Lactantius*, chap. 32, art. 1). Would you perhaps call the Junos, the Cereses, and the Dianas more chaste? But the pagans themselves, as we have demonstrated in the preceding dissertations, publicized their unbridled lust and shamelessness. For this reason our Author thus ridicules the mad impiety of the pagans who worshipped these goddesses: “I should wish you at least to worship Lais and Phryne among your Junos, Cereses, and Dianas” (Tertullian, [Col. 1101A] *Apologeticum*, chap. 13, p. 347). Lais, moreover, publicly practiced the harlot’s trade; of her Propertius writes:

> The house of Lais at Ephyra was not so filled  
> At whose door all Greece lay prostrate.  
> (Propertius, book II, Elegy 6, vv. 1–2.)
