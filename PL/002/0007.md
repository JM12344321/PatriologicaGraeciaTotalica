## CHAPTER LI

### Thereafter Cerdon, Marcion, and Marcion’s Disciples Lucanus and Apelles

To these is added a certain Cerdon. He introduces two first principles, that is, two gods: one good and the other savage—the good god being higher, while the savage god is this world’s creator. He repudiates the prophecies and the Law, renounces God the Creator, and teaches that Christ, the Son of the higher God, came. He denies that Christ existed in the substance of flesh, declares that he existed [Col. 0070C] only as a phantom, and says that he did not suffer at all, but only appeared to suffer; that he was not born of a virgin—indeed, that he was not born at all. He approves only the resurrection of the soul and denies that of the body. He accepts only the Gospel of Luke, and not even the whole of it. Of the Apostle Paul’s Epistles he accepts neither all nor complete versions. He rejects the Acts of the Apostles and the Apocalypse as false.

[Note: [Col. 0070D] After him emerged his disciple Marcion. He had been expelled from the Church by his own father because of fornication. His father was a bishop; and when, despite many supplications, he could not be readmitted to the Church by him, he went to Rome, where he was likewise rejected by the apostolic men. When he perceived this, he began to swear allegiance to Cerdon’s ravings and to embrace his impostures. They also report that when, during the pontificate of Anicetus, he met the blessed Polycarp at Rome and asked whether Polycarp knew him, the blessed Polycarp replied: “I know the firstborn of Satan.” LE PR.]

After him [Col. 0071A] there emerged a disciple of his [Note: [Col. 0071D] “Marcion, the son of a bishop, because of the violation of a certain virgin.” Epiphanius also relates this. Yet Septimius, in the book *On the Prescription of Heretics* and in the books against Marcion himself—where he discusses at length Marcion’s expulsion and his crimes—does not mention the violation of a virgin. It is therefore permissible to suppose that this *Catalogue of Heretics* is not Tertullian’s. For in the book *On the Prescription of Heretics* he even praises Marcionite continence, and at the beginning of the books against Marcion he calls Marcion a castrator of the flesh. RIG.] named Marcion, a Pontic by birth and the son of a bishop, who was cast out of the Church’s communion because of the violation of a certain virgin. Seizing upon the saying, “Every good tree produces good fruit, but an evil tree evil fruit,” he attempted to approve Cerdon’s heresy and taught the same things that the earlier heretic had taught before him.

After him there appeared [Note: [Col. 0071D] “A certain Lucanus,” etc. From his own name he had followers called Lucanists. There is also another disciple of Marcion named Lucianus, from whom came the heretical Lucianists. LE PR.] a certain Lucanus, a follower and disciple of Marcion. Proceeding through the same kinds of blasphemy, he too teaches the same things Marcion and Cerdon had taught.

After these comes Apelles, a disciple of Marcion, who, after he had lapsed into the flesh, separated from Marcion. He introduces one God in the infinitely higher regions. This God, he says, created many powers and angels; and accordingly he speaks of another [Col. 0071B] power whom he calls “Lord,” though he makes him an angel. He wishes it to appear that this world was fashioned in imitation of the higher world, and that the angel mingled repentance into this world because he had not made it as perfectly as that higher world had been fashioned.

He repudiates the Law and the Prophets. He says that Christ existed neither as a phantom, as Marcion says, nor in the substance of a real body, as the Gospel teaches; rather, because he descended from the higher regions, in the very act of descending he wove for himself a body made of the stars and the air. At his resurrection, Christ returned to each individual element, during his ascent, what he had borrowed during his descent; and thus, once the several parts of his body had been dispersed, he restored only his spirit to heaven.

Apelles denies the resurrection of the flesh. He uses only the Apostle, and that in Marcion’s version, [Col. 0071C] that is, not the whole. He says that souls alone are saved. He also has his own private and extraordinary readings, which he calls the *Revelations* (*Phaneroseis*) of a certain girl named Philumene, whom he follows as a prophetess. He has, in addition, books of his own entitled *Syllogisms*, in which he seeks to prove that everything Moses wrote concerning God is not true, but false.

## CHAPTER LIII

### Finally Blastus, Both Men Named Theodotus, Praxeas, and Victorinus

Those who belong to the party of Aeschines have this particular blasphemy: they add the claim that Christ himself is both Son and Father.

In addition to all these there is also Blastus, who wishes covertly to introduce Judaism. For he says that the Passover must be observed in no other way than according to the Law of Moses, on the fourteenth day of the month. But who does not know that the grace of the Gospel is nullified if one brings Christ back under the Law?

To these is added Theodotus, a Byzantine heretic, [Col. 0072C] who, after being arrested for the name of Christ, denied him and did not cease to blaspheme Christ. For he introduced a doctrine in which he said that Christ was merely a man and denied that he was God. After him another heretic named Theodotus burst forth. He too introduced a distinct sect and says that Christ himself was merely a man, alike conceived and born from the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary; but he says that Christ is inferior [Col. 0073A] to Melchizedek, because it was said concerning Christ: “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (Ps. 109:4). But after all these, a certain Praxeas also introduced a heresy that Victorinus took pains to reinforce. He says that God the almighty Father is Jesus Christ; he contends that the Father was crucified, suffered, and died. Furthermore, with profane and sacrilegious rashness, he asserts that the Father sits at his own right hand.

# QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN: ON THE CROWN

[Note: [Col. 0073B] “On the Crown.” This is the title given to this book in the manuscript of Agobard, [Col. 0074B] not, as commonly, *On the Crown of the Soldier*.

## ARGUMENT

[Col. 0073B]

Among the books Tertullian composed during his Montanist period, the first place belongs to the book *On the Crown*, published in the year of Christ 201, as Lumper clearly demonstrated in his introductory dissertation, following the highly learned Noesselt. The occasion for writing this treatise was supplied to its author by the soldier’s deed elegantly related below in the opening. Some Christians censured that deed and reproached the soldier for rashly exposing the Church, which was enjoying a long peace, to danger. They further asserted that wearing this [Col. 0074B] crown was an ornament neither good nor evil by its nature. Tertullian alone, in keeping with his harsh temperament, championed the soldier’s cause by writing the book *On the Crown*. He bitterly assailed the pastors as lions in peace and deer in battle, because to be crowned in this way was a sign of idolatry; and when asked in what passage of Scripture the wearing of such crowns was forbidden, he said that tradition was sufficient.

Accordingly, in this book he exalts tradition and custom to no small degree and offers many noteworthy examples of ceremonies that, he contends, were received from tradition. How rich the book is in memorable matters and ancient customs will be apparent from the synopsis of the whole work itself.

After resolutely establishing that wearing a crown was contrary to Christian custom and tradition, he describes in passing the ceremonies of baptism, the sacraments of the Most Holy Eucharist, offerings for the dead and for the anniversaries of the martyrs, the Lord’s Day, fasting, and the sign of the cross upon the forehead.

Then, returning to his purpose, he attacks the use of crowns altogether. He maintains that it is forbidden even by nature, that it positively reeks of superstitious origins, or at least that it was unfamiliar to the patriarchs, the prophets, the Levites, and also to the temple of God, the ark of the covenant, the tabernacles, and the shrines of the martyrs. Finally, he maintains [Col. 0075B] that no crown should be used, whether a military crown, a triumphal crown, a camp crown, a laurel or golden crown granted to magistrates, a crown of festivity, marriage, or freedmen, or the crown of an athletic contest—since Christ submitted to a garland of thorns, while a crown has been laid up in heaven for Christians. EDD.
