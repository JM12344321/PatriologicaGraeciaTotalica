Nor does Tertullian allow the reply that these men [Col. 0709D] were enrolled among the gods only after their death. For no human being can confer divinity upon himself. If, therefore, it was given to anyone either before or after death, he must certainly have received it from another, more powerful God. Yet there was never any reason whatever why that supreme God should make certain men gods after their death. For he has never needed anyone’s assistance, whether in creating the world or in doing anything else. Indeed, rain, the heavenly lights, the stars, thunder, lightning, and the various kinds of crops came into being before all the fictitious gods of the gentiles. Indeed, the first of all human beings was created only after everything necessary for his life. Liber, Ceres, Bacchus, and Minerva, therefore, were no more made gods [Col. 0710A] for discovering crops than Lucullus was for bringing cherries from Pontus to Italy.

Nor can it be said with any better reason that divinity was bestowed upon human beings on account of their merits and virtues. For it follows from this that there is some supreme god who dispenses divinity to those who deserve it. Moreover, the gentiles were compelled to admit that their gods had been wicked, impious, criminal, and thoroughly depraved men, and that others like them were rightly and deservedly condemned by men of good character. Grant, however, that they were upright; they were nevertheless no better than Socrates and Aristides, no braver than Themistocles and Alexander, no more fortunate than Polycrates and Sulla, no richer than Croesus and Crassus, no more eloquent than Demosthenes and Cicero, no wiser than Cato, and no more equitable than Scipio. Why, then, did the pagans leave the latter [Col. 0710B] in the underworld while raising the former to heaven as gods (chap. 11)?

But say further: who are these gods of the gentiles? “I see absolutely nothing in them,” says Tertullian, “except the names of dead men. I hear nothing but fables, and I recognize sacred rites established on the basis of those fables.” What, moreover, of the fact that their images, fashioned from the material of ordinary vessels and implements—or even from those very vessels and implements—were finally made gods by a sacrilegious consecration? Who, furthermore, does not see that while those beautiful images are being made they suffer the same things as Christians do when tortured by the gentiles? For they are made from metals, while Christians are condemned to the mines. They are placed upon crosses and stakes, scraped with claws, and burned with fire; and by all these methods [Col. 0710C] Christians are removed from the world. They differ from them, however, in this: the images are at first without a head, whereas the Christian’s head is finally cut off after he has been most unjustly condemned. Moreover, Christians are banished to islands in which, as no one is unaware, almost all the gentiles’ gods were born and died. Nor could they answer that these things were merely Christian insults against the gods, since Seneca attacked those same gods with many expressions far more bitter. Christians therefore rightly refused to worship those images of the gods which, defiled by kites, mice, and spiders, are neglected, mocked, and destroyed by the gentiles themselves.

Indeed, different men worshiped different gods, and no one could be a god without a decree of the Senate. Each man, according to his need or his power, [Col. 0710D] pledged, sold, or converted his household gods, now into a ladle, now into a cooking pot. Their public gods, meanwhile, they held subject to tax in the auction room and offered for sale beneath the spear and at the proclamation of the public crier. Nor is this at all surprising. For the holier the gods, the more heavily taxed they were. The mendicant religion of the pagans went round the taverns and charged a high fee for admission to some temple.

But come now: in what way, I ask, did the pagans honor their gods? Precisely as they honored dead emperors; indeed, they paid those emperors while alive the same honor as they paid their dead gods. In the same fashion they also venerated the prostitute Larentina, Simon Magus, some god of Synedus or other, and impure Junos, Cereses, and [Col. 0711A] Dianas. What greater insult, therefore, could any human being offer to the ancient gods (chaps. 12, 13)?

Were the gods not also held in no less derision by those who sacrificed all the vilest things to them and stole a third part of the tithes owed to Hercules? Did not others proclaim in their books, to the gods’ very great disgrace, that they had fought among themselves over the Trojans or Achaeans; that Venus had been wounded; that Mars had almost wasted away in chains; that Jupiter had been delivered from some monster and was now weeping, now shamefully in heat? The poets, moreover, sing of Apollo serving Admetus and Neptune serving Laomedon, while Pindar tells of Aesculapius being punished with a thunderbolt. Furthermore, tragedians continually sing of the gods’ miseries and errors. Among the philosophers, [Col. 0711B] Socrates, although he swore by the oak, the goat, and the dog, was nevertheless condemned to death because he was destroying the gods. But after rescinding his condemnation, they finally rendered him splendid testimony. Lastly, a certain Diogenes mocked Hercules, and Varro introduced three hundred Jupiters (chap. 14).

Tertullian presses the point further: in the gentiles’ comedies and tragedies are represented an adulterous Anubis, a male Moon, a scourged Diana, Jupiter’s testament, and three hungry Herculeses. The actors also exhibited the Sun in mourning, Cybele sighing for a disdainful shepherd, Jupiter’s epitaph, and the judgment of Juno and Minerva; and finally, in those performances, some utterly infamous person represented the likeness of a god, while a body rendered effeminate by ridiculous art represented Minerva or Hercules. [Col. 0711C] Nor should one expect anything better from the amphitheater. There the part was played of the castrated Attis, of Hercules burning, of Mercury testing the dead with a cautery amid the noonday spectacles, and of Jupiter’s brother dragging away the corpses of dead men with his hammer. Tertullian omits many other things of this kind and reproaches the gentiles because within the gods’ temples, between the very altars, and in the booths of priests and temple-keepers they arranged adulteries and conducted procuring. Who, then, he asks, were guilty of offending the divine majesty: those who treated their gods with such derision, or the Christians, who denied that these were gods and who, for that reason, did not know their temples by day and, if it could have been done, would have stripped them bare entirely (chap. 15)?

But the pagans objected in reply that Christians worshiped an ass’s head [Col. 0711D] as a god. Yet this, says Tertullian, is sheer fantasy, for which Cornelius Tacitus gave occasion when he falsely recorded that the Jews had consecrated the likeness of this animal because, when they were suffering from thirst in Egypt, certain wild asses had shown them a spring. But the slander of this utterly mendacious writer became perfectly evident when Pompey entered the Jews’ temple and found no image there. Yet the more false this was, the more certainly true it was that all beasts of burden and geldings, together with their Epona, were worshiped and honored by the gentiles.

Nor did they reproach Christians with the worship of Christ’s cross on any better grounds. For what could they condemn in it? Did they not have on public display the Attic Pallas and the Pharian Ceres, [Col. 0712A] which could not be distinguished from the upright post and timber of a cross? Furthermore, in the images of their own gods, in victories, trophies, military standards, the cloths of banners and cantabra, or in their inner ornaments and robes, they worshiped crosses set beneath them.

Others suspected, somewhat more plausibly, that the Christians’ God, like that of the Persians, was the Sun. But this false suspicion arose because those same Christians prayed to God while facing the sun and indulged in rejoicing on the day of the Sun, or the Lord’s Day. Yet this was certainly all the less deserving of the gentiles’ censure because they had devoted Saturn’s day to idleness and feasting.

Lastly, our author derides a certain Jew, [Col. 0712B] plainly an utter scoundrel, who, being an animal-fighter, displayed during the circus games most recently held in that city, he says, a placard on which the Christians’ God was carried around depicted with ass’s ears, hoofed on one foot, carrying a book, and wearing a toga, with this ridiculous inscription: “The Christians’ God, Onochoetes.” But just as false and mendacious as this picture was, so true was it that the gentiles worshiped gods composed of the limbs of human beings and beasts (chap. 16).

Lest the gentiles should thereafter slander the Christians’ true doctrine through willful ignorance and their customary shameless lies, Tertullian expounds it with the utmost brevity and sincerity as follows. “The God whom we worship,” he says, “is one, who created the whole universe out of [Col. 0712C] nothing as an adornment of his majesty. Although he is known, he is nevertheless invisible, inestimable, immeasurable, and known only to himself as great as he is.” That this one true God really exists is demonstrated both by the world and by all the other things which he commanded to come into being by his word, ordered by reason, and established by his power. Indeed, this is also most clearly confirmed by the testimony of our soul, Christian by nature, which, in whatever condition of infirmity, error, or depraved lust it may be, when it recovers its senses or returns to itself, cries out not to gods but to one God and invokes him as judge (chap. 17).

No less evident an argument for the divine existence is drawn from the sacred books of the Old Testament, which were written by men filled with the divine spirit and who [Col. 0712D] confirmed their writings by various genuine miracles. Aristaeus, moreover, testifies that these books, first composed in the Hebrew language, were translated into Greek with the utmost fidelity by seventy Jews under the direction of Ptolemy Philadelphus and with the approval of Menedemus. At that time they were still preserved in Ptolemy’s library, written in Hebrew and Greek characters; and the Jews read them publicly every Sabbath. There could therefore be no doubt about their truth and integrity (chap. 18).

Their antiquity, moreover, vindicates their very great authority. For the archive of one Prophet—that is, the books of Moses—is older than the other writings of the gentiles, than their gods, temples, oracles, and sacred rites, indeed than all Greek [Col. 0713A] characters and most nations and cities. For Moses, a contemporary of Inachus, far surpasses in antiquity not only Homer and the fall of Priam, but also the most ancient Danaus. The other prophets, though later than Moses, nevertheless precede the Greek sages, lawgivers, and historians by no short interval of time. Since, however, it would take too long to set forth something whose proof is easiest for everyone, Tertullian omitted this demonstration, while nevertheless indicating the sources and citing various writers from whom that antiquity of Sacred Scripture can become perfectly evident to anyone (chap. 19).

Moreover, he derives its supreme authority not only from this antiquity, but especially from the fact that it is wholly divine. For its authors, inspired by the divine spirit, [Col. 0713B] foretold the destruction of the world and many other things which we know actually occurred. We should therefore not doubt the other things that were likewise foretold by them. It must accordingly be held as settled and certain that there exists only the one God proclaimed everywhere by them, and that the Christian religion, which rests upon these books of theirs, is true (chap. 20).

But then the gentiles rose up and cried out that this religion of the Christians was new, since they differed utterly and completely from the Jewish religion both in name and in rites. Tertullian answers them, however, that the Jews flourished in the highest prosperity so long as they earned God’s favor by their remarkable righteousness and the faith of the patriarchs. But after trampling upon his laws and falling into the gravest crimes, as punishment [Col. 0713C] they were scattered and wander about, homeless exiles from heaven and country, so that they were not even permitted to greet their own homeland by setting foot upon it. Yet not only were these things foretold by their sacred prophets, but also that another people would arise, to whom God would transfer greater favor than he had given them. Christ is the judge and teacher of this chosen people, likewise foretold by those same prophets. And he, not wholly unknown to the philosophers Zeno and Cleanthes, is God’s [non-Latin text omitted] word, reason, wisdom, and true Son. For he was begotten not in the manner of human beings or manufactured gods, but as a ray from the sun, so spirit from spirit and light from light; he did not withdraw from the source, but proceeded from it in such a way as not to be separated from the substance of his Father, from whom he is another in measure, not in number, [Col. 0713D] in degree, not in condition, and is altogether one God with him. Then, at the appointed time, having descended into a virgin, he became a man united with God.

Neither the gentiles, who had fabricated other similar tales, nor the Jews, who knew that he had been promised by their prophets, could dismiss these things as old wives’ fables. For the Jews erred in this: because they did not understand what was said in those sacred Scriptures about his first and second coming, they denied that he had already come to us. Their blindness, perversity, and wickedness, moreover, were so great that they accused of magic him who, by performing miracles, openly showed himself to be [non-Latin text omitted] of God. But when he drew very many to himself through his heavenly teaching, they became exasperated, delivered him to Pontius Pilate, [Col. 0714A] and by the violence of their votes extorted from him the sentence that Christ should be affixed to the cross, as he had foretold. When he died, there occurred an altogether extraordinary eclipse of the sun, recorded in their archives by those same Jews, who did not know that it had been announced by the prophets. His body was then laid in a tomb by them. But when on the third day he miraculously rose again, they spread the story that it had been stolen by his disciples. He, however, withdrew from the crowds after returning to life; and after associating with certain disciples for forty days, he instructed them in the doctrine to be disseminated everywhere, and thereafter ascended into heaven surrounded by a cloud. Pilate, who at that time was a Christian in conscience, reported all these things to Tiberius, who indeed would have believed in Christ had he not been Caesar and ruled over pagans. But the fiercer Jews most cruelly harassed the disciples of Christ [Col. 0714B] as they preached the doctrine entrusted to them by him; and Nero was the first to shed their blood at Rome.

Tertullian, moreover, firmly asserts that this is the true summary of Christian doctrine and that the gentiles cannot deprive him of credibility. For Christians were thoroughly persuaded that it had never been lawful for anyone, and much less for themselves when explaining the tenets of their religion, to lie. What evil, then, could there be in their religion and worship, when they openly declared that they worshiped God through Christ? Did not the Jews venerate God through Moses, the Greek gentiles through other men, and the Romans, initiated by Numa Pompilius, through their superstitions? The question that should rather have been asked was whether Christ’s divinity is true, through knowledge of which [Col. 0714C] a human being is reformed for the better (chap. 21).

Christians also believed that demons exist, not unknown to philosophers—especially Socrates and Zeno—to poets, or to magicians, and invoked by the common people when cursing or wishing evil upon others. These are of a spiritual substance, as are the angels; but having fallen into sin, they have plotted humanity’s destruction from the beginning of the world. Endowed with spiritual powers as well as a spiritual substance, they not only corrupt crops and human health, but also disturb the human mind with depraved impulses and turn it away from the thought of divinity. Because by their native swiftness they are everywhere in a single instant and know God’s dispositions, they emulate divinity by stealing divination. Moreover, they pronounce ambiguous oracles, cure those whom they have injured, perform false miracles through various [Col. 0714D] deceptions, produce apparitions, defame the souls of the dead, strike down children to elicit an oracular utterance, send dreams, and by their agency goats and tables were accustomed to divine. Yet the pagans, who ascribed all these things to their gods, were acknowledging beyond doubt that those gods were demons.

Nor could they answer that their gods should be judged not from what happened in profane places, but only from what happened in their temples. For the madness of one who hurls himself down from a tower consecrated to these gods does not differ from that of another who throws himself from the roof of a neighboring house. Nor is the insanity of one who cuts his arms and genitals in honor of a goddess any different from that of another who cuts his own throat. But if these arguments, [Col. 0715A] says Tertullian, appear of little weight, other arguments demonstrate most conclusively that the gods of the gentiles and demons are the same. Let someone be brought forward who is suffering from a god, who inhales over altars and conceives a divine presence; let the virgin goddess who promises rain come forth; let the god Aesculapius be brought into the midst to heal some dying man: then, if each and every one of these does not, when questioned by a Christian, confess that they are demons, Tertullian permits that the blood of that same Christian be shed immediately. Yet nothing is simpler, clearer, and more effective than this demonstration. Either the demon will lie, and is not a god; or it will speak the truth, and consequently the gods really are demons. Whoever, therefore, says that these things are done by the gods must assert that they are subject to Christians and, consequently, [Col. 0715B] that their status as gods is as false as the Christian religion is true. For all this power arises from the name of Christ, by which demons, when adjured by Christians, are compelled not only to depart from the human bodies they possess, but also to confess that they are not gods but unclean spirits, already condemned because of their wickedness; that there is only one God; and that the things said shortly before about Christ are therefore true (chaps. 22, 23).

Tertullian therefore rightly concludes from these arguments that Christians are wrongly accused of offending the divine majesty. For even if it were established that the gentiles’ gods were true gods, they themselves, like Plato, believed that supreme power belonged to one—namely Jupiter—and the offices to the rest. What wrong, then, were Christians committing when they worshiped and venerated one supreme God, just as other people [Col. 0715C] acknowledged a single emperor? Was this less lawful for them than it was for every individual, city, and province to have some god of its own and peculiar to itself? Yet some, with no one opposing them, privately worshiped Jupiter; others, Faith; the Egyptians, birds and beasts; the Syrians, Astarte; and so the rest, each his own god. In a word, everyone was permitted to worship whomever he wished except the true God. But was not this rather the greatest crime against the divine majesty of which they falsely accused Christians? And certainly it was all the greater, the more cruelly they sought to compel Christians to worship false divinities and utterly to deny the true God and creator of all things (chap. 24).

The Romans nevertheless objected that, as a reward for their piety and religion [Col. 0715D] toward their gods, they had received dominion over the whole world. “What then?” says Tertullian. Had they obtained it from their native and household gods, such as Sterculus, Mutunus, and the prostitute Larentina? Who would believe it? Or from foreign and immigrant gods, who should have been more concerned to protect their own homeland than Rome? Yet Cybele, after being brought to Rome, was so little concerned to announce the death of Emperor Aurelius to her great priest, the archigallus, that she calmly allowed him to offer his arms and impure blood for the safety of this emperor, who had already departed this life eight days earlier. By what reasoning, moreover, did Jupiter so patiently endure his own Crete, where he was born, died, and was buried, to be shaken by the Roman fasces? [Col. 0716A] Could Juno calmly endure Carthage, once so dear to her and preferred by her to Samos, to be destroyed by the Romans so that they might extend the boundaries of their empire more widely? Yet if matters really stand thus, why did the Romans pay her less honor than they paid Larentia, that most debauched she-wolf?

Moreover, several of their gods, such as Saturn and Jupiter, had reigned. From which Roman god, then, did they receive their kingdom? Was it from some Sterculus? Who but a fool would dare say so? There were also many kings who had never worshiped either the Roman gods or any others. Finally, Roman dominion grew before Roman religion. For before Numa Pompilius established empty superstitions at Rome, and before there were any images of the gods among them, the Latins had had many [Col. 0716B] kings. The Romans, therefore, says Tertullian, were not religious before they were great; and so they were not great because they were religious.

But he presses the point still more sharply: “How,” he asks, “did these men invade the kingdoms of others?” Certainly not by piety or religion, but by wars and victories, and consequently by the indiscriminate slaughter and ruin of walls and temples, priests and citizens, sacred and profane things. Is this, then, the piety on account of which the gods both gave dominion over the world to the Romans, who injured them, and took it away from others who worshiped their own gods with no less piety? Finally, before the foundations of Rome were laid, there reigned not only the Babylonians, Medes, Egyptians, Assyrians, and Amazons, but also the Jews, who rejected and despised all the gods of the Romans and the other gentiles. [Col. 0716C] It is not, therefore, the gods of the Romans or of other nations who dispense kingdoms, but that one omnipotent God alone, to whom, says our author, belong the world that is ruled and the human being himself who rules. It was therefore not the Christians who worshiped him, but the Romans and other gentiles, who were convicted of the crime of offending the divine majesty, because they not only worshiped false gods but also attempted by every kind of torture to compel those same Christians to worship them (chaps. 25, 26).

Those pagans who possessed greater clemency tried to persuade the Christians at least not to refuse so obstinately, and at such certain risk of death, to sacrifice to the gods, while preserving in their hearts their resolve to worship the true God. But this counsel of false compassion was inspired in them by the fraud and cunning of the demons, who, fearing the Christians’ power, [Col. 0716D] wage war against them with implacable hatred. They regard it as no small consolation in their own forecondemnation if they can sometimes weaken the Christians’ constancy and injure them. Christians therefore resist them and are persuaded that they never triumph more gloriously over the demons’ malice than when they are condemned for their steadfast profession of faith (chap. 27).

Furthermore, the pagans, through the impious deceits and arts of the demons, wished not only to force Christians against their will into worshiping the gods—a worship that must either be wholly free and voluntary or be no worship at all—but also to sacrifice to those same gods for the emperor’s safety. Therefore, because they avoided making these sacrifices, they accused them of a crime greater [Col. 0717A] than offending the divine majesty. Why should they not, since they feared the living emperor far more than their dead gods, and would therefore more readily swear falsely by all the latter than by the emperor’s genius? Yet what is more contrary to right reason than to sacrifice to gods for the safety of a human being—a safety which they can neither give nor preserve? For how, Tertullian presses, will they preserve others when they cannot protect their own temples and statues without the guards of Caesar’s soldiers? What, moreover, of the fact that many temples and gods depend upon Caesar’s nod and will? Christians therefore prayed for the emperor’s safety, with far better right, not to those false gods but to the true, living, and eternal God. For even the emperors themselves, if they are of sound mind, desire the favor of him from whom they have [Col. 0717B] their humanity, their life, and their rule over other human beings. For their safety, therefore, their prosperity, their longer life, and the long duration of their empire, Christians poured forth prayers with hands outstretched and head uncovered, without a prompter beforehand—prayers arising from chaste flesh, an innocent soul, and the Holy Spirit. But they did not offer, as did the pagans, grains worth a single as, nor the tears of an Arabian tree, nor the blood of an ox wasted by disease or age, nor a filthy conscience, nor victims slaughtered by the most vicious priests. Tertullian therefore challenges the pagan governors, if it could ever be right, to torture, rack, and kill Christians while they pray to God in this manner for the emperor’s safety (chaps. 28, 29, 30).

How sincere was the spirit, and how free from feigned flattery [Col. 0717C]—which could have profited them against the pagans who charged them with this—when Christians thus prayed to almighty God, Tertullian demonstrates from the fact that in their sacred writings, which they did not conceal, everyone is commanded to pray to God not only for his enemies but also, by name, for the emperors themselves. He adds that they had another reason, or rather necessity, for praying for the emperors and the stability of the Roman Empire: namely, that the end of the world, which was destined to perish together with it, might be delayed. Accordingly, they willingly swore by the safety of the emperors, but by no means by their genii, whom they were accustomed to drive away by adjurations (chaps. 31, 32).

Furthermore, says our author, the Christians’ piety and religious duty toward the emperor were all the greater because they knew with greater certainty that he had been chosen and [Col. 0717D] appointed by the true God. They could therefore obtain the emperor’s safety from this God all the more readily because by these prayers they acknowledged that the emperor was less than God. It was unlawful for them to call the emperor a god, since he is a human being and, while triumphing, is reminded of his human condition. Indeed, Emperor Augustus did not wish to be called lord. And rightly so, for there is one Lord of all, including the emperor—namely, almighty God. Finally, how could the emperor, while still alive, be called by the name of God, which the pagans did not bestow upon Caesars before their apotheosis (chaps. 33, 34)?

With the same ease and clarity with which Tertullian had thus far proved that Christians were not guilty of offending human majesty, [Col. 0718A] he shows that they could not on that account be called public enemies by the pagans because they did not celebrate the Caesars’ festivals in the pagan manner. For who but an impious man would deny that these observances should be conducted with great moderation, propriety, sobriety, and chastity? Yet the gentiles celebrated them with the utmost wantonness, drunkenness, shamelessness, riotous running about, insults, and laurels and lamps hung like brothel signs at the doorposts of houses. In addition, while celebrating these solemn festivals, they did not spare the emperors themselves: while insincerely wishing them many years of life, they were at the same time considering the creation of another, new emperor.

Nor does our author allow the reply that these are merely the desires of the blind and ungrateful mob, not of others. [Col. 0718B] For Cassius, Niger, and Albinus, the emperors’ bitterest enemies, were not plebeians; nor were the others, more audacious than Sigerius and Parthenius, who besieged Caesar between two laurels; nor, finally, were those men who were then daily recognized as accomplices in these criminal factions. Yet these very men made sacrifices for the emperors’ safety, swore solemnly by their genius, and celebrated their festivals like everyone else. Astrologers, haruspices, augurs, and magicians likewise rendered the same duties, while by demonic arts they inquired into the Caesars’ future death and desired it to be near (chap. 35).

The loyalty, piety, and religious duty owed to emperors therefore do not consist in these observances, which were often seen to be performed by their enemies, but in those habits and works of a good mind which one renders to emperors and to everyone else [Col. 0718C] indiscriminately, expecting no reward except from God. Christians performed these duties, for they are commanded to wish or do evil to no one, to think evil of no one, to hate no one, to love their enemies, and, when injured, not to repay like with like. This was indeed observed by them so diligently that, although pelted with stones by the mob, burned with fire by the fury of others, or exhumed from their graves by Bacchanalian frenzies, they never wished to avenge the many grave injuries inflicted upon themselves or their brethren. Yet they could have exacted vengeance for them, either secretly with a few small torches or openly with greater military forces than the pagans possessed. For, dispersed throughout the whole world, they had filled cities, towns, camps, and everything except the temples of the gods. And even if they could have mustered smaller forces, for what war [Col. 0718D] were those not ready and fit who willingly allowed themselves to be slaughtered for professing their religion? Grant also, if you wish, that they were unarmed. Yet they could very easily have avenged themselves merely by deserting or separating from the gentiles. For their multitude was so great that, had the gentiles been left to themselves, they would have been appalled at their own desolation and would have had more enemies than citizens. Finally, it would surely have sufficed for Christian vengeance if they had not driven the demons from the possessed bodies of the pagans. By granting the gentiles this single and very great protection, they therefore showed that they were enemies not of the human race, but of error, as we have thus far proved (chaps. 36, 37).

Our author then passes to another, no less shameless slander, by which Christians were accused of the false crime of unlawful association. For what, he asks, [Col. 0719A] is an unlawful faction except an association of men who disturb cities and kingdoms out of ambition for office or glory? Yet Christians, driven by no ambition, had always renounced the assemblies and public affairs of the gentiles, just as they had the pleasures of the circus, theater, arena, and other spectacles. Yet surely, by rejecting these things, they caused the pagans no loss or injury, just as they did not when Christians sometimes pursued other pleasures that the pagans in turn rejected (chap. 38).

Moreover, to show everyone still more plainly that Christians did nothing with the turbulent spirit of an unlawful faction, but everything solely from a disposition of true religion and piety, Tertullian briefly and sincerely describes what was done in their [Col. 0719B] assemblies. There, he says, they did violence to God by pouring forth prayers for the emperor and the authorities, for the stability of the world and the peace of affairs, and for delay to be imposed upon the final end of the world. Then the divine Scriptures were read, by which they might nourish the faith of those present, raise their hope, and establish their confidence. Exhortations, corrections, and divine censure for some grave sin followed. These assemblies were presided over by approved elders, who had obtained this honor not by payment but by merit. On one day each month, everyone voluntarily contributed a modest sum according to his means, to support all kinds of needy persons, the aged, children, exiles, those consigned to prisons, and others condemned to the mines. The pagans therefore strove in vain to defame this mutual charity of the Christians, by which they called one another brothers, [Col. 0719C] united as they were by true religion, and shared their goods with others—goods by which the pagans’ mutual affection was invariably divided. For the Christians held everything in common except their wives, whereas the pagans, while keeping everything else, shared their wives with their friends, as did Socrates and Cato.

Tertullian continues: the Christians’ supper, he says, was not lavish and costly like that of the Megarians or the others censured by him there, but was rightly and deservedly called by the Greeks [non-Latin text omitted] and by the Latins “affection.” It begins with prayers, and afterward everyone eats and drinks with such moderation that they remember that God must be worshiped throughout the night. After water for the hands and the lights have been brought, each person is invited, as he is able, to sing something to God from [Col. 0719D] the sacred Scriptures. Others, conversing among themselves, know that the Lord is listening. Finally, prayer ends the banquet just as it began it, and afterward everyone departs not in the turbulent and wanton manner of the pagans, but with the utmost modesty. From these practices, thus explained, Tertullian concludes that unlawful and condemnable factions existed not in the Christians’ assemblies, but in those of the gentiles themselves.

Nor did the latter have any better right to cry out that these same Christians were the authors of all public calamities. For before their name had been heard, several islands named by our author were swallowed up either by the waves of the sea or by earthquakes. It is also established that previously, before any Roman [Col. 0720A] gods existed, the whole world perished in a universal flood, not merely a flood of the plains, as Plato supposed. Moreover, before the Jews migrated into Egypt, and thus long before Christ founded his religion, avenging fire had consumed Sodom and Gomorrah, Volsinii, and Pompeii. No one is unaware, moreover, that the Romans were routed in the battle of Cannae and that the Capitol was captured by the Senones when the Romans worshiped their own gods and no Christians worshiped the true God. Finally, the same devastation and ruin of cities and temples had occurred before. Yet these things could not be ascribed to the manufactured gods whose temples they were, but to the impiety of the human beings who worshiped them and who, either ignorant or contemptuous of the true God, the source of every good, had defiled themselves with every kind of vice and crime. [Col. 0720B] To this is added the fact that, from the time when Christ’s religion arose, public calamities of this kind were undoubtedly lighter. For the innocence of its followers tempered the iniquity of the age. And indeed, whenever some calamity oppressed the world, the gentiles vainly wearied their false gods with their customary rites, whereas the Christians obtained the mercy of the true God through continence, fasting, and other acts of repentance. It was therefore not these worshipers of the true God, but the gentiles who despised him, who were the cause of public calamities (chaps. 39, 40).
