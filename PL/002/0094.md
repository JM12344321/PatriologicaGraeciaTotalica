From this point, when mature age has made you a man.

That is, when you have grown into a man and are *in your prime* (ἐν ἀκμῇ); Synesius writes, “having now enrolled yourself among men” (ἤδη εἰς ἄνδρας ἀγγειλάμενος). LE PR.]

once they have passed the transition-point of their age. Truth demands this, and against truth no one can prescribe—

[Note: [Col. 0888B] **No one can prescribe.** Lacerda has adduced many passages to explain the verb *praescribere* as meaning “to raise an objection” or “to produce exceptions,” in accordance with juristic usage. Here, in my judgment, it means simply that nothing can detract from the truth by any argument whatsoever, and nothing can diminish its splendor or majesty. LE PR.]

[Col. 0889A] neither length of time, nor the patronage of persons, nor the privilege of regions. For it is generally from one of these that custom, having taken its origin in some ignorance or simplicity, is strengthened into usage by succession and is then asserted against the truth. But our Lord—

[Note: [Col. 0889D] **Christ called himself the truth.** Christ himself testified in countless passages, which I omit, that he is the truth. Hence I have often wondered that certain men who display the arrogance of the Pharisees should have descended to such impiety as to say that Christ lied, or at least spoke equivocally, eight times in chapter VIII of the Gospel of blessed John. But this is neither the place nor the time to lament that overthrow of Christian morals and discipline. LE PR.]

Christ called himself the truth, not custom. If Christ is eternal and prior to all things, truth is likewise an eternal and ancient reality. Let those, therefore, for whom it is new consider that, in itself, it is old. It is not so much novelty as truth that refutes heresy. Whatever savors of opposition to the truth will be heresy, even if it is an ancient custom.

For the rest, it is through his own fault that anyone remains ignorant of something. What is unknown ought to have been sought out, just as what is recognized ought to be received. The rule of faith, indeed, is altogether one, alone immovable and incapable of reformation: namely, the belief in one almighty God, the Creator of the world, and in his Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin Mary, crucified [Col. 0889B] under Pontius Pilate, raised from the dead on the third—

[Note: [Col. 0889C] *On the third* [*tertio*], Paris edition.]

day, received into heaven, now sitting at the right hand of the Father, and destined to come to judge the living and the dead, with the resurrection of the flesh as well.

While this law of faith remains, the other matters of—

[Note: [Col. 0889D] **Discipline and conduct.** Severe discipline is always understood by the term *conduct* (*conversatio*). In Theodoret it is called ἄσκησις, “ascetic discipline.” St. Jerome, book I *Against Jovinian*: “We are armed with the same manner of life with which Christ was armed.” LE PR.]

discipline and conduct admit the novelty of correction, as the grace of God works and advances even to the end. For how could it be that, while the devil is always at work and daily adds to the contrivances of iniquity, the work of God should either have ceased or stopped advancing? It was precisely for this reason that the Lord sent the Paraclete: because human weakness could not grasp everything at once, discipline was gradually to be directed, ordered, and brought to perfection by that vicar of the Lord, the Holy Spirit.

“I still have many things,” he says, “to tell you, but [Col. 0889C] you cannot yet bear them. When that Spirit of truth comes, he will lead you into all truth and will announce to you the things that are to come” (John 16:12). But [Col. 0890A] he had also previously declared this concerning the Spirit’s work. What, then, is the administration of the Paraclete except this: that discipline is directed, the Scriptures are revealed, understanding is reformed, and progress is made toward better things?

Nothing exists without its proper age, and all things await their time. Finally, Ecclesiastes says: “There is a time for every matter” (Eccles. 3:17). Look at creation itself, advancing gradually toward fruit. First there is the grain; from the grain a shoot arises, and from the shoot a sapling struggles forth. Then its branches and foliage gain strength, and the full form implied by the name “tree” unfolds. From there comes the swelling of the bud—

[Note: [Col. 0889D] **From there comes the swelling of the bud.** A *swelling* (*tumor*) is anything that is conceived; but *germen* and *gemma*—“shoot” and “bud”—are the same in this instance. LE PR.]

and the flower is released from the bud, and from the flower the fruit opens. It too is for a while crude and unformed; gradually advancing through its proper age, it is trained into gentleness of flavor.

[Note: [Col. 0889D] **It is trained into gentleness of flavor.** For in fruit that is still crude and unripe there is, as it were, a wild savagery of juice and flavor; but it is trained into gentleness [Col. 0890D] as it is gradually ripened to maturity. RIG.]

So too righteousness—for the God of righteousness and of creation is the same—was first in its rudimentary stage, when nature feared God. Then, through the Law and the Prophets, it advanced into childhood. Then, through the Gospel, it surged into youth. Now, through the [Col. 0890B] Paraclete, it is being ordered into maturity. He alone must be called and revered as the teacher appointed by Christ. For he does not speak on his own—

[Note: [Col. 0889C] “He will speak,” Rhen., Seml., Leop.]

but speaks what is commanded by Christ. He alone is the guide who goes before us, because he alone comes after Christ. Those who have received him place truth before custom. Those who have heard him prophesying—not long ago, but right down to the present—

[Note: [Col. 0889C] Thus Rig., on the authority of the manuscripts of Wouwer and Ursinus. The Dijon manuscript, Rhen., and others read: “right down, not long ago” (*usque non olim*). But Rig. plausibly conjectured that the words “not long ago” were added for the sake of explaining the phrase, and that only the words “right down to the present” (*usque nunc*) should be retained.]

cover their virgins.

## CHAPTER II

But for the moment I do not wish to ascribe this practice to truth—

[Note: [Col. 0890C] “Of truth,” Seml.]

Let it be custom for the time being, so that I may oppose custom with custom. Throughout Greece and certain barbarian regions associated with it—

[Note: [Col. 0890D] **Throughout Greece and certain barbarian regions associated with it.** By the “barbarian regions of Greece” he means certain regions outside Greece but adjoining it, with which the Greeks had dealings or wars. Horace:

> Greece, clashing with barbarism in a protracted war.

**Throughout Greece and certain barbarian regions associated with it.** I think that certain cities or regions of Greece are meant, although others believe that he indicates cities neighboring Greece and adjacent nations. LE PR.]

a great many churches conceal their virgins. This institution is also found somewhere beneath this very sky, lest anyone ascribe that custom merely to Greek or barbarian heathenism. But I have cited those churches [Col. 0890C] which the apostles themselves or apostolic men—

[Note: [Col. 0890D] **Apostolic men.** By “apostolic men” understand the leading figures of the Christian religion, who were either instructed and trained by the apostles or performed deeds worthy of apostolic teaching. It seems rather inappropriate to explain the expression as referring only to bishops or solely to the supreme pontiff, since it is also attributed to Constantine the Great in many passages, as in Theodoret, book I, chapter 25: τὰς ἀποστολικὰς φροντίδας ἐν τῇ ψυχῇ περιέφερεν, “he constantly bore apostolic concerns in his soul”; and elsewhere. LE PR.]

founded, and, I think, before certain others.

[Note: [Col. 0890D] **And, I think, before certain others.** He spares the names of those who, although later than the apostles and apostolic men, were said to have given authority to the custom that virgins should by no means be veiled. RIG.]

Those churches, therefore, possess the same authority of custom; they can oppose to these later churches both earlier times and earlier predecessors. What shall we observe? What shall we choose?

[Note: [Col. 0891C] “Shall we choose” [*deligimus*], Seml.]

We cannot reject a custom that we cannot condemn, since it is not foreign to us, because it belongs not to outsiders but to those with whom we share the right of peace and the name of brotherhood. We and they have one faith, one God, the same Christ, the same hope, and the same sacraments of the washing.

[Note: [Col. 0891C] **Let me say it once: we are one Church.** In his book *On the Unity of the Church*, blessed Cyprian likewise adduces Scripture to prove this same point, as well as many other passages; we refer the reader to that book. Both authors interpret the text as meaning one body, one Church. PAM.]

Let me say it once: we are one Church. Thus whatever belongs to our people belongs to us. Otherwise, you divide the body.

Yet here—

[Note: [Col. 0891C] “So here, as in all things,” Seml.]

as in all matters characterized by differing institutions—

[Note: [Col. 0891C] *Institutus*, wrongly in Fran.]

and by doubt and uncertainty, an examination ought to have been undertaken to determine which of two such divergent customs was more consistent with God’s discipline. Certainly that custom should be chosen which encloses virgins—

[Note: [Col. 0891D] **Which encloses virgins.** With a veil. RIG.]

and keeps them known to God alone. For besides the fact that they must seek glory from God, not from human beings—

[Note: [Col. 0891C] “Besides” [*praeterea*], Seml.]

they must even blush at their own good. You would embarrass a virgin more by praising than by reproaching her, because the brow of sin is harder, having been taught shamelessness by sin itself and in sin itself.

For no one would have approved that custom which denies the existence of virgins by exhibiting them, except certain persons—

[Note: [Col. 0891C] “Certain women of the same kind,” Seml.]

of the same character as the virgins themselves. For the sort of eyes that will wish to see a virgin are the sort possessed by the virgin who wishes to be seen. The same kinds of eyes—

[Note: [Col. 0892C] “Of the same eyes,” Seml.]

desire one another.

[Note: [Col. 0891D] **It belongs to the same lust to be seen and to see.** For this reason Jerome, speaking of this subject to Eustochium, says: “No gaze is safe.” It is as though he said that neither seeing nor being seen is safe. And Ambrose, book I of *On Duties*, chapter 18, says: “Let a woman neither wish to see men nor to be seen.” These precepts are intended to make a virgin understand how precious virginal modesty is. For that reason a virgin is called by Cyprian, in *On the Dress of Virgins*, a “candidate for modesty,” as though this were her sole pursuit; and Manilius, book III:

> It is always too little merely to love; they will wish to be seen to love as well.

LAC.]

It belongs to the same lust to be seen and to see. It is as fitting for a holy man to blush if he has seen a virgin as for a holy virgin to blush if she has been seen by a man.

## CHAPTER III

But those most holy predecessors were unwilling even to discriminate between the customs. Nevertheless, among [Col. 0891C] us, until very recently, both customs were tolerated. The matter was left to individual choice, so that each woman might choose either to be covered or to be exposed, just as she might choose to marry; for marriage itself is neither compelled nor prohibited. Truth was content—

[Note: [Col. 0892C] Some previously read *paucis* wrongly; read *pacisci cum*, “to make terms with.”]

to make terms with custom, so that silently, under the name of custom, it might enjoy its own rights at least in part.

But since recognition of the truth had begun to advance, so that through permission for both practices the superiority of the better part might become evident, that adversary of good things—and much more of good institutions—

[Note: [Col. 0891D] **That adversary of good things, and much more of institutions.** That is: but especially of good institutions and good disciplines. RIG.]

immediately did his work. The virgins of men canvass men against the virgins of God, their foreheads wholly bare and their rash audacity aroused.

[Note: [Col. 0892C] “Into audacity rashly,” Seml.]

And virgins are put on display who can request something from men—to say nothing of such a deed as this: that their rivals, all the freer in proportion as they are handmaids of Christ alone, should be surrendered to them.

“We are scandalized,” they say, “because some women walk differently from others”; and they prefer to be scandalized rather than challenged to do better. A scandal, unless I am mistaken, is an example not of a good thing but of an evil one, which builds a person up toward sin. Good things scandalize no one except an evil mind. If modesty, shamefastness, and disdain for glory—which seeks to please God alone—are good, let those who are scandalized by such goodness acknowledge their own evil.

What if the incontinent were also to say that they were scandalized by the continent? Must continence be revoked? And lest women married many times be scandalized, must monogamy be rejected?

[Note: [Col. 0891D] **And lest women married many times be scandalized.** This is certainly how the text must be read, not as it is found written and printed everywhere: “And lest many be scandalized by us.” RIG.]

Why do they not complain instead that the insolence and shamelessness of ostentatious virginity are a scandal to them?

[Note: [Col. 0892C] **Of ostentatious virginity.** He censures the ostentatious virgin as one who lives not for herself but for the public and for spectators, as Seneca puts it near the end of letter 94. For, he says, it is characteristic of vice, not virtue, to be displayed and stared at. [Col. 0892D] Thus ostentatious virginity is wholly vicious. LAC.]

On account of heads of this kind, then—heads put on display in the marketplace—are holy virgins to be dragged into the church, blushing because they are recognized in public, trembling because they are uncovered, summoned—

[Note: [Col. 0892D] **Trembling because they are uncovered.** Fear befits virgins, who ought to tremble at every cast of an eye; but when they are stripped bare, the Author says, they are terrified as though summoned to rape. In this discipline, as in other matters, the ancient Church was then illustrious. LE PR.]

[Note: [Col. 0892C] “Summoned” [*arcessitae*], others; Jun.]

as though to rape? For they are no less unwilling to suffer this.

[Note: [Col. 0892D] **For they are no less unwilling to suffer this.** “This” means to be exposed publicly and to have the head uncovered. But I prefer the reading of certain copies, “For they are no less willing to suffer even this,” so that “this” refers to rape, which is the more immediate reference. RIG.]

Every public exposure of a good virgin is an experience of rape. And yet to suffer violence in the flesh is the lesser thing, because it comes from the function of nature. But when the spirit itself is violated in a virgin, once her veil has been removed she has learned to lose what the veil had been guarding.

O sacrilegious hands—

[Note: [Col. 0892D] **O sacrilegious hands!** These virgins had put on a form of dress consecrated to God, and to touch it was the greatest sacrilege. Dionysius Exiguus, in the *Life of Pachomius*, says that this habit was consecrated and that Pachomius, on receiving him, consecrated him with the monastic habit. And Jerome writes to Marcella: “Having put on a fuller tunic, she suddenly consecrated herself to the Lord.” LAC.]

that could strip away a form of dress consecrated to God!

[Note: [Col. 0893C] **A form of dress consecrated to God.** Some of the virgins had placed a veil upon their heads, while others had remained with their heads free and uncovered. Tertullian calls the latter “virgins of men” and the former “virgins of God.” But the virgins with uncovered heads, offended by that distinction marking veiled virginity, lodged a complaint about scandal and demanded that virgins be forbidden to wear a veil, lest the veiled women should wear something that made the unveiled women objects of unfavorable comparison. Perhaps the bishop had granted the request. Tertullian became indignant with him as with a sacrilegious person. “What worse thing,” he says, “would any persecutor have done? You have stripped the girl bare from the head, and now she is no longer a virgin in her own possession as a whole.” RIG.]

What worse thing—

[Note: [Col. 0893C] “Worse” [*prius*], Fran., Rig., Seml.]

would any persecutor have done, if he had learned that the virgin had chosen this for herself? You have stripped the girl bare from the head—

[Note: [Col. 0893D] **You have stripped the girl bare from the head.** He is indignant, as though about to add, “down to her lowest ankles.” RIG.]

and now she is no longer wholly a virgin in her own possession: she has been made another person.

[Note: [Col. 0893D] **And now she is no longer wholly a virgin in her own possession.** For she blushes throughout her entire body, as though deprived of her virginity by the wanton eyes of the spectators. RIG.]

Rise up, therefore, Truth; rise up and, as it were, burst forth from your patience. I do not want you to defend any custom, for now even that custom—

[Note: [Col. 0893C] Seml. adds “custom.”]

under which you used to enjoy your own rights is being erased.

[Note: [Col. 0893C] “Is being conquered,” Seml., Leop.]

Show that you are the one who veils virgins.

[Note: [Col. 0893C] Thus Wouwer and Rigalt, and better, in Seml.’s judgment; Seml. reads “who veils” in the singular.]

Interpret your own Scriptures, which custom does not know. For if it had known them, it would never have existed.

## CHAPTER IV

But because it is also customary to argue from the Scriptures against the truth, the objection is immediately raised against us that the Apostle made no mention of virgins when he laid down the rule concerning the veil, but named women only. If he had wished virgins also to be covered, he would have pronounced on virgins by name—

[Note: [Col. 0893C] “By name,” Jun.]

along with women, just as, our opponent says, in the passage where he treats of marriage he also declares what is to be observed concerning virgins. Accordingly, virgins are not contained within the law of veiling the head, since they are not named in that law; indeed, from this they are shown to be unveiled—

[Note: [Col. 0893C] Fran. adds “not,” but Jun. removes the negation. Seml.: “by this they are veiled.”]

because those who are not even named are not commanded.

But we turn the same argument back upon them. For he who knew how elsewhere to mention both classes—

[Note: [Col. 0894C] “He knew how to mention others,” the manuscript of Wouwer and Rig.]

I mean virgin and woman, that is, non-virgin—for the sake of making a distinction, shows, in those passages in which he does not name the virgin and makes no distinction, that they share the same condition. Otherwise he could have established a difference here too between virgin and woman, just as elsewhere [Col. 0893C] he says: “The woman and the virgin are divided.”

Therefore, by not dividing those whom he did not divide, he rendered them mutually inclusive.

[Note: [Col. 0893D] **He rendered them mutually inclusive** (*inalteravit*). I think this is correct, although the manuscripts have *in altera iniit*. *Inalteravit* means that he put one within the other, joined them, and merged them, so that both are now one and mutually contain each other. A little later he himself says: “He did not separate her from the woman, and by not separating her he joined her to the one from whom he did not separate her.” RIG.

**He rendered them mutually inclusive.** The Author uses this in the sense of “did not alter.” Likewise elsewhere he uses *incriminari* in the sense of “not to accuse.” PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0894C] Fran.: *In altera ivit*; others: *iniit*.]

Yet the fact that woman and virgin are divided in that other passage [Col. 0894A] will not support the same division here as well, as certain people wish.

[Note: [Col. 0894C] “Certain” [*quidem*], Fran.]

For how many things said elsewhere have no force in a place where they were not said, unless the same ground exists there as elsewhere, so that a single statement may suffice? But the reason for the distinction between virgin and woman there is entirely separate from the present case.

“The woman and the virgin are divided,” he says (1 Cor. 7:32). Why? Because the unmarried woman—that is, the virgin—thinks about the things of the Lord, so that she may be holy in both body and spirit. But the married woman—that is, the non-virgin—thinks about how she may please her husband. This is the interpretation of that distinction. It has no place in the present chapter, in which nothing is pronounced either about marriage or about the mind and thought of woman and virgin—

[Note: [Col. 0894C] **Of woman and virgin.** They commonly distinguish the terms so that a *woman* is properly one who has married and has children, while a *virgin* is intact and truly uncorrupted. Jerome speaks according to this distinction when expounding the passage of Hosea, “I will betroth you to myself,” and so forth. Yet this distinction is so far from being invariable that a virgin is, rather, also understood under the designation “woman,” as Tertullian says here and will prove below, and so forth. LAC.]

[Note: [Col. 0894C] “Womanly,” Seml.]

but about covering the head. Since the Holy Spirit wished there to be no difference in this matter, by the single designation “woman” [Col. 0894B] he intended the virgin also to be understood. By not naming her separately—

[Note: [Col. 0894D] **By naming her separately.** Take note of this expression, which overturns the grammarians’ distinction between proper and common nouns. For here Tertullian uses “to name properly” of the noun that they call common or appellative. RIG.]

he did not separate her from the woman; and by not separating her, he joined her to the one from whom he did not separate her.

Is it something new, then, to use a principal term while nevertheless understanding the remaining classes within that term, where there is no necessity to distinguish the whole category item by item? A natural economy of expression is both pleasing and necessary, since a prolix discourse—

[Note: [Col. 0894D] **A prolix discourse.** That is, diffuse. You may also find this word in the books *Against Marcion*, and likewise in *On the Dress of Women*: “But to occupy the other parts of the body with the elaborate follies of pomp and luxury.” RHEN.]

is burdensome and futile. Thus we are content with general terms that comprise within themselves the meaning of particular terms.

Let us proceed, then, to the term itself. “Female” (*foemina*) is the natural designation; “woman” (*mulier*) is the general form of the natural designation.

[Note: [Col. 0894D] **The natural designation, etc.** That “woman” is a general designation even among the jurists is clear from Ulpian, in the law beginning *Quaeritur*, *Digest*, *On the Aedilician Edict*, where even one who has not known a man is called a woman. PAM.]

The general term includes the particular as well: virgin, married woman, widow, and however many additional designations of age there may be. The particular, therefore, is subordinated to the general, because the general is prior; the subsequent to the antecedent—

[Note: [Col. 0894C] Thus the manuscript of Wouwer; Paris and Venice read *subcessivum*.]

and the partial to the universal. It is understood—

[Note: [Col. 0894C] “Will be understood,” Seml.]

within that to which it is subordinated, and is signified within it because it is contained within it. Thus neither hand nor foot nor any limb requires separate mention when the body has been named. If you say “the world,” heaven will be there also, together with the things within it—the sun, moon, constellations, stars, earth, and seas, the entire inventory of the elements. You have named everything when you have named that which consists of everything. So too, by naming “woman,” he named whatever belongs to woman.

## CHAPTER V

But since they use the designation “woman” in such a way that they do not think it applicable to anyone except her who has experienced a man, we must prove that the proper scope of this designation pertains to the sex itself, not to a particular status within the sex, and that virgins too are commonly included under it.

[Note: [Col. 0895D] **That virgins too are commonly included.** They are comprehended under the common designation “woman.” RIG.]

When this second kind of human being was made by God [Col. 0895B] as a help for the man (Gen. 2:18), that female was immediately called “woman,” while she was still blessed, still worthy of paradise, and still a virgin.

[Note: [Col. 0895D] **While she was still blessed, etc.** Tertullian proves his position in this way; Ambrose, in *On the Institution of a Virgin*, chapter 5, insisted upon the same point: “Finally, virginity first received this designation, namely the designation ‘woman.’ For when God had taken one of Adam’s ribs and filled up the flesh in its place, he built it, Scripture says, into a woman. Certainly she had not yet known a man, and yet she was already called a woman.” LAC.]

“She shall be called woman,” he says (Gen. 2:23). You therefore have a designation that, I will not merely say, is shared with the virgin, but is properly hers, since it was the virgin who received it at the beginning.

But certain ingenious persons wish the words “she shall be called woman” to have been spoken about the future, as though she would become a woman when she surrendered her virginity, because Scripture also adds: “Therefore—

[Note: [Col. 0895C] Others add “it adds.”]

a man shall leave father and mother and shall be joined fast to his woman, and the two shall be in one flesh.”

[Note: [Col. 0895D] **He shall be joined fast to his woman.** He translated literally from the Greek, καὶ προσκολληθήσεται τῇ γυναικὶ αὐτοῦ, “and he shall be joined fast to his woman,” using a compound derived from κόλλη, which means “glue.” PAM.]

[Note: [Col. 0895C] “Into one flesh,” Fran.]

Let them first show where this clever subtlety of theirs resides. If she was called “woman” with reference to the future, what designation did she receive in the meantime? For she could not have existed without a designation for her present condition. Besides, what sense does it make for her to be designated by the name by which she would be called in the future, while receiving no designation at all in the present?

Adam assigned names to all the animals, and to none—

[Note: [Col. 0896C] “No one,” wrongly in the Venice edition.]

on the basis of a future condition, but according to its present formation. Whatever condition each was to serve, it was called what God had intended from the beginning. What, then, was she called at that time?

In fact, whenever she is named in Scripture, she is called “woman” before she is married, and never “virgin” while she is a virgin. At that time this alone was her designation, and no statement had yet been made—

[Note: [Col. 0895D] **It was spoken prophetically.** For prophets are accustomed to speak of future things as though they were present. The expression that follows, “according to the prophetic mode,” has the same meaning; likewise, “let no one interpret the designation as belonging to the prophecy.” RHEN.]

in the prophetic mode. For when Scripture relates that the two were naked, Adam and his woman—

[Note: [Col. 0895D] **That the two were naked, Adam, etc.** The words of the common Latin interpreter in Genesis chapter 2 are: “Now each of them was naked, namely Adam and his wife.” But Tertullian is consistent in his translation, for where the Greek has γυνή he always renders it “woman,” unlike the interpreter, who renders it now “woman,” now “wife.” LAC.]

this does not have the character of a statement about the future, as though Scripture called her “his woman” in anticipation of her becoming his wife. Rather, even while unmarried she was his woman, since she came from his substance: “This,” he says, “is bone from my bones and flesh from my flesh; she shall be called woman.”

[Note: [Col. 0896D] **Flesh from my flesh; she shall be called woman.** Here he omitted the Greek word αὕτη, “this woman,” and therefore joined the two clauses without punctuation, although the Greek and Latin read differently: “This woman shall be called.” PAM.]

Hence, through the unspoken consciousness of nature, the soul’s own divinity brought it into ordinary speech, though human beings were unaware of it—just as we shall elsewhere be able to show that many other things are customarily done and said from Scripture—so that we call our wives “women.” Although we do speak certain things improperly, even the Greeks, who more commonly use the term for “woman” to mean “wife,” have other terms proper to a wife.

[Note: [Col. 0896D] **They have other terms proper to a wife.** Pertinent here above all is the fact that in Plato, book IX, a wife is called γυνὴ γαμετή, “a wedded woman,” as likewise in Xenophon’s *Oeconomicus*. Then there are these synonyms: σύγγαμος, “spouse”; ἄλοχος, “bedfellow”; ἄκοιτις, “consort”; and παράκοιτις, “one who lies beside.” PAM.]

But I prefer to ascribe this usage to the testimony of Scripture. For where two are made into one flesh [Col. 0896B] through the bond of marriage—

[Note: [Col. 0896D] **The bond of marriage.** In the poet, Dido calls it the nuptial bond:

> That I would wish to unite myself to anyone in the bond of marriage,

*Aeneid*, book IV. Gregory of Nyssa, *On Virginity*, chapter 3, calls marriage ἄρκυα, “nets,” and describes it as ἀφύκτοις πάγαις ἐγκρατηθῆναι, that is, “to be caught in inescapable snares.” LE PR.]

she who is flesh from flesh and bone from bones is called, according to her origin, his woman; from his substance she begins to be reckoned as having become his wife. Thus “woman” is not by nature the designation of a wife—

[Note: [Col. 0896C] “Thus ‘woman’ is by nature the designation of a wife, and ‘wife’ by condition,” Latin edition.]

but “wife” is by condition the designation of a woman. Finally, someone can be called a woman without being a wife, but a wife cannot be called someone who is not a woman, because neither can she be one.

Once, therefore, the designation of the new female—namely “woman”—had been established, and what had come first had been explained, that is, once the designation had been assigned, Scripture then turned to the prophetic mode and said: “Because of this woman a man shall leave father and mother.” The designation is so completely separate from the prophecy, just as it is also separate from the person herself, that he certainly did not speak about Eve herself, but about those future females whom he had designated in the womb of the female race. Otherwise, Adam was not going to leave father and mother, whom he did not have, because of Eve.

Therefore the prophetic statement does not pertain to Eve, because it does not pertain to Adam either. It was a prediction concerning the condition of husbands, who were to leave their parents on account of a woman. This could not apply to Eve, because it could not apply to Adam. If this is so, it is evident that she was not called “woman” on account of a future condition that did not pertain to her.

In addition, he himself gave the reason for this designation. For after he had said, “She shall be called [Col. 0897A] woman,” he added—

[Note: [Col. 0897C] Rig., following Wouwer, inserted the word from a manuscript; Seml. deleted it after Rhen.]

“because she was taken from her own man”—

[Note: [Col. 0897C] Jun. inserts “he says.”]

while he too was still a virgin. But we shall also discuss the designation “man” in its proper place.

Let no one, therefore, interpret as belonging to the prophecy a designation derived from another meaning, especially since it is evident where she received a designation referring to the future: namely, where she is called Eve by a personal designation, because the natural designation had preceded it. For if Eve is the mother of the living, there she is indeed named from the future; there she is indeed announced as a wife and not a virgin. This will be the designation of one who is to marry, since a mother comes from one who has married.

Thus it is also demonstrated here that she was not then called “woman” with reference to the future, since she was subsequently to receive a designation belonging to her future condition. Enough has been said in answer to this part.

the pattern of this designation in accordance with Genesis, assigning it to the sex by calling the Virgin Mary a “woman,” just as Genesis calls Eve a woman.

[Note: [Col. 0897C] **Calling the Virgin Mary a woman.** Since he adduces only two passages for this, one from Luke 1 and the other from Galatians 4, one may add John 2: “Woman, what have I to do with you?” Likewise John 19:26: “Woman, behold your son.” PAM.]

For in writing to the Galatians he says: “God sent his Son, made from a woman” (Gal. 4:4).

[Note: [Col. 0897D] **Made from a woman.** To Pamelius’s references add these words of Ulpian, *Digest*, *On the Aedilician Edict*: “It is certain that what is bequeathed to women is also owed to virgins.” LAC.]

It is, of course, agreed that she was a virgin, although Ebion resists this. I recognize that the angel Gabriel was sent to a virgin; but when he blesses her, he ranks her among women, not among virgins: “Blessed are you among women” (Luke 1:28).

[Note: [Col. 0897D] **Blessed are you among women.** This rendering is more explicit than the Vulgate’s “among women” (*in mulieribus*), although blessed Jerome’s translator also has “among” in the same chapter, in Elizabeth’s blessing. PAM.]

The angel too knew that even a virgin is called a woman. But someone has fancied that he ingeniously answered both these passages as follows: since Mary was betrothed, she was therefore called a woman by both the angel and the Apostle, for a betrothed woman is in a certain sense married. Yet there is sufficient difference between “in a certain sense” and the reality, at least in the present passage; elsewhere, indeed, it may properly be understood in that way. Here, however, they called Mary a woman not as though she were already married, but simply as a female—even if she had not been betrothed—since she had been so called from the beginning. For that from which the pattern descends must establish the precedent.

As regards the present chapter, if a betrothed woman is here equated with a married woman, so that Mary was called a woman not because she was female but because she was married, then Christ was not born of a virgin, because he was born of a betrothed woman who, by that designation, had ceased to be a virgin. But if he was born of a virgin, although of one who was betrothed yet remained intact, acknowledge that even a virgin, even an intact virgin, is called a woman.

Here certainly nothing can appear to have been said prophetically, as though the Apostle, by saying “made from a woman,” had named her a future woman, that is, a married woman. For he could not have meant the woman she would become afterward, from whom Christ was not to be born—that is, a woman who had experienced a man—but the woman who then existed, who was a virgin and was called a woman by virtue of the proper scope of this designation, according to the pattern established at the beginning—a scope vindicated for the virgin [Col. 0898B] and thus for the whole class of women.
