## CHAPTER XIII

This order therefore contains the first procession of Aeons who are simultaneously born, marry, and beget; Sophia’s most perilous fall arising from desire for the Father; Horos’s most timely assistance; the expulsion of Enthymesis and the Passion joined to it; the schooling given by Christ and the Holy Spirit; the protective [Col. 0563B] reformation of the Aeons; the peacock-like adornment of the Savior; and the posting before him of angels comparable to him. [Note: [Col. 0564D] “The posting before him of comparable angels.” He calls the angels the Savior’s *antistates*: attendants standing before him, or presenting themselves to him to perform ministries. They are “comparable” because their kind could be compared with the Savior’s substance. RIG.]

“As for what remains,” you say, “farewell and applaud.” No; rather, I say, hear what remains—and hiss it off the stage. These events, however, are said to have run their course within the assembly of the Pleroma: the tragedy’s first scene. The buskin-strutting of the next act lies beyond the curtains—I mean outside the Pleroma. And yet, if such things happen beneath the Father’s bosom, within the enclosure of Horos the guardian, what will they be like outside, now in the open, where God was not?

For when Enthymesis—or henceforth Achamoth [Note: [Col. 0564D] “Achamoth, under this one untranslatable name.” More audacious than Tertullian—or perhaps more skilled in Hebrew—are recent translators of the Fathers who derived this word’s origin from Hebrew sources. Feuardent, as learned men consider correct, derives it from *Hokhmah*, “Wisdom,” whose root was *ḥakham*, “wise.” *Achamoth* is either the same noun used in the plural for emphasis (ἔμφασις), of which you have an example at Proverbs 1:20. Moreover, because Tertullian, otherwise most learned, stumbles over this foreign gloss, one must by no means agree with Semler, who infers from it that in Tertullian’s time Valentinian doctrine had not yet been accurately investigated by Christians, and therefore—an argument Semler pursues hand and foot—that the antiquity of the books of Saint Irenaeus must be doubted. Everyone sees how tasteless such an argument is, hanging upon a mere point: namely, a little Hebrew word, from a language which, it is agreed, had not yet been separated from barbarous idioms, had not received citizenship among Romans and Greeks, and was not thoroughly cultivated and known “inside and skin,” especially by Fathers of the Latin Church, until a late date. EDD.]—for henceforth she is designated by this single untranslatable [Note: [Col. 0563C] “Translatable,” Frankfurt] name—was expelled, together with the fault of her inseparable Passion, into regions alien to the light, which belongs to the Pleroma, into that void and emptiness of Epicurus, she was pitiable even with respect to her location. While this is her condition, Christ bends down from above [Note: [Col. 0564B] “Christ is brought down from above,” Junius], and through Horos is brought downward so that he may give form to the abortion from his own powers—but form of substance alone, not also of knowledge. Yet meanwhile she is left with a certain small endowment [Note: [Col. 0564B] “She is left with a certain small endowment: a repeated fragrance,” etc., Junius; meanwhile “fragrance” is the uncorrected reading of Latinus]: the fragrance of incorruptibility, by which, becoming aware of her fall, she might be prepared for the desire for better things. Having performed this merciful act, not without the fellowship of the Holy Spirit, Christ returns [Col. 0564B] to the Pleroma.
