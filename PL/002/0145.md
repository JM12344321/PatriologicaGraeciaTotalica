[Col. 1362B] **SAECULUM** (“age,” “world”). “Who made the age a body of times.” Zephyrus understands this of that indeterminate age in which neither earlier nor later was discerned, once it had been reduced to order and succession. Heraldus, however, prefers to understand it of the world: for the world is the body of times, from whose creation we number ages, years, months, and days; beyond it we cannot distinguish temporal successions, but there is eternity itself, and God himself is found to be both time and all things to himself. *Saeculum* may also simply be taken as a body of one hundred years. II, 57 A, “Now bound to the world.” See **OBSTRINGI**. II, 197 B, “In short, even to the present day the man exists and lives as a heretic concerning the world and nature as well,” i.e. his teaching is heretical in its treatment of the world and nature, since in Stoic fashion he makes matter eternal. II, 898 B, “Accordingly, let the age, God’s rival, see to it,” i.e. this impious age that strives against God—or else, “with nature as its guide, it will lead.” Pamelius understands this of Christians; Le Prieur, of the custom of the pagans, who cut off girls’ hair. II, 952 A, “From that final departure of the world.”

**SAEVUS** (“harsh”). II, 70 B, Cerdo’s other god; Irenaeus [Col. 1362C] renders it δίκαιον, “just,” i.e. one who presses his right to the utmost. For the utmost right is the utmost wrong.

**SALACITAS**, lust. II, 593 C, “Through voracity lust passes.”

**SALTICUS** (“dancing”). II, 137 B, “He is mutilated by an insulting slaughter for the profit of a dancing girl”: *puella saltica*, i.e. a little dancing girl, so called from dancing.

**SALTUS** (“leap”), concerning the incontinence of lust. I, 324 A, “Its companion is lust, whose leaps are everywhere,” etc.

**SALUTIFICATOR**, σωτήρ, “savior,” an author of salvation, as in Book V *Against Marcion*, chap. 15, II, 778 A: “Nor is there one savior, if there are two authors of salvation.”

**SAMIA**, a kind of cake regarded as a delicacy. II, 326 C, “That you would ever cook cakes and *samiae*”; others read *psamia*.

**SANGUINANS** (“bleeding”). II, 408 A, “Because the law excludes one from contact with a bleeding woman,” i.e. αἱμορροούσης, “a woman suffering a flow of blood.”

**SAPERE** (“to savor,” “to have the sense of”). II, 705 A, “Thus too the divine pronouncement has its proper sense.”

**SAPA**. II, 326 C, “Not, however, as though [producing] *sapa* from stones.” They called *sapa*—Varro says in Book I *On the Life of the Roman People*, as cited [Col. 1362D] by Nonius—what they had boiled down from must to half its original quantity.

**SAPIENTIA** (“wisdom”), the Christian religion. II, 1045 A, “When he first puts on this wisdom,” i.e. as soon as Christians assumed the cloak.

**SAPIENTIALIS** (“sapiential”). II, 670 A, “The vital and sapiential faculty, which they call τὸ ἡγεμονικόν,” that is, the ruling faculty.

**SAPOR** (“savor”). II, 851 A, “Not the substance itself, but the substance’s savor, by which the substance has savor”—with *sapit* used actively. So too *Against Marcion*, Book II, chap. 19: “through the same savor of suffering.”

**SARABARA**, a flowing garment worn by Persian nobles. Hesychius: Σαράβαρα, τὰ περὶ τὰς κνημίδας ἐνδύματα, “*sarabara*: garments worn around the shins.” I, 1171 A; II, 1043 A.

**SARMENTITII** (“faggot-men”). Why Christians were so called Tertullian himself explains at I, 531 A.

**SATIARE** (“to sate”). II, 124 B, “If it has sated the wound,” i.e. if the missile, driven into the wound, has penetrated it.

**SATURITAS** (“satiety”). II, 962 B, “Nor is there any cultivation of satiety in sackcloth.” [Col. 1363A] He says “cultivation of satiety” because joy proceeds from satiety, and from joy comes concern for more refined adornment, which is incompatible with the grimness of ashes and the harshness of sackcloth.

**SATURNALIA**. II, 119 A, “Christ commanded that such peace be purchased from the soldiers by Saturnalian gifts.” Either *Saturnalia* should be written, or it must be said that *Saturnalia munera*, “Saturnalian gifts,” are meant: these Christians sent to the soldiers or officials of the proconsul or governor on the occasion of the Saturnalian celebration, so that they might find them more favorable and merciful. The Saturnalia were days of gifts and New Year’s presents. Hence Martial’s line, 4:

> Sabellus had no Saturnalia more profitable  
> in ten years.

**SCAMMA**. Among the Greeks this denotes the arena in which athletes met in competition, because it was customarily surrounded by a trench, from σκάπτειν, “to dig.” Hence Jerome’s proverbial expression, addressed to Pammachius, “to depart from the *scamma* and the place of contest,” meaning to depart from one’s subject or cross the lines. I, 614 B, “And brought him forward into this arena.”

**SCANDALUM** (“scandal,” “stumbling block”). II, 320 C–D, “Before the scandal of Marcion.”

**SCANTINIA**, or the Scatinian law, enacted by Scatinius, tribune [Col. 1363B] of the plebs, against the passive and effeminate who practiced abominable sexual acts—that is, either themselves submitted to treatment as women or committed violence against boys. It prescribed a fine of ten thousand sesterces. Caelius, Epistle 13, writes that his enemies threatened him with prosecution under this law. II, 947 C, “Just as that man of yours from Uthina did not fear the Scantinian law.”

**SCAPULAE** (“shoulders”), the lower portions of mountains, which are often torn away by torrents and swept down together with them. II, 1033 A.

**SCENA** (“stage”). II, 336 A, “He ought to have run through the stage-play of the phantom.” The sense is: Marcion’s Christ ought to have served the stage completely and performed the play he had undertaken; for if he bore imaginary flesh, he ought also to have had [something else] imaginary—the object is absent from the supplied text—since in this way he would have run through the stage-play of the phantom.

**SCHOLA** (“school”). I, 455 A, “Schools of beasts”: the shows and circuses containing cages from which providers of games released lions, bears, and similar wild beasts trained to tear human beings apart, while men were trained to fight the beasts. During those spectacles, moreover, voices were sometimes heard [Col. 1363C] containing oblique attacks even upon their own Caesars.

**SCIAPODES** (“Shadow-Feet”). Hesychius: Σκιάποδες, οἱ ἐν Λιβύῃ πλατεῖς ἔχουσι τοὺς πόδας, καὶ ποιοῦσι σκιὰν αὐτοῖς ἐν καύματι, “The Shadow-Feet are those in Libya who have broad feet and use them to make shade for themselves in the heat.” And Stephanus of Byzantium: Σκίαποδες, ἔθνος Αἰθιοπικόν, ὡς Ἑκαταῖος ἐν περιηγήσει Αἰγύπτου, “The Shadow-Feet, an Ethiopian people, according to Hecataeus in his *Periegesis of Egypt*.” I, 570 A.

**SCIBILIS** (“knowable”). II, 511 A, “Not knowable to everyone.”

**SCIDIUM**. I, 621 A, “I shall test them with cheap wood-splinters.” *Scidia* are σχίδακες, κλάσματα ξύλων, “splinters, fragments of wood,” cheap pieces cut from felled trees. Vitruvius, Book II, chap. 1: “They block the gaps with splinters and mud.”

**SCINDERE** (“to split”). II, 368 C, “Within these he splits open the great and entire difference,” i.e. by splitting, as it were, he opens and exposes it, just as Cicero writes to Atticus of “splitting open his grief.”

**SCIRE** (“to know”), meaning “to be wise.” I, 436 A, “And rightly, if you know,” i.e. if you are wise; “so far as I know,” i.e. if I have any wisdom, if I remember correctly. I, 446 A, “Know from them,” i.e. learn and understand from them the source from which you wish to derive wisdom.

**SCOBINA**, an instrument for more polished adornment. Gloss: κοσμητήριον, “toilet implement.” I, 340 A.

**SCORTUM** (“hide”; ordinarily “prostitute”). II, 1042 A, “He has already portrayed Omphale represented in Hercules’ *scortum* [Col. 1363D].” This is understood of the lion’s skin, σκῦτος.

**SCRINIUM** (“archive”), a written document deposited and preserved in it. I, 381 A, “And meanwhile the sacred archive of a single prophet outlasts the ages.”

**SCRIPTURA** (“writing”). II, 585 A, “For us, in the writing of this seed.” Others read *inscriptura* as one word, i.e. ἐγγραφή, “enrollment,” by which, as he said above, we were enrolled in the class of the animal nature. Thus the enrollment of this seed is the rule laid down beforehand for the animal seed.

**SCROBULUS**. II, 903 A, “Or the *scrobuli* of the barbarians.” As Salmasius advised, this should read *stropulos*. According to Festus, *stropus*—also *stroppus* or *struppus*—is, as the philosopher Atteius thinks, a στρόφιον, “headband,” which priests wear on the head as an insignia; some say that it is a crown, or an insignia placed upon the head in lieu of a crown, such as a *strophilum*. From this come *stropiola* or *strophiola*, and *stropuli*. Le Prieur prefers *crobylos*. The commentator on Thucydides, Book I: κρώβυλος δ’ ἐστὶν εἶδος πλέγματος τῶν [Col. 1364A] τριχῶν ἀπὸ ἑτέρων εἰς ὀξὺ ἀπολῆγον, “A *crobylus* is a kind of interweaving of hair tapering from different parts to a point.” Hesychius: Κρώβυλος ὁ μαλλὸς τῶν παίδων, ἢ ὁ κόρυμβος τῆς ἐμπλοκῆς ἥ ἐστιν ἀνηνεγμένη ἀπὸ μέσου τοῦ μετώπου ἐπὶ τὴν κορυφήν· οὕτω δὲ ὁ ῥήτωρ Ἡγήσιππος, καὶ δήγγος ἐκαλεῖτο κρώβυλος, etc., “A *crobylus* is the lock of boys, or the topknot of braided hair carried upward from the middle of the forehead to the crown; so too the orator Hegesippus, and a *dēngos* was called a *crobylus*,” etc. See **CICADA**.

**SCROFA**, sow. II, 251 C, “Up to the offspring of thirty aeons it poured forth a swarm of divinity, like an aeonian sow.” Rigaltius emends this to “Aeneas’ sow,” for it alludes to that sow of Aeneas which, as Virgil says in *Aeneid* III,

> A huge sow found beneath the ilexes along the shore  
> lay there, having borne a litter of thirty young.

Rigaltius likewise reads in Jerome’s epistle to the Galatians, “As many offspring as Aeneas’ sow produced,” where the common text reads “the Aeonian sow.”

**SCRUPEUS**, rocky. II, 1011 A, “The rocky school is Chiron’s cave.”

**SCRUPULOSITAS** (“scrupulosity”). II, 39 B, “All these provocations to scrupulosity.” II, 957 B, “Toward every scrupulosity concerning food.” II, 905 A, “Descending from the constraints or scrupulosity,” i.e. from the peevishness of parents whom no one [Col. 1364B] pleases.

**SCRUPULOSUS** (“like little stones”). II, 1228 A, “God inflicted stony wounds upon the ears”—from which the little stones of gems might hang.

**SCYTALOSAGITTIPELLIGER** (“club-arrow-hide-bearer”), an epithet of Hercules, because he carried a club—in Greek σκυτάλη—arrows, and a lion’s hide. I, 1041 B.

**SECTA** (“sect,” “way”), in a favorable sense, of the Christian religion. I, 501 A, “While a veil is drawn over the goodness of this way.” II, 116 C, “Under the ancient redemption of his person and way.” II, 1008 C, “In the Apostle’s own way.” He used *secta* for conduct. Justinian does the same near the end of the title *On Legacies*, where he discusses shameful legacies: “The way of my times,” he says, “does not permit a testamentary disposition of this kind to be valid.” He therefore calls it “the Apostle’s way” in the same sense as, shortly afterward, “the Apostle’s nature and purpose,” the form and rule of his teachings. So above, “from Peter’s form.”

**SECUNDATUS** (“second rank”). II, 695 A, “If we give primacy to one and second rank to the other.”

**SECUNDUM** (“second”). I, 503 A, “Whom philosophers [Col. 1364C] assign as second after the gods,” i.e. they regard demons as occupying the place next after the gods. It is therefore no wonder that philosophers do not put demons to flight, since they are their friends, regarding them as second only to the gods.

**SEDATIO** (“cessation”). II, 441 A, “So that the cessation of the Law and the Prophets was effected by another power”: ἠρέμησις, “rest,” as though to say their placement in a seat of repose and leisure.

**SEMAXII** (“half-axle men”). Why Christians were so called Tertullian himself explains at I, 531 A.

**SEMEL**, ἐφάπαξ, ἁπαξαπλῶς, “once for all,” “altogether,” “utterly.” I, 328 A, and often.

**SEMINALE**, anything belonging to seed or derived from seed. II, 261 A, “The Great Mother reaped down into the earth together with the seed-bearing elements.”

**SEMITRACTATUS** (“half-treated”). II, 103 A, “I carried away with me a subject only half treated.”

**SEMPER** (“always”). II, 1029 C, “The rulers always of Africa.” A Hellenism, as in Terence: “And yesterday always gentleness.” Expressions of this kind are extremely frequent in Tertullian.

**SENECTUS** (“old age”). II, 133 B, “After the old age of the stadium,” i.e. after the stadium, or place appointed for contests, had undergone a kind of old age because it had not been frequented for many years. [Col. 1364D]

**SENESCERE** (“to grow old”). II, 1040 A, “The pursuits of the wrestling school growing old badly.” They are so called either because Severus revived them when they had nearly fallen into disuse, or because prizefighters rarely grow old.

**SENEX** (“old”). I, 527 A, “Old boys,” who preserved boyish virginity into old age.

**SENTENTIA** (“judgment,” “assertion”). II, 815 B, “Is it then a matter of fixing an assertion even upon little vessels and implements?” i.e. asserting it fearlessly and steadfastly.

**SENTENTIALITER** (“categorically”). II, 783 B, “Or because the Lord himself pronounced categorically and definitively.”

**SENTIRE** (“to feel,” “to direct one’s mind”). II, 276 C, “After so great an age he turned his thought toward man’s salvation,” i.e. directed his mind toward this plan. I, 1239 B, “So long as you have been disposed toward the Lord,” i.e. devoted to the Lord. I, 1264 A, “If, when afflicted by loss, we have felt impatiently.”

[Col. 1365A] **SENSUS** (“sense”). II, 213 B, “Sophia in sense.” Pithoeus’ book reads: “Sense and Sophia.” Junius proposes “Sophia’s sense.” II, 798 B, “It is indeed possible to be wise even through common conceptions in matters concerning God.” Common conceptions are κοιναὶ ἔννοιαι, or notions approved by the perception and consensus of nearly everyone.

**SEPIA** (“cuttlefish”). II, 306 A, “But indeed, these cuttlefish.” He aptly compares the Marcionites to cuttlefish. For, as Pliny relates in Book IX, chap. 29, cuttlefish are a kind of fish that, when they perceive themselves being caught, discharge ink—which serves them as blood—and hide themselves by darkening the water. In the same way, when the Marcionites perceive that they are being exposed, that is, when they are convicted of error, they spew darkness into this blasphemy and thus obstruct and divert their accuser by alleging things that blacken and obscure the Creator’s goodness.

**SEPTENTRIONES**, the constellation composed of seven stars which the Greeks called ἅμαξα, “the Wagon,” and ἄρκτος, “the Bear.” II, 771 B.

**SEQUESTRARE** (“to sequester”). II, 578 B, “He had sequestered it in his son the Demiurge,” [Col. 1365B] i.e. had preserved it set apart; a forensic term. II, 834 B, “Bodies treated with preserving spices are sequestered for burial, mausoleums, and monuments.” II, 848 B, “If [it is intended] rather to sequester faith in the future resurrection,” i.e. to confirm it securely, as though by the good faith of a stakeholder. II, 669 B, “And in what lodgings within the body they are sequestered,” i.e. in what seats the individual parts are kept. II, 759 B, “In whom every hope is sequestered.”

**SEQUESTRATORIUM**, a place where money is deposited and received. II, 872 C, “Because the earth too is a repository for seeds.”

**SERICUM**, silken clothing. II, 1042 A, “What that Hercules of Omphale’s must have been like in silk.” Richerius reads *in theristro*, i.e. θερινὸν ἱμάτιον, “a summer garment.”

**SI** (“if”) often means “as if,” “as though.” I, 406 A, “As if fruits, as if crops.” I, 438 A—or rather: “Let it first be established, then, whether they can impart these things.” II, 222 A, “The question, of course, is whether it was the earth that was made.” I, 467 A, “If perchance,” i.e. if there is perchance, as is supposed, any injury. II, 156 B, “If not,” i.e. but if in some respect it can no longer [Col. 1365C] be rooted out. II, 187 C, “If not, believe at least because of the works,” i.e. if by any argument or means, by any circumstance or cause.

**SIBYNA**, a kind of hunting spear, as the author explains at II, 361 C. Hesychius: σιβύνη, ὅπλον δόρατι παραπλήσιον, “a *sibyna*, a weapon resembling a spear”; and elsewhere, ζηβύνη, ὁλοσίδηροι ἄκοντες, “a *zēbyna*, javelins made entirely of iron”; and shortly afterward, ζιβύνη, ὁλοσίδηρον ἀκόντιον, ἢ λόγχη, ἢ σπάθη, ἢ μάχαιρα, “a *zibyna*, an all-iron javelin, or lance, or broad sword, or knife.” Festus explains it as a kind of Illyrian missile resembling a hunting spear. Ennius: “They remain, stabbing with curved daggers and *sibynae*.”

**SICCARE** (“to dry”). II, 577 B, “While the mud survived, it dried,” i.e. it was dried.

**SIGAEUM**, a promontory of the Troad; Achilles is believed to have been buried there.

**SIGERIUS**, a German by nationality and Domitian’s chamberlain, according to Dio. Though he had received very great favors from Caesar, he conspired with Parthenius, Stephanus, and others to kill Domitian. I, 457 A.

**SIGILLARIUM**. II, 563 A, “A figurine-maker’s shop,” just as a district of Rome was called *Sigillaria*, τὰ ἀγαλματοπωλεῖα, “the image-makers’ shops.” [Col. 1365D] Suetonius, *Claudius*, chap. 16, and *Nero*, chap. 28. II, 573 A, “And as though by an external puppet-master’s impulse,” i.e. he was moved to external actions by the internal direction of that other, just as articulated and self-moving figurines—νευρόσπαστα and αὐτόματα—are customarily moved by hidden craftsmanship and mechanical power. II, 654, “As though by a puppet’s motion, with an inward force moving the surface,” i.e. by an interior movement accomplished through a certain artifice and mechanical power.

**SIGNACULUM** (“mark,” “seal”). I, 392 A, “Concerning the bodily mark itself.” Minucius Felix: “Thus, finally, we are distinguished not by a mark upon the body, as you suppose, but by the sign of innocence and modesty.” II, 541 A, “The complete seal upon the tongue,” by which reverent silence was commanded. I, 656 A, “Against whom we abjure at the seal of faith,” i.e. in baptism we solemnly profess under oath that we have renounced her and will steadfastly continue to renounce her.

**SIGNARE** (“to seal”), to conceal. I, 401 A, “Midday was withdrawn when the sun sealed the world,” i.e. when it concealed and sealed away its light. Compare Job 9:7. II, 577 [Col. 1366A] A, “When Horus marked the final line.” II, 122 A, “Distrust in immediately sealing the heel of the beast,” where *signare* means to enclose as if under seal and keep shut. II, 282 B, “How will he have someone in whom to mark his goodness?” i.e. display it, someone upon whom he may bestow his goodness. I, 1296 A, “When you sign your little bed, when you sign your poor body”—with the sign of the cross. II, 927 B, “Sealed by age,” i.e. one who because of age is free from lust and will remain untouched.

**SIGNIFICANTIA** (“signification”). II, 549 B, “The significations will appear within the limits of the pages.” II, 304 C, “Not to touch upon any of the law’s hidden significations”—its spiritual significations, of course, etc. Rigaltius reads “of the signifying law.” “The hidden things of the signifying law,” i.e. the mysteries of the figurative law. He himself immediately explains it: “of the spiritual and prophetic law, of course.” II, 483 A, “But shadowed by significations.”

**SILICERNIUM**, a funeral banquet offered to old men. I, 347 A. See Donatus on Terence, *Adelphoe*, p. 299.

**SILVA** (“wood”). II, 51 A, “By what right, Marcion, do you fell my wood?” [Col. 1366B] i.e. why do you break into my possession? Why do you put your sickle into my harvest? I, 286 A, “You cut down and fell that entire old and squalid wood of laws with the new axes of imperial rescripts and edicts.” I, 244 A, “So that you may understand with how mighty an axe censorship cuts down, uproots, and eradicates the whole wood of lusts.”

**SIMPLICITAS** (“simplicity”). II, 543 A, “By beating themselves they scatter your simplicity,” i.e. they distract your simple mind, which deals straightforwardly with the truth, into various thoughts, beating their breasts just as though they agreed with the doctrine and were concerned solely and most earnestly with repentance. Rigaltius reads “your foolish simplicity,” with this sense: If you contend with them at close quarters, they will indeed beat themselves, but they will take care that nothing may be inferred from their self-beating except that they are foolishly simple; their chief concern will be to pass for foolishly simple rather than wicked.

**SIMPULUM**, a vessel used for pouring libations of wine at sacrifices; it is also called *simpuvium*. I, 347 A. See Kipping, *Roman Antiquities*.

[Col. 1366C] **SINGILARIUM**. See **SIGILLARIUM**.

**SINGULARIS**, μόνιος, “solitary.” I, 313 A, “Solitary Christians,” i.e. those alone who have neither mother nor sister living or at hand. Thus Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, Book III: “The solitary God.”

**SINGULARITER** (“singly”). II, 914 C, “To think about the end of your singularity,” i.e. about your own end, since you now live alone, having survived your wife.

**SIPARA**. These are hanging curtains belonging to girls and theaters. I, 368 A.

**SIPARIUM**, the curtain of a mime-show, which blocked the audience’s view while the acts of the plays were being changed. II, 563 B, “But beyond the curtain there is another tragic performance,” i.e. another tragic scene, another tragic act.

**SITIRE** (“to thirst”). II, 809 A, “Who perhaps still thirst to know God.” Those who do not yet know thirst, and therefore are ignorant. II, 835 B, “Nevertheless, they thirst for an interpretation of their own simplicity.” II, 886 D, “You will be able to thirst for no doctrine.” II, 723 B, “Adam first drank deeply of sleep [Col. 1366D] before he thirsted for rest.”

**SOL** (“sun”). II, 338 A, “I suppose, first anointed in the sun.” He alludes to ἡλίωσις, or exposure to the sun, whereby those who had been anointed warmed themselves to dry and strengthen their limbs. Persius, *Satire* 4:

> Or if, anointed, you idle and fix the sun upon your skin.

Junius prefers “at the bath-seat”; see that entry.

**SOLIDUM** (“the whole”). I, 1275 A, “Only see that you can receive the whole: this is entrusted to the good faith of my admonition.” He alludes to the capacity of husband and wife to take an entire inheritance, which was permitted only in certain cases under the Julian and Papian laws. On this matter title XVI appears among the fragments of Ulpian, III, 168: “And they believe that the unmarried and childless cannot receive the whole under God’s testament.” “To receive the whole” is ἴσων ἀξιοῦσθαι, “to be deemed worthy of equal rights,” which is the same as becoming sole heir and entering upon the entire inheritance. Under the Julian law this did not happen in the case of the unmarried, for their legacies lapsed and went to the treasury. Hence the Julian law was called the law of lapsing legacies.

**SOLIUM** (“throne”). II, 158 A, “And thus the Latins express the throne itself in vocal form.” The sense is that the name *solium* has now acquired that meaning in Latin ears. For *solium* is commonly said to derive from *sedere*, “to sit,” but *solium* also derives from *solus*, “alone,” so that it means the seat of one alone, the condition or dignity of one alone—monarchy. Of the same kind is this passage in Festus: “*Solia* are seats upon each of which no more than one person can sit; hence those who hold banquets for the gods are said to spread *solia*,” etc. II, 618 A, “I suppose, first anointed at the bath-seat.” *Solium*, so called from sitting, is a vessel in which people bathed while seated. See Suetonius, *Augustus*, chap. 82; Pliny, *Natural History*, Book XXVI, chap. 19.

**SOLOX**, coarse wool. I, 1042 B, “The coarse wool of a running-cloak.” Turnebus proposes: “the color of a running-cloak.”

**SOLUM** (“ground”). “Flesh upon the ground”: in solitude, in the sadness of desolation. Or: upon the bare earth, upon naked rock.

**SOPHISTA** (“sophist”). II, 548 A, “Miltiades, the sophist of the churches.” He is so called because he possessed great ability in disputing about subjects of every kind and passages of Scripture. II, 963 A, “All the sophists are troubled concerning the dream of the king of Babylon.” By “sophists” he means those elsewhere called wise men, interpreters of dreams, and diviners.

[Col. 1367B] **SORDES** (“filth”). II, 806 B, “And filth as an appendage of this duty”: an uncultivated squalor, as formerly in sackcloth and ashes, concerning which see *On Repentance*, chap. 9. II, 991 A, “The poison of lust adhered everywhere, and the cast filth”—this is the correct reading, not “lots”—“was incapable of being washed away, because the waters themselves had not yet been washed.” J. F. Gronovius, in his little book *Observations on Ecclesiastical Writers*, writes the following on this passage:

> Thus reads Ursinus’ manuscript; but closer to what was once commonly printed was: “milky filth not capable of possessing”; from which we shall plainly see what Scaliger’s hand fashioned: “and the filth of struggle not suitable for reins.” But we understand this no more readily.

“Milky” cannot be approved—not, I say, because of the κακέμφατον, “ill-sounding double meaning” (for many things in Tertullian could be censured under that name), but because that fluid belongs to the fig, not to its leaves, whose likeness he employs. Thus Jerome too, to Eustochium: “‘Increase and multiply’: this decree is fulfilled after paradise, nakedness, and the fig leaves that inaugurated the irritation of marriage.” I think it should read: “The poison of lust adhered everywhere, and hidden filth incapable of being washed away”—that is, concealed, [Col. 1367C] internal filth and, as he himself said, filth that adhered. As in the poet: “And he is consumed by a hidden fire.” Water was insufficient to wash this away: though powerful to cleanse external filth, it was itself also polluted by certain hidden stains before it was cleansed from them through Christ’s baptism. For those who take the conjunction *quod* as a pronoun referring back to *virus*, “poison,” are mistaken. This is a very common idea among the Fathers: Christ’s baptism sanctified the waters and, together with the Holy Spirit, made them capable of washing away those hidden and secret stains of original sin. Optatus of Milevis, Book I: “But Christ’s flesh, when it descended into and ascended from the Jordan, should not have been said by you to have been submerged. His flesh is found to be holier than the Jordan itself, so that by his descent he cleansed the water rather than being cleansed by it.” Jerome, urging Marcella to move to Bethlehem: “To see Lazarus come forth bound in his grave-clothes, and the streams of the Jordan made purer for the Lord’s washing.” In the epitaph of his mother Paula: “The Lord washed through his baptism waters polluted by the Flood and stained by the destruction of the entire human race.” [Col. 1367D]

**SOROR** (“sister”). II, 1031 A, The sister-city is Utica, a colony of the Tyrians. II, 739 A, “He tears apart the concretion of the sister substances.”

**SORS** (“lot”). II, 991 A, “And the cast lots”: read “filth”; see that entry.

**SPADO** (“eunuch”). II, 930 C, “Among alien eunuchs,” i.e. among heretics who abolish marriage. Heretics are alien and foreign to Catholics. He calls those who hate marriage eunuchs.

**SPADONATUS** (“eunuchhood”). I, 1327 A, “And they assign themselves to eunuchhood.”

**SPARSILE** (“scattered thing”). II, 984 A, “Such and so great are their scattered offerings.” Pamelius produced this reading from an English manuscript, and Pithoeus noted that the Reims manuscript read *spartilia*; others read *futilia*, “trifles.”

**SPARTEOLI** was the name given to soldiers who kept watch at Rome to ward off fires [Col. 1368A] and patrolled the districts of the city. Their name was derived either from ropes, which were extensively used in extinguishing fires, or from a kind of footwear, or from garments of esparto grass in which they were clothed. I, 474 A, “At the smoke from the Serapian dinner the fire-watch will be roused.”

**SPATHA**. I, 1332 A, According to Vegetius, longer swords are called *spathae* because they resemble a palm branch, *spatha*.

**SPATHALIUM** is a woman’s ornament fashioned from various materials into a flexible strand, ἀπὸ τοῦ σπαθᾶν, “from *spathân*, ‘to beat close with a weaving-batten.’” I, 1332 A.

**SPECIATUM**, εἰδοποιηθέν, “formed into its particular kinds.” II, 233 C.

**SPECIOSITAS** (“comeliness”). I, 1319 A, “But even the apparatus of natural comeliness must be effaced.”

**SPECTUS**, sight, view. II, 410 A, “In the sight of a hundred thousand people.” Pliny, *Natural History*, Book II, chap. 54: “The Etruscans divided the sky in that field of view into sixteen parts.”

**SPECULUM** (“mirror”). II, 996 A, “Even if everything could correspond to the mirror,” i.e. the image, example, or parable.

[Col. 1368B] **SPECULATORIA** (“scout’s”). II, 76 B, “He discharged the most burdensome scout’s [boot] from his feet.” This concerns the military boot, especially that of the soldier who had served among the scouts. Such service was extremely restless, burdensome, and harsh, since he wandered here and there running, reconnoitering, and carrying reports. Thus, after becoming a Christian from a booted scout, he discharged from his feet that exceedingly burdensome and troublesome scout’s boot, and at once began to stand upon holy ground—that is, on that very spot courageously to meet death for Christ’s name and to lead a holy and innocent life, wholly unlike that of the scout, who generally acted as executioner at the tribune’s command.

**SPELAEUM** is the name of a place. Justin Martyr, *Against Trypho*: Καὶ σπήλαιον καλοῦσι τὸν τόπον, ἔνθα μύειν τοὺς πειθομένους αὐτῷ παραδιδόασιν, “And they call the place a cave, where they hand over for initiation those who are persuaded by him.” Porphyry, in his *Commentary on the Cave of the Nymphs* from *Odyssey* XII, relates that this *Spelaeum* was first consecrated by Zoroaster in the mountains of Persia, near springs. II, 102 A, “Who, when he is initiated in the cave.”

[Col. 1368C] **SPERARE** (“to hope”), meaning “to fear.” II, 1034 B, “So that Campania may fear all the more on account of its mountains.” Virgil, *Aeneid* I: “Fear the gods.” Also, “to trust.” II, 1042 A, “Which, in Omphale’s house, I suppose was made with balsam or *telinum*.” I, 1318 A, “It is therefore more useful if we expect that we can sin, for by expecting it we shall fear.” I, 1247 B, “Or likewise they would sit by themselves by day” (*idem* may represent *iidem*; the relevance of this reading to **SPERARE** is uncertain in the supplied text).

**SPICULUM** (“dart”). II, 122 A, “A barbed dart,” that is, hooked at the tip; that chain of joints draws it back in the act of wounding and, as it withdraws, pours its poison from a little duct into the sore (*venula* conjectured for the supplied *venuta*).

**SPIRITALIS** (“spiritual”). II, 931 A, “But among us, whom the recognition of spiritual gifts rightly causes to be called spiritual.”

**SPIRITUS** (“spirit”). II, 194 D, “Speech, however, is constructed from spirit, and, so to speak, spirit is the body of speech.” II, 194 D, “Thus, even if the Spirit of God could suffer anything in the Son.” The author means Christ’s divine nature, for the negation of that hypothesis immediately follows: “But it is enough that the Spirit of God suffered nothing in his own name.” II, 892 C, “But when the spirit itself is violated in the virgin,” i.e. violence [Col. 1368D] was done to the virgin’s mind and purpose, which sought through the aid of a veil to provide for her chastity and sanctification in mind and body. II, 658 B, “John, having come to be in the Spirit of God.” A Hellenism: ἐν πνεύματι θεοῦ γενόμενος, “having come to be in the Spirit of God.”

**SPONSIO** is money formally pledged after securities have been deposited, or money wagered on a gladiator’s victory. I, 648 B, “Now excited by the wagers.” See Turnebus, Book VIII, chap. 4, and XVIII, chap. 33.

**SPORTULA** (“little basket,” “dole”). II, 342 C, “Like a petty thief [stealing] a dole,” i.e. by clandestine fraud and under a counterfeit name, as thieves of doles customarily do in the vestibules of houses.

**SPURCILOQUIUM** (“filthy speech”). II, 800 B, “But furthermore, however much filthy speech is permitted,” etc.

**SPUTUM** (“spittle”). I, 1323 B, “Or by attaching bosses of spittle to their necks,” i.e. bubbles and blisters of the leather that was laid over them. Thus Scaliger reads and interprets the passage; others read “of shields.”

[Col. 1369A] **STABULUM**, lodging, a shelter intended for dwelling and staying. I, 491 A.

**STADIUM**. II, 133 C, “After the old age of the stadium,” i.e. after the stadium, or place appointed for contests, had undergone a kind of old age because it had not been frequented for many years. II, 81 A, “But I do not suppose that she walked veiled in her husband’s stadium.” By “stadium” he understands a ὕπαιθρον, an open-air promenade, which we also call a *xystus*. Such promenades were also customarily planted with trees to provide shade.

**STATICULUM** (“little standing image”). II, 127 A, “And you shall break their little standing images,” τὰς στήλας, the pillars upon which standing men or idols rest.

**STARE** (“to stand”) is said of the sky during prolonged heat or frost, when it does not dissolve into rain. I, 479–480 A, “If the sky has stood still.”

**STATIO** (“station,” “fast”). II, 24 A, “Where is the station of believing?” The entire period is allegorical, drawn from military life. When you believe, he says, you have, as it were, fixed the stakes of your tent and station so that they may not be moved farther forward; you have reached the fruit of seeking—that which you were probing in your search. God has prescribed this boundary as a trench for you, and you must not seek beyond it. For a *statio* is a place where mortals customarily halt and converse. I, 1183 A, “If *statio* derives its name from the military example.” II, 732 B, “The station of the senses”: he means the region about the heart, περικάρδιον, the blood surrounding the heart. II, 925 B, “After you failed to remain continent in the second station,” i.e. in marriage. II, 967 C, “Which commanded even the elements to stand fast.” II, 954 C, “Because we generally prolong our station-fasts until evening.” II, 92 A, “Will he now observe station-fasts for another more readily than for Christ?” II, 966 A, “Likewise our station-fasts as unworthy, though some were indeed continued until late.” II, 733 A, “Because Daniel too grew thin from food during a station-fast of three weeks.” I, 1181 A, “Likewise concerning the days of station-fasts.” II, 971 B, “And interposing the half-fasts of the stations.”

**STATUS** (“state”). II, 584 A, “According to the earthy state derived from Cain”: by *status* he means their condition and character.

[Col. 1369C] **STELLA** (“star”). II, 977 A, “And they long for the presence of the lingering star.” He means the star which Plautus calls *vespergo*, Ennius *vesper*, and Virgil *Hesperus*. By “authority” he means presence. For although hungry, they did not dare touch any food before the appearance of the evening star. II, 266 A, “To live by the Creator’s very stars as well,” i.e. to make one’s life depend upon the movement of the stars.

**STEMMA**, a line of descent. II, 42 B, “The lineage of Apelles.”

**STERCEIAE**, women who clean or rock cradles. II, 554 B.

**STIGMA** (“mark,” “tattoo”). II, 903 B, “Or the tattoos of the Britons,” i.e. the various and many-colored designs with which the Britanni or Britons at that time variegated their bodies and even their heads. See Herodian, Book III.

**STILLICIDIUM** (“drop”). II, 21 B, “Unless it is a drop from a bucket,” from Isaiah 40:15; i.e. unless they are the few called from the innumerable multitude of nations or Gentiles.

**STYLUS** (“pen,” “written account”). II, 1035 A, “Your pen does not usually go farther,” i.e. [Col. 1369D] among you the written account of the Assyrians does not usually extend beyond Ninus. II, 52 A, “An adulterating pen.” II, 198 A, “Twice a falsifier, both with the branding iron and with the pen.” II, 549 A, “No one is said to be so idle with his pen.” II, 798 B, “This subject too has from us a most complete written treatment of every state of the soul.”

**STIPENDIARI**, to perform military service or receive one’s pay. II, 338 A, 618 B, “Then armed with cloths and paid with butter,” concerning Pontic infants.

**STIPARE** (“to pack”). II, 906 B, “They pack the shoe, which has many forms, lest it become baggy.” To prevent a shoe from becoming baggy when it was looser than the size of the feet, they customarily packed it with a shoe-tree or any other suitable material. He distinguishes the many-formed shoe from the uniform and flat one worn by more modest women. See Balduinus, *On the Shoe*, chap. 19.

**STOMACHUS** (“stomach”). II, 30 A, “So that someone undergoes an overturning either of the stomach or of the brain.” In the same sense he said in *Against Marcion*, Book I, chap. 14: “Every alteration arising from innovation must enter into a difference from those things whose alteration it is.”

[Col. 1370A] **STRANGULABILIS** (“liable to strangulation”). II, 703 C, “Liable to strangulation even through submersion in pools.”

**STRENA** (“New Year’s gift”). I, 674 B, “Even New Year’s gifts to be sought after.”

**STRICTURA** (“restriction”). II, 965 B, “But this restriction of drink is also a portion of xerophagy,” i.e. dry food too is indicated through this restricted form of drinking.

**STRINGERE** (“to draw”). I, 527 A, “Or drawing thunderbolts from heaven,” i.e. calling them forth and thus, as it were, drawing a fiery sword from the clouds as from a sheath. II, 501 C, “For from this he expressed what he touched upon in the first epistle,” i.e. what he indicated briefly.

**STRONGYLA**, a statue upon a shield, with its face rendered in relief together with the breast. II, 1041 A.

**STROPHA**, trick, cunning, imposture. II, 334 C, “How much more worthily would necessity have administered faith than a trick.” I, 660 B, “Not tricks, but acts of simplicity.” II, 698 A, “For he who devised such a trick.”

**STROPHIOLUM** (“little headband”). II, 101 B, “Why do you condemn your head to a little headband or dragon-bearing ornament?” The ancients, of course, wore thinner crowns, calling them *strophia*; hence *strophiola*, as Pliny explains in *Natural History*, Book XXI, chap. 2.

**STROPULUS**. See **SCROBULUS**.

**STRUCTE** (“elaborately”). I, 1329 B, “To go forth more elaborately,” i.e. better equipped, with greater attention to accessories and feminine finery. II, 922 A, “More fully and elaborately.”

**STRUCTIO**, instruction. I, 1251 A, “Among us, however, reason is instruction.”

**STRUERE**, to equip, adorn. II, 163 B, “Speech, however, is constructed from spirit.” II, 902 B–903 A, “Equipped with experiences of every emotion.” I, 1257 A, “How would one thus equipped assist in sinning?”

**STRUES** (“structure”). II, 567 A, “Into this entire structure of the world,” i.e. its construction.

**STUPIDUS** (“insensible”). II, 694 B, “So that one was insensible to the seed, the other untouched.”

**STUPORATUS** (“stupefied”). I, 1320 A, “All glory is vain and stupefied,” i.e. dull and foolish. Catulus used the word in this sense: “Such is that fool of mine: he sees nothing, hears nothing.”

[Col. 1370C] **STUPRUM** (“debauchery”), for adultery. II, 14 B, “[One] guilty of adultery in the case of David.”

**SUADERE** (“to persuade”). II, 547 A, “Otherwise, it has been permitted this heresy only to color itself so greatly.” The verb *suadere* is transferred from the dye-works of dyers. Festus: “A color is called *suasus* when it is produced by a smoky dripping upon a white garment.” Plautus: “Why did you dye your little cloak with a *suasus*, shameless woman?” Nor are there lacking those who say that every color produced by dyeing is called *suasus*, because it is, as it were, “persuaded” to pass from white into another color. Hence *suadere* means to apply some color without a vessel or dyeing-vat. This heresy, says the author, has been permitted only to “color itself” so much, i.e. to add to itself as much coloring, cosmetic, and deceitful pigment as public prostitutes and brothel-whores commonly do. II, 1003 C, “Persuading everyone to tears.”

**SUASORIA** (“suasory exercise”). II, 957 B, “And we know what the suasory exercises for fleshly comforts are like,” i.e. we know what sorts of declamations the Psychics customarily compose in recommending fleshly comforts. Among rhetoricians two kinds of themes [Col. 1370D] were treated: suasories and controversies. Suasories, as the lighter exercises requiring less judgment, were assigned to boys as the rudiments of speaking; controversies, or judicial exercises, were assigned to the more mature.
