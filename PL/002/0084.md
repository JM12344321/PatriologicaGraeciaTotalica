## CHAPTER III

[Note: [Col. 0798D] “I shall therefore also use the opinion of some Plato.” This must undoubtedly be understood of every rational soul, for it is of that soul that Plato speaks both in the *Phaedo* and elsewhere whenever he declares the soul immortal. ## CHAPTER IV

Heretics therefore begin from this point at once and from it lay their foundation; they then build into their argument those considerations by which they know minds are easily captured, drawing upon the favorable agreement of common conceptions.

[Note: [Col. 0799D] “Would you hear anything else first,” etc. The heretics against whom Tertullian is arguing said that our flesh was evil because it had been made by an evil god. So too did the pagans: according to Epiphanius in the *Anchored One*, the Epicureans and other philosophers launched many attacks upon our flesh and generation, as is evident from Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* III, and Lactantius, *On the Workmanship of God* III. LAC.] Would you hear anything else sooner or more often from a heretic than from a pagan? [Note: [Col. 0799C] “As” is absent from Rhenanus.] Do you not immediately and everywhere hear abuse of the flesh—of its origin, its material, its fall [Note: [Col. 0799C] Others read “cause.”], and its every end? Do you therefore wish, wise man, to persuade yourself [Note: [Col. 0800C] “To be persuaded,” Junius and Rhenanus.] that this flesh, though snatched from your sight, touch, and memory, will one day recover itself—whole from corruption, solid from dissolution, full from emptiness, indeed something from nothing at all? And this despite the fires and waters, the bellies of beasts, the crops of birds, the stomachs [Note: [Col. 0800C] “Castings,” Semler.] of fish, [Note: [Col. 0800D] “And even the peculiar appetite of the times themselves.” He alludes to the gluttonous cruelty and cruel appetite of Vedius Pollio, who plunged condemned slaves into ponds of lampreys—not only so that he could watch an entire person torn apart at once, as Pliny reports, but also so that he could eat human flesh in the fish, as Tertullian teaches in *On the Pallium*: “I likewise plunge the knife,” he says, “into the cruelty with which Vedius Pollio exposed slaves to the attacks of lampreys. We, to be sure, express these things somewhat more decently out of regard for literary propriety. Yet how much filthy speech from both pagans and heretics may be encountered in their disputes! [Note: [Col. 0800C] “How much filthy speech their disputes permit,” Rhenanus and Pamelius.]

## CHAPTER V

Since, therefore, all the uninstructed still reason from common conceptions, while the doubtful and simple are disturbed anew by those same conceptions, and since everywhere this is the first [Note: [Col. 0800C] “First of all,” Rhenanus.] [Col. 0800C] battering ram directed against us, by which the condition of the flesh is assaulted, it is necessary that we first fortify the condition of the flesh. [Col. 0801A] You must repel reproach with praise [Note: [Col. 0801C] “Reproach having been driven away by praise,” Rhenanus and the Pithoean manuscript.]. Thus the heretics provoke us to employ rhetoric [Note: [Col. 0801D] “Thus they make us employ rhetoric.” The ancient Fathers were very reluctant to divert the streams of philosophy and eloquence that their modesty restrained. Simon Magus (*Recognitions of Blessed Clement* II), Julian, and Porphyry, as cited by Eusebius, therefore charged Christians with ignorance of philosophy and eloquence. The charge’s falsity is demonstrated by the Fathers’ most elegant and eloquent volumes, for, compelled by heretics, they poured forth full streams. LAC.] as well as philosophy.

Even if that futile and paltry little body [Note: [Col. 0801D] “That little body, which they finally call evil.” I think the author has Marcion’s heresy in view, since, by saying that the body was created by an evil god and from evil matter, he indirectly concluded that the body itself was also evil. The Manichaeans later followed that error more openly. PAM.]—which they do not shrink from calling evil—had been the work of angels, [Note: [Col. 0801D] “As Menander and Marcus maintain.” That angels created the world, and consequently human flesh as well, was a heresy Menander shared with his teacher Simon Magus, as may be seen above in *Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 46. PAM.] as Menander and Marcus maintain; or even if it had been the construction of some fiery being, likewise an angel, as Apelles teaches—the patronage of a secondary divinity would suffice to confer dignity upon the flesh. We recognize angels as coming after God.

Indeed, whoever the supreme god of each heretic may be, I could reasonably derive the dignity of the flesh from him too, since the will to produce it would have been present in him. For surely he would have prevented the making of something which he knew would be made, had he not wished it to be made. Thus even according to them the flesh is likewise a thing belonging to God. No work fails to belong to the one who permitted it to exist.

It is well, however, [Col. 0801B] that most doctrines, even the harsher ones, concede to our God the entire fashioning of the human being. You know sufficiently how great he is, since you have believed him to be the only God. Let the flesh now begin to please you, since its craftsman is so great.

“But the world too,” you say, “is God’s work, and nevertheless the fashion of this world passes away, as the Apostle also testifies (1 Cor. 7). Nor will the world’s restoration be prejudged [Note: [Col. 0801C] “Is prejudged,” Rhenanus.] merely because it is God’s work. And if the universe cannot be re-formed after its passing, what hope is there for one of its parts?”

Certainly—if the part is placed on the same level as the universe. But we appeal to the difference between them. First, all things were made by God’s Word, and without him nothing was made. The flesh too came into being by God’s word, for the sake of its form, lest anything should exist without the Word, since he first said, “Let us make man.” But it was fashioned additionally by his hand, for the sake of its preeminence, lest it be equated with the universe. [Col. 0801C] “And God fashioned man,” Scripture says.

There was undoubtedly a substantial reason for the difference, namely, the respective condition of the things made. For the things being made were lesser than the one for whom they were being made: indeed, they were being made for man, to whom they were soon assigned by God. Rightly, therefore, [Note: [Col. 0801D] “As servants, by command and authority.” Tertullian speaks acutely here, as though God, even then in the creation of things, were inaugurating or furnishing those very things for service under human authority. They were made by command; man, by contrast, since he was to become their lord, was made not by an imperative but in the image of God, who commands, and was made supreme by the Lord himself. For according to certain Fathers, man’s status as God’s image consisted in his dominion over all things. Isidore of Pelusium, Book III, Epistle 95. LAC.] as servants, by command and authority, all things had proceeded from the power of a mere spoken summons [Note: [Col. 0801C] “Vocal,” Pamelius and Rhenanus.]. By contrast, man, as their lord, was constructed by God himself precisely so that he could be lord, since he was made by the Lord. [Col. 0802A]

But remember that “man” properly designates the flesh, which first took possession of the name “man”: “And God fashioned man, mud from the earth.” He was already “man” while still mud. “And he breathed into his face the breath of life; and man”—that is, the mud—“became a living soul.” “And God placed the man whom he had fashioned in paradise.” Thus, first the fashioned object was “man,” and afterward the whole composite was “man.”

I have commended this point so that you may know that absolutely everything provided and promised by God to man is owed not to the soul alone but also to the flesh—if not by its participation in the human kind, then certainly by the privilege of the human name.

## CHAPTER VI

I shall therefore pursue my argument—if only I can vindicate for the flesh as much as the one who made it conferred upon it. Even then it could glory that its littleness, the mud, [Col. 0802B] came into the hands of God, whatever those hands may be; it was sufficiently blessed if it had merely been touched.

For what if, without any further workmanship, the fashioned object had immediately been completed by God’s touch? So great was the business being conducted through the construction of this material! Thus it is honored as many times as it experiences God’s hands: while it is touched, while portions are taken from it, while it is drawn out, while it is shaped.

Consider God wholly occupied and devoted [Note: [Col. 0801D] “Dedicated,” Rhenanus and Semler.] to it—with hand, mind, work, counsel, wisdom, providence, and especially with that affection which traced its contours [Note: [Col. 0802C] “Kept tracing,” Pamelius and Franecker; “befitted,” Rhenanus; “dictated,” others.]. For whatever form the mud was taking, Christ was being contemplated as the man who was to come, since he too was to be mud; and the Word as the flesh he was to become, since he too was then to be earth [text and punctuation uncertain] [Note: [Col. 0802D] “Christ was in view as the man who would be, because both mud and flesh; the Word, because also earth at that time.” These words seem clearer with the punctuation found in the Pithoean manuscript: “Christ was in view as the man who would be, because both mud and flesh; the Word, because also earth at that time.” RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0802C] “The Word, flesh,” Franecker and Pamelius.].

For thus ran the Father’s preliminary address to the Son: “Let us make man in our image and likeness.” And God made man. That which he fashioned, of course, [Col. 0802C] he made in God’s image; that is, in the image of Christ. For the Word too is God, who, being constituted in the form of God, [Note: [Col. 0802D] “Did not consider it robbery to be made equal to God.” “The divinity of God and Lord—that is, of Father and Son—is equal,” says Gregory of Elvira in *On the Faith*; he then adds: “For the will of God and of any other, yet not God, cannot be equal.” RIG.] did not consider equality with God a robbery [Note: [Col. 0802C] “Equal,” Rhenanus; “equal things,” Gelenius; “to be equal,” others.].

Thus that mud, even then putting on the image of Christ who was to come in the flesh, was not merely God’s work but also his pledge. What purpose does it now serve, in order to darken the flesh’s origin, to bandy about the name “earth” [Note: [Col. 0802D] “Adam: man, or earthborn, or native, or red earth,” says Blessed Jerome in *On Hebrew Names*. EDD.] [Col. 0803A] as though it denoted an element filthy and prostrate? Even if some other material had been suitable for forming [Note: [Col. 0803C] “Hammering out,” Rhenanus.] man, it would still have been necessary to consider the eminence of the craftsman, who would have judged that material worthy by choosing it [Note: [Col. 0803C] Others add “in.”] and would have made it worthy by handling it.

The hands of Phidias, [Note: [Col. 0803C] “The Olympian Jupiter from ivory,” etc. Pliny, Book 34, chapter 8: “An almost countless multitude of artists,” he says, “won fame through smaller statues and images. Before them all, however, was Phidias the Athenian, who made the Olympian Jupiter, indeed from ivory, but also produced statues from bronze.” RHEN.] which fashioned the Olympian Jupiter from ivory, are adored [Note: [Col. 0803C] “It is fashioned and adored,” Pamelius and Rhenanus.]. It is no longer regarded as the tooth of an animal—and a particularly stupid animal at that—but as the supreme deity of the age, not because an elephant supplied it but because Phidias was so great.

Would the living and true God not have cleansed any baseness of material through his workmanship and cured it of every weakness? Or are we to conclude that man fashioned God more honorably than God fashioned man?

Now, even if the mud is a stumbling block, the flesh is already something else. I now have flesh in my grasp, not earth—even though the flesh too hears, “You are earth, and into earth you will go.” Its origin is recalled; its substance is not revoked. [Col. 0803B] It has been granted to become something nobler than its origin [Note: [Col. 0803C] The Pithoean manuscript inserts “in.”] and more fortunate through transformation. Gold too is earth, because it comes from earth; but only until it becomes gold. Once it is gold, it is an entirely different material, more splendid and noble, derived from a humbler [Note: [Col. 0803C] “More fading,” Rhenanus and Junius.] matrix.

[Note: [Col. 0803D] “Thus it was permissible for God too to refine the gold of the flesh, its material hammered free from what you consider the filth of mud.” One is said to be *excusatus* when placed outside the jurisdiction of a law, burden, or accusation—that is, when he has shown that his circumstances do not require him to be oppressed by some burden or accusation or to be assessed or held by some law. Thus “we excuse the mouth from food” means that we abstain, and “they excuse our neck from so great a yoke.” So too here the material of flesh would seem “excused” from the filth of mud, as though free of muddy filth; and in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, “the flower of virginity excuses human lust,” that is, refuses the stains of human lust. Nevertheless, we believe our Septimius had another meaning in mind. He says that both gold and flesh come from earth, but that both, perfected by the craftsman’s hands, pass from a humbler matrix into a far different, more splendid, and nobler material, so that they are no longer called earth but gold and flesh. Therefore, just as the craftsman was permitted to hammer gold from earth and so purify the material from the violence of its ore that at last [Col. 0804C] it advances to the most perfect excellence, so too the supreme craftsman of all, God, was permitted to hammer the gold of our flesh from mud, bake and refine it in the furnaces of divine powers, and assign it to the class of the noblest material, with that former class of earth or mud [Col. 0804D] hammered free from filth. He used *census* for material, of whatever class it may be reckoned. By *excusatum*, moreover, he meant “hammered out,” from *cudere* and *excudere*, from which come *cusare* and *excusare*, just as from *quatere*, *jacere*, and *premere* we form *quassare*, *jactare*, and *pressare*, and thence *quassatum*, *jactatum*, and *pressatum*, where elsewhere one finds *quassum*, *jactum*, and *pressum*. RIG.] Thus God too was permitted to refine the flesh’s gold, its material hammered free from what you consider the filth of mud.

## CHAPTER VII

But [Note: [Col. 0803C] Others add “lest.”] perhaps the dignity of the flesh may seem more diluted because the divine hand did not properly handle the flesh itself as it handled the mud. Yet since it handled the mud for the purpose that flesh might subsequently come from mud, it was surely conducting the business of the flesh [Note: [Col. 0803C] “For the flesh,” Rhenanus.].

But I would still have you learn when and how [Note: [Col. 0803C] “How and when,” Rhenanus.] the flesh blossomed [Note: [Col. 0803C] Others read “flowed.”] from the mud. [Note: [Col. 0804D] “For those coats of skins which Adam and Eve put on after being stripped of paradise were not, as some maintain, themselves the transformation of the mud into flesh.” He attacks the opinion of the Valentinians, who said that the human being created by God possessed the rank of spiritual substance, but that after he was expelled from the region of paradise a fleshly surface was woven over him, as Tertullian says in *Against the Valentinians*. Or, as Irenaeus said against them, he was clothed in a “leathern tunic,” *dermatinon chitōna* (δερμάτινον χιτῶνα). They called the flesh subject to sensation “this perceptible little flesh,” *touto de to aisthēton sarkion einai legousi* (τοῦτο δὲ τὸ αἰσθητόν σαρκίον εἶναι λέγουσι), as Irenaeus says in Epiphanius. RIG.] For those coats of skins which Adam [Col. 0804A] and Eve put on after being stripped of paradise were not, as some maintain, themselves the transformation of mud into flesh [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Leathern,” Semler and Pamelius.]. Some time earlier Adam had already recognized in the woman the flesh [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Of the woman,” Rhenanus and Semler.] propagated from his own substance [Note: [Col. 0804C] “As flesh,” Rhenanus and Semler.] [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Had known,” Franecker.]: “This now is bone from my bones and flesh from my flesh.” Moreover, the portion taken from the male was completed in the woman with flesh; it should, I suppose, have been completed with mud if Adam were still mud.

The mud, therefore, was obliterated and swallowed up into flesh. When? When the human being became a living soul through God’s breath, which was evidently a vapor [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Vaporous,” Rhenanus.] suitable [Note: [Col. 0804C] Rhenanus and the Pithoean manuscript insert “also.”] for baking, as it were, the mud into another quality—into flesh, just as clay is baked into pottery. In the same way, a potter may, by the measured breath of fire, reconstitute clay into a stronger material and form [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Contract,” Rhenanus and Semler.] from one thing another form, more suitable than the former and now possessing its own kind and name.

For although it is written (Rom. 9), [Col. 0804B] “Will the clay say [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Does the clay say,” others.] to the potter?”—that is, will man say to God?—and although the Apostle speaks of “earthen vessels” (2 Cor. 4:7), nevertheless man is clay because he was previously mud, and flesh is pottery because it was formed from mud through [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Through” is absent from Rigault and Venice.] the vapor of the divine breath. [Note: [Col. 0804D] “The flesh, which the coats of skins—that is, the skins laid over it—subsequently clothed.” Thus Septimius considers the coats of skins to have been literal skins, not, as the Valentinians held, the flesh itself. RIG.] The coats of skins—that is, the skins laid over it—subsequently clothed that flesh.

So true is this that, if you remove the skin, you have laid the flesh bare. Thus what today becomes a covering removed from the body was its garment when first laid over it. Hence the Apostle too, by calling circumcision “the stripping off of the flesh,” confirmed [Note: [Col. 0804C] “Affirmed,” Rhenanus and Semler.] that the skin was a tunic.

Since these things are so, you possess both mud made glorious by God’s hand and flesh made more glorious by God’s breath. By that breath the flesh at once laid aside the rudiments of mud and received the adornments of the soul.

You are not more attentive than God: you do not mount Scythian and Indian gems and the gleaming pearls of the Red [Col. 0805A] Sea in lead, bronze, or iron, [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Nor even encircle them with silver.” Rhenanus correctly conjectured this reading. He interprets *oblaquees* as “surround, encompass, enclose,” deriving the word from *lacus*; similarly, the following *descrobes* means “you insert,” from *scrobs*, “a pit.” The old reading *ferroginis* for *ferruginis* in the English and Vatican manuscripts should also be noted. PAM.] nor even encircle them with silver, but set them in the choicest and most elaborately worked gold. Likewise, for every most precious wine and ointment, you first ensure the suitability of its vessel [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Likewise, for wines” is not read in the Pithoean manuscript or Rhenanus.]. Similarly, you match the dignity of the sheath to swords of perfectly tempered iron [Note: [Col. 0805D] “A sword of perfectly tempered iron.” We emended this from the Pithoean manuscript; previously the reading was “of visibly tempered iron.” By “perfectly tempered iron” he means iron of perfect excellence, whose edge is pure—not only outstanding in the natural quality of its vein but also in its manufacture and in the nobility of the water into which it is repeatedly plunged while glowing. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Visible,” Franecker; “perfectly,” Rhenanus.] [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Iron,” Rhenanus.].

Yet is God supposed to have entrusted the shadow of his soul, the breath of his spirit, the working of his mouth, to some utterly worthless hilt [Note: [Col. 0805D] “Entrusted it to some hilt.” He calls the body the soul’s *capulum*, that is, its receptacle. RHEN.], and by placing it unworthily to have thereby condemned it? But did he merely place it there—or did he not rather implant and intermingle it with the flesh in so intimate a union that it may be considered uncertain whether the flesh carries the soul about or the soul the flesh, whether the flesh appears to the soul or the soul to the flesh [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Obeys,” Rhenanus and Semler.]?

Yet it must rather be believed that the soul is borne upon and rules the flesh, since it is nearer to God [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Even if,” Pamelius and Franecker.]. This too redounds to the flesh’s glory: it both contains the soul, which is nearest to God, [Col. 0805B] and makes the flesh itself a participant in the soul’s dominion.

For what use of nature, what fruit of the world, what savor of the elements does the soul not feed upon through the flesh? And why should it not, since through the flesh it is supported [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Is supported,” Rhenanus.] by every instrument of sensation—sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch? Through the flesh it is sprinkled with divine power, accomplishing everything through speech, even when speech is silently conceived beforehand. For speech too comes through an organ of the flesh; arts are through the flesh; studies and intellectual abilities through the flesh; works, business, and duties through the flesh. Thus indeed [Note: [Col. 0805D] “The soul’s entire living belongs to the flesh.” This is most truly said while the soul is united to the body, because it performs nothing except through the organs of the flesh. Indeed, intellection itself, which is, as it were, the soul’s peculiar possession, does not occur without dependence upon material species and images. LAC.] the soul’s entire living belongs to the flesh, [Note: [Col. 0805D] “So that the soul’s not living is nothing other than,” etc. This statement of the author is also true in that sense. For unless the soul acts through bodily ministries, it does nothing and is, as it were, [Col. 0806C] dead. Accordingly, when the material organs of the flesh are impeded, the soul becomes sluggish and unable to act. For this law or compact was established while flesh and soul are united: the soul can accomplish nothing without the flesh’s assistance. Moreover, by the soul’s “living” and “life,” Tertullian here understands the soul’s animation of the body and the life it causes in the body by that animation [Col. 0806D]. LAC.] so that for the soul not to live is nothing other than to depart from the flesh. [Col. 0805C] Likewise, even the actual dying belongs to the flesh, to which living also belongs.

Furthermore, if all things are subjected to the soul through the flesh, they must be subjected to the flesh as well [Note: [Col. 0805C] “Are subjected,” Rigault, Venice, and Semler. Perhaps “it must be subjected.” EDD.], since it is necessary that you use them through it [Note: [Col. 0806C] Pamelius, Rigault, and Venice add “since you use it.”]. Thus, although the flesh is reckoned the soul’s minister and servant, [Col. 0806A] it is found to be its partner and coheir. If it shares in temporal things, why not also in eternal things?
