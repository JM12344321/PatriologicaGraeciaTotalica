## PG 158.574

The author opens by calling Mohammed “thrice-accursed” and “most godless.” He says that Mohammed was called Machoumet by the Hagarenes who obeyed him, was regarded among them as a great prophet, and was honoured as God’s equal. He claims that Mohammed appeared during the reign of Heraclius, was born somewhere in Ethiopia to Ethiopian parents, and, having grown up, came to the state the author has described by advancing toward evil.

The story attributes this development to Gerasimos, a monk said to be an extreme Arian and Pneumatomachian [OCR damage affects the latter word], originally from Isauria. Gerasimos was allegedly raised in Constantinople and the Roman city, repeatedly urged by orthodox Christians to repudiate Arianism and other heresies, and refused. Although otherwise clever, he was supposedly exiled by the Orthodox to Coele Syria so that he would not become a stumbling block leading many to destruction.

Gerasimos lived in a cave beside an imperial road, begging to survive because he was hated. He especially pursued astronomy and prognostication. One day he saw, among travellers, a young Machoumet, driving a few camels from Mesopotamia toward Jerusalem for trade.

The narrative claims that Mohammed was orphaned while still very young [OCR: `νόος`, probably `νέος`], wandered from door to door, and lived on leftovers. A very rich widow named Fatouma saw him, took him in, reared him, and sent him daily to pasture her camels. When he was grown, she fell in love with him and, the author alleges, had an illicit sexual relationship with him. When this was exposed, she publicly made him her husband to avoid shame. She gave him some property and sent him trading.

The author says that she was distressed because Mohammed repeatedly suffered what the text calls being “moon-struck”—a term the narrative treats as seizures—and this disgraced her. She told him to ask everywhere whether he could find a physician to free him from it.

Mohammed met Gerasimos beside the road. They questioned one another: Gerasimos asked Mohammed’s city of origin, and Mohammed asked why Gerasimos waited there. Hearing that the monk practised medicine, Mohammed was delighted, described his condition, and, on learning that the monk claimed to be able to cure it, paid him expenses. He promised to take Gerasimos back from Jerusalem to his Mesopotamian home, for his own cure and for the treatment of many others, since the country lacked doctors; Gerasimos could thereby earn money and live comfortably and without pain.

At Jerusalem, the narrative says, Mohammed spoke with heretics, especially Jews and Nestorians, because he was very fond of learning, and he also encountered learned Jews.

## PG 158.575

According to the author, Jews taught him monotheism, circumcision, the names of the prophets, and Abraham’s noble ancestry. Pneumatomachians taught him opposition to the Spirit. Hemerobaptists taught daily washing, supposedly for remission of sins, by what the author calls a false interpretation of Isaiah’s “Wash yourselves and become clean.” Nestorians and Arians taught that Christ was not truly God but a great prophet sent for the world’s salvation. The author says Mohammed gathered these and other “seeds of impiety,” while still young and a fire-worshipper.

On his return, he met two Jewish physicians travelling to Mesopotamia. They said that they too would cure him, and, pleased by this promise, he continued with them. At Gerasimos’s cave he collected the monk under their agreement, placed him on a camel, and continued homeward. The monk taught him Arianism in full. The Jews made circumcision a condition or pact and persuaded him to choose their religion, on condition that they cure him.

After they arrived safely in Mohammed’s homeland, Mesopotamia, he housed the monk and the Jews near his own home, giving Gerasimos a tiny separate house close by. Disputes then broke out: Gerasimos and the Jews each insisted that Mohammed had been taught their religion. Gerasimos wanted to leave, but was asked to remain and direct what should be done.

The monk made a calculation or cast [*thematíon*, perhaps a horoscope] about Mohammed and found, the story says, that he would become great and prosper if he adopted a heresy and religion. Gerasimos told him that obedience to his advice would make him great very soon. Once Mohammed agreed, Gerasimos instructed him to explain his episodes of falling to the ground by saying that he saw the angel Gabriel coming and speaking to him, and that the angel’s dazzling face made him fall down.

The monk, according to the text, saw the locals as barbarous, superstitious, and ready to accept whatever nonsense he planned to tell them. He told the people present, who were watching the man whom the author calls demon-possessed, that Mohammed became inspired when he saw God and the angels. When Mohammed rose, he repeated the monk’s explanation that he fell because he saw an angel. Gerasimos called him a prophet and thereby deceived his listeners.

Mohammed’s wife worried that he would consume all her wealth, but Gerasimos reassured her that Machoumet was a great prophet. She believed him and, in the author’s misogynistic phrasing, spread the claim among the other women. The error then passed from women to men; the author says that the men, whom he calls barbarous and very ignorant, believed Mohammed to be God’s prophet.

Seeing his plan succeed, Gerasimos decided to give them a creed and a law, allegedly to establish Arianism and the other heresies for which he had been exiled. The author says he wrote a book called the Korran [Qur’an], “a law of God,” into which he planted all his own impiety. It allegedly denied that God has Word or Spirit, denied Christ’s divinity while calling him a great prophet, and added many other foolish claims. Gerasimos gave the book to Mohammed and announced to the people that Gabriel had brought it down from heaven to Machoumet while he slept. The people believed this account, as the monk had arranged the new law.

The Jewish physicians then became displeased with Mohammed and refused to cure him because, the text says, he had followed the monk’s instructions rather than their religion. To satisfy them too, Mohammed adopted Jewish practices: circumcision and an unclear phrase probably concerning the naming/invocation of prophets. Concerning the writings of Moses and the other prophets, he did what seemed right to the monk.

Once the locals had accepted Mohammed’s new law, they found themselves without a governing authority; at the monk’s instigation they made Mohammed their leader, so that he was at once lawgiver, prophet, and king. He formed a military force and raided neighbouring towns.

Heraclius heard of this and sent an army. When the Romans arrived, they killed Mohammed’s followers wholesale. Mohammed fled with a few others and sent an embassy to the emperor, styling himself the emperor’s slave. He asked to be allowed to reside in one part of Ethiopia while paying tribute; Heraclius agreed.

After Heraclius died, disturbances in the Roman empire gave Mohammed the opportunity to raid Syria. He grew from little, or nothing, to great power; the author attributes this to “our sins” and says he destroyed most of the Roman empire. His successors, the author adds, utterly ruined, destroyed, and devastated the rest. The narrative closes by calling this its complete “truth” in summary.
