I. After the character of prophets and the fruits of their visions—from which, in controversies of this kind, the strongest presumption has always arisen—the first indication of a prophetic Spirit is derived from the fulfillment of oracles. For if oracles lack fulfillment, doubt immediately enters the mind as to whether the Holy Spirit inspired the prophet’s mind, or whether there was instead either a deception by a malignant spirit or an error of human perception. The frequently mentioned Asterius convicts the spirit of Maximilla [Col. 0152D] of falsehood chiefly by this argument: that the outcome did not correspond to the prophecies. For although she had predicted that wars and disturbances would be imminent after her death, both the empire and the Christian commonwealth enjoyed a long peace. In the same book also, says Eusebius, speaking of Asterius’s writings against the Montanists [Note: [Col. 0152D] “Again in the same work, after interposing various other remarks to refute Maximilla’s false prophecies, he indicates both the time at which he wrote these things and recalls her predictions, by which he censures her falsehood, speaking as follows: ‘And how has this lie not already become manifest? For more than thirteen years have passed, down to the present day, since the woman died, and neither a local nor a universal war has occurred in the world; rather, by the mercy of God, a lasting peace has prevailed especially for Christians.’ These things, too, come from the second treatise.” Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 16.], “[Col. 0153A] After inserting several remarks to refute that false prophecy of Maximilla, he both specifies the very time at which these things were written and recalls that woman’s predictions, in which she had foretold that wars and disturbances were imminent. He refutes the falsehood of those predictions in these words: ‘Has not,’ he says, ‘this lie also become manifest to everyone? For from Maximilla’s death until the present day more than thirteen years have now elapsed; and yet there has been no war on earth, either local or universal. On the contrary, by a certain exceptional mercy, a stable and enduring peace has remained for Christians.’ And these words are found in the second book.”

By contrast, the Church of Smyrna demonstrates that Polycarp [Note: [Col. 0153D] “An apostolic and prophetic teacher . . . for every word that he uttered from his mouth both was fulfilled and will be fulfilled.” Encyclical Epistle on the Martyrdom of Polycarp.] “was an apostolic and prophetic teacher” from the fact that “every word which he uttered from his mouth [Col. 0153B] either had been fulfilled or awaited fulfillment.” By the same argument we too shall demonstrate that our martyrs were filled with a prophetic spirit. For everything revealed to them, and every word which they uttered from their mouths, *was fulfilled* (ἐτελειώθη), although these things belonged to the class whose foreknowledge pertains to divine wisdom alone—namely, future contingencies, especially those which depend upon the free will of God himself or of human beings.

While the matter was still uncertain—whether there would be a passion or a reprieve—Perpetua understood from her first revelation that there would be a passion; and the passion actually followed. Nor did the martyrs merely suffer; they suffered also in the order in which Perpetua had foreseen that they would. For Perpetua saw Saturus ascend the ladder first [Col. 0153C]; and Saturus was in fact the first to complete his martyrdom, as the author of the Passion observes: “Saturus, who had also ascended the ladder first, was the first to render up his spirit. For he was also waiting for Perpetua.”

The procurator Hilarianus had already pronounced sentence upon them all and condemned them to the beasts when, in another revelation, the same holy Perpetua learned that she would fight, as she says, not against the beasts but against the devil; that is, her final and most severe contest would not be against the beasts, but against the devil, with whom she had seemed to wrestle in the arena. The event occurred just as it had been foreseen. When exposed to the beasts, she experienced no pain from them; but she suffered most bitterly when, pierced between the ribs by the gladiator, she cried aloud. Afterward, by offering him her throat, she triumphed over the devil, who had attacked her through the agency [Col. 0153D] of the gladiator.

Now I ask whether Basnage concedes that Perpetua wrote these revelations in her own hand, as the Acts state, or whether he denies it altogether. For if he does not believe that they were written by Perpetua, why does he not attribute to the author of the Passion, as to an impostor, everything in them that offends his mind, instead of accusing the holy martyrs themselves on account of things fraudulently attributed to them by another? But if he concedes that Perpetua committed her revelations to writing, then, since he sees that they were confirmed by the outcomes [Col. 0154A] of subsequent events, why does he call into doubt that the Holy Spirit was their author?

And not only Perpetua, but also the other fellow-soldiers in the same contest, can readily be shown by the same argument to have been illumined by prophetic light. For each obtained the kind of contest which he had desired and perhaps had foreknown would occur. On this matter the writer of the Acts must be heard:

“But he who had said, ‘Ask, and you shall receive,’ gave to those who asked the end that each had desired. For whenever they spoke among themselves about what they wished for in their martyrdom, Saturninus declared that he wished to be exposed to all the beasts, so that he might wear a more glorious crown. Accordingly, at the opening of the spectacle, he and Revocatus encountered the leopard [Col. 0154B], and on the platform they were also mauled by the bear. Saturus, however, detested nothing more than a bear; but he already felt confident that he would be finished by a single bite of the leopard. Accordingly, when a boar was brought out, it was instead the huntsman who had supplied that boar who was gored by the same beast, and he died after the days of the games. Saturus was merely dragged about. And when he had been tied down on the bridge for the bear, the bear refused to come out of its cage. Thus Saturus was recalled unharmed for a second time.”

These desires of the martyrs were not merely ordinary wishes, but, so to speak, prophetic wishes, as the words of Saturus which the author adds shortly afterward plainly demonstrate:

“Likewise Saturus, at another gate, was exhorting the soldier Pudens, saying: ‘Here I am, just as I promised and predicted. Up to this moment I have felt no beast at all. And now believe with your whole heart. See, I go forth [Col. 0154C] there, and I am finished by a single bite of the leopard.’”

The event corresponded to the prophecy. For, as the author of the Passion adds:

“Immediately, at the end of the spectacle, he was exposed to the leopard, and by a single bite he was so drenched in blood that, as he returned, the people shouted back to him in testimony of a second baptism: ‘Saved and washed! Saved and washed!’ Plainly he was indeed saved, who had become illustrious through this spectacle.”

But even if nothing prophetic had occurred here, who, upon attending to that marvelous providence of God toward the martyrs and toward the fulfillment of their wishes, does not immediately understand how pleasing to God their confession was, and therefore their faith as well—since, as the Apostle says, without faith it is impossible to please God?

II. There is also another argument of very great weight, from which the highest authority accrues to prophets. It is drawn [Col. 0154D] from the miracles which divine omnipotence manifested either through them or, on their behalf, directly by itself. These miracles—with which there are no Acts of martyrs that do not abound—were not lacking for our martyrs either, as is already evident from what has just been said. For it did not occur without a miracle that the boar sent against Saturus turned its rage and attack instead upon the huntsman himself, or that the bear incited against Saturus refused to come out of its cage.

Here there is less reason to doubt the reliability of the Acts than in passages dealing with visions, since they report events performed openly and in the middle of the amphitheater—events which no writer of those times, however shameless, would have dared to fabricate.

It must also be counted among the miracles that, while Perpetua [Col. 0155A] was being mauled by a most savage cow, she was so deeply in ecstasy that she experienced no sensation of pain. Hence she “did not believe that what had already occurred had occurred until she recognized certain marks of the assault upon her body and clothing.” This too happened openly, in the middle of the arena, while everyone looked on in amazement.

Nor—lest perhaps Basnage draw so marvelous an event into suspicion of Montanism—is the event without parallel in other martyrs who are free from every suspicion of that kind. Entirely parallel to this passage of our Acts is another concerning the blessed Blandina in the Epistle of the Christians of Lyons, where they relate how she too [Note: [Col. 0155D] “At last she was enclosed in a net and thrown before a bull. After she had been tossed high into the air for a considerable time by the animal, she no longer had any perception whatever of what was happening, because of her hope and possession of the things believed, and because of her communion with Christ; and she too was finally sacrificed.”] “was enclosed in a net and exposed to a bull. After being tossed high into the air by it for a long time, she no longer felt anything at all of what was being done, both because of her hope and apprehension of the good things in which she believed, and because of the intimate communion [Col. 0155B] which she had with Christ in prayer; at last she too was slain like a sacrificial victim.”

We have another remarkable example, in the same Blandina, of the removal of the sensation of pain amid punishments. As it is written in the aforementioned Epistle of the Christians of Lyons [Note: [Col. 0155D] “But the blessed woman, like a noble athlete, renewed her youth in the confession; and her recovery, rest, and freedom from pain amid what was happening consisted in saying, ‘I am a Christian, and among us nothing wicked is done.’”], “Like a noble athlete, she recovered her strength and courage in the very act of confession. Her refreshment and rest, and the thing that removed every sensation of her present pain, was the utterance of these words: ‘I am a Christian, and among us nothing evil is done.’”

No less remarkable is what ecclesiastical histories have handed down concerning Theodore [Note: [Col. 0155D] Rufinus, *Ecclesiastical History*, book X, chapter 36. Saint Augustine, *The City of God*, book XVIII, chapter 52. Socrates, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, chapters 18 and 19. Sozomen, book V, chapters 19 and 20. Theodoret, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, chapters 10 and 11.]. By order of Sallustius, praetorian prefect under Julian, he was tortured for a long time and with extreme cruelty; yet amid the torments, with a cheerful and serene countenance, he repeatedly recited that verse of the Psalm: “Let all who worship carved images and who trust in their idols be confounded.”

“We ourselves,” says Rufinus, “later saw this [Col. 0155C] Theodore at Antioch; and when we asked him whether he had felt the pain, he said plainly that he had indeed felt the pains only slightly, but that a certain young man had stood beside him and, as he perspired, wiped away his sweat with a cloth of dazzling whiteness and frequently poured cold water upon him. He had been so delighted by this that he became sadder at the moment when he was ordered to be taken down from the rack.”

Finally, Saint Flavianus, of whom mention has been made more than once above, himself relates this revelation of his [Note: [Col. 0155D] In Ruinart, *Acts of Saints Montanus, Lucius, etc.*, § 21.]:

“When our bishop alone had thus far suffered, this was shown to me: it was as though I were asking Cyprian himself whether it was painful to endure the blows. Since I was to become a martyr, I was seeking his advice about enduring the passion. He answered me and said: ‘It is another flesh that suffers [Col. 0155D] when the mind is in heaven. This body feels nothing at all when the whole mind has devoted itself to God.’”

To these [Col. 0156A] words of the holy martyr, the compiler of the Acts adds:

“O words of a martyr encouraging a martyr! He denied that there was pain in the blow of the passion, so that the one who himself was destined to be killed might be animated with greater constancy, because he need not fear even the slightest sensation of pain in the blow of the passion.”

Saint Augustine readily persuaded himself that Perpetua’s mind, no less than Cyprian’s, was in heaven when, exposed to the beasts and mauled by them, she experienced no sensation of pain. He illuminates this passage of the Acts in these words [Note: [Col. 0156D] Sermon 280, formerly 103, *On Various Subjects*.]:

“Where was that woman when she did not feel herself fighting against the most savage cow, and when she asked whether that which had already happened was still going to happen? Where was she? What was she seeing, that she did not see these things? What was she enjoying, that she did not feel these things? By what love was she carried away from herself, by what spectacle was she drawn aside, by what cup was she made drunk, etc.?”

[Col. 0156B] The premature delivery of Felicitas must also be numbered among the miracles. For although it is not absolutely a miracle for a woman to bring forth her child in difficult labor, the circumstances accompanying Felicitas’s delivery plainly suggest that it occurred through the special providence of God, and not according to the common and ordinary laws of nature. For immediately after the prayer which the martyrs had poured out to God that he might hasten the delivery, “the pains came upon her.” On this subject the same celebrated Saint Augustine must be heard [Note: [Col. 0156D] Sermon 281, otherwise *On Various Subjects*, 104.]:

“Finally, the delivery was timely though the month was untimely. For it was divinely brought about that the burden of her womb should be laid down before its proper time, lest the honor of martyrdom be postponed beyond its proper time. It was divinely brought about, I say, that the child should be brought forth on a day not due to it, provided that Felicitas, who was due to so great a company, [Col. 0156C] should be restored to it, etc.”

Finally, it does not appear to have occurred without a miracle that, when her father, enraged beyond measure, refused to return Perpetua’s infant so that he might remain with her in prison as before and be nursed by her, no pain came either upon the child through the withdrawal of milk or upon the mother through the inflammation of her breasts:

“Then, because the infant had been accustomed to take the breasts from me and to remain with me in prison, I immediately sent the deacon Pomponius to my father, asking for the infant. But my father refused to give him. And as God willed, neither did the child desire the breasts any longer, nor did they cause inflammation in me, so that I should not be tormented by anxiety for the infant and by pain in my breasts.”

If anyone is unwilling to regard all these things as true miracles, he must at least admit that they display the special [Col. 0156D] providence of divine goodness toward God’s martyrs. As for the credibility of the events, the very simplicity of the narrative—which is especially evident in Perpetua’s words—does not permit doubt.

III. The arguments which we have pursued thus far show generally that Perpetua and her fellow martyrs were by no means lacking in the prophetic spirit. Those which I shall now add plainly demonstrate how exceedingly far those same holy martyrs stood from the doctrines and institutions of the Montanists.

The opinion of the Montanists, as has frequently been stated from Tertullian, was that before the day of the solemn judgment, martyrs alone are received into heaven. Tertullian confirms this opinion [Col. 0157A] in his book *On the Soul* by the authority of Perpetua, “that most courageous martyr,” who, he says, “as the day of her passion approached, in a revelation of the glory of Paradise, saw there only her fellow martyrs.”

But Tertullian either is plainly mistaken or is misleading his readers. For Perpetua does not say that she saw only martyrs in Paradise; indeed, she clearly and openly testifies to the opposite: “And there we began to recognize many brethren, and martyrs as well.” Thus Perpetua—or rather Saturus—saw there not only fellow martyrs, but also fellow Christians, and not a few of them. For Saturus received this revelation and committed it to writing in his own hand.

The reason why they make special mention of martyrs is readily apparent. At the very threshold of Paradise they had encountered four martyrs who had been consummated shortly before in the same persecution under Severus [Col. 0157B]. When they asked them about the others, they were admonished by the angels that they should first enter and greet the Lord:

“There we found Jocundus, Saturninus, and Artaxius, who had suffered in the same persecution and had been burned alive; and Quintus, who had also departed from prison as a martyr. And we asked them where the others were. But the angels said to us: ‘Come first, enter, and greet the Lord.’”

When these things had been done, they testify that they saw many brethren, and specifically martyrs as well, in order to declare more plainly and clearly that their desire had been fulfilled. Nor should it seem surprising if a mind rejoicing in the martyr’s robe of white and itself approaching martyrdom speaks particularly and distinctly of the reward of martyrs. For the mind gladly recalls those things toward which it is affectionately disposed, and employs them often [Col. 0157C] and repeatedly.

IV. There is another feature which I would note in this vision of Saturus, and it seems to carry no small weight in vindicating the orthodoxy of our martyrs against Basnage. He maintains that the Montanists had no association with Catholics, even with Catholic martyrs, but shunned all communion with them because they held them up to mockery and contempt and denounced them as “psychics.”

If that is so, we shall have to accuse of Montanism not only Saturus, Perpetua, and their fellow martyrs, but also Jocundus, Saturninus, Artaxius, Quintus, and the other martyrs for whom they were asking and whose glory they afterward saw, who had already suffered in the same persecution under Severus. For Saturus and Perpetua [Col. 0157D] would have asked only for martyrs belonging to their own communion. We must therefore either say that all the aforementioned martyrs belonged to the flock of the Montanists, or enroll them all alike in the list of Catholic martyrs. But who could patiently and calmly endure that, on account of certain slight conjectures which occurred to Basnage, so many martyrs should be erased from the Catholic diptychs and the Montanist rolls enriched with their names?

V. But to continue the argument begun above and to demonstrate still more clearly from Perpetua’s revelations that, contrary to Montanist teaching, it was not only martyrs who were placed in heaven, we must recall that vision of the holy martyr in which she understood that her brother Dinocrates had been transferred [Col. 0158A] out of punishment. For Perpetua describes the place and glory to which he had been transferred in words which plainly indicate that he was received into the same place and glory in which Saturus afterward saw the martyrs, as is manifestly evident from a comparison of the two visions.

Perpetua speaks thus of her vision:

“I see that the place which I had seen as dark is now bright, and Dinocrates is there, with a clean body, well clothed, and taking refreshment. And where the wound had been, I see a scar. The pool which I had seen before had its rim lowered as far as the boy’s navel, and he drew water from it without ceasing. Above its rim was a bowl full of water. Dinocrates approached and began to drink from it, and the bowl did not become empty. When he had drunk his fill, he departed from the water and began joyfully to play in the manner of children [Col. 0158B].”

Entirely consonant with these things is what Saturus afterward saw:

“And we saw,” he says, “nearby a place whose walls were as though built of light. Before the entrance of that place stood four angels, who clothed those entering with white robes. And when we had been clothed, we entered and saw an immeasurable light, etc. And the elders said to us: ‘Go and play.’ And I said: ‘Perpetua, you have what you desire.’ She said to me: ‘Thanks be to God; just as I was cheerful in the flesh, I am even more cheerful here now.’”

And below:

“And while we were speaking with them, the angels said to them: ‘Allow them to refresh themselves. . . .’ We were all nourished by an indescribable fragrance which satisfied us.”

How well Perpetua’s revelations concerning the glory of Dinocrates and Saturus’s concerning that of the martyrs harmonize with one another! The place in which Perpetua saw Dinocrates was bright; the walls of the place [Col. 0158C] in which Saturus saw the martyrs were as though built of light. Dinocrates had a clean body and was well clothed; the angels clothed the martyrs in white robes. Dinocrates, having drunk his fill, played in the manner of children; the elders encouraged Perpetua and Saturus to play. Perpetua saw Dinocrates refreshing himself; of Perpetua and Saturus the angels said: “Allow them to refresh themselves.” Dinocrates drank from the water without ceasing and was, as it were, made drunk by that draught; Perpetua and Saturus were nourished by an indescribable fragrance which marvelously satisfied them.

Who can deny that the same glory was represented under such closely corresponding symbols and figures? Who can deny that the water for which Dinocrates at first panted, which he afterward drank without ceasing, by which he was satisfied, and which never failed him, foreshadowed that perennial fountain of eternal life and the torrent of delight of which the prophet once sang [Note: [Col. 0158D] Psalm XXXIII, 10.]:

“They shall be made drunk from the abundance of your house, and you shall give them drink from the torrent of your delight; for with you is the fountain of life, and in your light we shall see light”?

By these words he surely described no other glory and happiness than that of heaven—that is, the glory and happiness of blessed eternity and the clear vision of God.

Since, therefore, Dinocrates did not die a martyr, it is entirely false when Tertullian says that Perpetua saw only martyrs in Paradise. It is false when Basnage says that, in accordance with Montanist opinion, Perpetua placed all souls except those of martyrs [Col. 0159A] outside Paradise, in a dark place. And finally it will be false that Perpetua and those martyrs who joined her as companions in death had come from the school of the Montanists and were imbued with their doctrines and discipline.

VI. From the same vision of Saturus there arises another inference by which I may readily establish that Perpetua and her fellow martyrs were exceedingly far removed from communion with the Montanists, but both lived in the peace and unity of the Catholic Church and shed their blood gloriously for Christ:

“And we went out,” says Saturus, “and before the doors we saw Bishop Optatus on the right and Aspasius, the presbyter and teacher, on the left, separated and sorrowful. They threw themselves at our feet and said to us: ‘Reconcile us, because you have departed and leave us thus.’ And we said to them: ‘Are you not our pope, [Col. 0159B] and are you not a presbyter? Why do you cast yourselves at our feet?’ And we threw ourselves down and embraced them. . . . And while we were speaking with them, the angels said to them: ‘Allow these people to refresh themselves; and if you have any disagreements among yourselves, forgive one another.’ And they disturbed them. And they said to Optatus: ‘Correct your people, because they assemble before you as though returning from the circus and quarreling over their factions.’”

Basnage makes this Optatus a bishop of the Carthaginian Montanists and Aspasius a presbyter of the same sect, and he believes that one or the other composed the Acts of our martyrs.

By contrast, the most learned Pagi, drawing upon an observation supplied to him by the distinguished abbé Louis de Four, teaches that Optatus was bishop of the Carthaginians—that is, of course, of the Catholics—and the successor [Col. 0159C] of Agrippinus, and that the list of those bishops in Schelstrate’s dissertation *On the African Church* must be supplemented from these Acts. I consider this opinion altogether to be followed, and I confirm it from the fact that there appears to have been no Montanist bishop at Carthage at that time. Tertullian certainly never mentions any such person in his books; indeed, in several passages he indicates most clearly that there was then none.

I seem to myself to infer this with the greatest clarity from all those passages in which he presses the authority of traditions against heretics. For he maintains that Christ communicated his doctrine to the Apostles; that the Apostles communicated it to apostolic men whom they appointed bishops in the individual cities; and that these men communicated it to their successors—that is, to those who afterward succeeded the apostolic men by legitimate ordination. [Col. 0159D] He lays down that only those churches should be regarded as true and apostolic which, in compiling their records, carried the catalogue of their bishops back either to one of the Apostles or to one of the apostolic men who had nevertheless persevered with the Apostles; or which, if founded later, had received the transmission of the faith from apostolic churches and had never thereafter fallen away from their communion. Of this kind were the African churches, which traced their origin back to the Roman Church.

He challenges the heretics to disclose the origins of their churches and either carry the catalogue of their bishops back to the Apostles or to apostolic men, or show that their first bishop was given to them by apostolic churches together with the seed of the faith. Thus he argues against the heretics [Col. 0160A] both in his book *On Prescription against Heretics* [Note: [Col. 0160D] Chapter 32 and following.] and in the fourth book *Against Marcion*, where, among other things, he assails the Marcionites in these words [Note: [Col. 0160D] Chapter 5.]:

“He certainly has churches too, but churches of his own, as late as they are adulterous. If you inquire into their register, you will more readily find it apostatic than apostolic, with Marcion, of course, as founder, or someone from Marcion’s swarm. Wasps too make honeycombs; the Marcionites too make churches.”

Tertullian certainly would never have used this argument if the Carthaginian Montanists had possessed at that time a single bishop separated from the Catholic bishop. For nothing would have been more straightforward and easy than to turn that argument back upon its author and press the Montanists to publish the catalogue of their bishops, so that it might be seen whether they perhaps owed their origin to the Apostles, to apostolic men, or at least [Col. 0160B] to apostolic churches, or rather to Montanus or someone from Montanus’s swarm.

But Tertullian, as we have shown above, did not yet consider himself cut off from the communion of the apostolic churches, and he thought that Catholics and Montanists were combined into one body of the Church, governed in its different members by different institutions. Nor did he believe that diversity of disciplines, provided the rule of faith remained intact, harmed the unity of the churches. Hence those words of his when speaking of the apostolic churches: “We are one Church [Note: [Col. 0160D] *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 2.].” But how could they be one Church if they had erected chair against chair and appointed bishop against bishop?

Yet just as Tertullian, when disputing against heretics, exalts the order of bishops, so in [Col. 0160C] his books against Catholics he depreciates it and places it almost on a level with the laity. When disputing against heretics, he recognizes no true Church without apostolic bishops; yet in his books against Catholics he locates the Church in three laymen. Thus, in the book *Exhortation to Chastity* [Note: [Col. 0160D] Chapter 7.]: “Where three are, there is a Church, even though they are laymen.” And in the book *On Flight in Persecution* [Note: [Col. 0160D] Chapter 14.]: “You cannot go about to every individual; let the Church for you consist even in three.” And in the book *On Modesty* [Note: [Col. 0160D] Chapter 21.]: “The Church is not the number of bishops.”

As I considered why he blurted out propositions so contrary to one another in these books, only one explanation occurred to me. Since the Catholic bishops were repelling the Montanists from communion, while they themselves had no other bishop of their own sect, he devised this for his own consolation: that by divine law the clergy possess no authority over the laity [Col. 0160D], no authority which could bind laymen in a case of necessity. In this way laymen separated from communion by the clergy became clergy for themselves, priests for themselves, and could both baptize and offer for themselves. Consequently, no obligation remained to subject themselves thereafter to the discretion of the clergy for the sake of receiving the sacraments.

“Are not we laymen also priests?” he says in the cited passage of the *Exhortation to Chastity*. “It is written: ‘He has also made us a kingdom and priests to God and his Father’ [Col. 0161A]. The authority of the Church has established the distinction between the order and the people, and the honor has been sanctified by the assembly of the order. Thus, where there is no assembly of the ecclesiastical order, you both offer and baptize, and you alone are a priest for yourself. But where three are, there is a Church, even though they are laymen. For each person lives by his own faith, and there is no partiality with God, etc. Therefore, if you possess the right of a priest within yourself wherever necessity exists, you must also possess the discipline of a priest wherever it is necessary to possess the right of a priest. Do you, being twice married, baptize? Do you, being twice married, offer? How much more is it a capital offense for a layman who has married twice to act as a priest, when even a priest himself, after marrying twice, is denied the exercise of the priesthood?”

Is this not an excellent indication that there was then no bishop of the Montanists at Carthage? For if the authority of the episcopal order must be pressed [Col. 0161B] against heretics, the registers of the Catholics are brought forward. But if battle must be joined on behalf of the Montanists against the Catholics, the authority of bishops is despised and the Church is located in three laymen.

Therefore, Bishop Optatus, who is mentioned in the Acts of our martyrs, is wrongly said to have been bishop of the Carthaginian Montanists; rather, he presided over the Catholic communion. This is also confirmed by those words of the angels in which he is ordered to correct his people: “Because,” they say, “they assemble before you as though returning from the circus and quarreling over their factions.” These words do not properly apply to someone who presided over a sect consisting of a few people, as that of the Montanists then was in Africa, but to one who governed a people and a multitude, in which [Col. 0161C] the Catholics justly gloried, as we learn from Tertullian himself [Note: [Col. 0161D] *On Fasts*, chapter 11.].

I now think it is clear from this argument that Perpetua, Saturus, and their fellow martyrs belonged to the Catholic communion and not to the flock of the Montanists: for their Catholic bishop cares for them, hastens to them, communicates with them, casts himself at their feet, converses with them, and finally implores their aid; and the martyrs themselves expressly call him their pope and bishop.

VII. Finally, an inference in favor of the holy martyrs arises both from the Church’s concern that they should be treated splendidly and generously in prison, and from the readiness with which the martyrs themselves accepted the refreshment offered to them. They were entirely free from that moroseness which is generally especially conspicuous in hypocrites and heretics—particularly [Col. 0161D] those who boast of the severity of their morals and the harshness of their discipline, as the Montanists did.

The passages of the Acts which bear upon this matter should be noted. Chapter I, § 2:

“Thereupon Tertius and Pomponius, the blessed deacons who ministered to us, arranged by payment that, after being released for a few hours, we might refresh ourselves in a better part of the prison.”

Chapter III, § 1:

“Then, after a few days, Pudens, the soldier who was an *optio* and was in charge of the prison, began to hold us in great esteem, understanding that there was great power in us. He admitted many brethren to us, so that both we and they might refresh one another.”

Chapter V, § 3:

“When they were being treated more harshly by the tribune because, [Col. 0162A] owing to the warnings of exceedingly foolish men, he feared that they might be removed from prison by some magical incantations, Perpetua answered him to his face and said: ‘Why, in any case, do you not permit the most illustrious condemned persons—Caesar’s prisoners, of course, who are to fight on his birthday—to refresh themselves? Is it not to your glory if we are brought out in better condition on that occasion?’ The tribune shuddered and blushed, and therefore ordered that they be treated more humanely, so that her brethren and the others should be given permission to enter and refresh themselves with them, the *optio* of the prison himself now also being a believer.”

And chapter V, § 4:

“On the preceding day also, at that final supper which they call the free meal, so far as it lay in their power, they celebrated not a free meal but an *agape*, etc.”
