Someone may well say so. But what are the “processions” (*pompae*) mentioned afterward by Minucius, which Christians avoided no less than the spectacles? *Pompa* is called πομπή in Greek, from the verb πέμπω, which in Latin means “I send,” “lead forth,” or “convey.” It was a solemn public procession and conveyance through the streets of cities, customarily held at triumphs, supplications, and other solemnities, especially the circus games. [Col. 0538A] In it, chosen men advanced, each in his proper order, together with flute-players and others; at the very end, images of the gods were carried. Hence Macrobius says: “Images of the gods are borne on a carrying-frame in the procession of the circus games” (Macrob., *Saturnalia*, book I, ch. 23). Tertullian likewise says: “The ceremonial apparatus of the circus games is somewhat more pompous. These games, to which this name properly belongs, are preceded by a procession; whose procession it is, it proves in itself by the sequence of idols, the train of images, the chariots, the sacred cars, the covered wagons, the seats, the crowns, and the spoils. Moreover, how many rites and sacrifices precede, intervene, and follow; how many colleges, priesthoods, and offices are set in motion—the people of that city know, in which the assembly of demons has established its seat” (Tertullian, *On the Spectacles*, ch. 7, p. [Col. 0538B] 93). He reported certain further details elsewhere. But you will find all these matters described individually and graphically by Dionysius of Halicarnassus (the same, *On Idolatry*, ch. 11, p. 110; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities*, book VII, p. 475 ff.). We have also made some notes on them in our annotations on certain books of Ambrose (Ambrose, *Exposition of Psalm 118*, sermon V, and *On Duties*, book I, ch. 18, § 73, p. 21).

Although from this, and especially from Tertullian’s words, it is easy to understand why those spectacles of the pagans were forbidden to Christians, our author nevertheless gives certain specific reasons for this prohibition: “For at the chariot games,” he says, “who would not shudder at the madness of the people quarreling among themselves; at the gladiatorial games, [Col. 0538C] the training in homicide; and even on the stage, no less frenzy and more protracted obscenity?” In the royal manuscript the reading is “no less in frenzy” (*non minor furore*), a sense by no means absurd. However that may be, Tertullian had preceded our author in his *Apologeticum*, where we similarly read: “We have nothing to do with the madness of the circus, the shamelessness of the theater, the savagery of the arena, or the vanity of the wrestling-ground.” Again elsewhere he said: “Why do you not frequent those solemn pleasures—the raging circus, the savage amphitheater, and the wanton stage?” Finally, in yet another book: “Even the faithful man perishes when he slips away into the spectacle of chariot-racing frenzy, gladiatorial bloodshed, theatrical foulness, and athletic vanity; into the games and banquets of a secular solemnity; into the service and ministry of another’s idolatry” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, [Col. 0538D] ch. 38, p. 34; the same, *Against Marcion*, book I, ch. 27, p. 451; *On Modesty*, ch. 7, p. 722). Hence Jerome writes the following to Marcella: “Let Rome keep her riots to herself; let the arena rage, the circus run mad, and the theaters luxuriate.” For “one who believes in the Lord Jesus,” as he said elsewhere of Saint Hilarion, “takes no delight in the frenzies of the circus, the blood of the arena, or the licentiousness of the theater” (Jerome, Epistle 45, to Marcella, p. 554; and *Life of Hilarion*, p. 75).

Moreover, in the already cited book *On the Spectacles*, after Tertullian had made clear to everyone what the circus was, why it was so named, and what took place there, he then clearly explained what these words of our author—“the madness of the people quarreling among themselves”—mean: [Col. 0539A] “When frenzy,” he says, “is forbidden to us, we are excluded from every spectacle, even from the circus, where frenzy properly presides. Look at the people coming to the spectacle already in a frenzy, already tumultuous, already blind, already excited by their wagers. The praetor is too slow for them; their eyes are forever rolling about with the lots in his urn. Then they hang anxiously upon the signal; one cry comes from a single madness. I recognize madness from its vanity: ‘He has sent it!’ they say, and they announce to one another what was seen simultaneously by everyone. I have proof of their blindness. They do not see what has been sent: they think it is the napkin, but it is the figure of the devil cast down from on high”—another reading has “gluttony” (*gula*). “From that point they proceed into rages, passions, dissensions, and everything forbidden to the priests of peace. Hence curses and insults [Col. 0539B] without justice, and even votes of hatred without any deserved ground for love” (Tertullian, *On the Spectacles*, ch. 8, p. 93; ibid., ch. 16, p. 97). He adds more, which you should not begrudge the effort to read. You may also consult what he added about those same spectacles, where, like Martial, Suetonius, and others, he mentioned the napkin (*mappa*), which, according to some, the praetor customarily sent to the victor, or, as others prefer, which served as the signal for beginning the games (Juvenal, end of Satire 11; Martial, book XII, Epigram 29; Suetonius, *Life of Nero*, § 22). The author of the iambic poem published among the works of Gregory Nazianzen describes in detail the frenzy of the circus—its anger, quarrels, insults, hatred, fights, wounds, and killings (Gregory Nazianzen, *Iambic Poem* 3, p. 292).

[Col. 0539C] The “training in homicide” which Minucius next notes “at the gladiatorial games” is explained more clearly by Cyprian: “A gladiatorial show,” says that most holy martyr, “is prepared, so that blood may delight the lust of cruel eyes. The body is filled out into flesh with richer foods, and the robust mass of its limbs grows fat with layers of grease, so that, fattened for punishment, the man may perish at a higher price. A human being is killed for a human being’s pleasure; and the ability to kill is a skill, a practice, an art. Crime is not merely committed but also taught. What can be called more inhuman or more cruel? It is training, so that someone may be able to perish; and glory, because he kills” (Cyprian, *To Donatus*, p. 5). See further, if you please, Tatian, Arnobius, Lactantius, Prudentius, and what we have observed elsewhere about those gladiatorial contests [Col. 0539D] (Tatian, *Oration Against the Greeks*, p. 161; Arnobius, book II, p. 72; Lactantius, book VI, ch. 20, p. 617; Prudentius, *Against Symmachus*, book II, p. 429; in the hymn on Saints Fructuosus, Augurius, etc., p. 135; and *Hamartigenia*, p. 322; vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 800).

Finally, concerning theatrical obscenities, Cyprian likewise expounds somewhat more fully what our author said: “In the theaters,” he says, “you will see what must cause you both grief and shame. The tragic buskin recounts the crimes of ancient poems: the old error concerning parricides and incest is reenacted in a performance fashioned after the likeness of reality, lest the crime once committed fade from the memory of passing generations. Every gathering is reminded by hearing that what was done can be done. . . . Then, in the mimes, one delights, under the instruction of obscenities, [Col. 0540A] either to recognize what he has done at home or to learn what he might do. Adultery is learned while it is watched; and through the evil of public authority acting as a procurer for vice, the matron who perhaps went to the spectacle chaste returns from the spectacle unchaste. . . . They portray a shameless Venus, an adulterous Mars, and that Jupiter of theirs, chief no more in sovereignty than in vices. . . . They imitate the gods whom they venerate; and for these wretched people, crimes become religious acts as well” (Cyprian, ibid., p. 5 ff.).

Earlier, however, Tertullian had said: “We separate ourselves from the theater,” he says, “because it is the private council-chamber of shamelessness. . . . Its highest appeal has been elaborately fashioned chiefly from filthiness: such filthiness the Atellan farce acts out; the mime even represents it through women, destroying the shame proper to their sex, [Col. 0540B] so that they blush more readily at home than on stage; and the pantomime suffers it in his body from childhood, so that he may become an artist. Even prostitutes themselves, victims of public lust, are brought forward on the stage, etc.” (Tertullian, *On the Spectacles*, ch. 17, p. 98). Before both of them, Theophilus of Antioch and Clement of Alexandria—and afterward Lactantius, Jerome, the author of the iambic poem ascribed to Gregory Nazianzen, and others—rightly and deservedly inveighed against these theatrical obscenities. Who, moreover, does not know that the tragedies and comedies of pagan poets, both Greek and Latin, teem with such things, and that, to the ruin of many, they have remained in print far longer than many extremely useful works of the ancient Fathers of the Church? (Theophilus, *To Autolycus*, book III, [Col. 0540C] p. 126 ff.; Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus*, book II, ch. 6, p. 169, and book II, ch. 11, p. 254; Lactantius, *Institutes*, book VI, ch. 20, p. 619 ff.; Jerome, Epistle 18, to Marcella; in Gregory Nazianzen, vol. II, p. 191.)

## ARTICLE II. How Minucius replies to Caecilius’ other reproaches—that Christians did not take part in public banquets and sacred contests, and abstained from foods offered to idols—where it is shown what those public banquets of the pagans were and how greatly they differed from those of the Christians

Caecilius reproaches the Christians for three further reasons: because they refused to celebrate public banquets with the pagans, to take part in their sacred contests, and to taste beforehand foods and drinks from which portions had been offered [Col. 0540D] to the gods. If you wish to know what those pagan banquets were and why they were instituted, these words of Tertullian will instruct you: “A great duty indeed—to bring hearths as well as couches out into public, to feast street by street, to make the city reek with the atmosphere of a tavern, to turn mud into mire with wine, and to run about in gangs committing outrages, shameless acts, and enticements to lust. Is public joy expressed in this fashion through public disgrace? Are such solemn days becoming to emperors, when they are unbecoming to everyone else? Will those who observe discipline out of respect for Caesar abandon it on Caesar’s account? Will license for bad behavior count as loyalty, and an occasion for extravagance be reckoned religion?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35).

From these and other words of his, anyone may readily understand [Col. 0541A] that those banquets were celebrated with the utmost disgrace on the solemn days of emperors and Caesars—namely, their birthdays, as he relates elsewhere; likewise their quinquennial celebrations, which we have touched upon briefly; their decennial and vicennial celebrations; at the beginning of each year, as will be explained in its proper place; and with the greatest solemnity at triumphs (Tertullian, *To His Wife*, book II, ch. 6; *On the Spectacles*, ch. 6; *Dissertation on Lucius Caecilius*, chs. 8 and 13, arts. 7 and 3).

With no less corruption of morals, the pagans also held other banquets on certain fixed festivals of the gods. Such indeed were those of which the same Tertullian says: “I do not recline at a public banquet during the Liberalia, as is customary for beast-fighters taking their final meal” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 42, p. 38). [Col. 0541B] For this reason Christians utterly abhorred them as impious and polluted by the vice of idolatry. Indeed, Bishop Martialis, as Cyprian attests, was convicted of idolatry because he had frequented the shameful and filthy banquets of the pagans (Cyprian, Epistle 67, p. 173).

Accordingly, since Minucius well knew that no pious person could be ignorant of these matters, he judged that no other reply need be made to Caecilius’ unjust rebuke than to demonstrate to him and all other pagans how greatly our banquets differed from theirs: “The banquets we hold,” he says, “are not merely chaste but also sober. For we neither indulge in feasting nor prolong the banquet with unmixed wine, but temper our cheerfulness with gravity.” We have already touched somewhat upon these matters and shall discuss them more fully in the following discourse on Tertullian’s *Apologeticum* [Col. 0541C] (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, book II, dissertation 1, ch. 6, art. 4, p. 715).

We have discussed the “sacred contests,” as Caecilius calls them, in the first volume of our *Apparatus* and in the preceding article, where we dealt with gladiators and spectacles (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, pp. 774 and 800). Minucius, moreover, seems to have answered this reproach with nothing beyond what we observed in the cited passage of that same *Apparatus*. What has been said sufficiently shows how dreadful their cruelty was.

The third heading of Caecilius’ reproach is that Christians refused to taste foods from which portions had been offered to the gods. Minucius readily admits this and at the same time gives the reason for such abstinence: “As for the fact,” he says, “that we disdain the remains of sacrifices [Col. 0541D] and cups from which libations have been made, it is not a confession of fear but an assertion of true liberty. For although everything that comes into being, as the inviolable gift of God, is corrupted by no act, we nevertheless abstain, lest anyone think either that we are yielding to the demons to whom the libation was made or that we are ashamed of our religion.”

Although Minucius, as we have already noted, never argued against the pagans who despised our Sacred Scripture by expressly appealing to its authority, and therefore passes over entirely in silence the law enacted by the Apostles concerning abstention from things offered to idols, he nevertheless seems to allude to what Paul taught concerning it and the reason for this abstinence in the First Epistle to the Corinthians (1 Cor. 8:1 ff.; 10:19 ff.). Tertullian, however, did not hesitate to cite Paul’s very words [Col. 0542A] on this matter: “Nor,” he says, “do we eat food offered in sacrifice or at funeral rites, because I cannot eat the Lord’s supper and the supper of demons” (Tertullian, *On the Spectacles*, ch. 13). Finally, Clement of Alexandria had earlier handed down much that is entirely consonant with what our author said (Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus*, book II, ch. 10, p. 213).

Celsus, however, in Origen pressed the Christians more sharply with this so-called *argumentum ad hominem*: “If idols are nothing,” as the Christians maintained, “what harm is there in enjoying the feasts?” But Origen completely overturned that futile argument by means of the words of Paul’s Epistle to the Corinthians already cited by us, and added: “Even if there are demons to whom the victims are sacrificed, we ought not to become participants in their sacrifices, [Col. 0542B] ἐπισταμένους διαφορὰν τραπέζης Κυρίου, καὶ τραπέζης δαιμονίων· καὶ διὰ τὸ ἐπίστασθαι, πάντα μὲν πράττοντας, ἵνα τραπέζης Κυρίου ἀεὶ μετέχωμεν· παντὶ δὲ τρόπῳ φυλαττομένους, ἵνα μηδέ ποτε κοινωνοὶ γινώμεθα τραπέζης δαιμονίων—knowing the difference between the table of the Lord and the table of demons; and, because we know it, doing everything so that we may always partake of the Lord’s table, while guarding ourselves by every means lest we ever become sharers in the table of demons” (Origen, *Against Celsus*, book VIII, p. 394). See Cyril of Alexandria replying to a similar argument advanced by Julian the Apostate, and Cyril of Jerusalem, Oecumenius, Estius, and other interpreters of Scripture on the cited words of the Apostle (Cyril of Alexandria, *Against Julian*, book VII, [Col. 0542C] p. 233; Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechesis* 4, p. 34).

## ARTICLE III. An examination of Caecilius’ other reproaches, in which he censures Christians because they did not adorn their heads with flowers or their bodies with perfumes, reserved ointments for funerals, denied crowns to the dead, and did not cremate but buried their bodies; including a discussion of the custom of burying bodies and its antiquity

Caecilius reproaches Christians for other practices that were far more worthy of praise than blame: “You do not,” he says, “bind your heads with flowers; you do not adorn your bodies with perfumes; you reserve ointments for funerals; you deny crowns even to tombs.” That accusation is threefold, [Col. 0542D] as you see. Minucius replies to the first charge that Christians rightly and deservedly reject not the legitimate use of flowers, but the abuse of crowns. Hear, I ask, his words: “Who is there who doubts that we enjoy the flowers of spring, since we gather both the spring rose and the lily and whatever other flower possesses an attractive color and fragrance? We use them both scattered, soft and loose, and woven into garlands with which we encircle our necks. As for the fact that we do not crown our heads, pardon us: we are accustomed to draw in the breath of a lovely flower through our nostrils, not to inhale it through the back of the head or the hair.”

Here, however, he seems to follow Tertullian as a predecessor, who had dispelled this absurd pagan accusation with a precisely similar answer: “I do not care for a crown upon my head,” he says; “what concern is that of yours? Having bought the flowers all the same, how shall I use them? I think they are more pleasing free, loose, and [Col. 0543A] wandering everywhere; but even if gathered into a crown, we know the crown by our nostrils. Let those who smell through their hair see to it” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 42, p. 38). But in another book he pursued the matter more fully, showing that the use of flowers is lawful, provided their color and fragrance are perceived by no senses other than sight and smell, which divine Providence appointed for this purpose: “Use flowers,” he says, “by sight and smell, for that is the fruit proper to those senses. . . . But what savor of a flower is there in the head? What perception does a crown afford except only that of a bond, by which neither the color is seen, nor the fragrance inhaled, nor even the rashness approved? It is as contrary to nature to pursue a flower with one’s head as food with one’s ear or sound with one’s nose. Everything contrary to nature deserves among all people the designation of monstrosity, but among us also the verdict [Col. 0543B] of sacrilege against God, the Lord and Author of nature” (the same, *On the Crown*, ch. 5, p. 123). Indeed, even in Lucian, though he was a pagan, those who wore crowns are censured, ὅτι μὴ ἴσασι τοῦ στεφάνου τὸν τόπον, “because they did not know the proper place for the crown”—where it should be put (Lucian, *Dialogue on the Conduct of Philosophers*, p. 24).

Justin Martyr, however, gave another reason why Christians disapproved of the use of crowns: because pagans customarily bestowed them upon lifeless images of the gods (Justin, *Apology* 2, p. 57). Moreover, Tertullian, whom we have just commended, proves by various arguments that the origin of that custom derived from the impious worship of those gods. He later added: “By this we may be sufficiently instructed how alien we ought to judge the [Col. 0543C] institution of the crowned head, approved by them and thereafter administered in their honor,” just as he shows in yet another book, “whom the world believed to be gods” (Tertullian, *On the Crown*, chs. 7 ff., pp. 124 ff.; and *On the Spectacles*, chs. 7, 11, and 12, pp. 93 and 95). Therefore Cyprian, his follower, congratulated Christ’s confessors with these words: “Your head remained free from the impious and criminal covering with which the captive heads of those sacrificing there were veiled. The forehead made pure by the sign of God could not bear the devil’s crown; it reserved itself for the Lord’s crown” (Cyprian, *On the Lapsed*, p. 122). Consult further, if you please, Mader’s special book *On Crowns*, and, if you wish, what we said about them in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus*, as well as what must still be said later (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, p. 801 [Col. 0543D] ff.; and below, ch. 19, art. 2).

But Caecilius and other pagans blamed Christians on this ground not only because they denied crowns to the living, but also because they denied them to the dead: “You deny crowns,” he said, “even to tombs.” What, then, does Octavius say to this? He readily concedes that it is entirely true and marvels that the dead are crowned by the pagans: “I marvel at you all the more in this,” he says, “how you bestow upon a lifeless person either a torch, or upon one who does not feel a crown, since the blessed person does not need flowers and the wretched person cannot rejoice in them” (ibid., p. 341). But many consider the text of our author corrupt here; in some editions the particle *non* (“not”) and the noun *exanimi* (“lifeless person”) have been omitted. This correction, however, conflicts with the manuscript codex [Col. 0544A] and increases the difficulty rather than removing it. It appears contrary to Minucius’ thought. For it is not remarkable that a torch should be given to one who “feels,” but that it should be given to a dead person, who can see nothing. If the codex’s reading is preserved, our author’s meaning seems to be: “I indeed wonder why you bestow crowns upon one who does not feel and is dead.” For that is no less absurd than bringing a torch to one “lifeless,” and therefore insensible. A dead person is either “blessed” or “wretched”: if blessed and enjoying eternal felicity, he has no need of flowers; if wretched and tormented by eternal punishments, he cannot rejoice in flowers, since he can be affected by no sensation of joy.

By this argument he assails the pagans, who not only customarily crowned the dead, [Col. 0544B] but had even been commanded to do so by a most ancient law. If you wish, you may have Cicero as witness, where he discusses the laws of the Twelve Tables: “That provision,” he says, “is already an indication that the ornaments of praise pertain to the dead, since the law commands that a crown won by valor may without penalty be placed both upon the man who had not won it and upon his parent” (Cicero, *On the Laws*, book II, p. 341, line 39). Pliny teaches when it was to be placed: “What honor, then,” he says, “was it that it might without penalty be placed upon the dead man himself and his parents, while he was laid out indoors or was being carried outside?” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book XXI, ch. 3, p. 107)—that is, while the dead man’s body was kept at home and was then carried out and the funeral procession held. Lucian also wrote of all the dead: Στεφανώσαντες ὡραίοις ἄνθεσι προτίθενται λαμπρῶς ἀμφιέσαντες, [Col. 0544C] “Having crowned them with the loveliest flowers and splendidly clothed them, they lay them out” (Lucian, *On Mourning*, p. 810). But he ridicules and rejects this custom and other pagan burial rites. Why, then, should Christians not have rejected that use of crowns, which we have discussed elsewhere? (Vol. I of the *Apparatus*, book III, dissertation I, ch. 19, art. 6, p. 802 ff.) Minucius gives this reason why they rejected it: “But we adorn funerals with the same tranquility with which we live; nor do we attach a withering crown, but await from God one living with eternal flowers” (Minucius, p. 342); that is, we expect it.

Although Christians did not encircle the heads of the dead with any crown, they nevertheless customarily anointed their bodies with ointments (ibid., p. 98). For what could be more fitting than that they should imitate the example, set before them by the Evangelist, of Christ’s anointed body [Col. 0544D] (John 19:39 ff.)? Nor did Caecilius dare to condemn this custom. For when, as Pliny says, the use of ointments had reached them in the time of Darius, “then the honor began to be extended to the dead as well” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book XIII, ch. 1, p. 56). Let us hear, then, what Caecilius himself censures in Christians: “You do not adorn the body with perfumes; you reserve ointments for funerals” (Minucius, p. 97). Thus he reproaches them not because they poured ointments over the dead, but because they did not anoint themselves while still alive. Octavius, however, scorning this wholly perverse accusation, appears to have made no reply to it, unless perhaps in what he had said in the same passage: that Christians did not reject the lawful use of flowers and therefore also of ointments, but rather the intolerable pagan abuse of them, as we have shown elsewhere [Col. 0545A] (vol. I of the *Apparatus*, pp. 803 ff.). Tertullian, moreover, related more concerning the pious and praiseworthy Christian rite of anointing dead bodies; this must be examined by us in its proper place.

Lastly, Caecilius attacks Christians for still observing the ancient custom of burying the dead: “They execrate funeral pyres and burials by fire, as though every body, even if withdrawn from the flames, were not nevertheless dissolved into earth through years and ages; and as though it made any difference whether wild beasts tear it apart, the seas consume it, the soil covers it, or flame removes it. If corpses possess feeling, every burial is a punishment; if they do not, the very speed with which they are destroyed is a remedy” (Minucius, p. 97 [Col. 0545B] ff.). We have already observed that these words were drawn from Seneca (above, ch. 2, art. 5).

What does Minucius reply to such insipid mockery? “Nor, as you believe,” he says, “do we fear any harm from the mode of burial; rather, we maintain the ancient and better custom of interment” (Minucius, p. 255). If you doubt that the custom of burying corpses was indeed the most ancient and best, Cicero will teach you in these words: “For my part, the most ancient kind of burial appears to me to have been that which Cyrus uses in Xenophon. The body is returned to the earth, and when thus placed and laid out it is covered, as it were, by the covering of its mother. We have learned that our King Numa [Col. 0545C] was laid to rest by the same rite in that tomb not far from the altar of Fontus; and we know that the Cornelian clan practiced this burial by interment down to our own memory. Sulla, when victorious, ordered the remains of Gaius Marius, laid beside the Anio, to be scattered. . . . Perhaps fearing that this might happen to his own body, he was the first of the patrician Cornelii who wished to be cremated by fire” (Cicero, *On the Laws*, book II, p. 340, line 45 ff.).

Concerning Numa, Plutarch also handed down the following for remembrance: “His body, in accordance with his command, as the report goes, πυρὶ μὲν οὐκ ἔδοσαν τὸν νεκρὸν, αὐτοῦ κωλύσαντος· δύο δὲ ποιησάμενοι λιθίνας σορούς, ὑπὸ τὸν Ἰανικλὸν, τὴν μὲν ἑτέραν ἔχουσαν τὸ σῶμα—they did not give the corpse to the fire, since he himself had forbidden it; instead, they made two stone chests and buried them beneath the Janiculum, one of which contained his body” (Plutarch, *Life of Numa*, p. 74). From these accounts, therefore, [Col. 0545D] it is clear that the custom of burying corpses was extremely ancient. Pliny likewise subscribed to this view, as is evident from these words of his: “Cremation itself was not an ancient institution among the Romans: the dead were buried in the earth. But after they learned that those buried in distant wars were being dug up, cremation was then instituted. And yet many families preserved the ancient rites; thus, in the Cornelian family, no one is reported to have been cremated before the dictator Sulla. He wished this because he feared retaliation after Gaius Marius’ corpse had been dug up. But *sepultus* should be understood of one laid to rest in any manner whatever; *humatus*, of one covered with earth” (Pliny, *Natural History*, book VII, ch. 54, pp. 91 ff.). As you see, he follows Cicero κατὰ πόδα, “step by step,” and clearly explains the difference between *sepultura* (“burial” in the general sense) and *humatio* (“interment in earth”).

The same Cicero bears witness that this custom prevailed not only among [Col. 0546A] the Romans but also among other nations. He committed the following concerning the Egyptians, Persians, and Magi to writing and memory: “The Egyptians embalm the dead and keep them at home. The Persians also preserve them after coating them with wax, so that the bodies may endure as long as possible. It is the custom of the Magi not to bury the bodies of their people unless they have first been torn by wild beasts” (Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations*, book I, p. 133, line 23). He adds that the Hyrcanians considered it the best burial when the corpses of the dead were torn apart by dogs.

Concerning the Athenians, he records the following in the second book *On the Laws* cited by us: “At Athens, that custom, as they say, endured from Cecrops: the established practice of burying in the earth. After the nearest relatives had done this and the earth had been drawn over the body, it was sown with crops, so that the bosom and lap, [Col. 0546B] as it were, of a mother might be granted to the dead person; while the ground, purified by the crops, might be restored to the living” (ibid., *On the Laws*, book II, p. 342, line 9). Nor should Servius be passed over, who observes the following in his commentary on the sixth book of the *Aeneid*: “Those who have written on the kinds of piety wished the first place to belong to burial. Hence, although it was unlawful for pontiffs to look upon a corpse, it was nevertheless a greater violation if, after seeing one, they left it unburied. One kind of burial was the casting of dust upon it. Hence the verse: ‘Or cast earth upon me, for you can.’ Horace says: ‘There is no long delay; you may run on after casting dust three times’” (Servius on *Aeneid* VI.175 ff., p. 409; and on book II, pp. 618–19). But he discusses the matter elsewhere as well; consult him, together with [Col. 0546C] Macrobius, who openly testifies that in his own age there was no custom of burning the bodies of the dead (Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, book VII, ch. 7). In another book, however, he wrote that the institutions of very many nations or regions ordained that the bodies of the dead should not be carried to burial without song (*Commentary on the Dream of Scipio*, book II, ch. 3). Lilius Gyraldus discusses at greater length the various rites of burial in a special book written on that subject; Alexander ab Alexandro in the sixth book of his *Days of Genius*; and Tiraquellus in his annotations on that book.

## ARTICLE IV. How holy the morals of Christians were and how corrupt those of the pagans; why Caecilius calls the Christians’ discipline inferior; how greatly those same Christians surpassed the pagan philosophers in piety and learning; yet that pagans and Christians were friends and addressed one another by the name of brothers

[Col. 0546D]

From what has thus far been said, it can be obscure to no one how falsely Caecilius boasted—as he himself later rightly acknowledged—that throughout his entire speech he had proved Christians to be people not merely rude and uneducated, but also impious and defiled by the greatest crimes, hitherto unheard of. Octavius, however, further refutes this great calumny, already more than sufficiently answered, with these words: “If we Christians are compared with you”—that is, the pagans—“although in some respects our discipline is inferior, we shall nevertheless be found far better than you.” By these words [Col. 0547A] he confidently asserts that Christian morals were far purer and holier than those of the pagans.

But someone will ask: what does Octavius mean when he says, “In some respects our discipline is inferior” to that of the pagans? These words may have a twofold meaning. First: although among certain indolent and slothful Christians our discipline is inferior, these Christians are nevertheless better than the pagans. The second meaning is this: Christians are found better than the pagans, although in certain matters their discipline is inferior to that of the pagans—for example, in the multitude of laws, ceremonies pertaining to the worship of the gods and to religion, temples, altars, sacrifices, processions, the use of crowns, and other rites that we have discussed above. Consider whether this latter meaning may appear better than the former, because Minucius compares Christians with pagans, not Christians with themselves.

[Col. 0547B] How upright, chaste, and religious the morals of Christians were, and how corrupt, depraved, and wicked those of the pagans, Minucius—or Octavius in his name—shows by a brief comparison, which Tertullian pursues at greater length (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 46, p. 39 ff.): “You,” he says, “forbid adultery and commit it; we are born as men only for our own wives.” Tertullian, whom we have just cited, confirms this by the example of Speusippus: “I hear,” he says, “that a certain Speusippus of Plato’s school perished in the act of adultery; the Christian is born a male for his own wife alone” (Tertullian, passage cited, p. 40). But we have already related certain matters concerning the chastity of Christians and the infamous [Col. 0547C] shamelessness of the pagans (above, ch. 10, arts. 1 and 2; and ch. 16, art. 2), and shall say more below.

Minucius then continues: “You punish crimes once committed; among us, even to think of sin is to sin. You fear witnesses; we fear even conscience alone, without which we cannot exist.” Tertullian, however, says: “Democritus, by blinding himself because he could not look upon women without desire and suffered pain if he did not possess them, confesses his incontinence by his correction of it. But the Christian, while keeping his eyes unharmed, does not see women: he is blind in mind against lust” (Tertullian, passage cited, p. 40). This requires no proof. For who does not know the law established by Christ: “Everyone who looks at [Col. 0547D] a woman in order to desire her has already committed adultery with her in his heart” (Matt. 5:28)? From this Tertullian concludes that divine law is more perfect than human law: “Which is more perfect,” he says, “to prohibit adultery, or to restrain even from the solitary lust of the eyes?” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 44, p. 39). But more on these matters in the following dissertations (*Dissertations on Tertullian*, ch. 12, art. 4).

Finally, our author added the following concerning accused persons cast into prison: “The prison seethes with people of your number; no Christian is there unless he is either accused on account of his religion or a deserter from it.” These words agree so closely with Tertullian’s that they appear to have been excerpted and transferred from him. For he says: “So many criminals are enumerated among you under the indictments of various crimes: what murderer there, [Col. 0548A] what cutpurse, what temple-robber, what corrupter, or what thief among bathers, is also recorded as a Christian? Or, when Christians are brought forward under their own designation, which of them is also such as all those criminals? It is always from your people that the prison seethes; always from your people that the mines groan; always on your people that the beasts are fattened; always from your people that the providers of public shows feed their herds of condemned criminals. No Christian is there unless for this alone; or if for something else as well, he is now no longer a Christian” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 45, p. 39). That is: no Christian was cast into prison on account of any crime whatsoever, but solely on account of the name “Christian” and the profession of the Christian law. But if he was imprisoned for some crime, he was no longer a Christian—that is, he had fallen away from Christian profession. [Col. 0548B] This is precisely what we have seen stated by our Minucius. In another dissertation (*Dissertations on Tertullian*, ch. 12, arts. 3–4), we shall show how much more extensively Tertullian develops this comparison between Christians and pagans. Meanwhile, you may read what Tertullian, Lactantius, and Eusebius committed to writing in confirmation of these points (Tertullian, *To Scapula*, ch. 2; Lactantius, *Institutes*, book V, ch. 9, p. 383; Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, ch. 2, p. 160).

Our Minucius proceeds and establishes another comparison between Christians and pagan philosophers in the following manner: “We despise the haughty brows of the philosophers, whom we know to be corrupters, adulterers, and tyrants, and always eloquent against their own vices. We profess wisdom not in outward dress but in the mind; [Col. 0548C] we do not speak grandly, but live so. We glory that we have attained what they sought with the utmost exertion and were unable to find.” Cyprian undoubtedly borrowed this very comparison from this passage of our author, as well as several of his words that we quoted above (above, ch. 2, art. 39, p. 4).
