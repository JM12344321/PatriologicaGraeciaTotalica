# The Book on Patience

[Col. 1249]

# THE BOOK OF QUINTUS SEPTIMIUS FLORENS TERTULLIAN ON PATIENCE. (C)

## Synopsis

[Col. 1249] The treatise *On Patience*, which many believe was composed around the year 200, while Tertullian was still a Catholic—an opinion embraced especially by the most learned Tillemont, D. Cellier, and P. Dominicus Schram—is a distinguished exhortation, written with great eloquence, to embrace patience, I might almost say to endure martyrdom. It is as though, when Septimius was writing these things, a new tempest were already threatening and the sufferings of Christians were about to be renewed.

Whatever is to be held as settled concerning that matter [Col. 1250], or whatever the time or crisis in which this little book was composed, no reason is omitted in it by which Christians might be stirred to patience and deterred from impatience. For at the threshold of the work, after the author has, as though laid prostrate in public, disclosed his own weakness and lack of self-command, and has briefly described patience with great praise, he proceeds to touch upon each motive to be proposed for embracing it, namely:

I. God’s patience toward us. | II. |

II. Christ’s patience. | III, IV. |

III. The origins of impatience and its evils, as detected in the devil himself. | V. |

IV. Faith to be proved by patience. | VI. |

V. Temporal things consequently despised. | VII. |

VI. Injuries borne with equanimity. | VIII. |

VII. Grief arising from the loss of one’s loved ones moderated. | [Col. 1250] IX. |

VIII. The desire for vengeance extinguished. | X. |

IX. The enemy’s provocations now rendered ineffectual. | XI. |

X. Patience as the mother of the virtues. | XIII. |

XI. And as an inexhaustible source of strength. | XIV. |

XII. Examples of patience, and finally its reward and fruit. | XIV, XV. |

EDD.

## CHAPTER ONE

[Col. 1249A]

I confess before the Lord God that I have ventured quite rashly, if not even shamelessly, to compose a work on patience, though I am utterly incapable of practicing it [Note: [Col. 1249B] “I am” Paris.], being a man possessed of no good quality [Note: [Col. 1249B] “A man possessed of no good quality.” Almost everywhere he speaks of himself in the most abject terms. Thus, in the book *On Repentance*: “You, then, a sinner like me—or rather less than I, for I acknowledge my own preeminence in offenses—seize upon it in this way.” And in book II of *On the Dress of Women* [Col. 1250B]: “May God permit me to rebuke this very thing, since I am indeed to be rebuked in every respect”; and in the same work: “Would that I, most wretched man,” etc. RIG.]. For when people undertake to demonstrate and commend any subject, they ought first to be found themselves engaged in its practice, and to direct the steadfastness of their admonition [Note: [Col. 1249B] “Commendation” Latinus.] by the authority of their own conduct, lest their words blush at the deficiency of their deeds.

And would that this blushing brought some remedy, so that shame at not exhibiting what we are proceeding to recommend to others might become our instructor in exhibiting it! Yet there is such an insupportable greatness in certain good things, just as there is in certain evils, that divine inspiration’s grace alone brings about our ability to apprehend and practice them. For whatever is supremely good belongs supremely to God, and none but he who possesses it dispenses it, as he deems each person worthy.

Thus it will be at least a consolation to discuss what it is not granted us to enjoy, after the fashion of the sick, who, though deprived of health, cannot refrain from speaking about its blessings. So I, most wretched man, always sick with the fevers of impatience, must sigh after, invoke, and plead for the health of patience that I do not possess, whenever I remember and ponder, in contemplation of my weakness, that the good health of faith and the soundness of the Lord’s discipline do not readily come to anyone unless patience sits beside him. Patience has been so placed at the head of the things of God that no one who is a stranger to patience can fulfill any commandment or accomplish any work pleasing to the Lord.

Even those who live blindly [Note: [Col. 1250B] “Blind” Rhen., Fran.] honor its excellence by calling it the highest virtue [Note: [Col. 1250B] “Even those who live blindly.” This is the reading of Fulvius Ursinus’s codex. > Those who pretend to be Curii and live Bacchanalia.

LE PR.] [Note: [Col. 1250B] “May honor” Fran., Paris.]. Philosophers [Col. 1251A], indeed, who are regarded as creatures possessing some degree of wisdom [Note: [Col. 1251B] “Carnal” Wouw., MS.], place so high a value upon patience that, although they disagree among themselves through the diverse whims of their schools and rivalries of opinion, nevertheless, united in their regard for patience alone, they have entrusted to this single virtue the peace of their studies. They conspire in its favor and enter into alliance over it; they [Note: [Col. 1251B] “To it” Fran., Paris.] [Note: [Col. 1251C] “They pursue it.” So the ancient copies have it. RIG.] pursue it [Note: [Col. 1251B] “It” absent from Rigalt.] with one accord in their aspiration to virtue; every display of wisdom they advance on the basis of patience. It is a great testimony to patience that it promotes even the vain disciplines of the world to praise and glory. Or is this instead an injury, when a divine thing is rolled about amid secular arts? But let them see to that: soon they will be ashamed when their wisdom is destroyed and disgraced together with the world.
