He had also prepared poisons in thunderstones, hyacinths, and emeralds, with which to kill himself if anything more serious threatened. He had also constructed a very high tower, with gold and jeweled tablets laid beneath it, from which he might throw himself, saying that even his death ought to be costly and display luxury, so that it might be said that no one had ever perished in this manner.” **HAV.**], and one who leaps across neighboring rooftops in another; and so that one kind of power may be declared present in him who cuts off his genitals or his arms, another in him who cuts his own throat. The outcome of the frenzy is alike, and there is one cause of the instigation.

But enough of words thus far. From this point comes a demonstration of the thing itself, by which we shall show that the character of both names is one and the same.

Let someone be brought forward here before your tribunals who is known to be driven by a demon. If commanded to speak by any Christian, that spirit will as truthfully confess itself a demon as elsewhere it falsely professes itself a god. Let someone likewise be produced from among those who are thought to suffer from a god—those who, breathing over altars, conceive the divine power from the savor; who are healed by belching [Note: [Col. 0413C] Thus all the manuscripts; Rigalt emends it to “attempt”; Havercamp, “bend.” I do not know whether one should read “are seized” or “rush forward.”], and who utter prophecies while panting [Note: [Col. 0413D] “They utter prophecies while panting.” This did not occur without confusion of the voice and extremely forceful breathing. Lucan, V.148:

From a tranquil breast she reports invented  
Words, with no murmur of a confused voice  
Bearing witness that her mind is impelled by sacred frenzy.

And shortly afterward:

No words broken by a trembling  
Sound, no voice sufficient to fill the space  
Of the capacious cavern, etc.

Add verse 170 and those that follow. But these matters cannot be learned better than from Donatus’ note on Terence’s *Phormio*, Act IV, Scene IV, on the words “The soothsayer forbade it.” There that very ancient and excellent interpreter writes: “A soothsayer is one who prophesies under divine inspiration. He is called *hariolus*, as though *fariolus*, from fate and from speaking, since F is used for H, and likewise H for F, in many expressions. Or is it because they imitate aspiration in exhaling? For by their breath they are accustomed to expel, as it were, their mortal soul, so that they may receive a divine one; hence they also utter a sound of this kind, OE, because the sound OE conveys the force of exhalation.” **HAV.**].

That very Heavenly Virgin [Note: [Col. 0414C] “The Heavenly Virgin.” Junius understands Juno, the principal divinity of the Carthaginians, concerning whom we have spoken above at ch. XII. La Cerda also discusses her excellently at the following chapter, showing that she is called the Virgin, and that the mention of rains, which descend from the air, is not inappropriately joined to her here. Hence Orpheus certainly says in his hymn to her:

Ὄμβρων μὲν διερῶν, ἀνέμων τροφὲ, παντογένεθλε (“O nourisher of drenching rains and winds, all-generating one”).

“Mother of rainstorms, producer of winds of every kind.”

Fabulous antiquity did not pass over Juno the Virgin in silence; Pausanias is my witness, near the end of his *Corinthian Matters*: Καὶ πηγὴ Κάναθος καλουμένη· ἐνταῦθα τὴν Ἤραν φασὶν Αργεῖοι κατὰ ἔτος λουμένην παρθένον γίνεσθαι· οὗτος μὲν δή σφισιν ἑκ τελετῆς ἣν ἂγουσι τῇ Ηρᾳ, λόγος τῶν ἀποῤῥήτων εϛίν (“There is also a spring called Canathus. The Argives say that Hera, bathing there every year, becomes a virgin again; this is one of their secret accounts from the rite they perform for Hera”), that is: “And a spring whose name is Canathus. Among the Argives it is commonly reported that whenever Juno bathes in that spring each year, she becomes a virgin again.” This account flowed from the mysteries of the initiatory rites performed for Juno according to solemn custom. The Aldine manuscript very badly reads “the heavenly voice,” as does Rhenanus, “the heavenly power.” **HAV.**], who promises rains; that very Aesculapius, who reveals medicines and supplies life to Socordius, Thanatius, and Asclepiodotus, though they are to die on another day [Note: [Col. 0414D] “Though they are to die on another day.” Rigalt excellently restored this from the Fulda manuscript, which has “though he is to die on another day,” while Heraldus’ manuscripts have “though they are to die.” For he ridicules that grant of life [Col. 0415C] which must nevertheless be exchanged for death shortly afterward. The common reading is “though otherwise they are to die.” Aldus, however, reads “though they are to die on another,” as do the Leiden and Agobardine manuscripts, making it clear that τὸ (the Greek article “the”), “day,” dropped out.

In the *Jugurthine War*, ch. 106, Sallust makes Sulla, undaunted amid dangers, reason with Volux in this way: “Even if certain destruction were at hand, he would rather remain than, by betraying those whom he commanded, preserve by a shameful flight a life that was uncertain and perhaps destined shortly afterward to perish by disease.” **HAV.**]—unless they confess themselves demons, not daring to lie to a Christian, then and there shed the blood of that most insolent Christian!

What could be more manifest than this deed? What more trustworthy than this proof? The simplicity of truth stands in full view; its own power supports it. No suspicion will be possible. You will say that it is done by magic or some deception of that sort [Note: [Col. 0413C] Rhenanus and others read: “No suspicion will be possible; that it is done by magic,” etc. “Do not stand by the statements, if,” etc. Rigalt and Havercamp accepted the reading of the Fulda manuscript. The Bongars manuscript has: “that it is done by magic in what is said.” The Puteanus, Leiden, and Agobardine manuscripts read: “You say it is done. You will not say so, if,” etc.], if your own eyes and ears permit you. But what objection can be maintained against that which is shown in naked sincerity?

If, on the one hand [Note: [Col. 0413C] The Leiden manuscript reads “of nature”; hence Havercamp suspects “by nature,” or “truly by nature.”], they truly are gods, why do they falsely call themselves demons? Is it in order to obey us? Then your divinity is already subject to Christians; and that which is subject to a human being, and indeed to its own rivals—which adds to the disgrace—must certainly not be regarded as divinity.

If, on the other hand, they are demons or angels, why do they answer elsewhere that they act as gods? For just as those who are regarded as gods [Note: [Col. 0414C] The Paris manuscript reads “are called.”] would have been unwilling to call themselves demons if they truly were gods, precisely so as not to divest themselves of majesty, so too those whom you know directly as demons would not dare elsewhere to act as gods if there were any gods at all whose names they use. For they would fear to misuse the majesty of beings undoubtedly superior [Note: [Col. 0414C] Franeker reads “superior majesty”; the Paris manuscript, “a superior majesty.”] and therefore to be feared.

Thus the divinity that you maintain does not exist at all, because, if it existed, it would neither be laid claim to by demons [Note: [Col. 0414C] Rhenanus and others read: “would neither be laid claim to in confession,” etc. Rigalt and Havercamp deleted the two words “in confession,” which had been added for purposes of interpretation.], nor denied by gods. Since, therefore, both parties concur in a confession denying that they are gods, recognize that both are one kind of being—that is, demons.

But now seek gods in both places [Note: [Col. 0414C] Franciscus Junius, Havercamp, Semler, and Ritter punctuate: “demons indeed. In both places now seek gods,” etc.], for you have learned that those whom you had presumed to be gods are demons. By the same operation of ours, however, through which those same gods of yours disclose not only that they themselves are not gods [Note: [Col. 0414C] The Paris manuscript adds “are.”], nor are any others [Note: [Col. 0414C] The Paris manuscript adds “but.”], you also learn immediately who truly is God; whether it is he, whether he is the one God whom we Christians profess; and whether he is to be believed and worshiped as the faith and discipline of Christians have established.

Then and there they will say who this Christ is, together with his “fable”: whether he is a human being of ordinary condition; whether a magician; whether after death [Note: [Col. 0415C] Havercamp reads “cross.”] he was stolen from the tomb by his disciples; whether he is now, in short, among the dead; or whether he is not rather in heaven [Note: [Col. 0415C] Semler reads “more swiftly.”], and destined to come from there amid the convulsion of the whole world, amid the terror of the earth, amid the mourning of all—but not of Christians [Note: [Col. 0415C] Franeker reads “if not.”]—as the Power of God and the Spirit of God and the Word and Wisdom and Reason and Son of God.

Whatever you ridicule, let them ridicule it with you. Let them deny that Christ will judge every soul from the beginning of time when its body has been restored. Let them say before the tribunal that, if they please, Minos and Rhadamanthus [Note: [Col. 0415D] “Minos and Rhadamanthus.” St. Justin Martyr, *Apology* II, records Plato’s agreement with the poets on this matter in these words: “Plato,” he says, “taught that Rhadamanthus and Minos would punish those who came to them unjustly; we say that this same thing will happen, but through Christ.” Athenagoras adds in his *Oration for the Christians*: “But we say that whether it be Minos or Rhadamanthus, or the father of either of them, not even he will escape the judgment of God.” **PAM.**], in accordance with the common view [Note: [Col. 0415C] The Paris manuscript reads “meaning.”] of Plato and the poets, obtained this office.

Let them at least refute the mark of their own disgrace and condemnation [Col. 0415B]. Let them deny that they are unclean spirits—something that should have been understood merely from their food: blood and smoke and the stinking pyres of cattle [Note: [Col. 0415D] “The stinking pyres of cattle.” It is well known that the ancients loaded demons with abuse. Sulpicius, concerning St. Martin: “Why do you stand here,” he says, “bloody beast? You will find nothing in me, accursed one.” **LAC.**], as well as the utterly impure tongues of their own prophets. Let them deny [Note: [Col. 0415C] Franeker and Rigalt read “let them declare.”] that because of their malice they have been condemned in advance to that same day of judgment, together with all their worshipers and agents [Note: [Col. 0415C] The Puteanus and Leiden manuscripts, Rhenanus, Aldus, and Franciscus Junius read “operations.”].

Yet all this dominion and power of ours over them derives its efficacy from the naming of Christ [Note: [Col. 0416C] “All this dominion and power of ours over them derives its efficacy from the naming of Christ.” A notable passage of Irenaeus, book II, ch. 5, expresses this view: “All things,” he says, “are subject to the name of our Lord. And for this reason the Jews even now drive demons away by this very invocation.” **PAM.**], and from the recalling of those things which they expect to befall them from God through Christ as arbiter. Fearing Christ in God, and God in Christ, they are subjected to the servants of God and Christ.

Thus, when by our touch and our breath they are seized by the contemplation and representation of that fire, they depart from bodies at our command, unwillingly and in pain, blushing in your presence. Believe them when they speak the truth [Note: [Col. 0415C] “Truth” is absent from the Paris manuscript.] about themselves, since you believe them when they lie. No one lies to his own disgrace, but rather to his honor. Credence more readily inclines toward those who confess against themselves than toward those who deny in their own defense.

Indeed, these testimonies of your gods have been accustomed to make Christians, [Col. 0416A] because by believing them very deeply we believe Christ the Lord [Note: [Col. 0415C] Wouwer prefers “the Lord in Christ.”]. They themselves kindle faith in our writings; they themselves build up confidence in our hope.

Yet, as far as I know, you worship them even with the blood of Christians. They would therefore be unwilling to lose you, who are so profitable and so dutiful to them, or to be driven out someday by you after you have become Christians [Note: [Col. 0416C] Franeker reads “to be driven out someday by Christians”; Wouwer, from a manuscript, “lest they be driven out.”] [Note: [Col. 0416D] “If they.” The sense is rather obscure, but nevertheless it is this: Believe them when, adjured by us, they speak the truth. For if they were permitted to lie when a Christian wishes to prove the truth to you, they would certainly do so, lest they lose you, who are so profitable to them—indeed, lest they should be driven from some possession of theirs by you yourselves, perhaps Christians one day. **HAV.**], if they were permitted to lie under examination by a Christian who wished to prove the truth to you.
