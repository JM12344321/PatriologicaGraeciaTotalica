But not only do the Jews wander and roam here and there throughout the whole world; they are not even permitted, Tertullian continues, “under the status of foreigners to greet their native land, even by setting foot upon it” (Tertullian, loc. cit.). And when in another book he argues expressly against those same Jews, he openly asserts further that this was not merely not permitted to them, but actually forbidden, just as it had been foretold long before by the sacred prophet, namely Isaiah. Hear, I pray, his words:

> We observe that now no one of the race of Israel has remained in the city of Bethlehem; and since it has consequently been forbidden that any Jew should remain within the borders of that region, this too would be something fulfilled through the Prophet: “Your land is desolate; your cities are burned with fire,” that is, what [Col. 0784C] befell them in time of war (Isaiah 1:7); “foreigners will consume your country in your sight, and it will be desolate and overthrown by alien peoples.” And elsewhere it is thus said through the Prophet: “You will see the King with glory,” that is, Christ performing mighty works in the glory of God the Father, “and your eyes will see the land from afar” (ibid. 33:17). Because, in accordance with your deserts, after the conquest of Jerusalem you were forbidden to enter your land, you have been permitted only to see it with your eyes from afar.

(Idem, *lib. adv. Judae.*, cap. 13.) That earlier passage of Isaiah is likewise cited by Cyprian for the same reason (Cypr., lib. I *adv. Judae.*, § 6, p. 22).

Moreover, that prediction and the subsequent prohibition against entering their homeland are confirmed by the author of the Eusebian *Chronicle* in these words:

> The Jewish war that was being waged in [Col. 0784D] Palestine came to an end, the affairs of the Jews having been utterly crushed. From that time onward permission to enter Jerusalem was also taken from them, first by the will of God, as the Prophets had foretold, and then by Roman prohibitions.

(Euseb., *Chron.*, at the year of Christ 135.)

Earlier, moreover, Justin Martyr cites the same earlier prophecy of Isaiah as Tertullian concerning the expulsion of the Jews from their homeland (Justin, *Cohort. ad gent.*, p. 14); and, after adducing yet another, immediately adds: And that it is guarded by you, so that no one may be there, and death has been ordained against any Jew caught entering it. “You know very well both that precautions are taken and watch kept lest any of you should be there, and that capital punishment has been established against a Jew who enters it” [Col. 0785A] (Justin, *Apolog.* 1 *pro Christian.*, p. 84). Jerome, however, says: “After the final devastation, public laws were also posted, and the Jews were forbidden to enter the land from which they had been expelled” (Jerome, on Isaiah 6:11, p. 65). And again elsewhere: “No Jew is permitted by law to enter the land and city once holy” (Idem, on Ezekiel 18:16, p. 617).

If, however, you ask at what time and by which emperor that law was enacted, you may learn from Eusebius, who relates that in the twelfth year of the emperor Hadrian, after the overthrow of the city of Betthera, situated not far from Jerusalem: From that time onward, the entire nation was wholly forbidden by a legal decree and the ordinances of Hadrian to set foot even upon the land around Jerusalem, so that they might not behold from afar their ancestral [Col. 0785B] soil, Hadrian having so commanded, as Aristo of Pella records. “The entire nation of the Jews was forbidden to set foot in the region surrounding Jerusalem, so much so that they were not even permitted to look upon their native soil from afar, as Aristo of Pella writes” (Euseb., *Hist. eccl.*, IV.6, p. 118). But by Hilary’s time the severity of this law had been somewhat relaxed, as he indicates in these very words:

> Indeed, even now they are barred by an edict of the Roman emperor from entering that same city. And when they remember that the Holy of Holies is desolate, the prophecies silent, the sacrifices of atonement absent, and all the dignity of the Levitical ministry disfigured, they now turn back and, afflicted by hunger for the word of God and for saving food, are accustomed to walk around the city as a consolation for their present miseries.

(Hilary, *Tract. in Psalm.* LVIII, § 7, p. 130.)

[Col. 0785C] But that unhappy people could not enjoy even this meager consolation for their miseries without payment; they were compelled to purchase it. I should like you to hear Jerome, a most substantial witness to this fact:

> To the present day the faithless tenants . . . are forbidden to enter Jerusalem; and, so that they may be allowed to weep over the ruin of their city, they purchase the privilege—for those who once purchased the blood of Christ now purchase their own tears. . . . Tears are still upon their cheeks, their arms are bruised, their hair disheveled, and the soldier demands payment so that they may be allowed to weep longer.

(Jerome, on Zephaniah 1:15, p. 1655.)

Yet it was not only these things that had been foretold, as stated above, by the sacred voices of the prophets. The same voices, Tertullian says, always insisted that it would come to pass that, “in the last courses of the age, God would choose for Himself, from every nation and people [Col. 0785D] and place, worshipers much more faithful, to whom He would transfer a grace that was indeed fuller because of their capacity for the discipline of its author” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, cap. 21). In our manuscripts we read *eadem* rather than *eaedem*, and *auctioris* rather than *auctoris*, not altogether badly. Cyprian excerpted this, like the preceding material recalled by us shortly before, in much the same fashion:

> God had foretold beforehand that, as the age declined and the end of the world was now drawing near, He would choose for Himself from every nation and people and place worshipers much more faithful and of better obedience, who would draw indulgence from the divine gifts—the indulgence that the Jews, though they had received it, lost by despising religion.

(Cyprian, *De idolorum vanitate*, p. 15.) Neither author there indicates what those oracles [Col. 0786A] of the sacred seers were; but Tertullian cited some in another book, Cyprian more of them, and Lactantius still later, as we have demonstrated in the appropriate place (Idem, *Adv. Judae.*, cap. 12; Cyprian, lib. I *Adv. Jud.*, § 11, p. 23; *Dissertat. in Lactant.*, cap. 10, art. 1).

Thus Christians, “grafted from a wild olive into the Jews’ olive tree,” as Tertullian continues to put it, and substituted in their place, came to share with those same Jews the treasury of the sacred Scriptures of the Old Testament (Tertullian, *De testim. animae*, cap. 5). Earlier, moreover, the holy martyr Justin had firmly asserted that the teaching handed down in the sacred Scriptures of the Old Testament belonged to Christians, to whom those Scriptures were transmitted by [Col. 0786B] the Jews themselves, not without divine providence (Justin, *Cohort. ad gent.*, p. 14).

Although these books became common to Jews and Christians, it does not thereby follow that Christians were obliged to accept all the rites prescribed in them. For Christ quite rightly abolished many of them, such as sabbaths, feast days, circumcision, and the like, which had been prescribed for carnal Jews. For all these things were shadows and figures, and were given as a sign; as Tertullian clearly demonstrates in another book, they were bound to vanish and become obsolete at the advent of the reality, that is, of Christ (Tertullian, *Adv. Judae.*, caps. 3 and 4).

Thus the Christian religion, although introduced into the world by Christ during the reign of the emperor Tiberius, was nevertheless substituted for the Jewish religion, received its divine Scriptures as though by hereditary right, and, as we have said, was supported and sustained by them. But this will become clearer still after we have examined, in our customary manner, what our Tertullian maintains concerning those same sacred Scriptures of the Jews, or of the Old Testament.

## ARTICLE II. When and how the books of Sacred Scripture, first written in Hebrew, were translated into Greek by seventy-two translators; whether they worked in separate cells; with a discussion of Aristeas, Ptolemy Philadelphus, Pisistratus, their libraries and those of the Attalid kings, and Demetrius of Phalerum, the most highly approved of grammarians

Tertullian observes that from the beginning God sent [Col. 0786D] men distinguished for justice and innocence, who both recognized and proclaimed Him to be the one God (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, cap. 18). They taught that all things had been created by this one God; that man had been formed from the earth; that the succession of the seasons had been ordered; that signs of His divine majesty had been displayed; that laws had been established for morals and the proper conduct of life; and that, when the age had run its course, all men were to be raised again to life and judged, so that according to their deserts they might be visited with either eternal punishment or eternal happiness. But there in Tertullian’s text we read these words: “What rules He has determined for winning His favor, which you do not know or have abandoned.” These seemed so obscure and corrupt to Heraldus that he did not venture to pronounce any judgment upon them. Rigault, however, was not so timid: contrary to the manifest testimony of all the manuscript and printed copies, [Col. 0787A] he wished to write the particle *qui* in place of *quas*, and then *quas* in place of *quae*, though he supplied no reason. We will nevertheless readily acknowledge that this conjecture does not appear altogether unsuccessful; but it lacks the support of any manuscript whatsoever. Heraldus, later grown bolder, suspects that *prodacto* should be substituted for *producto*, that is, “consumed and completed.” He attempts to prove his emendation from other passages of Tertullian where *prodactum* is read with the same meaning. He could also have cited the fifth book of our Tertullian against Marcion, where the same word is similarly employed. But what can one do when all the manuscripts protest, and when an author uses barbarous and improper words? [Col. 0787B] Whatever his manner of speaking may have been, he plainly signifies by it the end and termination of the world (Idem, *Adv. Marcion.*, V.6).

Those men whom he calls distinguished for piety and justice were not only “preachers,” as he says, but are also called “prophets” from their office of foretelling, or announcing future events (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, cap. 18). Furthermore, they performed mighty works, or miracles, to establish belief in the divinity. Finally, they were “flooded with the divine Spirit”—these again are his own words—that is, they were so filled with it that it flowed and poured out beyond themselves.

All the marvelous things performed by them above the order of nature, and all the things foretold under the inspiration of the divine Spirit, “remain,” Tertullian says again, “in the treasuries of writings” (ibid.). But of what writings, I ask you? [Col. 0787C] Doubtless of none other than Hebrew writings. For in the same passage he relates that Ptolemy Philadelphus received from the Jews, who had formerly been Hebrews, the books written in their “own native characters” (ibid.), or in the Hebrew language, because, as he continues, “the prophets had always addressed them” (ibid.). Thus the books of Moses and the other sacred seers, written in the Hebrew language, had remained among the Hebrews and Jews, who certainly preserved them whole and uncorrupted with the utmost care.

But lest so great a treasure should always lie hidden among them alone, it was communicated first to the Greeks and then [Col. 0787D] to other nations through the celebrated translation of the seventy-two elders. Hence Tertullian says: “Nor do these sacred writings now lie hidden” (ibid.). That is how the text stands in Rigault’s edition. In the older printed editions and in our manuscripts, however, the adverb *now* is absent—and not entirely without good reason. For Tertullian signifies that they had ceased to lie hidden not merely in his own time, but from the very time of the Greek translators, as we have said.

He explains when and how this Greek translation of the sacred books was prepared by them as follows. When Ptolemy, surnamed Philadelphus, wished to enrich his library with a collection of every kind of book, [Col. 0788A] at the suggestion of Demetrius of Phalerum he requested and obtained from the Jews their sacred codices written in Hebrew, together with seventy-two men who translated them into Greek. Our Author then added in the same place: “The philosopher Menedemus also, a champion of Providence” (wrongly, in one Vatican manuscript, “a judge of the province”), “regarded them with admiration because of their agreement in judgment” (ibid.). But among the learned writers of our age there is no small disagreement over what is meant by the last words, “regarded them with admiration because of their agreement in judgment.” Some believe that they signify that Menedemus shared their opinion concerning divine providence governing all things. But Tertullian, others say, is not there discussing this providence of God, but only the Greek translation of the sacred Scriptures. [Col. 0788B] To what purpose, then, would he have inserted a reference to divine providence? They therefore think Tertullian’s meaning to be this: Menedemus examined the completed Greek translation of Sacred Scripture and perceived with admiration that it had been made in exactly the same manner through the translators’ common agreement. He was thus a champion and witness of divine providence, testifying openly that, by its wholly marvelous guidance, the Hebrew text of Scripture had been rendered into Greek by those translators in precisely the same manner.

But this, you will say, could not have occurred unless the translators had been placed separately in different cells and each had translated the Hebrew text into Greek. Yet that opinion concerning their separate cells is false. But what follows from that, if Tertullian followed earlier writers [Col. 0788C] who, after Justin Martyr, as we have shown elsewhere, consistently defended this opinion about the translators’ cells (*Apparatus*, vol. I, book II, diss. 2, cap. 7, § 5, pp. 390 ff.)? Is this not why Menedemus is called by him “a champion of Providence”: because he regarded these translators with admiration for their agreement in judgment, since, separated from one another, they translated the sacred Scriptures into Greek in one and the same manner?

But, someone will object, does not divine providence shine forth sufficiently in the fact that those translators, working together, rendered them into Greek in the same manner? Who does not know how difficult they are to understand, and that it must indeed seem marvelous that, in translating them into Greek, so many translators held one and the same judgment? [Col. 0788D] Yet who will deny that divine providence would have shone forth far more marvelously if they composed their translations separated from one another?

You will insist that these separate cells of the translators are not only indicated in obscure words, but that Aristeas is also cited as a witness, though he said nothing about them. It may be answered, however, that Tertullian’s words were sufficiently clear to people of his own age, and that he supplied only one authority because this belief concerning the translators’ distinct cells was then common and regarded as virtually beyond doubt. Who, moreover, will deny that he relates more things than are contained in Aristeas’s narrative? Whether this was in fact Tertullian’s opinion, we shall gladly hear you pronounce. We have discussed elsewhere [Col. 0789A] the history of Aristeas, the various opinions of learned men concerning it, and Menedemus (Idem, book I, diss. 2, cap. 1, pp. 223 ff.; and book III, diss. 2, cap. 3, art. 5, p. 922).

Philadelphus, moreover, who arranged for the Greek translation of Sacred Scripture, is called by Tertullian not only “most learned,” but also “most sagacious in all literature”; here, certainly, a substantive corresponding to the adjective “most sagacious” must be understood (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, cap. 18). This seems to be nothing other than “investigator” or “researcher,” for sagacity is said to be directed toward discovery or investigation. Philadelphus’s sagacity “in all literature” was therefore his most diligent search everywhere for all books written on any subject whatever. [Col. 0789B] Our Author says that, out of the highest zeal to preserve them, he had assembled a library from every kind of literature; Eusebius speaks similarly in his *Chronicle* (*Chron. Euseb.*, at the year 1734). With how great a number of books it was furnished we shall show in the following article. If anyone asks who Pisistratus was, whose zeal in this matter Tertullian says Philadelphus emulated, he may learn from these words of Aulus Gellius: “The tyrant Pisistratus is said to have been the first to make books of the liberal disciplines publicly available for reading at Athens.” There is frequent mention among various writers—Laertius, Pausanias, Thucydides, Aelian, Valerius Maximus, and others—of the tyranny of Pisistratus, which, as Herodotus relates, he recovered three times. We treated that tyranny of Pisistratus in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus*. We further read in the cited Pausanias that [Col. 0789C] the poems of Homer had once been scattered, and that Pisistratus collected them, thereby producing, as Aelian says, the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*. But it is better to hear Cicero:

> Who in those same times was more learned, or whose eloquence was better furnished with literature, than Pisistratus? He is said to have been the first to arrange the books of Homer, previously confused, in the order in which we now possess them. He was not, indeed, beneficial to his fellow citizens, but he flourished so greatly in eloquence that he excelled in letters and learning.

(Gellius, *Noct. Att.*, VI.17, p. 393; Herodotus, II, §§ 59 ff.; Laertius, *Life of Solon*, §§ 53 ff., 65 ff.; Pausanias, I, pp. 3 and 21; Thucydides, *Hist.*, VIII, p. 446; Aelian, *Var. Hist.*, VIII.13; Valerius Maximus, I, § 2; VIII.9, *External Examples*, § 2; *Apparatus*, vol. I, p. 1176; Pausanias, [Col. 0789D] VII, p. 235; Aelian, *Var. Hist.*, XIII.14; Cicero, *De orat.*, III, p. 194, line 37.)

Tertullian, however, does not venture to declare with certainty whether Philadelphus really emulated Pisistratus’s zeal in establishing so rich a library; he said only “I think,” that is, as seemed more probable to him. Yet if Vitruvius, who flourished in the age of Augustus Caesar, is to be believed, Ptolemy Philadelphus imitated in this endeavor the Attalid kings. I should like you to hear his words:

> When the Attalid kings, led by their great delight in philology, had established an excellent library at Pergamum for the common enjoyment, Ptolemy likewise, impelled by boundless emulation and eager zeal, strove with no less industry to establish one in the same manner at Alexandria. [Col. 0790A]

(Vitruvius, preface to book VII.) But others accuse him of error in this matter. For, they say, the Alexandrian kings were earlier than the Attalids. Yet nothing can be concluded from this against our Tertullian’s conjecture. Pisistratus and several others praised by Athenaeus and Epiphanius had established libraries, and Philadelphus could have imitated them (Athenaeus, *Deipnos.*, I, p. 3; Epiphanius, *De ponderibus et mensuris*, p. 166).

Furthermore, even if Tertullian’s opinion were certain, he is nevertheless lashed by Scaliger for having adorned Demetrius of Phalerum with no title other than “the most highly approved of grammarians.” “Indeed,” he says, “Demetrius’s gifts were little known to Tertullian” (Scaliger, notes on Eusebius’s *Chronicle*, at the year 1724, p. 133). Why so? By mentioning certain gifts of Demetrius, did he [Col. 0790B] deny the others? And who would believe Scaliger even on oath? Did Suetonius not somewhere remind us that grammarians were initially given the name “men of letters,” because they were able to say or prove something acutely and knowledgeably (Suetonius, *De illustribus grammaticis*, § 4, p. 821)? Tertullian praised Demetrius by precisely this name because he was discussing the administration of the library entrusted to him by Philadelphus. To whom could it better have been assigned than to the most highly approved of grammarians—that is, a man endowed with varied learning, who knew all books and their value exceedingly well?

## ARTICLE III. In which library of Philadelphus the books of Sacred Scripture were deposited; where that library was constructed; how many books it contained; whether it was wholly consumed by fire; how the sacred codices reached the Christians; and the Jews’ tax-paid liberty to read them publicly

[Col. 0790C]

After Tertullian had woven together that entire account of the codices of Sacred Scripture, whether copied in Hebrew or in Greek, he immediately relates where all those codices were preserved: “Today,” he says, “they are displayed in Ptolemy’s library at the Serapeum, together with the Hebrew texts themselves” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, cap. 18, p. 19). By these words he plainly declares that these Hebrew and Greek books of the Old Testament had been deposited in the Serapeum, that is, the temple of Serapis constructed at Alexandria. For Chrysostom expressly declares that they had been placed by this Ptolemy Philadelphus in the temple of Serapis, “in the temple of Serapis” [Col. 0790D] (Chrysostom, *Orat.* 1 *Adv. Judae.*, vol. I, p. 394, Morel ed.). Yet in that complex, which in magnificence yielded only to the Capitol, as Gellius and Ammianus Marcellinus report, there were doubtless several buildings separated from one another (Gellius, *Noct. Att.*, VI, final chapter). In some one of these, a single or double library of Philadelphus was housed. For our Author, in the words just cited, mentions several libraries, as does Ammianus Marcellinus, who, after describing the magnificence of the Serapeum, continues thus:

> In it were libraries beyond valuation; and the concordant testimony of ancient records says that seven hundred thousand volumes, assembled through the vigilant exertions of the Ptolemaic kings, were burned during the Alexandrian war under the dictatorship [Col. 0791A] of Caesar, while the city was being plundered.

(Ammianus Marcellinus, XXII.16, p. 343.)

Athenaeus, however, mentions only one. After listing certain men famous for amassing books, he says: “King Ptolemy among us, surnamed Philadelphus, purchased them all and arranged for their transfer, together with those he had bought at Athens and Rhodes, he transferred them to beautiful Alexandria into the beautiful Alexandrian library” (Athenaeus, *Deipnos.*, I, p. 3). Similarly, the author of the Eusebian *Chronicle* says: “Ptolemy Philadelphus caused the divine Scriptures to be translated from the Hebrew language into Greek speech by seventy translators; he kept them in the Alexandrian library that he had assembled for himself from every kind of literature” (Euseb., *Chron.*, at the year 1734).

Epiphanius, however, although he identifies two libraries, differs [Col. 0791B] from our Tertullian and Ammianus Marcellinus in asserting that the books of Sacred Scripture were placed in the Brucheion, while the translations of Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion were placed in the smaller Serapeum. For he concludes his account of their Greek translation with these words: The books, translated into Greek, were deposited in the first library, which had been built in the Brucheion. Still later another library was established in the Serapeum, smaller than the first and called its daughter; in it were deposited the translations of Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, and the others, after the two hundred and fiftieth year.

> Those books, translated into Greek, were deposited in the first library, which was founded in the Brucheion. Afterward another library was established in the Serapeum, far smaller than the former [Col. 0791C] and called its daughter; into it the editions of Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, and others were gathered after 250 years.

(Epiphanius, *De ponderibus et mensuris*, § 11, p. 168.) You see clearly that in this he agrees with the others in asserting that there was a double Alexandrian library, but differs in wishing one to have been founded in the Brucheion and the other, the smaller, in the Serapeum. Whether his opinion alone should be preferred to the others is for you to judge.

If you extricate yourself from this difficulty, you will immediately encounter another, far greater one. For Tertullian speaks of the Greek codices of the Old Testament as though they were still preserved and extant in that Alexandrian library in his own time. Ammianus Marcellinus, on the contrary, as we have seen, expressly [Col. 0791D] asserts that this very library was burned in the Alexandrian war under Caesar’s dictatorship (Marcellinus, XXII.16, p. 344). Aulus Gellius likewise attests that nearly seven hundred thousand volumes were then burned by auxiliary soldiers (Gellius, *Noct. Att.*, VI.17, p. 394). Dio too seems to support them, saying that Achillas came directly to Alexandria, that various battles were fought by him against Caesar the dictator, and that many fires occurred: The storehouses of both grain and a great many excellent books were burned, as is reported, “and that the storehouses or chambers containing grain and a great many excellent books were burned, as is reported” (Dio, *Roman History*, XLII, p. 228).

Moreover, Epiphanius recorded that the library [Col. 0792A] situated in the region of Alexandria called the Brucheion was deserted in his time (Epiphanius, *De ponderibus et mensuris*, § 10, p. 166). Yet, as we have seen, he had said that the codices of Sacred Scripture were deposited in it. Therefore, if this library was deserted—and all the more if the Alexandrian libraries had already been burned in Caesar’s age—how, I ask you, could Tertullian say: “Today they are displayed in Ptolemy’s library at the Serapeum, together with the Hebrew texts themselves, and the Jews read them publicly” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, cap. 18)? Some answer that there were at least two libraries there. One, they say, could have burned, while the other, in which those books had been deposited, remained entirely intact. But this answer plainly conflicts with Epiphanius himself, who asserts that the library [Col. 0792B] in which these books had been stored no longer existed in his time. Others therefore answer that, even if this and all the other Alexandrian libraries were burned, not all the books were consumed, and the Hebrew and Greek codices of Sacred Scripture were rescued from the fire.

In confirmation of this opinion one may adduce the testimony of Seneca, and after him Paulus Orosius, who record that only four hundred thousand books were then consumed (Seneca, *De tranquillitate animi*, cap. 9, p. 663; Orosius, VII.15). Yet if we believe Marcellinus and Gellius, previously cited by us, seven hundred thousand books had been deposited in that library. Three hundred thousand, therefore, were withdrawn from the flames. Doubtless, then, [Col. 0792C] these Hebrew and Greek volumes of Sacred Scripture were among that number, God so providing.

What of the fact that Chrysostom firmly asserts that the Greek translation of the prophetic books was preserved down to his own time in the Serapeum, or, as he puts it, the temple of Serapis? These are his Greek words: Καὶ μέχρι νῦν ἐκεῖ τῶν προφητῶν αἱ ἐρμηνευθεῖσαι βίβλοι μένουσι (“And to this day the translated books of the prophets remain there”) (Chrysostom, *Orat.* 1 *Adv. Judae.*, p. 595, vol. I).

Furthermore, even if someone should clearly prove, against this opinion of Chrysostom and others, that those codices were burned, he still could not deny that copies of them had been multiplied before that fire, and that several Hebrew copies had been transcribed by the Jews and Greek copies by others, and carefully preserved. [Col. 0792D] There can indeed be no room for doubt that many reached the Christians. For not a few Greek testimonies are found cited in the books of the New Testament.

Furthermore, Hebrew copies were preserved with the greatest diligence by those same Jews. For our Tertullian said of those living in his own time: “The Jews read them publicly; the liberty is tax-paid; it is open to all every Sabbath.” Do you hear how clearly he affirms that the Jews, assembling in their synagogues every Sabbath, publicly and in everyone’s hearing read these sacred codices written in their native tongue? This custom had indeed become established from ancient times, both before and after Christ the Lord. [Col. 0793A] For concerning Paul and his companions it is recorded in the Acts of the Apostles: “Passing through Perga, they came to Antioch in Pisidia and, entering the synagogue on the Sabbath day, sat down. After the reading of the Law and the Prophets, Paul, at the Jews’ request, addressed them” (Acts 13:14 ff.). Later you will find these words committed to writing in the same book: “From ancient times Moses has had in every city those who proclaim him in the synagogues, where he is read every Sabbath” (ibid. 15:21). This assuredly could not have happened unless the Jews already possessed these codices and devoted the greatest care to preserving them uncorrupted and wholly intact.

But what, you will ask, does Tertullian mean when he says that the Jews’ liberty to hold their assemblies every [Col. 0793B] Sabbath and read their books there was tax-paid? Some believe that these words designate permission granted to them by Philadelphus, provided that they paid a modest annual tax. But by what argument will it be proved that this tax was exacted by Philadelphus, who valued the sacred books of the Old Testament so highly and had heaped the greatest gifts upon their translators? It therefore seems far more probable to others that Tertullian is referring to the tribute imposed upon the Jews by the Roman emperors in order that they might be permitted to assemble each Sabbath according to their custom. Some think that the following words of Suetonius, dealing with the emperor Domitian, should be understood of this tribute:

> Besides all the others, the Jewish tax was exacted with the utmost severity. Informers denounced to its collectors those who either lived a Jewish life within the city without formally professing it, or, concealing their origin, [Col. 0793C] had not paid the tributes imposed upon that nation.

(Suetonius, book VIII, *Life of Domitian*, § 12.) But there he signifies that the Jews had to pay a tax in order to purchase the free profession of their sect, though it had then been increased rather than imposed for the first time. For in Dio’s epitome Xiphilinus relates that, after Jerusalem had been captured, the emperor Titus ordered the Jews who professed their ancestral laws to pay two drachmas annually to Capitoline Jupiter (Xiphilinus, epitome of Dio, *Life of Vespasian*, p. 318). Do you wish to hear his Greek words? Here they are: Καὶ ἀπ` ἐκείνου δίδραγμον ἐτάχθη τοὺς τὰ πάτρια αὐτῶν ἔθη περιστέλλοντας τῷ Καπιτωλίῳ Διῒ κατ` ἔτος ἀποφέρειν (“And from that time those observing their ancestral customs were ordered to pay two drachmas annually to Capitoline Zeus”).

They think, moreover, that Juvenal alluded to this tribute in the following verses:

> [Col. 0793D] Now the grove and shrine of the sacred spring are rented  
> To Jews, whose furniture is a basket and some hay.  
> For every tree has been ordered to pay a fee to the people,  
> And the wood begs now that the Muses have been expelled.

(Juvenal, *Satire* 3, lines 13 ff.)

This is therefore rightly called by Tertullian a “tax-paid liberty,” because they could not convene their assemblies or publicly read in their synagogues their sacred codices, most carefully preserved wherever they had been dispersed, unless the tribute had first been paid. From these Jews, therefore—their bitterest enemies—the Christians had received those codices of the Old Testament, about whose truth and integrity no person of sound mind will ever entertain doubt. For, as the ancient author of the book *Against Five Heresies*, once attributed to Augustine, rightly argues against the pagans:

> [Col. 0794A] The Jew has in his bookcase the codex in which these things are found written. My enemy is my witness. Ask him yourself; open it, read, believe.

(Augustine, *Appendix*, vol. VIII, *Against Five Heresies*, cap. 7, p. 9.)

The Christians therefore possessed these books exactly as they had been transmitted to them by the Jews. For they could not alter anything in their text without immediately being convicted of falsehood by the Jews, their enemies, who read them daily. Let us now investigate with our Tertullian what and how great their authority is.

## ARTICLE IV. How Tertullian demonstrates the authority of Sacred Scripture from its very great antiquity; what are the substances and materials of the ancient style; cities renowned for their histories; and hoary records

[Col. 0794B]

Tertullian openly vindicates the supreme authority of Sacred Scripture by a twofold argument. The first he derives from its very great antiquity, which we have discussed repeatedly in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus*; the second from its divinity, or from the divine Spirit under whose inspiration all its books were committed to writing. He presses the former argument against the pagans all the more willingly because, acknowledging its force and weight, they endeavored thereby to defend and confirm their own religion, false though it was. For he addresses them all in these words: “Among you, it is virtually a religious principle to assert credibility on the basis of dates” (Tertullian, *Apologet.*, cap. 19); that is, you contend that your religion and the worship of false gods are defended by their antiquity.

[Col. 0794C] Yet if this argument carries any weight, you cannot detract from the credibility of our Scriptures. For, Tertullian says, “the repository of a single prophet” (that is, the books of Moses) “surpasses in age all the substances and materials . . . of every ancient literary work of yours” (ibid.); that is, absolutely all your compositions and their subjects. Indeed, he says, it even surpasses their “origins,” that is, their beginnings and causes; their “orders and channels,” that is, the courses and conduits through which they have reached you.
