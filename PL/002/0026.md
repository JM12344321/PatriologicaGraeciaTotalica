For from the beginning of things, their Creator was made known together with them, since they were brought forth precisely so that God might be known. Even if Moses, who came later, appears to have been the first to enshrine the God of the world in the temple of his writings, recognition of God is not therefore to be dated from the Pentateuch; for the whole tenor of Moses’ writing does not establish knowledge of the Creator for the first time, but recounts it from the beginning—from Paradise and Adam—not as something to be reckoned from Egypt and Moses. Indeed, the greater part [Note: [Col. 0257D] “The greater part of the human race.” That is, the consensus of the nations, the common consciousness. RIG.] of the human race, who do not even know the name of Moses, much less [Col. 0257C] his written document, nevertheless know the God of Moses, even though so great [Note: [Col. 0257D] *Tanta*, Semler.] a dominion of his is overshadowed by idolatry [Note: [Col. 0257D] *Idololatriae*, Rhenanus.]. Yet they speak of him separately, as though by his proper name, simply as “God,” and as “the God of gods”; and they say, “If God grants it,” “What pleases God,” and “I commend it to God.” See whether they know him whom they attest to be capable of all things. Nor do they owe this to any books of Moses. The soul is earlier than prophecy. For consciousness of God has been the soul’s endowment from the beginning; it is one and the same in Egyptians, Syrians, and people of Pontus. Souls call the God of the Jews “God.” [Note: [Col. 0258D] Semler inserts *et*.] Do not, barbarous heretic, place Abraham before the world. Even if the Creator had been the God of a single family, he would still not have been later than your god, since he was known even to the people of Pontus before him. Accept, therefore, the pattern from your predecessor: you who are uncertain, from him who is certain; you who are unknown, from him who is known. God will never be hidden; God will never be absent. He will always be understood, always heard; he will even be seen, in whatever way he wills. [Col. 0258A] God has witnesses: the entirety of what we are and that within which we exist. Thus he is proved to be both God and the one God, since he is not unknown, while another is still struggling to be proved.

## CHAPTER XI

“And rightly so,” they say; “for who is not better known to his own than to strangers?” No one. I hold you to this statement as well. For how can anything be foreign to God, to whom nothing would be foreign if he existed? For it belongs to the very nature of God that all things should be his and all things should pertain to him—unless he wished immediately to hear from us: “What, then, has he to do with strangers?” He will hear that more fully in its proper place. For the present, it is enough that no being is proved whose possessions furnish no proof of him. For just as the Creator is thereby both God and God beyond doubt, because all things are his and nothing is foreign to him, so the other is for that very reason not God, because all things are not his and are therefore [Col. 0258B] foreign to him. Indeed, if the universe belongs to the Creator, I no longer see any place for another god. All things are full and occupied by their own author. If some space lies vacant in created things for some divinity, it will clearly lie vacant for a false one. [Note: [Col. 0258D] *False*, Rhenanus, Junius.] Truth lies open to falsehood. Why does so great a host of idols not receive Marcion’s god somewhere among them?

Accordingly, on this point also I demand conformity to the pattern of the Creator: God ought to have been known from the works of some world, human being, and age belonging properly to himself. For even the world’s error assumed gods for this reason, gods whom it sometimes admits to have been human beings, because each of them appears to have provided something for the uses and conveniences of life. [Note: [Col. 0257D] “For even the world’s error,” etc. The author discussed this most extensively above, *Apology*, ch. 2, also demonstrating [Col. 0258D] the vanity of those reasons for which the world presumed beings to be gods—namely, because they had provided something for human benefit. PAM.] Thus, following the pattern of God, people believed that to establish or disclose something suitable and necessary for human affairs was divine. So thoroughly was authority for false divinity borrowed [Note: [Col. 0258D] Semler inserts *si*; Rhenanus reads *si false*.] [Col. 0258C] from the source from which that of the true divinity had first proceeded. Marcion’s god ought at least to have brought forth one little chickpea of his own, so that he might be proclaimed some new Triptolemus. [Note: [Col. 0258D] “At least one little chickpea,” etc. Vatican MS 2 reads *saltim*, in the old manner, but I was unwilling to alter it. He fittingly employs the metaphor of the chickpea because Triptolemus was the first to transmit the method of sowing. Hence the author’s words in the book *To Herennius*: “If Triptolemus, when he distributed seed to everyone.” PAM.]

Or else produce a reason worthy of God why he created nothing, if he exists; for he would have created something if he had existed. This follows from precisely that prior judgment by which it is evident that our God exists only because he created this entire universe. For once and for all this rule will stand: they cannot both confess that the Creator is God and fail to prove, according to the pattern of him whom they themselves and everyone else acknowledge as God, the being whom they likewise wish to be believed a god. Since no one doubts that the Creator is God for the very reason that he created this entire universe, by that same reason no one ought to believe that one who created nothing is also God, unless perhaps some explanation is produced. This explanation must necessarily be seen as twofold: either he was unwilling to create anything, or he was unable. [Col. 0258D] There is no third possibility. But inability is unworthy of God; I wish to inquire whether unwillingness is worthy of him.

Tell me, [Col. 0259A] Marcion: did your god wish to be known at any time whatsoever? Or [Note: [Col. 0259D] *Annon?*, Semler; *an*, Franek.] did he descend, preach, suffer, and rise again with some purpose other than to become known? And without doubt, if he became known, he willed it; for nothing would occur in relation to him unless he willed it. Why, then, did he take such pains to procure knowledge of himself that he was displayed in the disgrace of flesh—a disgrace all the greater if the flesh was false? [Note: [Col. 0259D] *False*, Rhenanus.] For it is more shameful if he also lied about the substance of his body, he who even accepted upon himself the Creator’s curse by being suspended from a tree. How much more honorably would he have prepared the way for knowledge of himself through some evidences of his own workmanship—especially since he had to become known in opposition to one by whom, from the beginning, he had not been known through works.

And what sort of arrangement is this: that the Creator, who according to the Marcionites did not know that another god existed above him and who even swore and affirmed that he alone existed (Isa. [Col. 0259B] 40:8), fortified knowledge of himself with such great works, although under the presumption that he was the only God he need not have taken such care over it; whereas the higher god, though knowing that the lower god was so well furnished, made no provision for the equipment by which he himself might be recognized? [Note: [Col. 0259D] “Made no provision for the equipment by which he might be recognized.” That is, an adornment or prerogative of creation through which he might be known by what was, as it were, his own apparatus and his own glory. LE PR.] For he ought to have created works still more distinguished and sublime [Note: [Col. 0259D] *Superiora*, Ciacconius.], so that, following the Creator’s pattern, he might be known as God from his works and, from works more honorable than the Creator’s, as greater and nobler than the Creator.

## CHAPTER XII

Besides, even if we could confess that he exists, we would have to argue that he exists without a cause. For one who possessed no thing would exist without a cause, because every thing is a cause for the existence of someone to whom that thing belongs. Now, insofar as nothing ought to exist without a cause—that is, without a thing—because [Col. 0259C] if it exists without a cause, it is just as though it did not exist, since it does not possess the thing that causes its existence, namely the thing itself [Note: [Col. 0259D] *Res ipsa*, Semler.]; to that same extent I shall believe it more worthy of God not to exist than to exist without a cause. For he exists without a cause who, because he possesses no thing, possesses no cause. But God ought not to exist without a cause, that is, without a thing belonging to him. Thus, whenever I show that he exists without a cause, supposing that he exists, I establish that he does not exist; for if he had existed, he certainly would not have existed without a cause.

So too I say that he seeks faith from humanity without cause, since humanity has customarily believed in a God whose form is established by the authority of his works [Note: [Col. 0259D] *Formatam*, Junius.], whereas he provided nothing of the kind through which humanity learned of God. For even if many believe in him, [Col. 0260A] they do not therefore believe on rational grounds, since they possess no pledge of his divinity—works worthy of God. Accordingly, by reason of this cessation and failure of works, he is closely allied both to shamelessness [Note: [Col. 0260D] Perhaps *imprudentiae*, “imprudence.” Editors.] and to malice: [Note: [Col. 0259D] “Allied to malice.” A participant in malice. Cicero uses that expression in this sense concerning Sulla: “Allied to this suspicion.” LE PR.] to shamelessness [Note: [Col. 0260D] The same.], because he seeks a faith not owed to him, having provided nothing by which that faith might be prepared; and to malice, because he has made many guilty of unbelief by providing nothing to support faith.

## CHAPTER XIII

When at this stage we expel a god whom no creation has marked out beforehand by its testimony—a condition as peculiarly proper to and worthy of God as the Creator’s—the utterly shameless Marcionites curl their noses [Note: [Col. 0259D] “The utterly shameless Marcionites curling their noses.” Mocking slyly and holding people up to ridicule with hooked nose. LE PR.] and turn to disparaging the Creator’s works. “A magnificent work indeed,” they say, “and worthy of God—the world! Is the Creator, then, by no means God?” Certainly he is God. “Then neither is the world unworthy of God.” For God made nothing unworthy of himself, even if he made the world for humanity and not for himself, and even if every work is inferior to its craftsman. And yet, if it is unworthy of God to have made something of a particular kind, how much more unworthy of God is it to have made absolutely nothing, not even something unworthy, by which he might have given grounds for hoping that he was the author of worthier things!

To say something, then, even about the supposed unworthiness of this world—whose very Greek name signifies adornment and ordered beauty, not filth [Note: [Col. 0259D] A well-known saying of Pythagoras. Hence also Aristotle’s statement in the book *On the World*: “You may fittingly call the universe itself κόσμος (*kosmos*), that is, a composite structure of things arranged harmoniously in an orderly system.” EDITORS, after PAM.]—those very professors of wisdom, from whose talents every heresy draws its life, declared its allegedly unworthy substances to be gods: Thales declared water a god; Heraclitus, fire; Anaximenes, air; Anaximander, all the heavenly bodies; Strato, heaven and earth; Zeno, air and ether; Plato, the stars, which in his discussion of the world he calls a fiery race of gods. Considering, [Col. 0260C] that is, the magnitude, force, power, dignity, beauty, assistance, reliability, and law of each of the elements—which work together in the generation, nourishment, dissolution, and renewal of all things—most natural philosophers were afraid to assign a beginning and an end to the world, [Note: [Col. 0260D] *Constare*, Semler.] lest its substances, so great as they are, should thereby be regarded as less divine. These are also worshiped by the Magi of the Persians [Note: [Col. 0260D] *Quos*, Semler.], the hierophants of the Egyptians, [Note: [Col. 0260D] “And the hierophants of the Egyptians.” They were called ἱεροφάνται (*hierophantai*) because they displayed or performed the sacred rites; they were a class of priests among the Egyptians. PAM.] and the gymnosophists of India. [Note: [Col. 0260D] “The gymnosophists of India.” In *Apology* 32 he had likewise spoken of the gymnosophists as Indians. Sidonius Apollinaris, *Letters* VIII.3, however, appears to maintain that the gymnosophists belonged to Ethiopia and the Brahmans to India: “Now, devoting yourself wholly to reading, travel in a sense even yourself to the gymnosophists of the Ethiopians and the Brahmans of the Indians.” Clement of Alexandria does not depart from that position in *Stromata* I: παρ᾽ Ἰνδοῖς ἀπὸ τῶν βραχμάνων, “among the Indians, from the Brahmans.” LE PR.]

Common popular superstition, too, which is shared by idolatry generally, when it is ashamed of the names and fables of ancient dead persons represented in its images, retreats to interpretations based on natural phenomena and veils its disgrace with ingenuity. It figures Jupiter as a fiery substance and his Juno as an airy one, according to the sound of their Greek names; likewise Vesta as fire, the Camenae as water, and the Great Mother as the earth—reaped of her seeds, plowed by arms, and watered by baths. So too Osiris, [Note: [Col. 0261B] *Osirin*, Paris, Franek.] who is always buried, sought alive, and found with rejoicing, is interpreted as signifying the reliable recurrence of crops, living elements, and the returning year; just as they philosophize that the lions of Mithras are mysteries of a dry and burning nature. [Note: [Col. 0261C] “Just as they philosophize that the lions of Mithras are mysteries of a dry and burning nature.” Images of lions were employed in the mysteries of Mithras, or the Sun. They were *sacramenta*, that is, signs or mysteries of the sun’s dry and burning nature. For the heat grows intense and springs and rivers dry up when the Sun is said to enter Leo; then, finally, the abduction of Ceres’ daughter is celebrated—that is, the harvest takes place. The scholiast on Aratus: Κόρην γὰρ εἶναι τοὺς καρποὺς λέγουσι, καὶ μητέρα τὴν γῆν· καὶ γὰρ παρ᾽ Αἰγυπτίοις κατὰ τὸν Ἐμφὶ μῆνα, ὅτε ἐν λέοντι γίνεται ὁ ἥλιος, ἡ τῆς Κόρης ἁρπαγὴ τελειοῦται. “For they say that the Maiden is the crops and the Mother is the earth. Indeed, among the Egyptians, in the month Emphi, when the sun is in Leo, the abduction of the Maiden is celebrated.” Germanicus, on Leo:

“When Phoebus’ more violent orbit reaches him,  
The summer, already kindled in Cancer, will then be doubled;  
Then the waters run thin, then the earth is most sorrowful,  
And the joyful plowman safely stores his dense-packed grain.”

EDITORS, FROM RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0261D] “They philosophize about Mithras.” There is scarcely anyone who does not know that Mithras is a god of the Persians, and indeed their foremost god. Hesychius: μίθρης ὁ πρῶτος ἐν Πέρσαις θεός, “Mithras, the foremost god among the Persians”; there he also says that Mithras is the Sun. He was portrayed with the face of a lion, the most ardent of animals, because, as St. Jerome teaches in Book I *Against Jovinian*, he was begotten from the heat of lust, in the manner of Erichthonius. Hence the expressions “burning nature” are also used here. LE PR.]

It is enough that substances higher in position or status have been more readily regarded as gods than as unworthy of God. I shall descend to lowly things. A single tiny flower from the hedges—I do not say from the meadows; a single little shell from any sea—I do not say the Red Sea; a single feather of a grouse—I say nothing of the peacock: will any of these declare to you that the Creator is a filthy craftsman? [Col. 0261B]

## CHAPTER XIV

But when you also ridicule the smaller animals, which the supreme Craftsman deliberately enlarged in ingenuity or strength—thus teaching that greatness is proved in smallness, just as power is proved in weakness, according to the Apostle (2 Cor. 12:9)—imitate, if you can, the structures of the bee, the storehouses of the ant, the webs of the spider, the threads of the silkworm. Endure, if you can, those very creatures of your bed and mat, the poisons of the blister beetle, the darts of the fly, and both the trumpet and the lance of the mosquito. What will the greater creatures be like, when things so small can either help or harm you, so that even among small things you may not despise the Creator?

Finally, turn your attention to yourself; consider the human being within and without. You will find pleasing [Col. 0262A] at least this work of our God, which that lord of yours, the “better god,” loved: for its sake he labored to descend from the third heaven into these paltry elements; for its sake he was even crucified in this little chamber belonging to the Creator. Yet to this very day he has not rejected the Creator’s water, with which he washes his own; nor the oil with which he anoints them; nor the mixture of honey and milk with which he feeds them as infants; nor the bread by which he presents his own body [Note: [Col. 0261D] “The bread by which he presents his own body.” Bellarmine explains as follows, against the Calvinists who misuse these words: “The verb *to represent* is ambiguous: for it signifies making some thing present, either in the thing itself or in some sign [Col. 0262C] or image. And, to omit the testimonies of Cicero and even of the holy Fathers, who frequently use this word to signify that some thing is exhibited as truly and properly present, Tertullian himself can furnish examples of both meanings. In Book IV *Against Marcion*, around the middle, he says, ‘Thus, now presenting him: “This is my Son,”’ etc. There he says that God the Father, who had once promised the Son, presented that same Son when he said on Mount Tabor, ‘This is my Son’; there he very plainly calls exhibiting something in the thing itself ‘representing.’ And in the book *Against Praxeas* he calls the Son the representative of the Father, because the Father is truly in the Son. But the same author, in his book *On Repentance*, distinguishes adultery committed in the act itself [Col. 0262D] from adultery represented in the mind through lust; there ‘to represent’ means to exhibit something not in the thing itself but in an image. Since, therefore, the word is ambiguous, it cannot in any way be rightly inferred that Tertullian is speaking of representation in a sign rather than in reality. Indeed, rather the contrary: because that which is exhibited in reality is said more properly to be represented than that which is exhibited only in a sign, it will more probably be inferred that Tertullian speaks of a representation by which the Lord’s body is exhibited in reality. Hence Peter Martyr, in his book *Against Gardiner*, objection 160, when treating this passage of Tertullian, admits that nothing certain can be concluded from this passage alone, but that Tertullian’s meaning must be sought from other passages (*On the Sacrament of the Eucharist*, Book II, ch. 7).” And indeed the most learned cardinal cites several testimonies from our Author by which the doctrine of the real presence is confirmed in a remarkable manner. See especially *On the Resurrection of the Flesh*, ch. 8. EDITORS.]; even in his own sacraments he is reduced to begging from the Creator.

But you, a disciple above the master and a servant above the lord, think more loftily than he does, destroying the things he desires. I wish to see whether you act at least in good faith, and whether you yourself do not crave the things you destroy. You oppose heaven, yet you seize the freedom of heaven for your dwellings. You despise the earth, though it is clearly the womb of your flesh, which is now hostile to you [Note: [Col. 0261C] *Inimice*, Semler.]; and you wrench all its marrow from it for food. You reject the sea—but only until you reach its provisions, which you regard as holier food. If I offer you a rose, you will not disdain the Creator. Hypocrite, prove yourself a Marcionite by self-starvation (*apokarterēsis*) [Note: [Col. 0262B] *Hypocritam! ut*, Semler; *hypocrita, muta*, Rhenanus.]—that is, one who repudiates the Creator; for this practice ought to have been pursued among you as martyrdom if the world displeased you. Into whatever material you are dissolved, you make use of the Creator’s substance. [Note: [Col. 0262D] “Prove yourself a Marcionite by self-starvation.” [Col. 0263D] Even if, from hatred of the Creator, you abstain from everything created and waste away after renouncing all the supports of life, nevertheless, into whatever material your limbs are dissolved, they will be resolved into one of the substances established by God the Creator. RIG.—According to Suidas, the Greeks use ἀποκαρτερεῖν (*apokarterein*) when someone ends his life either by starvation or by hanging; hence ἀποκαρτέρησις (*apokarterēsis*). The author also uses this term in the *Apology*, writing: “Lycurgus chose ἀποκαρτέρησιν (*apokarterēsin*) because the Spartans had amended his laws.” Moreover, he here calls Marcion a hypocrite because he destroys and despises the Creator’s things not in good faith or sincerely, but only in pretense. RHEN.] [Note: [Col. 0262C] *Substantia*, Semler.] How great is the obstinacy of your hardness! You depreciate the things in which you both live and die.

## CHAPTER XV

After this—or before it—when you have said that he too possesses his own creation, his own world, and his own heaven, [Col. 0263A] we shall indeed examine that third heaven if we come to the point of scrutinizing your apostle. Meanwhile, whatever that substance may be, it certainly ought to have appeared together with its own god. But as matters now stand, how is it that the Lord was revealed in the twelfth year of Tiberius Caesar, yet by the fifteenth year of the emperor Severus absolutely no substance had been discovered which, as necessarily surpassing the Creator’s paltry things, would have ceased to be hidden once its own lord and author was no longer hidden? [Note: [Col. 0263D] “That the Lord, in the twelfth year of Tiberius Caesar.” This passage is reckoned among Tertullian’s errors. We discussed it earlier. He evidently thought that Christ began his preaching in the twelfth year of Tiberius and suffered death three years later. LE PR.] And therefore, if that substance could not be manifested in this world, how did its Lord appear in this world? If this world contained its Lord, why could it not contain the substance—unless perhaps that substance was greater than its Lord?

The question now concerns place, and it pertains both to that higher world and to its God himself. For see: if he too possesses his own world beneath himself and above the Creator, he must have made it in a place, in the space that lay vacant between his own feet and the Creator’s head. Therefore God himself was also in a place and made the world in a place; and that place will now be greater than both God and the world. For whatever contains something is necessarily greater than that which it contains. We must also see [Note: [Col. 0264D] “Whether any leftover plots still lie vacant there.” *Subsiciva* or *subseciva* frequently occurs among writers on the regulation of boundaries. Agennius Urbicus: “When, however, an allotment was made within an allotted tract of land, the entire extent could not be assigned to the veterans within four boundaries. Something remained there which took its name from the line that cut it off: a *subsecivum*, or leftover plot.” RIG.] whether any leftover plots still lie vacant there [Note: [Col. 0263C] *Subcisiva*, Franek; *subscidiva, subsciva*, Rhenanus.], in which some third god [Note: [Col. 0263C] *Aliquis*, Paris.] might squeeze himself together with his own world. Therefore begin now to count gods. For the place too will be a god—not only because it is greater than God, but because it is unborn and unmade, and therefore eternal and equal to God, since God has always existed within it.

Next, if that god fashioned his world from some underlying matter—unborn, unmade, and coeval with God—just as Marcion believes concerning the Creator, you reduce this also to the majesty of the place that enclosed both god and matter: two gods. For matter too is God according to the defining form of divinity, since it is unborn, unmade, and eternal. Or if he fashioned the world from nothing, Marcion will be compelled to believe the same about the Creator, to whom he assigns matter as the substance of the world. Yet that other god too will have had to make his world from matter, since the same reasoning that was opposed to the Creator will also confront him as equally God. Thus, for the moment, count three gods of Marcion for me: Maker, Place, and Matter.

Likewise he locates the Creator in a place, necessarily judging him according to the same condition; and he subjects matter to him, necessarily regarding it as unborn and unmade and, on that account, eternal like the Lord. Furthermore, by ascribing evil to matter—an evil unborn as matter is unborn, unmade as it is unmade, and eternal as it is eternal—he has thereby made a fourth god. You therefore have three substances of divinity in the higher regions and four in the lower. When their respective Christs are added to these—the one who appeared under Tiberius and the other who is promised by the Creator—Marcion plainly suffers misrepresentation from those who presume that he introduces two gods, since he assigns nine, although unknowingly.

## CHAPTER XVI

Since, therefore, no other world appears, just as its God does not appear either, the consequence is that they divide the two classes of things, visible and invisible, between two divine authors, and claim the invisible things for their own god. But who could persuade himself—unless possessed by a heretical spirit—that invisible things belong to one who produced no visible things beforehand, rather than to the one who, by making visible things, also established confidence in the invisible, since it is far more reasonable to assent on the basis of some examples than on the basis of none? We shall also see to which author the Apostle (Col. 1:16) assigns the invisible things when we have examined him too.

For now, by means of generally accepted perceptions and sound arguments, we prepare confidence in the advocacy of the Scriptures that is to follow, [Note: [Col. 0264D] “The advocacy of the Scriptures.” *Advocatio* properly means the crowd of advocates and friends who stand beside someone in court. Here he uses it to mean a defense. So too in the book *On Patience*: “Thus both advocacy and laughter are promised to such people as consolation.” RHEN.] confirming that this diversity of visible and invisible things must so fully be assigned to the Creator [Note: [Col. 0264C] *Deo*, Wouwer manuscript; *a Deo creatori*, Rhenanus.], since his entire operation consists of diversities: corporeal and incorporeal, animate and inanimate, vocal and mute, mobile and stationary, generative and sterile, dry and moist [Note: [Col. 0264C] *Siccis*, Rhenanus.], hot and cold.

Diversity has likewise composed the human being himself, both in body and in sensation. Some limbs are strong, others weak; some honorable, others dishonorable; some paired, others single; some corresponding, others unequal. So too in sensation there is now joy, now distress; now love, now hatred; now anger, now gentleness. If this is so—if this whole created order has been harmoniously composed of things opposed to one another—then invisible things must belong with visible things and must not be assigned to any author other than the one to whom their opposites also belong. These oppositions mark the Creator himself as diverse [Note: [Col. 0264D] *Notant*, Pamelius.]: commanding what he had forbidden, forbidding what he had commanded, striking and healing. Why, in this one category alone, do they understand him as uniform, assigning him only visible things, both life and death, evils and peace (Sir. 11:14; Isa. 45:7)? And certainly, if those invisible things are greater than the visible creatures—which are themselves great in their proper sphere—it is likewise fitting that the greater things belong to him to whom the great things also belong; for neither great things, much less greater ones, can properly belong to one of whom not even small things appear.

## CHAPTER XVII

Pressed hard by these arguments, [Note: [Col. 0265D] “Pressed hard by these arguments, they burst out saying.” The Marcionites, evidently, who taunted God the Creator as the creator of worms, locusts, bedbugs, and so forth. Arnobius, Book II *Against the Nations*: “For it does not follow, if we deny that flies, beetles, bedbugs, small moths, weevils, and larger moths are the work of the omnipotent King….” But St. Jerome speaks with still greater clarity in the preface to his commentary on the *Epistle to Philemon*: “But if they do not think that small things belong to those to whom great things also belong, they ought, following Valentinus, Marcion, and Apelles, to introduce for me one creator of the ant, worms, mosquitoes, and locusts, and another of heaven, earth, sea, and angels. Or does it belong rather to the same power not to deny in smaller things the ingenuity you have exercised in greater things?” LE PR.] they burst out saying: “This single work is enough for our god: that he delivered the human being by his supreme and preeminent goodness, which is to be preferred to all the locusts.” O greater God, whose work, though so great, could be found nowhere except in the human being belonging to the lesser god!

In truth, you must first prove that he exists through the things by which God ought to be proved—through works—and only afterward through benefits. For the first question is whether he exists; then, what sort of being he is. The one will be discerned from works, the other from benefits. [Note: [Col. 0265C] *Dei dignoscitur*, Pamelius.] Besides, his existence is not established merely because he is said to have delivered humanity; rather, if his existence has been established, then it will also be said that he delivered humanity, so that whether he did deliver it may likewise be established. For he could both have existed and not have delivered it. How, then, will he also be believed to exist because he is said to have delivered humanity, when he could have existed without delivering it?

Now, having been led aside at this point from the question concerning the unknown God, [Note: [Col. 0265C] *Deducta*, Semler; *deductis*, Venetian edition; *deductus*, Gorze manuscript.] it has been sufficiently established both that he created nothing and that he ought to have created something so that he might be known from his works. For if he had existed, he ought to have been known—and certainly from the beginning of things, since it would not have been fitting for God to remain hidden. I must return to the origin of the question of the unknown God, so that I may also shake out its other branches. For the first question concerning one who subsequently brought himself to people’s knowledge must be: Why later, and not from the beginning of things? Since he was necessary to them as God—and, as the better God, all the more necessary—he certainly ought not to have remained hidden. For it cannot be said that there was neither material nor cause for making God known, since from the beginning humanity existed in the world—the humanity he now assists—and so did the Creator’s malice, against which he assists humanity as the good God. Therefore he either failed to recognize both the cause and the material [Col. 0266A] of his necessary revelation, or hesitated, or was unable, or was unwilling. All these alternatives are unworthy of God, especially of the supremely good God. But we shall also treat this subject fully elsewhere by reproaching the lateness of his revelation, just as here we provide only a demonstration of it.

## CHAPTER XVIII

Come, then: let him have advanced into people’s knowledge when he willed, when he was able, when the fated hour arrived. LE PR.] or some malignant stars, or Saturn in quartile, or Mars in trine. For the Marcionites are very much astrologers [Note: [Col. 0266D] “For the Marcionites are very much astrologers.” They declared that they lived by the stars—that is, that their lives depended on the motion of the stars—although they refused to acknowledge the Creator of those stars. Here the name “mathematicians” does not condemn the whole of mathematics, but only that superstition which, though it is not a part of mathematics—I mean judicial astrology—wishes to be adopted among the branches of mathematical learning, something that cannot be done. He attacked judicial astrology elsewhere; see *On Idolatry*. LE PR.], and they do not blush even at deriving their life from the Creator’s own stars.

Here too we must consider the quality of the revelation: whether he became known in a manner worthy of God, so that it may be established whether he was truly made known, and consequently that the being whose revelation is shown to have been worthy of God may be believed to exist. For things worthy of God will prove him to be God. We maintain that God must first be known through nature and then recognized through teaching: through nature from his works, and through teaching from proclamations. But one who possesses no created order has no natural instruments available to him. Therefore he ought at least to have accomplished the revelation of himself through proclamations, especially since he had to be revealed in opposition to one who, through so many and such great works both of creation and proclamation, had nevertheless scarcely won human faith. [Note: [Col. 0266D] “Had filled human beings with faith.” So reads Ursinus’ copy. The common reading, which is also that of the Pithou manuscript, *hominem fide impleverat*, “had filled the human being with faith,” can nevertheless be retained. RIG.] [Note: [Col. 0266C] *Hominem fide*, Pamelius.]

How, then, was he revealed? If through human conjecture, deny that God [Note: [Col. 0266C] Pamelius: *Est, si per humanam conjecturam negat Deum*; Semler: *negas, Deum*; perhaps *nego*, Editors.] can be known otherwise than through himself. This conclusion follows not only from an appeal to the Creator’s pattern, but also from the condition both of divine greatness and of human smallness, lest the human being appear greater than God by having somehow dragged into the public sphere of recognition, through his own powers, a god who did not spontaneously wish to become known. [Note: [Col. 0266C] Latinus inserts *modo*; Pamelius reads *qui non eum non*.] For, as the experience of every age shows, human smallness knows more readily how to fashion gods for itself than how to pursue the true God whom people already understand by nature.

Otherwise, if a human being is to devise a god in this way—as Romulus devised Consus, Tatius Cloacina, Hostilius Pavor, Metellus Alburnus, and someone before the present time Antinous—then the same will be permitted to others. We know Marcion as a shipmaster, not as a king or emperor.
