## CHAPTER XIII

Let us nevertheless take comfort in the gentleness and mercy of God in such a way that we do not grant concessions to necessity even as far as associations with idolatry. Rather, let us avoid every breath of it, like a pestilence, even from afar—not only in the matters which we have already set forth, but throughout the whole succession of human superstition, whether devoted to its gods, the dead, or kings, since it pertains to the same unclean spirits: sometimes through sacrifices and priesthoods, sometimes through spectacles and things of that kind, and sometimes [Col. 0679B] through feast days.

But why should I speak about sacrifices and priesthoods? Concerning spectacles and pleasures of that kind we have already filled a volume of their own. What must be reconsidered here concerns feast days and other extraordinary solemnities, which we sometimes ascribe to our self-indulgence and sometimes to our timidity, sharing with the nations in idolatrous [Col. 0680A] matters contrary to the discipline of the faith.

On this matter I shall first take my stand: whether a servant of God ought to participate with the nations themselves in things of this kind, whether in dress, food, or any other form of their rejoicing. “Rejoice with those who rejoice, and mourn with those who mourn,” was said by the apostle concerning the brethren, as an exhortation to unanimity (Rom. XII.15). But as regards these matters, light has no communion with darkness, nor life with death; otherwise we annul what is written (2 Cor. VI.14; John XVI.20): “The world will rejoice, but you will mourn.” If we rejoice with the world, it is to be feared that we shall also mourn with the world. But let us mourn while the world rejoices, and afterward, when the world mourns, we shall rejoice (Luke XVI.19).
