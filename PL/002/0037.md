In the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius (Luke 3:1), he represents him [Note: [Col. 0369C] “God,” Par. Fran.] as having descended into Capernaum, a city of Galilee (Luke 4:31)—from the Creator’s heaven, of course, into which he had previously descended from his own. What order [Note: [Col. 0369C] “And what,” Seml.] was there, then, in his first being described as descending from his own heaven into the Creator’s? For why should I not also criticize those features that fail to satisfy the credibility of an orderly narrative—a credibility always lacking in falsehood? To be sure, let those points be regarded as stated once for all through the arguments we have already examined elsewhere: whether, in descending through the Creator’s domain—and indeed in opposition to the Creator himself—he could have been admitted by him and then dispatched from there to the earth, which likewise belonged to him.

For now, however, accepting that he descended, [Col. 0369B] I also demand the remaining sequence of his descent. Let it pass if somewhere it is written that he “appeared.” “To appear” conveys a sudden and unexpected coming into view, which at once strikes the eyes with something that has appeared without delay. But “to descend,” because it takes place [Note: [Col. 0369C] “goes,” Par. Fran.], is both visible and comes before the eyes; and, as an actual event, it also creates a sequence. It therefore compels one to ask in what form, with what equipment, by what force or measured motion, and even at what time of day or night he descended; furthermore, who saw him descending, who reported it, and who attested an event that would certainly not be readily believed even when someone attested it.

It is, finally, unworthy that Romulus should have had Proculus to affirm his ascent into heaven, while Christ—the Christ of God [Note: [Col. 0369C] “of God” is absent from Fran.]—should have found no herald of his descent from his own heaven, as though Romulus had not ascended by the same ladder of falsehood [Col. 0369C] by which this Christ descended.

But what did he have to do with Galilee, if he did not belong to the Creator, for whose entrance upon his preaching this region was appointed? Isaiah says (Isa. 9:1–2):

> [Note: [Col. 0369D] “Drink this first; do it quickly.” I believe the conjecture of the most distinguished Hugo Grotius concerning this interpretation of Isaiah’s words, in his annotations on the Gospel of Matthew, chapter 4, verse 14, to be correct. RIG.]  
> Drink this first [Note: [Col. 0369C] “Pass through,” Par. Rhen.], do it quickly, region of Zebulun and land of Naphtali, and the rest of you who [Note: [Col. 0370C] Rhen., from the Gorze manuscript, adds “inhabit the land.”] dwell by the sea, beside the Jordan [Note: [Col. 0370C] “and of the Jordan,” Seml.], Galilee of the nations: you people [Col. 0370A] who sit in darkness, see a great light; you who inhabit the land, sitting in the shadow of death, a light has risen upon you.

It is well, moreover, that Marcion’s God too is claimed as the illuminator of the nations; all the more, then, ought he to have descended from heaven—even if [Note: [Col. 0370C] “and (even if),” Jun.] he should certainly have descended into Pontus rather than into Galilee. But since both the place and the work of illumination correspond to the proclamation concerning Christ, we already begin to recognize him as the one who was prophesied. At his very first entrance he showed that he had come “not to abolish the Law and the Prophets” (Matt. 5:17) [Note: [Col. 0369D] “Not to abolish the Law and the Prophets.” Irenaeus (Book IV, 13) says that Marcion read the opposite. SEML.], “but rather to fulfill them.” Marcion erased this as an addition. But he will deny in vain [Note: [Col. 0370C] “denied,” Seml.] that Christ said what he immediately did, at least in part. For he meanwhile fulfilled the prophecy concerning the place, going straight from heaven to the synagogue—in order, as the saying goes, to [Col. 0370B] **DO THE THING FOR WHICH WE CAME**.

Marcion, remove this too from the Gospel:

> I was not sent except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel (Matt. 15:24);

and:

> It is not [Note: [Col. 0370C] Lat. inserts “good.”] right to take the children’s bread and give it to the dogs (Matt. 7:27),

lest Christ should evidently belong to Israel. Deeds suffice for me in place of sayings. Take away the words of my Christ; the facts will speak. See, he enters the synagogue—assuredly to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. See, he first offers the bread of his teaching to the Israelites—assuredly because he gives them preference as children. See, he does not yet distribute it to the others—assuredly because he passes them by as dogs. Yet to whom would he more readily have distributed it than to those who were strangers to the Creator, if he himself had not belonged first and foremost to the Creator?

And yet how could someone so sudden and so unknown have been admitted into the synagogue—someone whose tribe, people, household, and finally enrollment in the census [Col. 0370C] of Augustus were still known with certainty to no one? [Note: [Col. 0369D] “Finally, concerning the census of Augustus,” etc. What this census was, and to which census the statement should be referred, is the subject of considerable controversy. Baronius, in the *Apparatus to the Annals*, considers it the same census as the one mentioned by the Evangelist. This does not seem to me far from the truth. Casaubon, in his notes on Spartianus, explains it as a census ordinance, though I do not know on whose authority. I would more readily agree with Petavius, who writes that there were two censuses under Quirinius, if only that were certain. For if any room is granted for conjecture, I would offer my own conjectures with as much right as the learned Petavius. I would think that the ἀπογραφή (*apographē*, “registration”) conducted under Sentius Saturninus was the same as the [Col. 0370D] enrollment under Quirinius. I conjecture that Saturninus was the governor and Quirinius the procurator. Justin, indeed, designates him by this title in *Apology* II: ἐπὶ Κυρηνίου τοῦ ἡμέτερου ἐν Ἰουδαίᾳ πρώτου γενομένου ἐπιτρόπου, “under your Quirinius, when he first became procurator in Judaea.” Someone may object to me that this was insufficiently honorable after all the honors he had attained and the highest dignities he had discharged. But Augustus may have arranged it because of the greater confidence he had conceived in Quirinius’s ability; and thus Saturninus, in order in some fashion to favor the emperor’s will by complying with it, was governor in name only. For Eusebius, Book I, chapter 5, should be understood in this way: ἡγεμονεύοντος Κυρηνίου τῆς Συρίας, “while Quirinius was governing Syria.” In view of these considerations, I do not know whether anyone would dare pronounce Petavius correct when he holds that no procurators of Judaea existed at that time. LE PR.]

The Roman archives preserve that census as a most trustworthy witness of the Lord’s birth. They would certainly have remembered that he could not be admitted into the Holy of Holies unless they knew he had been circumcised. [Note: [Col. 0370D] “But even if the synagogue was entered indiscriminately.” That is, even by proselytes and Gentiles. Nor does Eusebius disapprove of laypeople ὁμιλεῖν (*homilein*, “speaking”) even in the Christian church, provided it is done τῇ τοῦ προεστῶτος ἐποπτείᾳ (*tē tou proestōtos epopteia*, “under the supervision of the presiding officer”). RIG.] But even if the synagogue was entered indiscriminately, one was nevertheless not admitted to teach unless he was thoroughly known, examined, and approved, having long before been commended for this very purpose—or [Col. 0371A] commended from elsewhere together with this commission.

Now everyone was astonished at his teaching, plainly “because,” it says, “his word was with authority” (Luke 4:32) [Note: [Col. 0371D] “Because his word was with authority.” That is, his word was powerful. Luke: ἐπὶ τοῖς λόγοις τῆς χάριτος, “at the words of grace.” RIG.], not because he taught against the Law and the Prophets. For divine speech certainly displayed both power and grace, building up rather than destroying the substance of the Law and the Prophets. Otherwise they would not have been astonished but horrified; they would not have marveled but would immediately have opposed [Note: [Col. 0371D] “become adversaries of,” alternative reading.] the destroyer of the Law and the Prophets—and above all, surely, the preacher of another god. For he could not have taught against the Law and the Prophets, and on that account against the Creator, without first making a declaration of a different and rival divinity.

Since, therefore, Scripture indicates no such thing, but only that the power and authority of his speech were the object of wonder, [Col. 0371B] it shows more readily that he taught in accordance with the Creator, because he did not deny him, than that he taught against the Creator, because Scripture did not indicate it. Thus either he must be recognized as belonging to the one in accordance with whom he taught, or he must be judged a traitor if he taught in accordance with the one against whom he had come.

In that same place a demonic spirit cries out (Luke 4:34):

> What have we to do with you, Jesus? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are: the Holy One of God.

Here I shall not reconsider whether this designation was also appropriate to one who ought not even to have been called Christ if he did not belong to the Creator. An account has already been demanded elsewhere concerning the names. But now I ask how the demon knew to call him this, when no proclamation concerning him had ever previously been issued by the unknown god, who had remained silent until that time. Nor could the demon testify that he was that god’s holy one, since this god was unknown even [Note: [Col. 0371D] “now unknown,” alternative reading.] to the demon’s own Creator.

[Col. 0371C] What deed characteristic of the new divinity had Christ yet performed by which he could be understood as the holy one of another god? Only this: he had entered the synagogue and had done nothing even in his discourse against the Creator. Therefore, just as the demon could in no way have recognized as Jesus and the Holy One of God someone whom it did not know, so it recognized the one whom it did know. For it remembered both that the prophet had proclaimed the Holy One of God and that Jesus was God’s name in the son of Nun. According to our Gospel, it had also received these words from the angel (Luke 1:35):

> Therefore that which will be born in you will be called holy, the Son of God, and you shall call his name Jesus.

And the demon certainly possessed some understanding—demon though it was—of the Lord’s dispensation, rather than of an alien dispensation not yet adequately known. For it began by saying, “What have we to do with you [Note: [Col. 0371D] Jun. adds “Jesus.”]?” It did not speak as though Jesus were a stranger [Note: [Col. 0371D] “not to a stranger, but to one with whom,” Jun.], since [Col. 0371D] the Creator’s spirits belong to him. Nor did it say, “What have you to do with us?” but “What have we to do with you?” It was lamenting itself and reproaching its own allotted fate. Seeing that fate already, it added, “Have you come to destroy us?” Thus thoroughly had it recognized Jesus as the Son of a judging, avenging, and—[Col. 0372A] so to speak—savage God, not of that supremely good god who does not know how to destroy or punish.

Why have we placed this passage first? To show that the Jesus recognized by the demon was no other [Note: [Col. 0371D] “and” omitted in another reading.], and that the Jesus attested by himself was no other than the Creator’s. “But,” you say, “Jesus rebuked him.” Certainly—as one who was malicious, insolent even in his confession, and [Note: [Col. 0372D] “as,” Seml.] offering wicked flattery, as though this were the highest glory of Christ: that he had come to destroy demons, rather than to save human beings. For he wished his disciples to glory, not in the subjugation of spirits, but [Note: [Col. 0371D] “In the white robe of salvation.” Understand “robe,” unless you prefer, [Col. 0372D] with Rhenanus, to take *candida* substantivally. This expression is commonly derived from the Romans, among whom candidates for public office wore a white robe and were therefore called *candidati*. LE PR.] in the white robe of salvation.

Or why did he rebuke him? If because he had lied altogether, then he was neither Jesus nor the Holy One of God at all. If because he had lied in part—because he had indeed regarded him as Jesus and the Holy One of God, but as belonging to the Creator—then Christ [Col. 0372B] most unjustly rebuked the demon for believing what it knew ought to be believed, and for not believing what it did not know ought to be believed [Note: [Col. 0372D] “judged,” Seml.]: namely, another Jesus and the holy one of another god. But if the rebuke has no more plausible basis than the one we propose, then the demon told no lie and was not rebuked for lying. For he was indeed Jesus, besides whom the demon could not have recognized another; and Jesus confirmed the one whom the demon had recognized, since he did not rebuke the demon for lying.

## CHAPTER VIII

According to prophecy, the Creator’s Christ was to be called a Nazarene; hence the Jews call us too Nazarenes [Note: [Col. 0372D] “Nazarenes,” Seml.] because of him. For we are those of whom it is said (Lam. 4:7):

> The Nazarenes were whiter than snow.

That is, those who had formerly been made sallow by the stains of transgression [Col. 0372C] and blackened by the darkness of ignorance.

The designation “Nazarene” was also to apply to Christ because of the hiding place of his infancy, to which he withdrew at Nazareth in order to avoid Archelaus, the son of Herod. I have not omitted this point because Marcion’s Christ ought to have renounced every connection even with places familiar to the Creator’s Christ. He had so many towns in Judaea available that had not been assigned [Note: [Col. 0372D] “made over,” Jun.] in this fashion to the Creator’s Christ through the Prophets. In any case, Christ will belong to the Prophets wherever he is found in accordance with the Prophets.

And yet even at Nazareth he is not recorded as having preached anything new, since he is reported to have been expelled for a different reason, on account of a single proverb (Luke 4:23). Noticing here for the first time that hands were laid upon him, I must now establish in advance something concerning his bodily substance: one who allowed himself to be touched—and indeed [Col. 0372D] with full violence, being detained, seized, and dragged all the way to the precipice—cannot be believed to have been a phantom. For even though he escaped through their midst, he had already first experienced their violence and was afterwards released, obviously because the disturbance subsided, as commonly happens, or because he broke through it. [Col. 0373A] Yet he did not evade them by means of a mist, which, had he been one, would have yielded to absolutely every touch.

> For nothing except body can touch or be touched.

[Note: [Col. 0373D] “For nothing except body can touch or be touched,” etc. He also cited this same statement in chapter 5 of *On the Soul*, in order to establish there that the soul is corporeal. It is found in Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things*, Book I, against whose delusions Lactantius argues in Book VII [Col. 0374D] of the *Divine Institutes*. LE PR.]

This is a saying worthy even of secular wisdom. In short, he himself soon afterwards touched others; laying upon them hands that could certainly be felt, he conferred the benefits of healing—benefits as real and as little imaginary as the hands through which he conferred them.

He himself, therefore, is the Christ of Isaiah as well, the restorer of health:

> He, it says, takes away our infirmities and bears our sicknesses (Isa. 53:4).

The Greeks are also accustomed to use “bear” in the sense of “take away.” For the present, the general promise is enough for me. Whatever Jesus heals, he is mine. We shall nevertheless come to the particular kinds of healing as well.

Moreover, liberation from demons [Col. 0373B] is itself a healing of health. Accordingly, evil spirits, as though following the pattern of the previous example, departed with testimony, crying out:

> You are the Son of God.

Let it be evident here too whose God. Yet in the same way they were rebuked and commanded to be silent. For Christ wished to be recognized as the Son of God by human beings, not by unclean spirits. This applies, at any rate, to that Christ to whom such conduct was appropriate, because he had sent before him those through whom he could be recognized, and who were certainly more worthy heralds. It belonged to the one who abounded in holy spirits to reject the proclamation of an unclean spirit.

But one who had never been announced—if he nevertheless wished to be recognized, and he had come in vain if he did not—would not have scorned testimony from an alien substance, whatever its character, since he had no testimony from his own and had descended into another’s domain. Moreover, precisely as the destroyer [Col. 0373C] of the Creator, he could have done nothing more useful than to be recognized and publicized by the Creator’s own spirits out of fear—except that Marcion denies that his god is feared. He maintains that the good god is not feared, but only the judge, with whom lie the grounds for fear: anger, savagery, judgments, vengeance, and condemnation.

Yet the demons were certainly yielding from fear. They were therefore confessing the Son of a God who was to be feared, though they would have had grounds not to yield if he were not to be feared. And he, driving them out by command and rebuke, rather than by persuasion as a good being, showed himself to be feared. Or did he perhaps rebuke them because he was feared, though he did not wish to be feared? And how did he wish them to depart, when they would not have done so except from fear? He therefore fell under the necessity of acting contrary to his own nature, although as a good being he could simply have spared them.

He also incurred another charge of betrayal: he permitted himself to be feared by the demons as though he were [Col. 0373D] the Creator’s Son, so that he no longer expelled demons by his own power, but through the Creator’s authority.

He goes out into the wilderness. This too is a region customary to the Creator. It was fitting that the Word should be seen there in a body where he had once acted in a cloud. The character of the place that had pleased the Law was also appropriate to the Gospel.

> Let the wilderness, therefore, receive joy.

Isaiah had promised this (Isa. 35:1).

When he was detained [Col. 0374A] by the crowds, he said (Luke 4:42):

> I must proclaim the kingdom of God to other cities also.

Had he already revealed his own god anywhere? I do not think he had, even by this point. But was he speaking about people who knew another god as well? I do not believe that either. Therefore, if neither had he disclosed another god, nor did they know any god besides the Creator, he was announcing the kingdom of the same God whom alone he knew to be known by those who were listening.

## CHAPTER IX

Among so many kinds of occupation, why did he turn specifically to fishing, so as to take Simon and the sons of Zebedee from that occupation into the apostolate? This cannot be regarded as a simple event, since an argument was to proceed from it when he said to Peter, who was alarmed at the abundant catch of fish (Luke 5:10):

> Do not be afraid; [Col. 0374B] from now on you will be catching human beings.

By this saying he suggested to them the understanding that a prophecy had been fulfilled: that he was the one who had declared through Jeremiah (Jer. 16:16):

> Behold, I shall send many fishermen, and they will fish for them—

human beings, that is. Finally, leaving their little boats behind, they followed him, understanding him to be the one who had begun to do what he had foretold.

It would be another matter if he had been eager to choose his men from the guild of boatmen because he was one day to have as his apostle Marcion, the shipmaster.

Against the *Antitheses* we have already laid the groundwork that the difference between the Law and the Gospel, as Marcion conceives it, does nothing to advance his case, since this difference too was ordained by the Creator and was finally proclaimed in the promise of a new law, a new word, and a New Testament. But because Marcion argues the point more elaborately before that wretched fellow of his—I know not [Col. 0374C] whom—his συνταλαίπωρος (*syntalaipōros*), that is, his “fellow-wretch,” and his συμμισούμενος (*symmisoumenos*), that is, his “fellow-object of hatred,” [Note: [Col. 0374D] “Before that certain fellow of his—I know not whom,” etc. Interpretations of the Greek words have slipped into the text from the margin. LE PR.] in connection with the cleansing of the leper, I shall not be reluctant to meet his argument and, first of all, to demonstrate the force of the figurative Law.

In the example of the leper, who was not to be touched but rather excluded from all association, the Law prohibited fellowship with a person stained by sins. The Apostle likewise forbids even taking food with such people (1 Cor. 5:11), for the marks of sins are shared, as though by contagion, if anyone associates himself with a sinner.

Accordingly, the Lord wished the Law to be understood more profoundly, as signifying spiritual realities through bodily ones. On this account he was not destroying the Law, but building it up even more in the way he wished it more appropriately to be recognized. He touched the leper. Although a human being could have been defiled by him, God certainly would not have been defiled, since he is incapable of contamination. No objection can therefore be raised [Col. 0374D] against him that he ought to have observed the Law and not touched an unclean person, since contact with an unclean person was not going to defile him.

I teach that this is more appropriate to my Christ by demonstrating that it is not appropriate to yours. For if he touched the leper as a rival of the Law, treating the Law’s command as nothing through contempt for defilement, how could one who had no body capable of being defiled have been defiled? A phantom [Col. 0375A] could not be defiled. Therefore, one who could not be defiled because he was a phantom will no longer be incapable of contamination by divine power, but by the emptiness of a phantom. Nor can he be thought to have despised defilement when he did not possess the material upon which it could act. Thus neither did he destroy the Law, since he escaped defilement because he was a phantom, not through a display of power.

Now if Elisha, a prophet of the Creator, healed only one leper, Naaman the Syrian, out of so many Israelite lepers (2 Kings 5:14), this does not establish a difference in Christ, as though Christ were better because, though an outsider, he cleansed [Note: [Col. 0375D] “cleanses,” Seml.] an Israelite leper whom his own lord had been unable to cleanse. The Syrian was more readily cleansed because the cleansing of the nations was signified [Note: [Col. 0375D] “signifies,” Seml.] in Christ, their light. They bristled with the seven stains of the chief sins: idolatry, [Col. 0375B] blasphemy, murder, adultery, sexual violation, false testimony, and fraud.

[Note: [Col. 0375D] “The seven stains of the chief sins.” Here you have a very different enumeration from the commonly transmitted list of the seven capital sins, since idolatry, blasphemy, murder, adultery, sexual violation, false testimony, and fraud are enumerated. Of these, idolatry and blasphemy can be referred to pride, murder to anger, adultery and sexual violation to lust, and false testimony and fraud to avarice; but envy, gluttony, [Col. 0376D] and sloth will still remain in addition. Cyprian also places three of those enumerated here—adultery, fraud, and murder—among mortal crimes in his book *On the Good of Patience*. Finally, the author of *On Modesty* numbers these same offenses among the graver and deadly, indeed mortal, sins, except that there he adds denial in place of false testimony and calls “fornication” what here, according to his custom, he calls sexual violation. PAM.]

For this reason Naaman washed seven times in the Jordan, as though under each individual heading, and also so that he might receive [Note: [Col. 0375D] “so many times,” Rig. Venet.] the expiation [Note: [Col. 0375D] “sing,” Rig. Venet.] of the entire week [Note: [Col. 0375D] Seml. inserts “and.”], because the power and fullness of a single washing was reserved for Christ alone, who was to perform on earth both an abridged word and, in the same way, an abridged washing.

Marcion also raises this objection: Elisha required material, used water, and used it seven times, whereas Christ, by his word alone and with that word used only once [Note: [Col. 0375D] “having discharged,” Seml.], immediately effected the cure. As though I would not dare to claim the word itself for the substance of the Creator! In every matter, the better claim to authorship belongs to the one who came first. It is plainly incredible that the Creator’s power should have effected by a word a remedy for a single affliction when by a word it once brought forth the immense mass of the world. From what, then, is [Col. 0375C] the Creator’s Christ more readily recognized than from the power of his word?

But he is supposedly another Christ because he acted differently from Elisha—because the master was more powerful than his servant. What are you maintaining, Marcion? That servants should perform deeds in precisely the same way as their masters? Do you not fear that it will turn to your disgrace if you deny that Christ belongs to the Creator because he was more powerful than the Creator’s servant? In comparison with Elisha’s littleness, he is recognized as greater—if, indeed, he is greater. For the cure is equal, although the method differs. What more did your Christ accomplish than my Elisha? Indeed, what great thing did the word of your Christ accomplish, when it accomplished what the Creator’s river had accomplished?

In accordance with these considerations, the remaining points are also answered. Insofar as the avoidance of human glory was concerned, he forbade the man to publicize it; but insofar as [Col. 0376A] the protection of the Law was concerned, he commanded its prescribed order to be fulfilled:

> Go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift that Moses commanded.

For he was still preserving the figurative proofs [Note: [Col. 0376D] “for example,” Seml.] of the prophesied [Note: [Col. 0376D] “prophet,” Seml.; “of the prophet,” Jun.] Law in their own images. These signified that a person who had once been a sinner, but was presently cleansed of stains by the word of God, ought to offer a gift to God at the temple—namely, prayer and thanksgiving in the Church through Christ Jesus, the universal priest of the Father.

He therefore added:

> That it may be a testimony to you.

Without doubt, this was the testimony by which he bore witness that he was not abolishing the Law but fulfilling it; and by which he bore witness that he himself was the one who had been announced as destined to take upon himself their diseases and sicknesses.

Marcion, the flatterer of his Christ, seeks to exclude this thoroughly [Note: [Col. 0376D] “entirely,” Par.] fitting and necessary interpretation of the testimony under the pretext of Christ’s gentleness [Col. 0376B] and mildness. “For,” he says, “being good, and knowing besides that everyone who had been freed from leprosy would perform the solemn requirements of the Law, he commanded it for that reason.”

What then? Did he persevere in goodness—that is, in permitting the Law—or did he not [Note: [Col. 0376D] “or not,” Seml.]? If he persevered in goodness, he will never [Note: [Col. 0376D] “nowhere,” Seml.] be the destroyer of the Law, nor will he be regarded as belonging to another god, since the destruction of the Law, through which he is claimed for another god, has ceased. But if he did not persevere in goodness when he later destroyed the Law, then he afterwards deposited false testimony among them through the healing of the leper; for he abandons goodness when he destroys the Law.

He is now evil when he overthrows the Law, if he was good when he permitted the Law. Yet by permitting obedience to the Law, he also confirmed that the Law was good, for no one allows obedience to an evil thing. Therefore, he is evil even on this account, if he [Col. 0376C] permitted obedience to an evil law; and on the other account he is worse, if he came as the destroyer of a good law.

Likewise, if he commanded the man to offer the gift because he knew that everyone freed from leprosy would do so, he could also have refrained from commanding what he knew would happen of its own accord. He therefore descended in vain as though he were going to destroy the Law, since he yielded to those who obeyed the Law. On the contrary, because he knew their customary practice, he ought rather to have acted beforehand to turn them away from it, if this was why he had come.

Why, then, did he [Note: [Col. 0376D] “therefore,” Seml.] not keep silent, so that the man might obey the Law solely by his own choice? For then Christ might, to some extent, appear to have granted this out of his own forbearance. But he also adds his own authority, intensified by the weight of the testimony. A testimony to what, then, if not to the vindicated Law?

Certainly it makes no difference how he confirmed the Law—whether as good, as superfluous, [Col. 0377A] as forbearing, or as inconsistent—provided that I drive you, Marcion, from your position. [Note: [Col. 0377D] “Drive you from your position.” That is: “I shall refute that objection of yours by which you say that the good god permitted the Law and the evil god took it away”; for this author argues here with great sharpness against these monstrous claims. LE PR.]

See: he commanded the Law to be fulfilled. In whatever manner he commanded it [Note: [Col. 0377D] “commands,” another reading.], in that same manner he could also have uttered beforehand the saying (Matt. 5:17):

> I did not come to abolish the Law, but to fulfill it.

Why, then, did you erase from the Gospel something that remains intact? For you have confessed that, out of goodness, he did what you deny he said. It is therefore established that he said it, because he also did it—and that you erased [Note: [Col. 0377D] “Rooted out,” Seml.] the Lord’s utterance from the Gospel, rather than that our people inserted it.

[Note: [Col. 0377D] “Erased from the Gospel,” etc. This refers to the corruption throughout almost the whole Gospel of blessed Luke, including, among other passages, chapter 4, which is under discussion here. LE PR.]

## CHAPTER X
