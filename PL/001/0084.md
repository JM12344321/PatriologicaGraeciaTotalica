In the second part of this book he specifically assails and overthrows the gods of the Romans (chap. 9). Varro, however, perversely divided them into uncertain, certain, and select gods. For who, our author asks, unless he is mad, honors a god who is uncertain and doubtful? But whoever has and venerates certain and select gods plainly despises and rejects the others. That division of the gods is therefore inept; they might have been distinguished somewhat better into common and particular gods. Yet the first, or common, gods are the same as the public and imported gods, who are included within the physical or mythical class of gods mentioned and refuted by us shortly before. There remain, therefore, [Col. 0763A] only the particular gods of the Romans, who either had been taken from conquered enemies and carried off to Rome, or were the Penates and Lares, whom each person worshiped in his own private house and with a peculiar rite. But all these were human beings who, on account of certain outstanding deeds, were made gods after death by a plainly senseless consecration. Yet what, Tertullian asks, were the merits of one of them—for example, Aeneas? Was it his devotion to his father and son? But either he betrayed his own country, as Antenor did, or, by abandoning it in flames and leaving Priam and Astyanax behind, he failed to imitate the Punic woman who cast herself with her sons into the fires of her burning homeland. Was he better than the Romans who swore, for the safety of their emperors, against their children and wives? Moreover, if he earned any merit by carrying his father on his shoulders, did not the Argive [Col. 0763B] youths bear their mother like draft animals, lest she incur guilt in the sacred rites? If he nourished his father, did not a certain daughter, with greater filial devotion, offer her breasts to her father when he had been imprisoned? They, therefore, were more worthy of divine honors than the cowardly and fearful Aeneas, who was never seen again after the battle at Laurentum. Did Romulus likewise merit the honor of divinity, when he killed his brother and abducted the maidens of others? Did Hercules, for removing Augeas’s dung; Penelope, for chastity; Sanctus, for hospitality, deserve to obtain the rights of divinity any more than Sterculus, the daughter of Faunus, or Ulysses?

What, moreover, is to be said of other, far more shameful gods? What divinity, I ask you, is there in Larentina, [Col. 0763C] a public prostitute and the nurse of Romulus? What of the woman who, after being violated by the custodian of Hercules’ temple following a ridiculous game, boasted that she had been embraced by Hercules himself, though he was already dead? Who could patiently endure the infamous Antinous being regarded by the Romans as a god? Yet they devised other gods no less shameful, whom they most foolishly proclaimed to preside over marriage and the procreation of children. Such are Consevius, Fluvionia, Vitumnus, Sentinus, Diespiter, Candelifera, Prosa, Farmus, Albana, Runcinia, Potina, Edula, Abeona, Domiduca, Volumnus, Voleta, Paventina, Venilia, Volupia, Praestitia, Peragenor, Consus, Juventa, Fortuna, Barbata, Afferenda, Mutunus, Tutunus, Pertunda, Subigus, Prema, and others whose functions even the slightest sense of modesty [Col. 0763D] forbids one to designate by their proper names (chaps. 10, 11).

Furthermore, in order to cut down all the gods of the Romans together and at one stroke, Tertullian attacks their very origin, which Varro begins with Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva, but which must be traced back to their parent Saturn (chap. 12). Yet Saturn—much less his sons—could never have been a god: he was said to have been born of Caelus, or Chronos, and Earth, and to have castrated his father. Nor should it be said that these things are to be explained allegorically of time and the fertility of the earth. For the most distinguished writers—Cassius Severus, Cornelius Nepos, Tacitus, Diodorus, and others—assert in the clearest terms that Saturn was in fact a man who, after traveling through various parts of the world, was received in Oenotria, or Italy, and gave his own name to the mountain and city where he had established his abode, as well as to all Italy. [Col. 0764A] But because he had arrived as if unexpectedly and his parents were unknown, he was said to have fallen from heaven. Moreover, lest the pagans dare to mutter any objection, Tertullian confirms this with the testimony of the Sibyl accepted by them.

Accordingly, since no room for doubt remains that Saturn was a man, it is securely inferred from this that those whom the pagans acknowledged to have sprung from him and afterward to have died and been buried were in reality human beings (chap. 13). But dead human beings cannot be gods unless they received their divinity either from themselves or from others. They do not, however, possess it from themselves. For they would not have allowed themselves first to have been human beings, subject to so many and such great human weaknesses. Nor were they endowed with supreme divinity by anyone else, unless by one more powerful than all the other fictitious gods, [Col. 0764B] who must therefore alone be called the true God. Furthermore, no reason whatever can be assigned why the true God should have made others gods—not, certainly, so that they might serve as an aid or adornment to him, for almighty God needs no assistance or embellishment. Nor did he make certain human beings gods because of their merits. For what did Saturn merit, who devoured his own children; or Jupiter, who was rescued from his father’s fury, committed adultery with his sister, and is said to have assumed the forms of a bull, a shower of gold, a swan, and others, so that he might commit further rapes and adulteries more safely? Human beings of this kind, so impious and criminal, therefore never merited divinity.

Nor are those gods whom Dionysius the Stoic distinguishes into native and made gods (chap. 14). For Tertullian overthrows this utterly senseless opinion by the single example of Hercules, which is nevertheless entirely [Col. 0764C] sufficient. Hercules was not worthy of divinity because he killed wild beasts, since more of them are killed by gladiators; nor because he traversed the world, for Asclepiades the Cynic and many philosophers roamed through every region of the whole world with greater toil in their mendicancy. Is he therefore a god because he went down to the underworld? But to which of them is the way open? Let us proceed, if you please. Did not that eminent Hercules violate women? Did he not desert the Argonauts’ expedition because a boy had been lost? Was he not ensnared by the poisons of his wanton wife, and, after lighting a pyre, did he not bring about his own death? What sane person, therefore, would believe him to be a god? In what manner, likewise, could Aesculapius be proclaimed a god, when, as Socrates of Argos relates, he was born of an uncertain father and, on account of his skill in medicine, was struck by Jupiter’s thunderbolt, as his mother also had been? [Col. 0764D] Yet the Athenians worship him and his mother, as Thesca does [the reading “Thesca” is uncertain].

Nor are the Castors, Perseus, Erigone, Jupiter’s catamite—that is, Ganymede—any more worthy of heaven and divinity; nor are the dogs, scorpions, and crabs that the Romans transferred to the stars. Who, indeed, could hear without laughter the names of those gods whom the Romans believed to have been placed over certain locations in cities and houses? For they are called Viduus, Caeculus, Orbana, Arguis, Lana, Ascensus, Levicola, Forculus, Cardea, Limentinus, and others appointed over each particular function of life. But if these, known almost to the Romans alone, had been gods, [Col. 0765A] there would have been no need whatever for all the rest (chap. 15).

“But we are plainly ungrateful,” the Romans said, “if we deny divine honors to those who discovered for us the necessities of life.” Then, says Tertullian, those honors ought to be bestowed upon Cato, who first brought a fresh fig to Rome, and Pompey, who first brought cherries there. But nothing is more futile than this reasoning. For the discoverer of fruits is not their author and creator; the true God is. And certainly new arts are discovered daily by human beings, yet no one would say that their inventors are gods (chap. 16).

The Romans insisted that by their piety and religion toward the gods they had merited dominion over the whole world. “Is that so?” Tertullian asks. Did the shameful Sterculus extend the boundaries of the Roman Empire? Did Jupiter, forgetting the cave of Ida, Crete his homeland, and the most pleasant fragrance of his nurse, prefer the Capitol to them? Did [Col. 0765B] Juno, utterly forgetful of Carthage, which she had once preferred even to Samos, wish it to be overthrown by the Romans? But these and other unwarlike gods had no power against the fates, nor could they defend their cities and provinces against the arms of their enemies.

Moreover, the Roman Empire had been founded before there were any images, worship, or sacred rites of the gods, all of which were afterward introduced by Numa Pompilius. That empire therefore grew through wars, and consequently through equal slaughter of priests and citizens, equal destruction of sacred and profane things, and as many sacrileges as trophies and triumphs. Finally, who does not know that the kingdoms of the Assyrians, Medes, [Col. 0765C] Persians, and Egyptians were utterly overthrown, although they worshiped their gods with no less piety than did the Romans? Therefore God alone, one and almighty, apportions kingdoms—not the gods of the Romans or of other nations, who are demonstrably as artificial as the religions of those who worship them are false and vain (chap. 17).

## ARTICLE III

### It Is Shown by Various Weighty Arguments That Tertullian Is the Author of These Books, and the New Opinion of Hoornbeek, Who Denies This, Is Refuted

Jerome has attested for us that, in addition to the *Apologeticum*, Tertullian published other books *To the Gentiles*, concerning which he wrote to Magnus in these words: “Who is more learned than Tertullian? Who more exact? His *Apologeticum* and his books *Against the Gentiles* embrace all the learning [Col. 0765D] of the age” (Jerome, Epistle 83, *To Magnus*, p. 656). Who, moreover, does not see that the books with which we are concerned are designated here and distinguished from the *Apologeticum*? For although in the Agobard manuscript, which will be discussed below, they are entitled *To the Nations*, who will deny that this title has the same meaning as *To the Gentiles*?

To this may be added Augustine, who, in refuting an opinion of our Tertullian, cited these books under his name, though somewhat obscurely: “I do not assert,” he says, “what Tertullian perhaps said more wittily than truly: ‘If gods are selected like onions, then certainly the rest are judged rejected’” (Augustine, *City of God*, book VII, chap. 1, p. 162). We know, indeed, that some have thought his *Apologeticum* to be cited there. But others were so persuaded that no books other than [Col. 0766A] those *To the Nations* were cited there that they wished to correct their text from it. And indeed we shall show later that Augustine there had our author’s books *To the Nations* in view rather than the *Apologeticum*.

Furthermore, these books are more than once inscribed with Tertullian’s own name in the ancient Agobard manuscript. For on its second page we read: “Here are the books of Tertullian: *To the Nations*, Book I. Likewise *To the Nations*, Book II.” At the end of the first book also: “Tertullian’s *To the Nations*, Book I, ends. Book II *To the Nations* begins.” Moreover, on its first page the copyist of this manuscript wrote in larger letters: “A book offered at the altar of Saint Stephen in fulfillment of the vow of Agobard, bishop.” Now this, they say, is Agobard himself, archbishop of Lyons, who flourished in the age of Charlemagne and his sons, and at the beginning of the ninth century. [Col. 0766B] Thus the manuscript was copied at that time from another, undoubtedly more ancient manuscript, in which—and doubtless in many others likewise—these books similarly bore Tertullian’s name at their head. It was therefore the common opinion, not only in Agobard’s age but also in earlier centuries, that Tertullian was the author of these books *To the Nations*.

But even if we lacked an argument of such force, the subject matter of these books, their age, and above all their style and mode of composition, together with their very close affinity with the *Apologeticum*, demonstrate that they proceeded from Tertullian. For from the analysis of them already presented by us, it is most clearly evident that their writer is wholly intent upon making it plain to everyone that Christians were most unjustly persecuted by the pagans contrary to the prescriptions of all laws, and were falsely accused of crimes of which the gentiles themselves were in fact guilty. [Col. 0766C] These books were therefore written in Tertullian’s own age, when the pagans still raged against the Christians with fury as blind as it was unrestrained. Finally, nothing is said in them, whether concerning the Christians or the gentiles, that does not accord with Tertullian’s age and point to his very period.

The distinctive style of these books also, and their unusual manner of expression and vocabulary, plainly show that Tertullian is their true father. For his peculiar and characteristic diction, unlike that of all others, shines forth everywhere throughout them and is very quickly detected by any reader. Moreover, there repeatedly occur in them not only the same distinctive and unusual words of Tertullian as in the *Apologeticum*, [Col. 0766D] but even whole identical sentences. Nor is it necessary to prove by a lengthy induction something so readily apparent and which will become manifest to everyone from what is said later. Does not so great an affinity and connection between the two works demonstrate clearly enough that they were composed by one and the same writer—namely, Tertullian?

We are therefore astonished at a certain more recent author, a man certainly of great reputation among his own people, who boldly declares that it does not at all appear to him that Tertullian was in fact the author of these books. But on what weighty arguments, do you suppose? On scarcely any others than those very grounds by which they are especially vindicated to him: “It does not appear to me,” he says, “that Tertullian is the author; the style and subject matter are not his, [Col. 0767A] though they are nearly the same as in Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*, from which they were rather drawn and fashioned in imitation of him, perhaps by some rival or devoted student of Tertullian” (Hoornbeek, *On the Theology of the Fathers*, book I, chap. 1, §10, p. 106). A novel and astonishing mode of argument indeed! The style, that learned man says, and the subject matter of the *Apologeticum* and of the books *To the Nations* are the same; therefore these books were composed not by Tertullian but by his rival or devoted student. What if we were similarly to conclude from this that the *Apologeticum* was composed not by him but by his rival or student? Will he perhaps answer that other writers assign that book to Tertullian? But we too have just shown that the books *To the Nations* are likewise ascribed to him by others.

Furthermore, that learned man admits with us, [Col. 0767B] as he cannot deny, that the style of these books and of the *Apologeticum* is the same. Yet neither he nor anyone else will ever deny that Tertullian’s style and mode of speaking are so characteristic and peculiar to him that scarcely anyone, if indeed anyone at all, could ever imitate them. Should it not therefore be concluded from this, as the more expert critics are accustomed to do, with far greater right, that the same Tertullian was in fact the father of all three books?

Hoornbeek further insists that their subject matter and argument are the same. But what follows from that? Could the same Christian author not compose two different works on the same subject—especially in defense of the Christian religion, which was persecuted by the pagans with as much injustice as savagery—or, by revising what he had written, rework, correct, enlarge, or abridge it? [Col. 0767C] Certainly the man who raises this objection against us will never deny it. For he himself, like everyone else, openly admits that Tertullian is the true author of the book *To Scapula*. Yet in that book the argument is the same as in the *Apologeticum* and the two books *To the Nations*—namely, the cause of the Christians, who were most unjustly persecuted—though it is treated more briefly. Moreover, the books *To the Nations* were addressed to persons other than those addressed by the *Apologeticum* and the book *To Scapula*. What of the fact that we shall demonstrate a little later that many more matters are treated in the *Apologeticum* than in the books *To the Nations*, and conversely? Finally, we shall show that Tertullian was compelled by cogent reasons to write these books after the *Apologeticum*.

Hoornbeek nevertheless presses the point that the author of the first book *To the Nations* said that two hundred years had not yet elapsed since the birth of Christ, [Col. 0767D] whereas in the book *On Monogamy* he says approximately one hundred and sixty (Tertullian, *On Monogamy*, chap. 3). But he strives in vain to confirm very slight arguments by another far slighter one. For even if it were established that no scribal error underlies the copying of the numerals, and if it were certain that the beginning of those years was reckoned from Christ himself, it still could not be concluded from this that the books *To the Nations* must be denied to Tertullian. For in the first of these very books we read: “Our era is not yet 250 years old” (the same, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7). And not much later: “As we stated above, our era is not yet three hundred years old” (ibid., chap. 9). Would you therefore say that this book was not published by one and the same author? What [Col. 0768A] sane person ever argued in that way? Who does not see that the same writer, who was not acting as a chronographer, could in fact say both that two hundred years had not yet elapsed since Christ, and elsewhere that approximately 160 years had elapsed? But we would certainly be ashamed and weary of refuting so vain and singular an opinion, were it not that some later writers seem to have subscribed to this new author whom they extol with great praises.

## ARTICLE IV

### At What Time These Books Appeared in Public

The precise time at which Tertullian devoted himself to composing these books *To the Nations* is neither readily nor easily stated. Certain chronological marks and indications nevertheless present themselves in them. For in the first [Col. 0768B] book we read of a sentence passed shortly before by Fuscianus, prefect of the City, against a certain father who had exposed his newborn son and, after some time had elapsed, bought and violated him without any longer recognizing him (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 16). Now Capitolinus mentions Fuscianus, prefect of the City, whom Pertinax immediately succeeded (Capitolinus, *Life of Pertinax*, shortly after the beginning). Pertinax was made emperor at the beginning of the year 193 and was killed after approximately three months. Tertullian must therefore be said to have published these books not long after the death of Pertinax.

Nor should you object that in the same book he wrote: “Our era is not yet 250 years old,” that is, from the rise of the Christian religion (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 7). For afterward he also wrote: “As we stated above, [Col. 0768C] our era is not yet three hundred years old” (ibid., chap. 5). Nothing at all certain or established can therefore be concluded from this. He doubtless preferred to assign the larger number of years so as to afford the pagans no occasion for caviling. Moreover, what certainty can be gathered from those two so disparate numbers, “not yet 250 years” and “300”? If, however, you wish to infer anything from them, you must first determine to what year Tertullian assigned the beginning of the Christian era.

Some therefore think that the date of these books can be recognized with greater certainty from other words of his, in which he addresses the pagans thus: “From among you nations there daily arise Caesars, and men styled Parthicus, Medicus, and Germanicus” (ibid., chap. 17). But these words, they say, refer to Caracalla, [Col. 0768D] who, after defeating the Germans and Parthians, was distinguished with the surname Germanicus and Parthicus in the year 216. This would not be a bad argument if these honorific surnames had been given to Caracalla alone.

The following words of our author therefore indicate the date of these books more clearly: “Syria still breathes with the odors of corpses; the Gauls still wash themselves in their Rhône” (ibid.). For Pescennius Niger and Clodius Albinus are undoubtedly designated there: after they had revolted from the emperor Severus, the former was routed and slain with his forces in Syria, the latter in Gaul. These events, which are mentioned in Tertullian’s *Apologeticum* as well, occurred in the year 194 or 195, or rather in 197, as we explained more fully above [Col. 0769A] (above, chap. 3, art. 2). Tertullian must therefore be said to have published these books *To the Nations*, as also the *Apologeticum*, shortly after that year.

Some, however, infer from his following words—“The character of the times demanded the harder temperaments of the vigor . . . of antiquity; but now the tranquility of peace produces gentler temperaments” (ibid., chap. 18)—that this statement was made by him when the Christians, at the beginning of Severus’s reign, enjoyed the desired tranquility of peace and leisure. But Severus stirred up persecution against them by edicts issued in the year 202. Therefore, they say, Tertullian had composed these books before that date.

It must, however, be observed that with these words he introduced the pagans as wrongly objecting that the temperaments of their ancestors had been harder, since they were better prepared than the people of his own age to endure patiently even the most bitter [Col. 0769B] tortures, whereas the tranquility of peace and leisure had made the latter’s temperaments gentler. If, however, you maintain and prove that these very words of our author must be understood of the true peace of the Christian Church, we shall readily agree with you. Nevertheless, no one will deny that it can be inferred more securely from what has been said above that these books *To the Nations* and the *Apologeticum* appeared in public not long after the year 197. Which of them, however, appeared first is another controversy among the learned, which we must now examine.

## ARTICLE V

### Whether These Two Books *To the Nations* Were Written Before or After Tertullian’s *Apologeticum*

[Col. 0769C] Those who think that the two books *To the Nations* were written after the *Apologeticum* attempt to establish their opinion by three arguments. First, they suppose that the *Apologeticum* was published around the year 200 and at the beginning of the persecution stirred up by the emperor Severus against the Christians, whereas the books *To the Nations* were published when Caracalla’s reign was already advanced and after that emperor had received the titles Parthicus and Germanicus. But we have already shown that the foundation of this latter assertion concerning Caracalla is thoroughly unstable.

Their second argument is that the defense of the Christians, which comprises the whole subject both of the *Apologeticum* and of the books *To the Nations*, is treated more fully in the latter than in the former. But we shall demonstrate later how little resemblance to truth this conjecture bears.

[Col. 0769D] They derive their third argument from words and sentences that occur in other books which Tertullian published after his defection to the Montanists—as though other expressions similar to these were not also found in his other books, and especially in the *Apologeticum*, where they admit that far more expressions identical to those in the books *To the Nations* occur.

As weak as these arguments are, so compelling do other considerations appear to us, which can be drawn from the books *To the Nations* themselves. For in them Tertullian promises more than once that he will explain many things elsewhere. Yet, lest some complain that anything doubtful and uncertain is being advanced, we shall not press the words that Tertullian added immediately after proving that Christians were persecuted solely on account of their name: “But while this reasoning is demonstrated in its proper place, [Col. 0770A] set forth the rationale by which you proceed in attacking the name” (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 3). For there he does not expressly promise that the matter will be treated by him later, but appears to us to signify that he has sufficiently explained the matter itself.

Yet in clearer words he certainly pledges that elsewhere he will institute a longer discussion of the future resurrection of bodies and of the eternal rewards or punishments of human beings after death. For he speaks in this manner: “We shall consider the credibility of these matters when they are arranged in their proper place” (ibid., chap. 7), that is, when they are disposed and prepared to be committed to writing. But where did he arrange and set this forth against the follies of the pagans? Certainly not in these books *To the Nations*, but toward the end of his *Apologeticum*, which one might say with good reason [Col. 0770B] he had in mind rather than any other book (the same, *Apologeticum*, chaps. 48 and 49).

Then, when he refutes other slanders of the pagans, who loudly declared that Christians were the cause of every public disaster, he says: “Later they will be blunted by an exposition of our entire discipline” (the same, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 10). Tell me, I ask, where did he expound this Christian doctrine in order to dispel these accusations of the gentiles? Certainly not in these books *To the Nations*, but in several chapters of the *Apologeticum*, as is evident even from the analysis of that book which we gave above (the same, *Apologeticum*, chap. 17, 18, and following).

Furthermore, he promises that he will at some point prove false the teachings concerning God not only of the pagan poets but also of those who profess the art of divination: “For the outline [Col. 0770C] of this kind,” he says, “must now be touched upon; its rationale will be given in its proper place” (the same, *To the Nations*, book II, chap. 5). In what book, then, did he give that rationale by which the pagans’ foolish accusations might be refuted? Was it not in the *Apologeticum*, where we read it explained at length (the same, *Apologeticum*, chaps. 21 and 22)?

Why say more? When he again checks the audacity of the gentiles, who most shamelessly charged the Christians with infanticide, he addresses them in these words: “If you have forgotten what you have accomplished through the slaughter of human beings and through infanticides, you will review it in its proper order. For now we defer many matters, lest we appear to treat the same things repeatedly everywhere” (*To the Nations*, book II, chap. 15). You plainly see two things expressly stated there by our author. First, that he himself would demonstrate more fully that the gentiles [Col. 0770D] were guilty of homicide and infanticide. Yet nowhere does he demonstrate this more fully than in the *Apologeticum*. Second, he openly declares that he had deferred many matters to be more fully elucidated by him elsewhere, lest he repeat and treat them again in other books. But where, I ask, did he discuss these matters against the gentiles more abundantly than in the *Apologeticum*?

Who, therefore, after accurately weighing all that we have reported, will not acknowledge that Tertullian, while writing these books *To the Nations*, referred us to his *Apologeticum*, which he was then contemplating composing (above, chap. 3, art. 1)? The books *To the Nations*, therefore, were published by him before the *Apologeticum*. But from the date we have assigned to the latter, it may be inferred clearly enough that, in order to fulfill his pledge to write the *Apologeticum*, he did not allow [Col. 0771A] a long interval of time to intervene, nor could he endure any delay before pleading the cause of the Christians before their governors, judges, and magistrates, just as he had done before all nations, and making their innocence clear to everyone. If, therefore, the *Apologeticum* was sent forth into public around the year of Christ 200, as we have said, these two books *To the Nations* had been published shortly before (ibid.).

## ARTICLE VI

### On the Subject of These Books, and Whether It Is Treated More Fully and Perfectly in Them Than in the *Apologeticum*

In the preceding article we cited several words of Tertullian in which he attests that he had omitted several matters from these books *To the Nations* which he had resolved to explain more fully in the *Apologeticum*. [Col. 0771B] But these words plainly contradict the opinion of those who contend that the subject of these treatises is handled more abundantly, more perfectly, and in a better order in the books *To the Nations* than in the *Apologeticum*. For by some reasoning unknown to me, they had persuaded themselves that the author’s keen, vehement, and acute genius, and his fiery acumen, flashes everywhere throughout these books, but that, swept along by its violent impetus, he wrote many things that were not sufficiently considered, polished, or refined. Yet this opinion plainly collapses if, as we have seen, the *Apologeticum* proceeded from him after the books *To the Nations*.

Furthermore, it is established that several matters of truly great importance are discussed in the *Apologeticum* which are omitted from the books *To the Nations*, although certain things are conversely found in these books [Col. 0771C] and not at all in the former. In order that these matters may become plainer and more evident to everyone, we have thought it proper to present an index of the chapters in which the same matters are treated. From this each person will readily judge in what respects these books differ from one another.

You see clearly from this index that the first book *To the Nations* agrees with the *Apologeticum*, [Col. 0772A] excepting its fifth chapter, in which an argument drawn by the pagans from certain evil Christians is refuted, and the final chapter, which is the conclusion of the whole book. The second book *To the Nations*, however, is divided into seventeen chapters, fifteen of which have nothing in common with the *Apologeticum*. In the first eight of these fifteen chapters, Tertullian attacks Varro’s fictitious threefold class of gods—physical, mythical, and plebeian—while in the latter seven he assails and overthrows the gods promoted by the Romans.

Of the fifty chapters of the *Apologeticum*, indeed, not even half agree with the two books *To the Nations*. It differs from them in the remaining chapters, in which Tertullian explains and asserts how true the teachings of the Christians are, [Col. 0772B] how chaste and incorrupt their morals, how pious and holy their solemn assemblies, and how religious their gatherings. But there is nothing about these things in the books *To the Nations*. In a word, these books demonstrate the innocence of the Christians, which was most unjustly assailed, and the injustice, depravity, corruption, and perversity of the pagans’ religion—and nothing more. In the *Apologeticum*, however, all these things are treated, and in addition the holiness and truth of the Christian religion. Who, therefore, can deny from this comparison of the books that their subject is explained and demonstrated far more fully and perfectly in the *Apologeticum* than in the books *To the Nations*?

## ARTICLE VII

### On the Title of the Books *To the Nations*; Why They Were Written by Tertullian and to Whom They Were Addressed; on Their Sole Manuscript, Their Various Editions, and the Notes and Observations of Certain Scholars upon Them

[Col. 0772C]

We observed that these books are entitled *To the Nations* in the Agobard manuscript (above, this chap., art. 3), but are cited by Jerome under the name of books *To the Gentiles*, although the two titles have the same meaning. Nevertheless, because those to whom these books are addressed are called by Tertullian himself “nations,” “wretched nations,” or “nations to be pitied,” the inscription prefixed in the Agobard manuscript certainly appears preferable (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, chap. 1, and book II, chap. 1).

Some, however, think it possible to infer from this title that these books were addressed to the common people and populace of the pagans, while the *Apologeticum* was addressed to their magistrates, [Col. 0772D] judges, and rulers. As regards the *Apologeticum*, indeed, this is certain and beyond controversy. Yet what prevents the two books *To the Nations* from having been written to absolutely all pagans, both peoples and judges and governors? Does Tertullian not assail in them not only the gentile populace but also the judges who, contrary to the prescribed form of equity and all laws, condemned Christians undefended and unheard? May it not therefore be said that Tertullian, when he beheld this injustice and extreme cruelty of the pagans, first addressed these books *To the Nations*—that is, to gentiles of every kind—in order to recall them, if possible, to a sounder mind? But because he had accomplished nothing, or certainly very little, he then sent his *Apologeticum* specifically to the [Col. 0773A] judges and governors, in order to compel them to examine the Christians’ cause and not to condemn the innocent without hearing it. Let impartial judges of these matters weigh and pronounce how much force these arguments possess.

As numerous and common as manuscripts of the *Apologeticum* are, so rare are manuscripts of these books *To the Nations* (above, chap. 2, art. 1). For only one survives for us, which Agobard, as they say and as we have already noted, archbishop of Lyons, had offered at the altar of Saint Stephen. But that manuscript has been so damaged in most of its margins by mold, filth, or rainwater that the final letters of its lines can no longer be read. Accordingly, in passages that are frequently corrupt, unless a more accurate manuscript is discovered, [Col. 0773B] a healing hand cannot be applied, nor can the author’s meaning be fully perceived.

In that ancient manuscript, moreover, the entire work was written in a single continuous text and without any division into chapters. James Gothofredus, however, divided it into various chapters and added chapter descriptions and summaries. Rigaltius omitted these, retaining only the chapter division, indicated in the margin merely by his own numerals. Everyone is therefore free to alter this division of the chapters and their descriptions, if he judges it appropriate.
