By the truth of the Church, we possess this Epistle as sent to the Ephesians (*On the Epistle to the Ephesians*, I and II), not to the Laodiceans. [Note: [Col. 0511D] “Not to the Laodiceans.” Those certainly err who think that the Epistle which Tertullian here seems, on Marcion’s authority, to indicate was written to the Laodiceans; for the passages quoted are found in chapter IV of the Epistle to the Ephesians. [Col. 0512D] The error arose from a rather inaccurate interpretation of Colossians IV, where τὴν ἐκ Λαοδικείας occurs; for they translated it “the one which is addressed to the Laodiceans,” when it should have been translated “the one which is from the city of Laodicea.” The epistle circulated today appears apocryphal and spurious. Blessed Jerome speaks of that Epistle to the Laodiceans as false and deserving rejection; and some think that it is the First Epistle to Timothy, which was written at Laodicea. Marcion, however, had entitled “To the Laodiceans” the Epistle which for us is “To the Ephesians.” LE PR.] But Marcion at one time took delight in tampering with its title, as though in this matter too he were an exceptionally diligent investigator. Yet nothing depends upon titles, since, in writing to certain persons, the Apostle wrote to everyone; at any rate, he proclaims that God in Christ to whom the things proclaimed properly belong.

To whom, then, will it belong, according to the good pleasure which he purposed in the mystery of his will, for the dispensation of the fulfillment of the times—to “recapitulate” (if I may put it so, in accordance with the force of the Greek word), that is, to bring back to their beginning, or to review from [Col. 0512C] the beginning, all things in Christ, both those in heaven and those on earth—unless to him to whom all things have belonged from the beginning, including the beginning itself, from whom come both the times and the fulfillments of the times [Note: [Col. 0512D] Junius inserts “and.”], as well as the dispensation on account of which all things are reviewed from the beginning in Christ?

But what beginning—that is, what point of origin—belongs to another god, who has no work at all? What times can there be without a beginning? What fulfillment without times? What dispensation without a fulfillment? Finally, what did he do on earth long ago, so that some lengthy dispensation involving the fulfillment of times might be reckoned to him, for the purpose of reviewing all things in Christ, including those in heaven? Nor shall we suppose that the things which [Col. 0513A] exist in heaven were done by one being other than him by whom, as everyone agrees, the things on earth were done. But if all these things cannot be assigned from the beginning to anyone other than the Creator, who will believe that they are reviewed by another in another Christ, rather than by their own author and in his own Christ?

If they belong to the Creator, they must necessarily be different from a different god. If different, they are surely contrary. How, then, can contrary things be reviewed in him by whom they are ultimately destroyed? For the words which follow also declare which Christ he means, when he says, “That we should be to the praise of his glory, we who [Note: [Col. 0513D] “Have hoped beforehand in Christ”: a literal rendering of the Greek προηλπικότας, “those who have hoped beforehand.” LE PR.] have hoped beforehand in Christ.” For who could have “hoped beforehand,” that is, have hoped in God before he came, except the Jews, to whom Christ was announced beforehand from the beginning? The one who was announced beforehand, therefore, was also the one hoped for beforehand.

Indeed, he applies this to himself—that is, to the Jews—in such a way as to draw a distinction when he turns [Col. 0513B] to the nations: “In whom you also, after you had heard the word of truth, the Gospel, in whom you believed and were sealed with his Holy Spirit of promise.” Whose promise? The one made through Joel (*Joel* II): “In the last days I shall pour out of my Spirit upon all flesh”—that is, upon the nations also. Thus both the Spirit and the Gospel will belong to that Christ who was hoped for beforehand because he had been proclaimed beforehand.

Moreover, the “Father of glory” is he whose Christ is sung in the Psalm as the King of Glory ascending (*Ps.* XXIII, 10): “Who is this King of Glory? The Lord of hosts, he is the King of Glory.” From him the spirit of wisdom is sought, with whom these several forms of spiritual gift are also enumerated among the seven spirits through Isaiah (*Isa.* XI). He will give enlightened eyes of the heart who also enriched the outward eyes with light, and to whom the blindness of the people is displeasing (*Isa.* XLII, 19): [Col. 0513C] “And who is blind but my servants?” And: “The servants of God have been blinded.”

With him also are “the riches of the inheritance among the saints,” for he promised that inheritance through the calling of the nations (*Ps.* II, 8): “Ask of me, and I shall give you the nations as your inheritance.” He worked his power in Christ by raising him from the dead, seating him at his right hand, and subjecting all things to him—the same one who also said (*Ps.* CIX): “Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.” For elsewhere too the Spirit says to the Father concerning the Son (*Ps.* VIII, 7): “You have subjected all things beneath his feet.”

If, from these statements which are recognized as belonging to the Creator, another god and another Christ are inferred, let us now look for the Creator. Clearly, I think, we find him when the Apostle says that they were dead in the offenses in which they had walked, according to the age of this world, according to [Col. 0513D] “the prince of the power of this air” [Note: [Col. 0513D] Semler and Oberthür omit “of this.”], who works in the children of unbelief. But even here Marcion cannot interpret “the world” as “the god of the world.” A created thing is not identical with its creator, something made with its maker, or the world with God.

Nor will he who is the prince of the power of the ages be called the prince of the power of the air. For the ruler [Col. 0514A] of higher things is never designated from the lower, even though the lower things too are assigned to him. Nor can he be regarded as the agent of unbelief, since he himself rather suffers unbelief at the hands of Jews and nations. It is enough, therefore, that these descriptions do not fall upon the Creator. But if there is also someone to whom they are more applicable [Note: [Col. 0513D] Pamelius: “it is applicable.”], the Apostle certainly knew this better.

Who is he? Without doubt, the one who sets the children of unbelief in opposition to the Creator himself, having gained possession of this air, just as the prophet reports him as saying [Note: [Col. 0513D] Pamelius also reads: “puts him forward as saying.”] (*Isa.* XIV, 14): “I shall set my throne in the clouds; I shall be like the Most High.” This will be the devil, whom elsewhere also—if indeed they wish the Apostle to be read in that way—we shall recognize as “the god of this age.” For in this way he has filled the whole age with the lie of his divinity. Had he not existed, these statements could indeed have been understood as referring to the Creator.

Moreover, although the Apostle had lived within Judaism [Note: [Col. 0513D] Latinius adds “had.”], it does not follow, merely because [Col. 0514B] he inserted the words concerning the offenses “in which we too all once lived,” that the Creator must be understood as the lord of offenses and the prince of this air. Rather, while in Judaism, he had been one of the children of unbelief, having the devil as his operator when he persecuted the Church and the Creator’s Christ.

“For this reason,” he says, “we too were children of wrath—but by nature.” This was lest, because the Creator called the Jews his children, the heretic should argue [Note: [Col. 0514D] Junius adds “this.”] that the Creator is the lord of wrath. For when he says, “We were by nature children of wrath,” whereas the Jews are not the Creator’s children by nature but by the adoption of their fathers, he referred the “children of wrath” to nature, not to the Creator. To sum up, he adds, “Just as the rest also”—who certainly are not children of God. It is apparent that he ascribes offenses, the desires of the flesh, unbelief, and wrath to the common nature of all human beings [Note: [Col. 0514D] Pamelius: “to be ascribed.”], although the devil has laid hold of nature [Note: [Col. 0514D] “Although the devil has laid hold of nature.” Thus Dionysius Cato said in his *Distichs* that the miser “lays hold of the form of a denarius.” RIG.], [Col. 0514C] which he himself has now corrupted by introducing the seed of offense.

“We are,” he says, “his workmanship, created in Christ.” To make is one thing, to create another. Yet he assigned both to one and the same being. A human being, moreover, is the Creator’s workmanship. Therefore the same one created him in Christ who also made him. With respect to substance, he made him; with respect to grace, he created him.

Examine also the connected words: “Remember that you were formerly nations in the flesh, [Note: [Col. 0514D] Oberthür inserts “who.”] called the uncircumcision by what is called the circumcision in the flesh, made by hand; that you were at that time without Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers to their covenants and promise, having no hope and without God in the world.” Without which God, and without which Christ, were the nations? Surely the one to whom belonged the commonwealth of Israel, the covenants, and the promise.

“But now,” he says, “in Christ, [Col. 0514D] you who were far away have been brought near by his blood.” From whom had they formerly been far away? From those whom he named above: from the Creator’s Christ, from the commonwealth of Israel, from the covenants, from the hope of the promise, and from God himself. If these things are so, then the nations are now brought near in Christ to those from whom they had once been far away.

But if in Christ we have been brought near to the commonwealth of Israel, which consists in the religion of God the Creator, and to the covenants, the promise, and the God of Israel himself, it is quite ridiculous to suppose that the Christ of another god brought us from afar to the Creator. The Apostle remembered that the calling of the nations, who were to be called from afar, had been proclaimed thus (*Isa.* XLVI, 12): “Those who were far from me have drawn near to my righteousness.” For the Creator’s righteousness, no less than his peace, was announced in Christ, as we have often already shown.

Accordingly, “he himself,” the Apostle says, “is our peace, who made the two one”—namely, the Jewish people and the Gentile people, what was near and what was far—“by dissolving in his flesh the intervening wall of hostility.” But Marcion removed the word “his,” so as to assign the flesh to the hostility, as though it were hostile through carnal vice, rather than hostile to Christ. As I have said elsewhere, so too here: [Note: [Col. 0515D] “Not Marrucinian, but Pontic.” The Marrucini were peoples neighboring the Ferentani and Vestini in Italy outside Latium, and therefore barbarians and uncultivated; hence *Marrucinus* means ἀμαθής καὶ ἀναίθητος, “ignorant and senseless,” since the name was customarily used as an insult against stupid people. I would think that “cliff-dwellers” means almost the same as “Marrucinians”; Tertullian uses that word twice in chapter 4 of *On the Pallium*. Juvenal, Satire 2, says:

> The rustic crowd, their plows laid aside, would listen to Montanus.

LE PR.] [Col. 0515B] Not Marrucinian, but Pontic man: above you confessed his blood, but here you deny his flesh.

If he made the law of commandments void in its ordinances, he certainly did so by fulfilling the law. For “You shall not commit adultery” is now without further function when it is said, “You shall not even look with lust”; and “You shall not kill” is without further function when it is said, “You shall not even curse.” You cannot turn the law’s helper into its adversary.

“He created the two in himself”: the same one who had made them was also creating them, in accordance with what was said above: “For we are his workmanship, created in Christ.” He made them “into one new human being, making peace.” If truly new, then truly a human being also, not an apparition; new, moreover, and born in a new way from a virgin, by the Spirit of God, “that he might reconcile both to God”—and to that God whom both races, Jewish and Gentile [Note: [Col. 0515D] Pamelius: “the Gentile people.”], had offended—“in one body,” he says, “when he had slain the hostility in it through the cross.”

Thus here too the flesh is a body in Christ, one which could suffer [Col. 0515C] the cross. When he had therefore announced peace to those who were near and to those who were far, and both together had obtained access to the Father, “we are no longer strangers or sojourners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God’s household”—surely of that God from whom we showed above that we had been alienated and situated far away.

“You have been built upon the foundation of the apostles.” The heretic removed the words “and the prophets,” forgetting that the Lord appointed in the Church prophets as well as apostles. He was afraid, naturally, that [Note: [Col. 0515D] Rhenanus: “whether perhaps.”] our building in Christ might be established upon the foundations of the ancient prophets also, although the Apostle himself never ceases everywhere to build us up from the prophets. For where did he obtain the expression that Christ is “the chief cornerstone,” except from the signification of the Psalm (*Ps.* CXVII): “The stone which the builders rejected, this has become the chief cornerstone”? As for [Col. 0515D] the hands of a mutilating heretic, I am not surprised if he removes syllables, since he very often steals away whole pages. [Note: [Col. 0515D] Pamelius: “steals away.”]

## CHAPTER XVIII

[Col. 0516A]

(*On the Epistle to the Ephesians*, III–VI.) The Apostle says that grace was given to him, the last of all, to enlighten everyone concerning “the dispensation of the mystery hidden from the ages in God, who created all things.” The heretic snatched away the preposition “in” and caused the passage to be read thus: “Hidden from the ages from the God who created all things.” But the falsehood leaps out. For the Apostle continues: “So that through the Church the manifold wisdom of God might be made known to the principalities and powers in the heavenly places.”

Whose principalities and powers does he mean? If the Creator’s, how can it be that the other god wished to display his wisdom to the Creator’s principalities and powers, but not to the Creator himself, when not even the powers could have learned anything without their own prince? Or, if he did not name God here because the prince himself is implicitly included among them, then he must also have declared that the mystery was hidden from the principalities and powers of him who created all things, likewise including God himself among them.

But if he says that it was hidden from them, he ought to have said that it was manifested to him. Therefore it was not hidden from God, but “in God, the Creator of all things”; it was hidden, however, from his principalities and powers (*Isa.* XL, 13): “For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?” [Note: [Col. 0516D] “Here the heretic was caught.” This refers to Marcion; the passage requires no explanation from me. LE PR.]

Caught here, the heretic perhaps altered the text so as to say that his god wished to make the dispensation of his mystery known to his own powers and principalities, although the God who created all things had been ignorant of it. And how was it appropriate to allege the ignorance of an alien creator, separated from him by a long interval, when even the members of the higher god’s own household did not know it?

Nevertheless, it was also destined to become known to the Creator. Was it not surely known to him that the mystery was to be revealed beneath [Col. 0516C] heaven [Note: [Col. 0516D] Oberthür, following Semler, adds “and.”], on his earth? From this, therefore, what we established above is confirmed. For if the Creator was one day to learn that hidden mystery of the higher god, and if Scripture read, “Hidden from the God who created all things,” the Apostle ought to have continued thus: “So that the manifold wisdom of God might be made known to them”—then also to the powers and principalities of whichever god would learn it together with the Creator. It is therefore evident that something has been removed, since even in this form the text remains secure for its own truth.

Now I too wish to weave a controversy for you out of the Apostle’s allegories. What novel [Note: [Col. 0516D] Junius: “new”; the Vatican manuscript: “which we know that he had in the prophets.”] forms would he have had in the prophets? “He led captivity captive,” he says. With what weapons? In what battles? From the devastation of what nation? From the overthrow of what city? What women, what children, or what petty kings did the victor bind in chains?

For when Christ is sung in David (*Ps.* XLIV, 4) as girded with a sword upon his thigh, or in Isaiah (*Isa.* VIII) as taking the spoils of Samaria and the might of Damascus, you portray him as a genuine [Col. 0517A] and visible warrior. Now therefore recognize that his armor and warfare are spiritual, if you have now learned that the captivity is spiritual, so that you may recognize this captivity also as his; or recognize it because the Apostle took mention of this captivity from the prophets, from whom he also took his commandments:

“Putting away falsehood, let each one speak truth to his neighbor.” [Note: [Col. 0517C] Semler omits “his.”] And, “Be angry, and do not sin”—the very words in which the Psalm (*Ps.* IV, 5) expressed its meaning. “Let not the sun go down upon your anger.” “Have no fellowship with the works of darkness.” “For with the righteous you will be righteous, and with the perverse you will act perversely” (*Ps.* XVII, 27). And: “Remove the evil person from your midst” (*1 Cor.* V, 13). And: “Come out from among them, and do not touch what is unclean” (*2 Cor.* VI, 17). “Separate yourselves, you who carry the vessels [Col. 0517B] of the Lord” (*Isa.* LII, 11).

So too the command not to “be drunk with wine, in which is disgrace” [Note: [Col. 0517C] Rigaltius; the Venetian edition incorrectly reads “disgraceful.”], comes from the passage where those who made the saints drunk are rebuked: “You gave my saints wine to drink” (*Amos* II, 12)—the wine which Aaron the priest and his sons were forbidden to drink when they approached the holy things (*Deut.* XXII [reference as printed]). And to teach people to sing to God in psalms and hymns belongs to him who knew that those who drank wine accompanied by drums and harps were rebuked by God (*Isa.* XI, 12 [reference as printed]).

Thus, when I find someone’s commandments, the seeds of his commandments, or their enlargements, I recognize his apostle. But from where does he confirm that women ought to be subject to their husbands? “Because,” he says, “the man is the head of the woman.” Tell me, Marcion: does your god establish the authority of his law from the Creator’s work? This by now is clearly too little, since he also derives from it the constitution of his own Christ and his own Church: “Just as Christ is the head [Col. 0517C] of the Church.”

Likewise, when he says, “He who loves his wife [Note: [Col. 0517C] Oberthür omits “his.”] loves his own flesh, just as Christ loves the Church.” You see your Christ and your Church compared with the Creator’s work. How much honor is bestowed upon the flesh under the name of the Church! “No one,” he says, “hates his own flesh”—except, plainly, Marcion alone—“but he nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the Church.” But you alone hate it, taking resurrection away from it. You will also have to hate the Church, since it is loved in the same way by Christ.

But Christ loved the flesh as he loved the Church. No one will fail also to love the image of the bride; indeed, he will preserve, honor, and crown it. The likeness shares in the honor of the reality. Must I now labor to prove that the God of the man and of Christ, of the woman and of the Church, of flesh and of spirit, is the same, when the Apostle himself applies—and indeed expounds—the Creator’s declaration: “For this reason [Note: [Col. 0517C] Semler: “this.”] a man shall leave his father and mother, and the two shall become one flesh” [Note: [Col. 0518C] Oberthür: “in one flesh.”]? “This mystery is great.”

Amid all this, it is enough that the Creator’s mysteries are great in the Apostle’s estimation, though insignificant among the heretics.

[Note: [Col. 0517D] “But I speak,” he says, “with reference to Christ and the Church.” These are Paul’s words to the Ephesians, chapter V, where he treats of conjugal love and says that marriage is a mystery, and indeed a great one; but immediately he adds, ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω εἰς Χριστὸν καὶ εἰς τὴν Ἐκκλησίαν, “But I speak with reference to Christ and the Church.” “This mystery,” he says, “is great; but I speak with reference to Christ and the Church.” He indicates that the union of man and woman is a great mystery because it signifies and figures something great, extending to Christ and the Church. The Apostle exhorts spouses to love one another with that love, namely, by which one is made from two, so that both are one flesh and one soul. He says that this love is a figure and mystery—or type, image, and mirror—of Christ’s love toward the Church, which ought to cleave and be joined to her bridegroom in such a way that one is made from two and both are one; and therefore she should live and be governed while animated by no spirit other than Christ’s, so that Christ lives in her and she in Christ.

The Apostle therefore treats two matters. He shows that the marriage of man and woman was instituted by God so that one might be made from two; therefore a human being ought not to separate what God has joined, nor ought there to be divorce between spouses, for if one spouse divorces the other, one of them separates things which have been joined by God. This is Paul’s first point.

He then adds that harmony between spouses ought to be holy and chaste, since Christ takes from it an example, mystery, and likeness of his own love toward the Church, such as the love of a bridegroom toward his bride ought to be; and this love ought to be repaid according to the same example of love, such as that of a bride toward her bridegroom. Paul says that this comparison is a great mystery. This should certainly teach the shepherds τῆς καλλίστης ζώων ἀγέλης ἀνθρώπων, “of the noblest flock of living creatures, human beings,” to impress it again and again upon the minds of the faithful. RIG.]

“But I speak,” he says, “with reference to Christ and the Church.” The mystery receives an interpretation, not a separation. He shows that the figure of the mystery had been provided beforehand by him to whom the mystery itself certainly belonged.

[Col. 0518B] What does Marcion think? The Creator could not have provided figures beforehand for an unknown god, especially since that god was his adversary. But if he was known, the higher god ought not to have borrowed anything from the lower, especially for the purpose of destroying it.

Let children also obey their parents. For even if Marcion removed the words, “For this is the first commandment with a promise,” the Law says (*Exod.* XX): “Honor your father and mother.” And: “Parents, bring up your children in the discipline and correction of the Lord.” For you have also heard it said to the ancients: “You shall recount these things in the ears of your children, and your children likewise in the ears [Note: [Col. 0518C] Pamelius: “into the ears.”] of their children.” Why now give me two gods, if there is one discipline? And if there are two, I shall follow the one who taught first.

But if our struggle is against “world-rulers,” oh, how many creator gods there now are! Why should I not also insist that a single “world-ruler” ought to have been named if the Creator was meant, since the powers mentioned beforehand would have belonged to him? Moreover, since above the Apostle commands us to put on the armor in which we may stand against the machinations of the devil, he has already shown that the things he adds after the devil—the powers and world-rulers of this darkness—belong to the devil, as we too assign them to the devil.

Or, if the devil is the Creator, who will be the devil under the Creator? Are there two devils, just as [Note: [Col. 0518C] Semler: “if.”] there are two gods, and powers and [Note: [Col. 0518D] “Against the world-rulers.” The Vulgate has “rulers of the world”; but since Tertullian preserves the etymology, not without a certain scrupulousness, he thus translates the Greek κοσμοκράτορας, “world-rulers.” [Col. 0519D] It signifies a demon; and by those “world-rulers,” he presently means “spiritual forces of wickedness,” translating πνευματικὰ τῆς πονηρίας. LE PR.] world-rulers in the plural?

[Col. 0519A] But how can the Creator be at once both the devil and God, when the devil is not both God and devil in the same respect? Either both are gods, if both [Note: [Col. 0519C] Oberthür omits “both.”] are now devils; or else the one who is God here is not the devil, just as the devil is not God.

I ask from what act of denunciation the very term “devil,” or “slanderer,” can apply to the Creator. Perhaps he denounced some intention of the higher god, since he himself suffered denunciation from the archangel—and a lying denunciation at that. For he had not forbidden the taste of that little tree to prevent them from becoming gods, but to prevent them from dying through transgression.

Nor did “spiritual forces of wickedness” signify the Creator merely because the Apostle added “in the heavens.” For the Apostle also knew that spiritual wickedness had been committed in the heavens by the angels who stumbled because of the daughters of human beings (*Gen.* VI).

And what sense would it make [Note: [Col. 0519D] Oberthür: “made.”] for him to censure the Creator through ambiguities and I know not what riddles, when, although already placed in chains because of his freedom in preaching, he surely afforded the Church an example of constancy in manifesting the mystery through the opening of his mouth—the constancy that he there instructed it to request from God?

## CHAPTER XIX

(*On the Epistle to the Colossians*.) In my prescription against all heresies, I am accustomed to establish a concise argument from the testimony of chronology, claiming priority for our rule over every later heresy. The Apostle will now prove this too when he says: “Concerning the hope laid up in heaven, which you heard in the word of the truth of the Gospel, which has come to you, just as it has throughout the whole world.” For if even then the Gospel tradition had flowed everywhere, how much more has it done so now!

Moreover, if what flowed everywhere is ours rather than that of any heresy—much less that of Marcion in the age of Antoninus [Note: [Col. 0519D] Rigaltius: “Antonian.”]—then ours will be the apostolic tradition. But even when Marcion’s tradition has filled the whole world, it will not even then be able to defend itself as apostolic. For even so it will be established that the apostolic tradition is the one which first filled the world—with the Gospel, namely, of that God who also sang these words concerning its proclamations (*Ps.* XVIII, 4): “Their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world.”

He calls Christ “the image of the invisible God.” But we too call the Father of Christ invisible, knowing that the Son was always seen in former times—if anyone was seen in the name of God—as the Father’s image. Thus Marcion must not tear apart, on this ground also, a distinction between a visible and an invisible god, since from of old the declaration of our God has been (*Exod.* [Col. 0520A] XXXIII, 20): “No one will see God [Note: [Col. 0519D] Rhenanus: “the Lord.”] and live.”

If Christ is not “the firstborn of creation,” as the Creator’s Word through whom all things were made and without whom nothing was made; if in him all things were not created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones, dominions, principalities, or powers; if all things were not created through him and in him—for these statements ought to have displeased Marcion—the Apostle surely would not have stated so unreservedly: “And he is before all.”

For how is he before all persons if he is not before all things? How is he before all things if he is not [Note: [Col. 0519D] “The firstborn of creation”: that is, the firstborn of all things formed and created by God. RIG.] the firstborn of creation, if he is not the Creator’s Word? From what will it be proved that he existed before everyone, when he appeared after everything? Who knows that someone existed earlier when he did not know that he existed at all?

Likewise, how did it please God that all fullness should dwell in him? First, what is [Col. 0520B] this fullness, unless it consists of those things which Marcion removed—the things created in Christ, in heaven and on earth, angels and human beings; unless it consists of those invisible and visible things, of thrones, dominions, principalities, and powers?

Or if our pseudo-apostles and Judaizing evangelists inserted these things on their own authority, and assigned them to the fullness of Marcion’s god, who created nothing, then what sense does it make for the Creator’s rival and destroyer to have wished the Creator’s fullness to dwell in his own Christ?

Finally, to whom does he “reconcile all things in himself, making peace through the blood of his cross,” unless to the one whom all those things [Note: [Col. 0520D] Oberthür: “all things.”] had offended, against whom they had rebelled through transgression, and to whom they had most recently belonged? For they could be conciliated to a stranger; but they could be reconciled to no one except their own master.

Thus he also restores us, who were once “alienated and enemies in mind through evil [Col. 0520C] works,” to favor with the Creator, against whom we had committed offense by worshiping the creation in opposition to the Creator.

Whenever, moreover, he says that the Church is the body of Christ—as here he says that he “fills up what remains of the afflictions of Christ in his flesh, for his body, which is the Church”—you must not on that account transfer every mention of “body” away from the substance of the flesh. For above he also says that we were reconciled “in his body through death”—surely in that body in which he could die through the flesh. He died not through the Church, but certainly for the Church, exchanging one “body” for another: the carnal for the spiritual.

But when he warns [Note: [Col. 0519D] One must beware of subtle argumentation and philosophy. Tertullian cited this passage of the Apostle in more than one place, though in a different form; for in *On Prescription*, chapter 7, he spoke of this philosophical vanity, and elsewhere he calls philosophers the patriarchs of the heretics. What that philosophy was would be doubtful if the Fathers did not indicate the Peripatetic philosophy, which, although it gained strength among Catholics through corrupt custom, nevertheless supplied the Carpocratians and the Gnostics with weapons for disturbing the truth. Saint Ambrose in *On Duties*, and Origen against Celsus, show that the sect founded upon Epicurus’s doctrine was more pernicious.

Moreover, during the reign of Philip Augustus, when a council was convened against the heresy of Amalric, Rigordus testifies in his *Deeds of Philip Augustus* that Aristotle’s *Metaphysics*, only recently brought into Gaul from Greece, was burned. And, as something to be noted in favor of the Peripatetics, I remember reading in Alexander Neckam’s work *On the Nature of Things* that learned men at that time held the opinion that they freely declared that Antichrist alone [Col. 0521D] could understand Aristotle’s books, which he would use to refute those who were to dispute against him. LE PR.] against subtle argumentation, philosophy, and [Note: [Col. 0520D] Pamelius: “and.”] empty deception, which is [Note: [Col. 0520D] Par.: “arises.”] “according to [Col. 0521A] the elements of the world,” he does not mean according to heaven or earth, but according to secular learning; and “according to tradition,” namely, the tradition of human subtle reasoners and philosophers.

It would indeed take too long, and belong to another work, to show that by this declaration all heresies are condemned, since they are all constructed from the resources of subtle argumentation and the rules of philosophy. But let Marcion recognize the principal boundary of his faith as coming from the school of Epicurus: he introduces a sluggish Lord so that he may say that the Lord is not to be feared; he places matter alongside God the Creator, borrowing from the portico of the Stoics; and he denies the resurrection of the flesh, on which likewise no philosophy agrees.

Our truth is so far removed from their inventions that it fears to provoke the wrath of God, trusts that he brought forth all things from nothing, promises that he will restore the selfsame flesh, and is not ashamed that Christ [Col. 0521B] was born from the womb of a virgin, although philosophers, heretics, and pagans themselves laugh. For God chose the foolish things of the world (*1 Cor.* I) to confound the wise—without doubt that God who, in view of this arrangement of his, had threatened beforehand that he would destroy the wisdom of the wise.

With this simplicity of truth, which is contrary to subtle argumentation and philosophy, we can entertain no perverse belief. Finally, if God gives us life together with Christ by forgiving our offenses, we cannot believe that offenses are forgiven by one against whom they were never committed, since he was formerly unknown.

Come now, when he says, “Let no one judge you in food or drink, or in respect of a feast day, a new moon, or Sabbaths, which are a shadow of things to come, but the body is Christ’s,” what do you think, Marcion? We are no longer reopening the question of the Law, except to note that here too he teaches how it has been set aside: namely, by being transferred from shadow to body—that is, from figures to the reality, which is Christ.

Therefore both the shadow and the body belong to the same one; that is, both his Law [Note: [Col. 0521D] Pamelius: “light.”] and his Christ. Assign the Law to one [Note: [Col. 0521D] Semler adds “God.”] and Christ to another god, if you can separate a shadow from the body whose shadow it is. Christ plainly belongs to the Law, if he is the body belonging to its shadow.

If, moreover, he censures certain people who, on the basis of angelic visions, were saying that one must abstain from foods—“Do not touch, do not taste”—and who wished to walk in humility of mind while not holding fast to the head, he does not on that account strike at the Law and Moses as though Moses had established the prohibition of certain foods on the basis of angelic superstition. For it is established that Moses received the Law from God.

Finally, he assigned this discipline “according to the commandments,” he says, “and doctrines of human beings,” to those who did not hold fast to the head [Col. 0521D]—that is, to Christ himself, in whom all things are reviewed and recalled to their beginning, including matters indifferent in respect of foods.

As for the remaining commandments, since they are the same, let it suffice that elsewhere we have already shown how they flowed from the Creator. When he proclaimed that the old things would pass away and that he would make all things new, and also commanded (*Jer.* IV, 3), “Break up for yourselves a new fallow ground,” he was already then teaching people to put off the old human being and put on the new (*On the Epistle to the Philippians*).
