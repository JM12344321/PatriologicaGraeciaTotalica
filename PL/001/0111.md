For he wrote to Paula: [Col. 1048C] “As men celebrated triumphs in the chariot, a companion remained close behind them, saying at every acclamation of the citizens: ‘Remember that you are a man’” (Jerome, epistle 22, *To Paula*). Yet if an emperor amid so magnificent a triumphal procession had to remember his humanity, how much more throughout the whole course of his life. Christians therefore could not call any emperor God, since, as Tertullian rightly says, they did not know how to lie, nor did they dare to mock him (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 449).

He presses the point further: Augustus, although the organizer of the Roman Empire, which had already been founded by Julius Caesar, did not wish even to be called “lord,” much less “god” (*Ibid.*, ch. 34, p. 450). If you desire a witness to this moderation, we shall summon Suetonius, who handed down the following concerning [Col. 1048D] the same Augustus: “He always recoiled from the title ‘Lord’ as from a curse and a reproach. When, as he watched the games, the line was delivered in a mime, ‘O just and good Lord!’ and everyone, exulting as though it had been said of him, loudly expressed approval, he immediately checked their unseemly demonstrations with hand and countenance; and on the following day he rebuked them in a most severe edict. Thereafter he did not permit himself to be called ‘lord,’ even by his children or grandchildren, either seriously or in jest, and he forbade such flatteries even among them” (Suetonius, *Life of Augustus*, § 53).

But someone will rise in opposition and cite to us another witness, Cornelius Tacitus, who also committed to writing that various rumors were spread after the death of the same Augustus, and that he had left nothing remaining of the honors due to the gods, since he had wished himself to be worshiped in temples, and in the likeness of divine powers, through flamens and priests (Cornelius Tacitus, *Annals*, book I, near the beginning). [Col. 1049A] Yet an answer can certainly be made to him in other words of Suetonius: “Although he knew that temples were customarily decreed even to proconsuls, he accepted them in no province except in the joint name of himself and Rome. In the city itself, indeed, he most obstinately refused this honor” (Suetonius, *Life of Augustus*, § 52). Thus, even if he permitted the practice that must be discussed later, it is nevertheless no less certain that he rejected the title “lord,” which his successors seized upon most eagerly. For Pliny the Younger, although a pagan, openly asserted in his Panegyric to the emperor Trajan that the time had passed when cowardly flatterers fawned upon the emperor as upon a god and divine power: [Col. 1049B] “For we speak,” he says, “not of a tyrant, but of a citizen; not of a lord, but of a father” (Pliny, *Panegyric to Trajan*, beginning). Christians therefore had all the greater reason for refusing to call the emperor God, because, as our Author says, this is a title belonging to the one true God alone (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 34, p. 449), as we have observed elsewhere (*Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. 12, art. 1).

Because, however, long usage had already established the practice of attributing the name “lord” to emperors and others, Tertullian accordingly says: “Certainly I shall call the emperor ‘lord,’ but in the ordinary manner of speech, when I am not compelled to call him Lord in place of God” (Tertullian, *ibid.*). But Rigault removed these two words, *voce communi* (“in the ordinary manner of speech”), both because he had learned that they were not found in the Fulda manuscript and because, in his view, they have the flavor of a note taken from the margin. [Col. 1049C] Yet those words occur in all our copies and in others, whether printed or manuscript. Moreover, there is no room for doubt that several of them surpass the Fulda manuscript in antiquity. How, then, could those words have been intruded from the margin into the text? Do they not also produce an excellent sense, one consistent with the Author’s purpose? “I call the emperor ‘lord,’” he says, “when I am not compelled by that name to acknowledge him as God, but when I utter that name in the ordinary manner of speech, or in the common meaning in which a son customarily addresses his father, a slave his master, and other men one another.” How ordinary and popular this form of address was you may learn even from Martial alone:

> You know that you were born of a slave-woman, and you admit it ingratiatingly  
> When, Sosibianus, you call your father “lord.”

[Col. 1049D] And elsewhere:

> When I call you “lord,” Cinna, do not be pleased with yourself:  
> I often greet my slave in just the same way.

(Martial, book I, epigram 82.) But it would be thoroughly wearisome to prove at greater length things so commonplace and everywhere familiar to everyone. In this vulgar and ordinary sense, therefore, our Author continues, I call the emperor “Lord”; but otherwise I am free in relation to him and am not his slave. “For my Lord,” he says, “is the one God, almighty and eternal, the same who is also the Lord of the emperor himself, and whom consequently both he and all other men ought to serve” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 34, p. 449).

Our Author further acknowledges that the name “father” is far more pleasing [Col. 1050A] than “lord,” because the latter is a name of power, the former of dutiful affection. Accordingly, from what has now been said he draws this conclusion: “So far is it from being proper for the emperor to be called god—which cannot be believed—not only by most disgraceful but also by pernicious flattery, but as if, while having one emperor, you were to call another one so” (*Ibid.*). Yet these words, “which cannot be believed,” undoubtedly appeared useless to Rigault, who excised them contrary to the testimony of every manuscript, whether printed or unpublished. Was it therefore lawful for him, on his own authority, to cut out from Tertullian’s text everything that seemed superfluous to him? Is not this the sense of the passage? The emperor ought to be the father of his country, and this name is far more pleasing than “lord.” You ought not, therefore, to call the emperor God, since it cannot be believed that he is God. For [Col. 1050B] by this most disgraceful and pernicious flattery you signify that you have another emperor who is not God but a man, a man who commands you, another man.

Finally, if the pagans had adhered to their own laws and customs, it was not lawful for them to call any emperor a god except after his death and apotheosis, that is, his enrollment among the gods by decree of the senate, as we saw above. For, says Tertullian, “it is a curse to call Caesar a god before his apotheosis” (*Ibid.*). Herodian, more accurately than the others, described the solemn ceremony of this apotheosis, first reminding us that it was Roman custom for emperors to be consecrated by their successors and thus declared to have been enrolled among the gods (Herodian, *History of His Own Times*, p. 540). Before that, therefore, no one was permitted to call them gods. [Col. 1050C] For this reason Cornelius Tacitus concludes the fifteenth book of his *Annals* with these words: “Divine honor is not accorded to a prince before he has ceased to live among mortals” (Tacitus, *Annals*, book XV, at the end). Tertullian therefore rightly says in another book: “But we do not call the emperor a god. For on this point, as people commonly say, you jeer. Indeed, those of you who call Caesar a god mock him by saying what he is not, and you curse him because he does not wish to become what you call him; for he would rather live than become a god” (Tertullian, *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 18, p. 583). For because he could become a god only after his death, he certainly preferred to live rather than lay down his life and afterward become a god—or rather, be adorned with this wholly mendacious title. Christians therefore could not, without the greatest injustice, be called guilty of treason against human majesty or public enemies [Col. 1050D] merely because they refused to call the emperor God. For that title belongs solely to him who is not a man but eternal and immortal.

## CHAPTER XXII  
Christians are neither public enemies nor factious persons; rather, the pagans who accused them of these crimes are such.

### ARTICLE I  
Christians are not public enemies: although they could very easily have done so, they never wished to avenge the injuries inflicted upon them by the pagans—whether by secretly setting fire to the city, openly declaring war, separating themselves from those same pagans, who were fewer in number, or refusing to cast demons out of possessed bodies.

With as much rashness as the pagans babbled that Christians were guilty of treason against [Col. 1051A] human majesty, so with equal shamelessness they accused them of being public enemies and enemies of the whole human race. Nothing more rank than this slander could ever have been devised. For by divine law (Matt. 5:39; Luke 6:28) they were forbidden, as Tertullian says (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 36 ff., p. 460; and *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 17, p. 583), to wish evil upon anyone, to do evil, speak evil, or think evil of anyone, and to repay an injury in kind. They obeyed these precepts with the greatest exactness, as is evident from what we previously said about their most holy morals (above, ch. 12, art. 1 ff.). They were therefore not public enemies—indeed, not enemies of any human being at all, much less of the emperor and of the entire human race. And certainly, although they were tortured with every kind of insult [Col. 1051B] and torment by emperors and other pagans, not one of them was ever found among the very many, to be discussed shortly, who had conspired against those same emperors and the commonwealth.

Yet this was not to be attributed to the weakness or powerlessness of those same Christians. For they could have avenged the injuries done them by the pagans, not only secretly and through concealed stratagems, but openly and in outright warfare. They could have done so secretly, since “even one night,” as Tertullian says, “could, with a few little torches, furnish abundant opportunity for vengeance” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 37, p. 462); that is, amid the silence of a dark night, they could have set cities on fire with a very few torches purchased at minimal cost. “But far be it,” he continues, “that a divine sect should be avenged by human fire, [Col. 1051C] or should grieve to suffer that by which it is tested” (*Ibid.*); or, as he himself wrote to Scapula: “Far be it that we should resent suffering the things we desire, so that we might contrive some vengeance ourselves, when we await it from God” (same author, *To Scapula*, ch. 2). He therefore offers three reasons why Christians refused secretly to avenge their offenses, although it was easy for them to do so. First, because theirs is a divine sect, whose laws forbid vengeance, and they considered it a sin not to obey them. Second, they awaited that vengeance from their almighty God, the most just punisher of crimes. Third, they most willingly endured all the torments inflicted upon them by the pagans, because they knew that these did not harm them but tested whether they were worthy of an eternal reward.

[Col. 1051D] For these reasons alone, and not from any weakness or powerlessness, they abstained from taking vengeance upon their pagan enemies. For nothing was easier or more readily available to them. We have shown above (above, ch. 12, art. 1) that they had filled every place throughout the whole world, with the sole exception of the temples of the false gods, and that their number was far greater than that of the pagans. What, then, could have prevented them from declaring war, defeating the pagans, and inflicting deserved punishments upon them when defeated (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 37, p. 462)? Yet suppose, if you wish, that they were inferior to them in both numbers and resources: were they thereby less fitted to wage war and conquer their enemies? Did the pagans not acknowledge that they did not fear death [Col. 1052A] (*Ibid.*)? Surely the less they feared it, the more fiercely they would have fought despite every uncertain turn in the outcome of war, and thus could have exacted the penalties due from their enemies. But, bound by their religion, they never wished to attempt it, whether armed or unarmed.

Furthermore, they had no need of any war or violence to obtain vengeance (*Ibid.*, p. 463). For separation and withdrawal from the pagans would have sufficed. The latter, then astounded at their isolation, would have had more enemies than citizens, and certainly very few people to govern: “For now,” says Tertullian, “you have fewer enemies in comparison with the multitude of Christians ([almost all citizens; almost all citizens ought to be accounted Christians; and you preferred to call them enemies of the human race rather than of human error])” (*Ibid.*). So indeed the passage stands in our [Col. 1052B] manuscripts and in printed editions older than Rigault’s, except that in some of them *habendo* is read in place of *habendos*, and these final words, “rather than of human error,” have been cut away. But Rigault, with still greater boldness, excised everything that we have ordered to be enclosed in brackets. Yet he said not a single word about so extensive an excision. Did he therefore suppose that the reason for it was known to everyone because Tertullian said shortly afterward: “You preferred to judge us enemies, though we are plainly not enemies of the human race, but rather of error” (*Ibid.*)? But was an author never permitted to draw the same conclusion in the same words from two arguments? Tertullian concludes that the pagans preferred Christians to be judged enemies not of error but of the human race, because they had failed sufficiently to observe [Col. 1052C] that Christians would leave them more enemies than citizens, first by withdrawing from them and, second, by refusing to cast out demons, as will soon be said. He deliberately said “citizens,” because, as we have seen, by sharing a common life with them they were their fellow citizens.

But someone may perhaps object that this entire argument of Tertullian rests upon an intolerable exaggeration of the number of Christians. We ask such a person, however, what benefit could Tertullian have gained from an ill-timed hyperbole? For there he addresses the pagans, who were not ignorant of the number of Christians and who, had he been convicted at once of lying, would have denied him credibility in everything else. Furthermore, we showed above (above, ch. 12, art. 1) how true are the things that he stated so expressly both there [Col. 1052D] and elsewhere concerning the immense number of Christians.

Finally, by another argument he demonstrates how easily Christians could have avenged themselves. They alone, he says, had the power to cast demons out of the possessed bodies of pagans, as we saw previously (above, ch. 8, art. 6; Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 37, p. 463). But if they had not driven them out, “this alone would have sufficed for our vengeance”—these are his own words—“that a vacant possession should thereafter lie open to unclean spirits” (Tertullian, *loc. cit.*). Rigault, however, solemnly advises us there that he learned from others that the Fulda manuscript has *pateretis* instead of *pateret*. You will doubtless marvel at the extraordinary diligence of a man who notes, upon the testimony of others, this variant reading of such slight importance, while entirely omitting the preceding one [Col. 1053A] and others of far greater importance, even though they are supported by the authority of all the manuscripts. Yet the word *pateretis*, he says, has the flavor of Tertullian’s style. Do all the other readings he omitted have less of the flavor of that style? Surely no one, not even Rigault himself, would dare seriously to affirm it. Moreover, the common reading of every book except the Fulda manuscript is no less Tertullianic, and produces the same sense: if Christians did not put demons to flight, the bodies of the pagans would lie open to them at will, like vacant property.

What means of avenging themselves could have been more prompt and readily available than this? But they so abhorred every act of vengeance that they preferred to expel demons from the bodies of their pagan enemies—and thus benefit them—rather than fail to expel those demons and so fail to benefit them. [Col. 1053B] The pagans were therefore most ungrateful when, making light, as Tertullian says, of so great a protection, they clamored that Christians, who were so necessary to them, were public enemies of the human race (*Ibid.*).

### ARTICLE II  
How absurdly the pagans charged Christians with being public enemies because they did not celebrate the imperial festivals as the pagans did: by bringing hearths and couches out into public, feasting street by street, transforming the city into the likeness of a tavern, turning the ground into mud with wine, running about in crowds toward acts of violence, shamelessness, and the enticements of pleasure, and fastening laurels and lamps to the doorposts of their houses as though these were new brothels.

[Col. 1053C] Since Christians utterly abhorred every act of vengeance and rendered good service to everyone, with what demented fury, you will ask, did the pagans so boldly shout that they were public enemies? For no reason at all, save one that was plainly absurd, as you will understand from these words of Tertullian: “Christians are therefore public enemies because they do not utter toward the emperors honors that are empty, deceitful, and rash; because, as men of true religion, they celebrate even their festivals with conscience rather than with wantonness” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 452). Thus the whole of that enormous crime of the Christians was that they observed the imperial festivals only according to their own pious and holy rite, and not according to the utterly corrupt rite of the pagans.

If anyone first desires to know what those festivals were, he must remember that they are called by our Author himself [Col. 1053D] “the solemn days of the princes,” and “the vows and rejoicings of the Caesars” (*Ibid.*), which were customarily celebrated at various times. For every year, on the third day after the Kalends of January, “vows are undertaken for the safety of the princes,” as Gaius says (*Digest*, book L, title 16, law 233); or, as Tertullian himself wrote, “at the beginning of the year, at the beginning of the month” (Tertullian, *To His Wife*, ch. 6). They were also observed annually on the emperor’s natal day, that is, the very day on which he had been made emperor, and especially in the fifth, tenth, fifteenth, and twentieth year thereafter. These festivals were called quinquennial, decennial, quindecennial, and vicennial. We have touched briefly upon them in another dissertation (*Dissertation on Lucius Caecilius*, ch. 11, art. 3), [Col. 1054A] where you will find cited Pagi (Pagi, *Hypatic Dissertation*, part 2, ch. 2) and others who discuss them at greater length.

During those festivals the pagans prayed to their fictitious gods for the safety of the emperors and citizens, for the eternity of the empire and its flourishing condition, as has been observed by Pliny (Pliny the Younger, letters 36, 37, 101, 106, and *Panegyric to Trajan*, after the midpoint), Brisson (Brisson, *On Formulae*, book II, p. 110), and others. Christians, therefore, who worshiped the one true God, and who could offer the emperors only true honors prescribed by the sacred laws of their religion, did indeed celebrate their festivals, though not, of course, with the same wantonness as the pagans but “with conscience,” as Tertullian says (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 452); that is, with a true and sincere disposition of mind, [Col. 1054B] in the manner they knew God required. Hence he says elsewhere: “As regards the honors due to kings and emperors, we have sufficiently prescribed for us that in every act of obedience, according to the Apostle’s precept, we ought to be subject to magistrates, princes, and powers—but within the limits of our discipline, insofar as we remain separate from idolatry” (same author, *On Idolatry*, ch. 15).

He thus acknowledges that the imperial festivals were celebrated by Christians according to this law of the Apostle, and with prayers poured out to God, as we saw above, for the emperor and empire from the innermost affection of the heart. They were not celebrated in the pagan manner, concerning which, as he says in addressing the pagans themselves, “neither moderation, nor reverence, nor chastity permits such a manner of demonstrating your loyalty and sincerity ([but the opportunity for pleasure, rather than a worthy motive, has persuaded you to adopt it])” (same author, [Col. 1054C] *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 454). Everything you see enclosed in brackets in this text was completely removed by Rigault and his followers, contrary to the testimony of the printed and unpublished manuscripts. Yet you will find no reason given by them for this excision, unless by their silence they wished to indicate that these words seemed to them to have been added by some unskilled scribe. But who will believe them when they remain silent in this way? The common agreement of all the manuscripts clearly proves that these are Tertullian’s genuine words. Furthermore, his entire genius and manner of expression can readily be discerned in them. Their sense is this: Christians are proclaimed enemies of the emperors and of the commonwealth because they do not observe the imperial festivals in the pagan manner, a manner which neither moderation, nor reverence, nor chastity permits them to adopt; [Col. 1054D] and which the opportunity for pleasure, rather than a worthy motive for demonstrating loyalty and sincerity toward the emperors, persuaded those same pagans to celebrate. He thus openly asserts that in these solemn festivals the pagans possessed no loyalty or sincerity. For at that time they made utterly false vows for the emperors’ safety, although they wished those emperors to die soon and often, as we shall see in the following articles.

He also rightly complains that three virtues—moderation, reverence, and chastity—were then utterly destroyed. For what could be more contrary to these virtues than to hold magnificent and splendid banquets, not secretly in private homes but openly and publicly in the streets—banquets polluted by the intoxication, [Col. 1055A] drunkenness, impurity, wantonness, lust, and other crimes of the feasters? But, if you please, let us hear Tertullian himself: “A grand duty,” he says, “of those pagans observing those imperial festivals was, to be sure, to bring hearths and couches out into public” (*Ibid.*). The older editions read “choruses,” but “couches” is undoubtedly better. For there he speaks of public banquets at which the pagans reclined at table. Hence he adds shortly afterward: “With couches as ornate and sumptuous as possible they divided the forum among themselves” (*Ibid.*). They therefore spread so many couches in the forum, and with such magnificence, that they seemed to have divided and filled it among themselves.

But because the forum, however large it might be, was not sufficient for everyone, others feasted “street by street” (*Ibid.*), that is, in every quarter of the city, on the public roads and at the crossroads. [Col. 1055B] Consider, then, I ask, whether the following words of Cicero do not contribute something to understanding this custom. He openly reports that Apronius had ordered couches to be spread for himself in the middle of the forum, and that he was accustomed every day to feast not only in public but at the public expense, while music played at those feasts and the largest cups were served (Cicero, *Against Verres*, book III, p. 136, line 43). Cornelius Tacitus also recorded somewhere that “nothing anywhere delighted the emperor Nero so much as arranging banquets in public places and using the entire city as though it were his house” (Tacitus, *Annals*, book XV, before the midpoint, p. 127). The pagans, believing that everything was permitted to them on these imperial feast days, were then accustomed not only to use the entire city as though it were a house, but, as Tertullian says, “to efface the city beneath the appearance of a tavern” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 452), [Col. 1055C] so that the entire city bore the appearance of a shop and an inn. There are some who, by way of explanation of this passage, adduce these verses of Martial:

> Barber, innkeeper, cook, and butcher keep to their own thresholds;  
> Now it is Rome; recently it was one great shop.

(Martial, book VII, epigram 60.) But others judge that Martial uses the word *taberna* there not for an inn but for a market, or a place where goods were customarily sold. Whichever meaning is adopted, it is certain that the pagans displayed such intemperance, shamelessness, and wantonness in these public banquets that they not only gorged themselves with food but, as our Author says, “made mud with wine” spilled through the middle of the city streets [Col. 1055D] and mixed with the earth (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 452). One might then see intoxicated and drunken men running about in crowds toward acts of violence, shamelessness, and the enticements of lust; or, as he says later (*Ibid.*, ch. 39, p. 477), “into bands of beatings”—men who struck others with blows and whips—and “into companies of running about,” that is, groups who ran throughout the whole city in the manner of Bacchants. How great the intemperance in these public banquets was may be inferred from what Seneca wrote concerning private ones: “You see those same men gazing from their couches upon their eating house, delighting their ears with the sound of voices, their eyes with spectacles, and their palates with flavors. Their entire body is stimulated with soft and gentle fomentations; and [Col. 1056A] lest their nostrils meanwhile remain idle, the very place in which funeral rites are performed for luxury is perfumed with various scents” (Seneca, *On the Happy Life*, ch. 11, p. 624). For if such were their private banquets, how much more so the public feasts held in honor of the emperors, at which they believed even more atrocious and shameful acts were permitted to them.

Rightly and deservedly, therefore, did Christians utterly detest these things as unworthy of any person in whom even the smallest remnant of shame and true religion remained. Thus they observed those festive and solemn days of the Caesars chastely and soberly. Keeping far away from the corrupt customs of the pagans, “we do not,” says our Author, “shade our doorposts with laurels, nor break into the daylight with lamps” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 451). For hidden traitors, in order more safely to conceal their hatred and hostility toward the emperor, [Col. 1056B] then decked their doorposts with the freshest laurels bearing the most branches, and clouded their vestibules with the tallest and brightest lamps (*Ibid.*). Why he uses this last verb may be understood from these verses of Persius concerning Herod:

> The days of Herod have come, and every window,  
> With lamps arranged in rows, has spewed a greasy cloud.

(Persius, satire 5, near the end.) Concerning the same lamps and the laurel with which the doorposts of houses were adorned, Juvenal has these verses:

> All things gleam; the doorway has raised long laurels  
> And celebrates the festival with early-morning lamps.

(Juvenal, satire 12, v. 96 ff.) Tertullian gives the following reason why Christians utterly rejected these superstitions: “Every day they light lamps for those who have no light”—namely, the light of true religion—“and fasten to their doorposts laurels that will later burn, over which flames hang threateningly; to them belong both testimonies of darkness and omens of punishment” (Tertullian, *On Idolatry*, ch. 15), [Col. 1056C] namely, of the eternal fire by which impious men are to be punished after this life. Then he addresses the Christian thus: “If you have renounced temples, do not make your doorway the temple”—that is, of some false god (*Ibid.*). For Ovid sang concerning the temple of Jupiter:

> Yet why is the doorway veiled with laurel placed upon it,  
> And why does the shady tree encircle the august house?  
> Is it because this house has earned perpetual triumphs,  
> Or because it has always been loved by the Leucadian god?  
> Is it because the house itself is keeping festival, or because it makes all things festive?  
> Or is this the sign of peace that it bestowed upon the earth?

(Ovid, *Tristia*, book III, elegy 1, v. 41 ff.) [Col. 1056D] This reason alone was therefore sufficient for Christians to reject this profane rite. But Tertullian suggests another: “If you have renounced brothels, do not clothe your house with the appearance of a new brothel” (Tertullian, *On Idolatry*, *loc. cit.*). For not only laurels but also lamps were customarily hung at the doors of these infamous houses. Hence the following complaint of a door in Propertius:

> I do not complain that I am wounded by the nocturnal brawls of drinkers,  
> Or that I am often struck by unworthy hands;  
> Nor do I lack shameful garlands to hang upon me,  
> And torches always lying as signs for those shut out.

(Propertius, book I, elegy 16.) Tertullian therefore earnestly warns his wife [Col. 1057A] that it is lawful neither for her nor for any Christian woman, “at every royal festival, to come forth from a doorway crowned with laurel and illuminated with lamps, as from a new council chamber of public lusts” (Tertullian, *To His Wife*, ch. 6). Hence in his *Apologeticum* he rightly concludes that it is neither honorable nor lawful, “when a public solemnity demands it, to clothe your house with the appearance of some new brothel” (same author, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 451).

We do not think that any person of sound mind can fail to understand from what has thus far been said that certain heterodox persons wholly and completely abuse this testimony of the Author when they infer from it that Christians are forbidden to use wax candles and lights in their churches. For he is not there discussing these, concerning which we have written above (ch. 15, art. 4, and *Dissertation on Lactantius*, ch. [Col. 1057B] 25, art. 2), but other candles and lights customarily employed in a profane and pagan rite. Yet from this it is very clearly established that Christians were not public enemies of either the commonwealth or the emperors merely because they celebrated the latter’s festivals in their own pious manner, and not according to the utterly condemnable and detestable manner of the pagans.

### ARTICLE III  
It is shown that the pagans were in fact public enemies of the emperor, whom they openly assailed with taunts and abuse, to which the Tiber and the school of beasts bore witness; and that they always desired the spectacle of one new Caesar after another, even while acclaiming him: “May Jupiter add years to you from our years.”

[Col. 1057C] Tertullian was not content to demonstrate more clearly than daylight itself that Christians were not public enemies either of the emperor or of the commonwealth; rather, he openly and clearly showed that the pagans themselves had offended in precisely the same class of wrongdoing for which they censured others without the least justification. Nor does he summon foreign or far-removed witnesses. “I call upon,” he says, “the Quirites themselves, the Romans, the native populace of the seven hills”—that is, the common people of the city of Rome—“to answer whether the Roman tongue spares any Caesar” (Tertullian, *Apologeticum*, ch. 35, p. 454); that is, whether it spares him the taunts, jeers, abuse, and insults with which they assailed their emperors. “The Tiber and the school of beasts,” he says, “bear witness” to so many insults (*Ibid.*). But what, someone will ask, could this testimony of the river Tiber have been? [Col. 1057D] Some reply that this name designates the sailors dwelling on the riverbank, fierce and insolent men who, in times of scarcity and grain shortage, were accustomed to inveigh against Caesar himself with the most wanton speech. But this explanation will scarcely commend itself to anyone, nor does it appear consistent with Tertullian’s thought. Others suppose that by the name “Tiber” is denoted a bridge or some place from which slanderers and detractors were cast into the Tiber; but that is perhaps no better. You will therefore more easily persuade us that Tertullian is alluding to certain statues placed in public locations at Rome and on the bank of the Tiber, upon which biting pamphlets of this sort were posted. For elsewhere Tertullian himself says: “I shall arraign the sacrileges of vanity, and examine the irreverence of the native people themselves, and the witty pamphlets known to the statues, and those indirect remarks sometimes uttered by the council, [Col. 1058A] and the curses that resound in the circus: if you are not rebels in arms, you are always rebels in tongue” (same author, *To the Nations*, book I, ch. 17, p. 583). Does he not indicate there in other words those two things that he calls “the Tiber” and “the school of beasts” in the *Apologeticum*? For witty pamphlets insulting the emperor were hung upon statues that had been placed in public locations, doubtless near the Tiber. Hence the Tiber could be called a witness to these witty and insulting remarks.

As for the school of beasts, perhaps there will be some who think that this name designates their cage, upon whose door were inscribed the witty and pointed sayings with which they stung the emperors. [Col. 1058B] But what if the “school of beasts,” or perhaps “of beast-fighters,” is understood as the whole circus, concerning which we have just heard Tertullian say: “The curses of the circus resound”? For scarcely anywhere else were the pagans accustomed to injure their emperors with greater license, more bitter taunts, or more scurrilous sarcasm. Do you wish this fact, although sufficiently well known, to be proved by some examples? Hear these words of Suetonius concerning Caesar Augustus, though he was an excellent prince: “Once, on a day of games, the entire populace both took as an insult against him and approved with the loudest assent a verse delivered on the stage concerning a *gallus* of the Mother of the gods beating a drum” (Suetonius, *Life of Augustus*, § 68):

> Do you see how the wanton fellow governs the world with his finger?

Afterward, concerning the emperor Tiberius: “His anxious mind was further tormented by various reproaches from every quarter, since every condemned man, without exception, flung every kind of abuse at him either to his face or through pamphlets placed in the orchestra” [Col. 1058C] (same author, book III, *Life of Tiberius*, § 66). Julius Capitolinus, moreover, relates the following concerning the emperor Verus: “He took such an interest in the circus games that he frequently both sent and received letters to and from provincials concerning them. Finally, even when present and sitting with Marcus, he endured many insults from the supporters of the Blues because he acted most disgracefully against them” (Julius Capitolinus, *Life of Verus*, p. 306). But what need is there to pursue at greater length events that occurred so often?
