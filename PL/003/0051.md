The other poet is thus called by his proper name by Minucius, just as he was later by Lactantius (Lactant., *Divine Institutes*, book I, chap. 5, p. 22): “What of Maro of Mantua? Does he not speak more openly, more nearly, and more truly?”—that is, does he not approach more closely to the true doctrine concerning God, and declare it more openly and truly? Yet our author does not give his verses in full, nor say in which book they occur, but presents only their sense and certain words, although excerpted from two of his books, in this form: “In the beginning, a spirit nourishes from within heaven and the earth and the other members of the world, and an infused [Col. 0426C] mind moves them; thence comes the race of men and cattle, and whatever else belongs to living creatures.” Elsewhere he calls this same mind and spirit “God.” For these are his words: “For God passes through all the lands, the expanses of the sea, and the profound heaven; from him come men and cattle, rain and fires.” It will therefore not be wholly useless—and for some will be worth the effort—to transcribe those verses in full and indicate where they are found. The former are read thus in the sixth book of the *Aeneid*:

> In the beginning, a spirit nourishes from within the heaven, the earth,  
> The liquid plains, the shining globe of the moon, and the Titanian stars;  
> And a mind, infused throughout its limbs, moves the whole mass  
> And mingles itself with the mighty body.  
> Thence come the race of men and cattle, the lives of flying creatures,  
> And the monsters that the sea bears beneath its marble surface.  
> (Virgil, *Aeneid*, book VI, v. 719 ff.)

The latter occur thus in the fourth book of the *Georgics*:

> [Col. 0426D] For God passes through all  
> The lands, the expanses of the sea, and the profound heaven.  
> From him come cattle, herds, men, and every race of wild beasts;  
> And each at birth summons from him its slender life.  
> (The same, *Georgics*, book IV, v. 220 ff.)

On these verses, and especially the former, Servius’s observations should be read. Minucius could have cited several other poets, especially Greek poets, whom we discussed in the preceding volume (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 784). But because, as is evident, no one denies that Homer was the leader and chief of all Greek poets and Maro of the Latin poets, he therefore considered their testimonies more than sufficient to prove that principal [Col. 0427A] dogma of the Christian religion.

He accordingly passes to the more celebrated philosophers and proves that, although not in the same words, they agreed in substance upon the same doctrine. He begins his account with Thales: “Thales of Miletus, who was the first of all to dispute about heavenly things, said that the origin of things was water,” as we saw elsewhere, “but that God was the mind which formed all things from water” (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 434 and 776). These words appear to have been excerpted from Cicero, where he discusses the same question as our Minucius. For there, like Minucius, he reviews the philosophers’ opinions and likewise begins with Thales: “Thales of Miletus, who was the first to inquire into such matters, said that water was the origin of things, but that God was the mind which fashioned all things from water” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 199, line 20). Lactantius later reported the same thing in almost exactly the same words as Minucius (Lactant., *Divine Institutes*, book I, chap. 5, p. 24).

But Minucius immediately added the following words, which have been so corrupted by the carelessness of scribes and copyists that, without some new manuscript codex, their true reading can scarcely be discovered by anyone. In our printed editions they are presented thus: “And (*et*; royal ms. *eo*; Rigaltius *eho*) the account of water and spirit is higher and more sublime than could have been (*potuerit*; royal ms. *poterit*, wrongly) discovered by man, having been handed down by God (*a Deo tradita*; royal ms. *traditum*).” At the beginning of these words, should we not read *sed* (“but”), or something of that kind, in place of *eo*? However that may be, Minucius’s meaning appears to be that the account of water and spirit—as, they say, the material and efficient causes of all things—is too high and sublime to have been discovered by man unless he were instructed by God himself. Thales therefore could not have discovered what was handed down by God alone. Nevertheless, he could have learned it from the Egyptians, with whom, as we have said elsewhere, he had associated. The Egyptians had received it from Moses, and Moses from God (vol. II, *Apparatus*, p. 1120 ff.).

“Next Anaximenes,” Minucius continues, “and after him Diogenes of Apollonia, held that the infinite and immeasurable air” [was God]. Cicero, however, explains the opinion of each separately. First, these are his words concerning Anaximenes: “Anaximenes established air as God, saying that it is generated, immeasurable and infinite, and always in motion” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 199, [Col. 0427D] line 25). Minucius deliberately omitted the words “that it is generated”; for by the former words Anaximenes strayed all the farther from our true doctrine concerning God, the nearer he appears to approach it in the following words, “infinite and immeasurable.”

Concerning Diogenes of Apollonia, Tullius later says: “But what perception, or what form of God, can the air possess which Diogenes of Apollonia employs as God?” (The same Cicero, ibid., p. 200, line 10.) That Latin orator is indeed silent about what sort of air it was that Diogenes of Apollonia employed as God; but Augustine explains it in these words: “Diogenes, another pupil of Anaximenes, said that air was indeed the matter of things, from which all things were made, but that it was endowed with divine reason, without which nothing could be made from it” (Aug., *City of God*, book VIII, chap. 2). You surely see how rightly Minucius said that Anaximenes and Diogenes held the same opinion concerning God, and that it was not altogether repugnant to the Christian view. For although Diogenes differs from others in saying that air was the matter of things, he nevertheless agrees with Christians in this: he appears to have held it certain that air, endowed with divine reason, was the efficient cause of all things.

There are indeed writers who suspect that by the term “infinite and immeasurable air” these philosophers understood not the air we breathe, but a certain divine spirit by which, as we heard Virgil sing, the entire machinery of the world [Col. 0428B] is nourished and animated. This suspicion appears to be supported by the following words of Plutarch, quoted by Eusebius, concerning Anaximenes’ opinion: “Anaximenes of Miletus taught that air was a principle of this kind; for from it all things arise, and into it in turn all things are dissolved. For our soul (*psychē*, ψυχή), for example, is air and sustains and preserves us; and likewise spirit and air (*pneuma kai aēr*, πνεῦμα καὶ ἀήρ) encompass the whole world. ‘Spirit’ and ‘air’ have the same meaning (*legetai de synōnymōs aēr kai pneuma*, λέγεται δὲ συνωνύμως ἀὴρ καὶ πνεῦμα)” (Euseb., *Preparation for the Gospel*, book XIV, chap. 14, p. 74). These words are found in the first book of Plutarch’s *Opinions of the Philosophers* (Plutarch, book I, *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*, chap. 3, p. 876, vol. 2). But there Anaximenes is reproved by him for assigning merely the matter of things and not their efficient cause. Earlier, however, Eusebius relates from another work of Plutarch, the *Stromata*, that Anaximenes asserted nothing beyond air—which he regarded as infinite—to be the principle of all things. But Laertius explains these matters as though Anaximenes had thereby established the first principles of things (Laert., book II, § 3, and book IX, § 57). Yet perhaps these writers either did not set forth the whole opinion of Anaximenes and Diogenes of Apollonia, or by “infinite” an immeasurable spirit must be understood. On the opinions of both, see the other authors cited by us elsewhere, and Augustine, Epistle CXVIII to Dioscorus, p. 338 (vol. I, *Apparatus*, book II, diss. 2, § 29, p. 435; and book III, diss. 3, chap. 1, art. 1, p. 776).

Minucius continues: “For Anaxagoras, God is said to be the ordering and motion [Col. 0428D] effected by an infinite mind.” Cicero likewise says: “Next Anaxagoras, who received instruction from Anaximenes, was the first to maintain that the ordering and motion of all things were designed and accomplished by the power and reason of an infinite mind” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 199, line 22). Elsewhere he puts it somewhat differently: “Anaxagoras held matter to be infinite, but its particles to be mutually similar and minute; these were at first confused and were afterward reduced to order by the divine mind” (*Academic Questions*, book IV, p. 33, line 12). But Laertius expounded his opinion much more clearly and reliably from Anaxagoras’s own books and on the authority of Timon:

> He was the first to set mind over matter, beginning his work in this manner, in language pleasingly and nobly expressed: “All things were together; then mind came and ordered them.” For this reason he was also surnamed “Mind” or “Intellect” (*ho nous*, ὁ νοῦς). Timon also celebrated this very point about Anaxagoras in the *Silloi*:
>
> And somewhere, they say, is Anaxagoras, that valiant hero,  
> Mind—for truly he had a mind—which suddenly arose  
> And compacted together all things formerly confused.

The Latin rendering is:

> This man was the first to add mind to matter, writing thus at the beginning of his work in pleasant and magnificent language: “All things were together; then mind approached and arranged them; for which reason it is called mind or intellect, ὁ νοῦς.” Timon sang this very thing about Anaxagoras in the *Silloi*:
>
> Now where Anaxagoras is, there is Mind, the most valiant hero,  
> Who thinks that Mind—for mind was not lacking to him—suddenly  
> Arranged those things which once had been disordered together.

Lucretius, Augustine, and we ourselves also say more about him in the places already cited (Lucret., book I, near the end).

Minucius next says: “For Pythagoras, God is the mind that passes through and pervades the whole nature of things, from which the life of all living creatures is also derived.” Our author excerpted this too from Cicero: “Pythagoras,” says Cicero, “held that mind pervades and passes through the nature of things, and that from it our souls are taken” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 199, line 37). Lactantius undoubtedly followed both. What Laertius and Plutarch record concerning the unity and duality asserted by Pythagoras also comes to the same point, as is clear from what we observed concerning that philosopher at the places already cited in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus* (Lactant., *Divine Institutes*, book I, chap. 5, p. 24; and *On the Anger of God*, chap. 11, p. 796; Laert., book VI, *Life of Pythagoras*; Plutarch, *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*, book I, p. 876; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 783).

After this Minucius says: “It is well known that Xenophanes taught that the whole infinite, together with mind, is God.” These are Cicero’s very words: “Then Xenophanes, adding mind, held that everything else which was infinite was God” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 199, line 4). Laertius, however, relates his view somewhat differently, though not in an altogether different sense: “The substance of God is spherical and has nothing in common with human beings. He sees wholly and hears wholly, yet does not breathe. He is at once all things: mind, wisdom, and eternity” (Laert., book IX, § 15). Here the word *sphairoeidē* (σφαιροειδῆ), “spherical” or “round” in substance, signifies nothing other than “infinite.” He used it because a round figure has neither beginning nor end. Cicero therefore reports the same opinion of Xenophanes elsewhere in these words: “Xenophanes said that all things are one, that this one is immutable and is God, never born, everlasting, and spherical in shape” (*Academic Questions*, book IV, p. 33). Before Minucius’s time, moreover, Clement of Alexandria openly taught that this opinion of Xenophanes agreed with Christian doctrine and with these words of Isaiah: “To whom have you likened me? says the Lord.” He proves it in this manner:

> Xenophanes of Colophon therefore rightly teaches that God is one and incorporeal, and adds:
>
> One God, greatest among gods and men,  
> In bodily form and in thought wholly unlike mortals.

The Latin rendering is:

> Xenophanes of Colophon, rightly teaching that God is one and incorporeal, adds:
>
> Greatest in the race of gods and men  
> Is the one God,  
> Who neither in body nor in mind is like  
> Any mortal.

Eusebius transcribed in full this passage, which we briefly touched upon elsewhere; yet in another place he gives words of Plutarch which represent Xenophanes’ opinion concerning God very differently. See Epiphanius and Theodoret (vol. I, *Apparatus*, [Col. 0430B] p. 1113; Euseb., *Preparation for the Gospel*, book XIII, chap. 13, p. 679, and book I, chap. 8, p. 23; Epiphan., *Exposition of the Faith*, p. 1087; Theodoret, Discourse 2, *On the Cure of the Greek Maladies*, p. 486).

## ARTICLE II  
### On the Other Gentile Philosophers: Antisthenes, Speusippus, Democritus, Strato, Epicurus, Aristotle, Heraclides of Pontus, Theophrastus, Cleanthes, Zeno, Chrysippus, Diogenes of Babylon, Xenophon, Aristo, and Plato

Let us, if you please, proceed with our Minucius to the other gentile philosophers. He relates as follows what Antisthenes and Speusippus thought concerning God: “Antisthenes knew many popular gods, but one principal natural God; Speusippus, however, knew God as a natural, animate power by which all things are governed.” He undoubtedly borrowed this from Cicero, who had spoken of both in almost the same words: “Antisthenes, in the book entitled *The Natural Philosopher*, says that there are many popular gods, but one natural God.... Nor does Speusippus speak very differently (*Seuxippus* in the royal manuscript, but wrongly), for, following his uncle Plato, he says that there is a certain power by which all things are governed, and that it is animate” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 200, line 22). Lactantius later committed the same opinion of Antisthenes to writing (Lactant., *Divine Institutes*, book I, chap. 5, p. 24; and *On the Anger of God*, chap. 11, p. 796). We have discussed this philosopher and Speusippus elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 1113, 1122, and 1134). Laertius [Col. 0430D], in Speusippus’s *Life*, asserts that he remained within the doctrines of his uncle Plato. But Numenius, quoted by Eusebius, complains that Speusippus undermined those doctrines. Finally, Laertius observes that Speusippus wrote a book of *Definitions*, which some think to be the work published among the acknowledged writings of Plato. At the beginning of that book the following definition of God is read: “God is an immortal living being, self-sufficient for happiness, an everlasting essence, and the cause of the nature of the good” (*Theos zōon athanaton, autarkes pros eudaimonian, ousia aidios, tou agathou physeōs aitia*; Θεὸς ζῶον ἀθάνατον, αὐτάρκες πρὸς εὐδαιμονίαν, οὐσία ἀΐδιος, τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ φύσεως αἰτία) (Laert., book IV, §§ 1, 5; Euseb., *Preparation for the Gospel*, book IV, chap. 5, p. 727; in Plato, vol. 3, p. 411).

Again, Minucius had excerpted from Cicero [Col. 0431A] what he next added about Democritus: “What of Democritus? Although the first inventor of atoms, does he not frequently speak of the nature which pours forth images, and of intelligence, as God?” Cicero similarly says of him: “What of Democritus,” he asks, “who at one time numbers images and their circuits among the gods, at another that nature which pours forth and emits the images, and at another our knowledge and intelligence?” (Cicero, cited book, p. 200, line 5). Augustine therefore writes the following to Dioscorus concerning that opinion of Democritus:

> How much better it would have been had I never even heard the name of Democritus than that I should reflect with grief upon some man, regarded as great in his own time, who thought that the gods were images flowing from solid bodies, although not themselves solid, and that by moving about this way and that through their own motion and gliding into human minds, they caused divine power to be conceived—although certainly the body from which the image flowed would be judged all the more excellent in proportion as it was more solid. And so, as these men say, his opinion fluctuated and wavered, so that at times he said that a certain nature from which the images flowed was God; yet this God could not be conceived except through those images which it pours forth and emits—that is, images which were borne, came, and entered our minds as though by a continuous emanation, in the likeness of a body, from that nature which he regards as somehow corporeal and everlasting, and therefore also divine—in order that we might conceive God or gods.  
> (Augustine, Epistle 118, p. 340)

But however much Democritus fluctuated and wavered, from what Augustine himself and Cicero handed down in writing concerning his opinion, who does not see that Minucius rightly and deservedly said that in at least some manner Democritus acknowledged the divine nature and that God is truly one? And that was sufficient for Minucius’s purpose.

He next sets forth Strato’s opinion in these few words: “Strato likewise [held] nature itself.” Yet the fewer these words are, the less clear they are. Cicero, whom we have so often praised, will therefore furnish you with their explanation: “Nor should we listen,” he says, “to Strato, the pupil of Theophrastus, who is called the natural philosopher and who holds that all divine power resides in nature, which possesses the causes of generation, growth, and diminution, but lacks all sensation and form” (Cic., [Col. 0431D] cited book, p. 200, line 45). Do you want more? Hear the same orator speak of him again:

> You deny that anything can exist without God. Here, coming at you from the other side, is Strato of Lampsacus, who grants this God exemption from a very great task indeed. But since the priests of the gods enjoy exemption from duties, how much more equitable is it that the gods themselves should enjoy it? He denies that he employs the gods’ workmanship in constructing the world. He teaches that all things, whatever they may be, were produced by nature—not as the philosopher does who says that these things are compounded of rough and smooth bodies and of hooked and barbed bodies, with void interposed. He judges these to be dreams of Democritus, who was not teaching but merely wishing. Strato himself, however, pursuing the individual parts of the world, teaches that whatever exists or comes to pass does so, or has done so, through natural weights and motions. [Col. 0432A] Thus he frees God from a great task, and me from fear.  
> (*Academic Questions*, book IV, p. 33, line 46)

If you desire another author to be cited, here at hand is Plutarch, who left the following written concerning Strato’s opinion:

> Indeed Strato, the foremost of the other Peripatetics, disagrees with Aristotle on many matters and maintains opinions contrary to Plato concerning motion, mind, soul, and generation. Finally, he denies that the world itself is a living being and says that what occurs by nature follows what occurs by chance.

The Latin version continues: “Chance supplies the beginning of motion to nature, and physical motions are then completed by nature in this way” (Plutarch, *Against Colotes the Epicurean*, p. 1115). We have already seen Cicero explain what Strato called “nature.” But we shall still have something to say about this opinion of his when we attempt to make clear to everyone how Lactantius refutes it.

Meanwhile, let us follow our Minucius: “Even that Epicurus,” he says, “who imagines the gods either idle or nonexistent, nevertheless sets nature above them.” Velleius the Epicurean, however, explains at length in Cicero this opinion of Epicurus concerning God. After proving by many arguments that the life of God is such that nothing happier or more wholly abounding in every good can be conceived, he gives this reason for his opinion: “For he does nothing, is entangled in no occupations, and undertakes no labors; he rejoices in his wisdom and virtue, and is assured that he will always possess both the greatest and everlasting pleasures.” Later, in the same place, he declares that Epicurus falsely taught that the world was produced by nature: “There was no need for craftsmanship, and that matter is very easy for nature” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 203, line 8). For, as we have already said, he babbled that all things had been made by a fortuitous collision of atoms. Finally, in the same place, Tullius said in the person of Velleius, or of the Epicureans: “And we piously and reverently worship the excellent and preeminent nature.” Our Minucius distinguishes the things Epicurus taught falsely from those by which, willing or unwilling, he confessed a nature more excellent than all others and therefore divine. Consult, if you please, Laertius and the notes of Ménage and Gassendi upon him, and another book by the latter (Laert., book X, § 123; notes of Ménage and Gassendi on the latter’s book IV, *On the Life and Character of Epicurus*, chaps. 1, 2, 3).

Next Minucius transcribed many of Cicero’s words reporting Aristotle’s opinion concerning God. For behold how he speaks: “Aristotle varies, yet nevertheless assigns a single power. For at one time he calls mind God, at another the world, and at another he sets God over the world.” Tullius says: “Aristotle, in the third book *On Philosophy*, throws many things into confusion, disagreeing with his master Plato alone. For at one moment he ascribes all divinity to mind, at another says that the world itself is God, and at another places some other being over the world” (Cic., cited book, p. 200, line 26 ff.). Minucius excerpted these words but omitted [Col. 0433A] the following: “And he assigns such functions to that being that it governs and preserves motion by a certain revolution of the world; then he says that the heat of heaven is God.... But when he also wishes God to be without a body, he deprives him of every sensation and of wisdom.” Lactantius nevertheless reported fewer details than either author concerning Aristotle’s opinion, in this manner: “Although Aristotle disagrees with himself and both says and thinks contradictory things, he nevertheless testifies that one supreme mind presides over the world” (Lactant., *Divine Institutes*, book II, chap. 25, p. 25; and *On the Anger of God*, chap. 11, p. 796). In another book he is briefer still: “Aristotle and his Peripatetics,” he says, “held nearly the same views as Antisthenes,” of whom we spoke shortly before. See Plutarch and Lescalopier, and, if you wish, what we have observed elsewhere (Plutarch, *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*, book I, chap. 7, p. 881; Lescalopier on the cited passage of Cicero, § 33, p. 54, and *On Aristotle’s Theology*, p. 184 ff.; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 781).

Furthermore, Minucius says, “Heraclides of Pontus, though in varying ways, ascribes divine mind to God, assigning supremacy at one time to the world and at another to the divine mind.” Cicero reports this and still more concerning him: “Heraclides of Pontus, from the same [Col. 0433B] school of Plato, filled his books with childish tales; and yet at one time he thinks the world is God, at another a divine mind. He also ascribes divinity to the wandering stars, deprives God of sensation, and wishes his form to be mutable; and in the same book he again numbers earth and heaven among the gods” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 200, line 39 ff.). Minucius therefore, as you see, reports only those elements of Heraclides’ opinion which served his purpose. Laertius wrote his life, and we discussed him in the passage cited shortly before in the preceding volume (Laert., near the end of book V; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 779).

Minucius then joins four other philosophers together: “Theophrastus, Zeno, Chrysippus, and Cleanthes are themselves manifold in their views, but all return to the unity of providence.” Immediately thereafter he reviews individually the opinions of the latter three, but says nothing further about the first. Who would dare assert whether this was omitted by Minucius himself or through a copyist’s drowsiness? If you wish to know Theophrastus’s opinion, these words of Cicero will disclose it to you: “Nor indeed is the inconsistency of Theophrastus to be tolerated; for at one time he assigns divine supremacy to mind, at another to heaven, and then to the signs and heavenly stars” (Cic., ibid., p. 200, line 42). See, if you please, what we have observed concerning him elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 781).

Having made these observations, let us now examine what Minucius determines concerning the other three. He begins with Cleanthes: “Cleanthes asserts that God is at one time mind, at another soul, at another ether, and most often reason.” Thus he began with the last, although he acknowledges, as does Cicero, whose footsteps he follows in his usual manner, that Cleanthes was Zeno’s pupil. Cicero committed the following account of his opinion to writing: “Cleanthes, who heard Zeno, at one time says that the world itself is God; at another he gives this name to the mind and soul of the whole of nature; while at another he judges the outermost and highest heat, poured around everything on every side, enclosing and embracing all things at the boundary—which is called ether—to be most certainly God” (Cic., cited book, p. 101, line 10). Cicero explains this still more fully, as we have noted elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 782).

Minucius next assigns the second place to Zeno, Cleanthes’s master, and sets forth his opinion concerning God as follows: “Zeno, his master, holds that the natural and divine law, at one time the ether, and at another reason, is the principle of all things. The same man, by interpretation—showing that Juno is air, Jupiter heaven, Neptune the sea, Vulcan fire, and similarly that the other gods of the common people are the elements—sternly accuses and refutes the public error.” Have not these words also been excerpted from the following passage of Cicero?

> Zeno holds that natural law is divine, and that it possesses the power of commanding what is right and forbidding the contrary.... Elsewhere he says that the ether is God.... In other books, however, he thinks that a certain reason extending throughout the whole of nature is endowed with divinity. He ascribes the same to the stars, and then to the years, months, and changes of the years. But when he interprets Hesiod’s *Theogony*, he wholly abolishes the customary and accepted conceptions of the gods. For he does not number Jupiter, Juno, Vesta, or anyone else called by such a name among the gods, but by a certain mode of signification assigns these names to inanimate and mute things.  
> (Cic., cited book, p. 200, line 47 [Col. 0434C] ff.)

Laertius testifies that Zeno’s opinion was: “There is one God, who is also mind, fate, and Zeus, and is called by many other names” (*Hena te einai Theon, kai noun, kai heimarmenēn, kai Dia, pollais te heterais onomasiais prosonomazesthai*; Ἕνα τε εἶναι Θεὸν, καὶ νοῦν, καὶ εἱμαρμένην, καὶ Δία, πολλαῖς τε ἑτέραις ὀνομασίαις προσονομάζεσθαι) (Laert., book VII, *Life of Zeno*, §§ 135 and 147). He later enumerated those names. Hence Tertullian says: “Among your own sages also it is agreed that the *Logos* (λόγος), that is, Speech and Reason, is regarded as the artificer of the universe. For Zeno defines him as the maker who formed all things in their ordered arrangement, and says that the same is called Fate, God, the mind of Jupiter, and the necessity of all things” (Tertull., *Apology*, chap. 21). Lactantius follows Tertullian (Lactant., *Divine Institutes*, book IV, chap. 7, p. 373). But we have already touched upon some of these matters [Col. 0434D] in the preceding volume of our *Apparatus* (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 780). We shall show in the following dissertation in what manner Zeno and the Stoics regarded the gods of the common people as elements (*Dissertation on Arnobius*, chap. 17, art. 5).

The third named by Minucius is Chrysippus, who, he says, “holding almost the same doctrines, believes divine power, rational nature, and fatal necessity to be God; and he imitates Zeno in the physiological interpretation of the poems of Hesiod, Homer, and Orpheus.” Cicero pursues these matters much more fully (Cic., cited book, p. 201, line 27). For he records the following as committed to writing in the first book of Chrysippus’s *On the Nature of the Gods*: “He says that divine power resides in reason and in the soul and mind of universal nature; he also says that the world itself is God, as is the universal diffusion of its soul; then also its ruling principle, which consists in mind and reason; and the common nature of things, containing the universe and all things; and further the force of fate and the necessity of future events.” After adding other things which you may read in the same place, Cicero reports the following from Chrysippus’s second book, likewise *On the Nature of the Gods*: “In the second book, however, he wishes to adapt the fables of Orpheus, Musaeus, Hesiod, and Homer to what he himself had said in the first book concerning the immortal gods, so that even the most ancient poets, who did not so much as suspect these doctrines, may appear to have been Stoics.” Concerning these and the other Stoics, see Laertius, who published their lives; Plutarch; Lipsius; and the observations made by us in the preceding volume (Laert., book VII, *On the Lives and Doctrines of Illustrious Philosophers*; Plutarch, *On the Opinions of the Philosophers*, book I, chaps. 7 and 8, and *On the Contradictions of the Stoics*; Lipsius, *Stoic Physics*, book I, diss. 3 ff.; vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 780).

Our Minucius continues and says: “For Diogenes of Babylon also, the method is to explain and discuss the birth of Jupiter, the origin of Minerva, and other matters of this kind as names of things, not of gods.” Cicero had preceded him in these words: “Diogenes of Babylon,” whom elsewhere he calls a great and weighty Stoic, “in the book entitled *On Minerva*, translates Jupiter’s giving birth and the virgin’s origin into philosophical terms, separating them from the fable” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 220, line 41; and *On Duties*, book III, p. 394). You will find other books of his—*On Divination*, *On Nobility*, and *On Laws*—cited by the same Cicero, by Athenaeus, and by Plutarch. If Laertius is to be believed, “he was a Stoic from Seleucia and was called the Babylonian because of the proximity of the cities.” If Gellius is to be trusted, he lived in the time of the poet Plautus and Carneades the Academic; if Lucian, he prolonged his life to the age of ninety-two (Cic., *On Divination*, book I, p. 255; Athenaeus, *Deipnosophists*, book IV, p. 168, and book XII, p. 526; Plutarch, *On Exile*, p. 605; Laert., book VI, near the end of the *Life of Diogenes*, § 81; Gellius, *Attic Nights*, book XVII, chap. 21; Lucian, *The Long-Lived*, p. 916).

Why say more? Minucius brings forward two other men who utterly denied that God’s majesty could be understood by human beings. The one, to use Minucius’s words, was “Xenophon the Socratic, who denies that the form of the true God can be seen and therefore says that it ought not to be investigated.” The other was “Aristo of Chios, who says that it cannot be comprehended at all.” Cicero explains the former more clearly: “In the writings in which Xenophon reported the sayings of Socrates, he makes Socrates argue that the form of God ought not to be investigated; he also calls both the sun and the mind God, and at one time speaks of one God, but at another of several gods.” What Cicero relates concerning him is in fact found in the fourth book of Xenophon’s *Memorabilia*. Our Minucius appropriated for his own purposes the first part of this proposition, and after him so did Lactantius and Theodoret. We have discussed it elsewhere (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 200, line 18; Xenoph., *Memorabilia*, book IV, p. [Col. 0436A] 802; Lactant., *On the Anger of God*, chap. 11, p. 795; Theodoret, vol. 4, discourse *On Faith*, p. 477).

Cicero explained Minucius’s few words concerning Aristo’s opinion in the following fuller and clearer terms: “He holds that the form of God cannot be understood, says that there is no sensation in the gods, and is altogether uncertain whether God is animate or not” (vol. I, *Apparatus*, p. 782; Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 201, line 8). It should be observed in passing that the royal manuscript codex of Minucius does not read “Aristo of Chios,” as the printed editions do, but “Aristo the Stoic.” You may therefore perhaps ask whether “Stoic” ought to be read there rather than “of Chios.” But however one reads it, the difference is slight. Aristo was from Chios by birth and a Stoic by philosophical sect. This is sufficiently clear from what we have observed about him elsewhere (vol. I, *Apparatus*, diss. 3, [Col. 0436B] chap. 2, art. 18, p. 1145 ff.).

Plato, finally, deservedly closes the ranks of these philosophers, being easily the chief of all the others. Minucius relates his opinion concerning God as follows: “For Plato in the *Timaeus*, God is, by his very name, the parent of the world, the artificer of the soul, and the maker of heavenly and earthly things; he prefaces his account by saying that God is difficult to discover because of his exceedingly great and incredible power and, when discovered, impossible to declare publicly.” Minucius therefore openly states that this opinion of Plato is clearer than all the others and almost wholly heavenly, “were it not that it is sometimes sullied by an admixture of civic accommodation”—that is, if Plato, fearing the hemlock and death of his teacher Socrates, had not sometimes been compelled cravenly and slavishly to flatter the false and empty opinions of the ignorant populace. Hence Justin Martyr says: “Here Plato, fearing those who embraced the worship of many gods, introduces his creator-god speaking things contradictory to himself” (*Enthaûtha ho Platōn tous tēn polytheotēta aspazomenous dediōs, ta enantia heautō ton kat’ auton dēmiourgon eisagei legonta*; Ἐνταῦθα ὁ Πλάτων τοὺς τὴν πολυθεότητα ἀσπαζομένους δεδιὼς, τὰ ἐναντία ἑαυτῷ τὸν κατ’ αὐτὸν δημιουργὸν εἰσάγει λέγοντα) (Justin, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, p. 21; Plato, *Timaeus*, p. 41, vol. 3), namely when speaking to the other gods, whom in the same place he had said were begotten by him.

When Cicero relates Plato’s opinion, however, he first reproaches his inconsistency: “It would take too long,” he says, “to speak now of Plato’s inconsistency: in the *Timaeus* he says that the father of this world cannot be named, while in the books of the *Laws* he thinks that one ought not to inquire at all what God is” (Cic., *On the Nature of the Gods*, book I, p. 200, line 11). Lactantius cited the same book and opinion of Plato (Lactant., *Divine Institutes*, book I, chap. 8, p. 41; and *On the Anger of God*, chap. 11, p. 795). Cicero, however, and afterward Eusebius record more there concerning Plato himself and his inconsistency, to which Lescalopier attempted to respond (Euseb., *Preparation for the Gospel*, book XIII, chap. 14; Lescalopier, *Exposition of Book I of Cicero’s On the Nature of the Gods*, § 30, p. 50). Minucius omitted these matters because they did not conduce to his purpose.

The words of Plato cited by Minucius and others are in fact read thus in his *Timaeus*: “It is difficult to discover the father of this universe, and, when he has been discovered, impossible to declare him to the multitude” (*eis pantas adynaton legein*; εἰς πάντας ἀδύνατον λέγειν) (Plato, vol. III, p. 28). Athenagoras transcribed the same words where, like our Minucius, he plainly demonstrates that the gentile philosophers acknowledged one supreme God (Athenag., *Embassy for the Christians*, p. 6 ff.). Cicero, in the *Fragments*, and Tertullian likewise cited those words of the philosopher, and we reported them elsewhere (Cic., *Fragments*, p. 26; Tertull., *Apology*, chap. 46, p. 40; vol. I, *Apparatus*, pp. 781 and 948, p. 155).

Minucius then adds the following: “These things are almost the same as our own. For we too know God and have called him the parent of all things.” Athenagoras similarly says:

> If, then, Plato is not godless when he conceives one unbegotten God as the creator of the universe, neither are we godless, since we know and hold fast to that God by whose Word all things were created and by whose Spirit they are sustained.

(*Ei toinyn ouk estin atheos Platōn, hena ton dēmiourgon tōn holōn noōn agennēton Theon, oude hēmeis atheoi, hyph’ hou logō dedēmiourgētai, kai tō par’ autou pneumati synechetai ta panta, touton eidotes kai kratountes Theon*; εἰ τοίνυν οὐκ ἔστιν ἄθεος Πλάτων, ἕνα τὸν δημιουργὸν τῶν ὅλων νοῶν ἀγέννητον Θεόν, οὐδὲ ἡμεῖς ἄθεοι, ὑφ’ οὗ λόγῳ δεδημιούργηται, καὶ τῷ παρ’ αὐτοῦ πνεύματι συνέχεται τὰ πάντα, τοῦτον εἰδότες καὶ κρατοῦντες Θεόν.)

The accompanying Latin rendering is:

> If, then, this opinion of Plato concerning the one unbegotten God of the universe is not impious, neither shall we be impious, since we acknowledge and worship that God by whose Word all these things were fashioned, just as by his Spirit they are held together.
