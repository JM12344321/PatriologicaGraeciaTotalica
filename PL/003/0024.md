### VIII

How consonant these things are with the discipline of the most ancient Catholic Church, which always showed the greatest [Col. 0162B] and maternal solicitude toward confessors held in chains; and how foreign they appear to the seemingly most rigorous institutions of the Montanists. Lucian, in his letter *On the Death of Peregrinus*, graphically and clearly describes in the following words the duties of piety and charity performed by the ancient Christians toward their brethren imprisoned for the faith, although he wickedly mocks them:

[Note: [Col. 0162D] “Those of them who held positions of authority even slept inside with him, after bribing the prison guards. Then various meals were brought in, and their sacred discourses were delivered.… Moreover, some came from certain cities in Asia, sent at the common expense of the Christians, to assist, defend, and console the man. They display an extraordinary speed whenever anything of this kind becomes public, for in a short time they spare no expense. Thus a great deal of money then came to Peregrinus from them on the pretext of his imprisonment, and he made no small income from it.”]

“Their magistrates even slept inside with him, after the guards had been bribed. Then various meals were brought in, and their sacred discourses were delivered.… Some also came from certain cities of Asia, sent at the common expense of the Christians, to bring aid to the man, defend him, and console him. They display a certain incredible speed if anything of this kind [Col. 0162C] becomes public. For they immediately spare no expense. Accordingly, much money was then sent to Peregrinus, and he made no small profit from this.”

But let us hear the Christians themselves discussing this subject. Saint Cyprian mentions this ancient custom of the Church in many passages of his letters, and he applied himself diligently and intelligently to this no less than to the other duties of pastoral care. For in his fifth letter, written from his place of retirement to the clergy, he gives the following instruction on this matter:

“As regards supplying expenses, whether for those who, having confessed God with a glorious voice, are being held in prison, or for those who suffer in poverty and want, I ask that nothing be lacking. For the entire sum that has been collected is there, distributed among the clergy for cases of this sort, so that several may have the means [Col. 0162D] to provide for the necessities and afflictions of individuals.”

Indeed, such was the Christian people’s affection toward the confessors that there was no need to spur them on in this respect; rather, it was necessary, according to the dictates of prudence, to regulate the brethren’s rush to the prisons to cherish and refresh the confessors. [Col. 0163A] Hence he immediately adds:

“I also ask that your skill and solicitude not cease to make provision for peace and quiet. For although the brethren, in their affection, eagerly desire to assemble and visit the good confessors, whom divine favor has already distinguished by glorious beginnings, I nevertheless think that this should be done cautiously—not in crowds, nor with a large multitude gathered at once—lest resentment be aroused by that very fact, access be denied, and, while in our insatiability we desire too much, we lose everything. Therefore take counsel and make provision so that this may be done more safely and with moderation. Thus also the presbyters who offer there among the confessors should take turns, each accompanied by a single deacon; for both a change of persons and the rotation of those assembling [Col. 0163B] diminish resentment.”

And in Letter 12—number 37 in Pamelius’ arrangement—he again writes to the clergy on the same subject:

“Although I know, dearest brethren, that you have frequently been admonished by my letters to show every diligence toward those who, having confessed the Lord with a glorious voice, are being held in prison, I nevertheless repeatedly press this duty upon you, lest anything be lacking in the care of those to whom nothing is lacking for glory. And would that the circumstances of my location and rank permitted me to be present there myself now. Eagerly and gladly would I fulfill, with solemn ministry, every affectionate duty toward our most steadfast brethren. But let your diligence represent my office and do all that ought to be done for those whom divine favor has distinguished by such merits of faith and courage.”

And farther on in [Col. 0163C] the same letter, he praises Tertullus’ diligence and solicitude in these duties of piety:

“Although Tertullus, our most faithful and devoted brother, in accordance with the rest of the solicitude and care that he devotes to the brethren in every service of active charity, is not lacking there either in the care of their bodies, etc.”

This care pertained especially to the deacons, as is evident not only from the Acts of our martyrs, but also from the letters of Cyprian himself and from the letter of Dionysius of Alexandria to Domitius and Didymus. There Dionysius commends the diligence of the deacon Eusebius, who devoted to the Alexandrian martyrs the care that Tertullus devoted to the Carthaginian martyrs:

[Note: [Col. 0163D] “Eusebius, whom God strengthened and prepared from the beginning vigorously to fulfill the ministries owed to the confessors held in prison, and, not without danger, to perform the burial rites for the bodies of the perfected and blessed martyrs.” In Eusebius, book VII, chapter 2.]

“Eusebius, whom God strengthened from the very beginning and impelled vigorously to render service to the confessors held in custody, [Col. 0163D] and to bury the bodies of the perfected and blessed martyrs, not without danger to his own life.”

Before Tertullian had completely estranged himself from the Church and begun to argue against it more violently, he spoke with praise of the same custom in his book *To the Martyrs*:

“Among the nourishment of the flesh, blessed designated martyrs, which both our lady mother the Church supplies to you in prison from her own breasts and individual brethren from their own private resources, receive [Col. 0164A] also something from us that may nourish the spirit. For it does no good that the flesh should be fattened while the spirit hungers.”

And farther on, in chapter two:

“Meanwhile, let us compare the very mode of life of the world with that of prison, and see whether the spirit does not gain more in prison than the flesh loses. Indeed, through the care of the Church and the brethren’s *agape*, the flesh does not lose even those things that are its rightful due.”

But after he had surrendered himself entirely to the discipline of Montanus and had begun to conduct his quarrels with the Catholics more bitterly and shamelessly, he represented the Church’s charity toward confessors far more spitefully than Lucian had done. At the same time, in his book *On Fasts*, he set forth in the following words the rigor of diet and discipline that Christians in chains were to observe under the laws and institutions of the Montanists:

[Note: [Col. 0164D] Chapter twelve.]

“Certainly it is your practice to set up taverns in prisons for martyrs whose fate is still uncertain, lest they miss their accustomed way of life, grow weary of life, or be scandalized by a novel discipline of abstinence. Even that Pristinus of yours, a martyr who was no Christian, would not have touched it. For some time, under the privilege of open custody, he had been stuffed with every indulgence—with every bath, as though baths were better baptisms; with every retreat of luxury, as though they were the more private sanctuaries of the Church; and with every enticement of this life, as though these were worthier of eternal life. He was, I suppose, bound by these things so that he would not wish to die. Finally, on the very day of his trial, you premedicated him with wine, as though with an antidote, and so enervated him that, when the governor questioned him, he could no longer answer which Lord he confessed, though merely tickled by a few claws—for this was all his drunkenness could feel. Thus, after the confession had already been wrenched from him, having nothing left but hiccups and belches, he digested his denial on the spot.”

These are Tertullian’s words about the Catholics. Although they are exaggerated beyond all measure in order to bring them into disrepute, [Col. 0164C] they nevertheless clearly show that Catholic martyrs in chains were treated splendidly and generously, insofar as the soldiers and guards permitted it.

Concerning the severity and strictness of the Montanists, however, he had previously prefaced the following:

“Even if the Paraclete had fallen silent from the time of John onward, we ourselves ought especially for this cause to have arisen as prophets … enjoining every *tapeinophrosynē* (ταπεινοφρόνησις, humility or self-abasement), since prison must be learned beforehand, hunger and thirst must be practiced, and endurance of both starvation and meager rations must be adopted, so that a Christian may enter prison in the same condition in which he would emerge from it—not to suffer punishment there, but discipline; not the torments of the world, but duties—and may thereby proceed from custody to the contest with greater confidence, having nothing left of the flesh: armored, as it were, in skin alone, dry and hardened against the claws, the fluid of the blood having already been discharged as though it were an impediment to the soul; while the soul itself is already hastening onward, since through frequent fasting it has learned to know death at close hand.”

This austerity of diet, which Tertullian here describes from the discipline of the Montanists, not only appears nowhere in the Acts of our martyrs; on the contrary, we saw above that they freely used food and drink. And when they were treated more harshly, we heard Perpetua rebuke the tribune in these words, saying to him with holy confidence:

“Why, then, do you not permit the most noble condemned persons to be refreshed—Caesar’s prisoners, that is, who are to fight [Col. 0165A] on his birthday? Or is it not to your glory if we are brought out for him in better flesh?”

These words certainly display not only a lofty confidence of spirit and character, but also demonstrate that Perpetua’s mind was entirely alien to the institutions of the Montanists and was occupied with thoughts different from Tertullian’s. For what could be more contrary than the views and words of Perpetua and Tertullian on this matter? He wishes the Christian to proceed from custody to the contest with nothing left of the flesh, armored in skin alone, dry and hardened, the fluid of the blood already discharged, and so forth. Perpetua, however, protests to the tribune about the meagerness of their diet and says—although playfully—that she wishes to be brought into the amphitheater in better flesh. He reproaches the Catholics because they prepared a richer banquet for Pristinus [Col. 0165B] on the very day of his trial. Perpetua and her fellow martyrs, however, on the day before they were exposed to the beasts, by no means rejected the supper that they called “the free meal,” and which was prepared most lavishly for those condemned to fight the beasts.

### IX

In this they certainly followed the custom of Catholic martyrs, who were accustomed to receive with thanksgiving the food brought to them. Alcibiades, one of the martyrs of Lyons, retained in prison the austere manner of life that he had followed before he was arrested for the faith. But it was shown to Attalus through a vision that Alcibiades was not acting rightly or in good order by practicing that harsh, austere, and singular manner of life.

We have witnesses of the greatest possible weight for this fact: the Christians of Lyons and Vienne themselves, in their encyclical letter concerning the martyrdom of their people, preserved in [Col. 0165C] Eusebius:

[Note: [Col. 0165D] “For a certain Alcibiades, one of their number, lived a very austere life and previously partook of nothing at all, but used only bread and water; and he attempted to continue living in this way even in prison. But after the first contest that Attalus completed in the amphitheater, it was revealed to him that Alcibiades was not acting rightly by refusing to use the creatures of God and by leaving to others an occasion of stumbling. Alcibiades was persuaded and thereafter partook freely of everything and gave thanks to God. For they were not left without the visitation of God’s grace, but had the Holy Spirit as their counselor.” In Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 3.]

“A certain Alcibiades, one of the number of those martyrs, followed a harsh and squalid manner of life. Taking no other food at all, he had until that time been accustomed to live on bread and water alone. When, after being placed in prison, he wished to retain the same way of life, it was revealed to Attalus, after the first contest that he had completed in the amphitheater, that Alcibiades was not acting rightly or in good order, since he neither used the creatures of God nor avoided becoming an example of scandal to others. Alcibiades therefore obeyed, and from that time onward began to partake freely of every kind of food, giving thanks to God. For divine grace had not deprived them of its presence; rather, they had the Holy Spirit as their counselor.”

Accordingly, our martyrs, obeying this same Spirit, practiced even in chains the common and ordinary mode of life [Col. 0165D] of which we have spoken—a way of life plainly different from that which the Montanists prescribed for their own martyrs.

### X

Now, although it already seems to me that I have presented all that could be argued on either side concerning our martyrs, bear with me nevertheless, reader, and allow me to detain you a little longer, until I place under a single view, as it were, everything that has perhaps been discussed at excessive length. I shall reduce to a few words the grounds supporting each opinion—both that which represents the holy martyrs as Montanists and that which has undertaken their defense—by comparing the institutions of the two parties.

But first we must recall that this is one of those controversies concerning which no judgment can safely be pronounced unless we attend to every indication and all the circumstances that either arise from the very matter under dispute or accompany it externally. [Col. 0166B] Concerning such questions, the following most wise rule has been established:

[Note: [Col. 0166D] See *Logic, or the Art of Thinking*, part IV, chapter 13.]

If all the circumstances are such as are only very rarely found conjoined with falsehood, it is the part of a prudent man to show himself compliant and readily to give assent. But if, on the contrary, these circumstances are of the kind that very frequently have falsehood attached to them, reason demands that we either withhold our assent or judge the report to be false, since it does not in any way appear to be true—even though, from another point of view, it may not be absolutely impossible for it to have happened, nor may it be certain, with what is called metaphysical certainty, of the kind enjoyed by the mathematical sciences, that it did not actually happen. For it would be the mark of an exceedingly imprudent man, devoid of judgment, to demand evidence and certainty of this kind in contingent matters [Col. 0166C] and particular events narrated in histories, which rest upon human testimony and human faith.

These principles being established, let us compare Basnage’s arguments with ours, reasons with reasons. It will be the task of a fair and prudent reader to award the case to the party whose reasons are very rarely—or at least less frequently—conjoined with falsehood than those produced by the opposing side. And since the contest on both sides has been waged with both external and internal arguments, we shall compare external arguments with external, and internal arguments with those of the same kind.

As regards the former, or external arguments, I have no doubt whatever that, according to the rule laid down and approved by the judgment of wise men, the case must be awarded to us—or rather to the holy martyrs. [Col. 0166D] For the external testimonies that we possess concerning the sound faith of our martyrs are of such a kind that, although one or another may be mistaken, or may even sometimes have been mistaken, they are all together found conjoined with falsehood not merely very rarely, but never at all. No example can be produced of any martyr or confessor whom the Church entered in the roll of the saints with the same solemnity as Perpetua and her fellow martyrs, but who was subsequently found, fifteen centuries after his death, to have been alien to the sound faith.

We therefore find Basnage lacking in prudence when, to diminish the force of the argument proposed, [Col. 0167A] he points to examples of certain heretics whose names some compilers of works of this kind inserted in their martyrologies on their own private judgment. For to weaken an argument drawn from the public and universal veneration of the saints, never interrupted through so many centuries, it would have been necessary to produce some heretic whose veneration in the Church was no less widespread than that of our martyrs.

In his apologetic letter on behalf of Saint Dionysius of Alexandria, defending Dionysius’ orthodoxy against the Arians, the great Athanasius uses this argument above all, regarding it as great and exceedingly evident:

[Note: [Col. 0167D] “It is therefore a great proof that they do not speak the truth even in this matter, but slander the man, that he was neither condemned for impiety by other bishops and expelled from the episcopate, as these men were from the clergy, nor did he himself leave the Church in defense of a heresy; rather, he fell asleep in it with a good reputation, and to this day his memory is ranked with that of the Fathers and is inscribed among them. For if he had held the same opinions as these men, or had not defended what he wrote, there is no doubt that he would certainly have suffered the same things as they did.” Montfaucon edition, no. 3.]

“It is certainly a weighty argument that in this matter they do not speak the truth, but slander the man: he was never condemned by other bishops for detected [Col. 0167B] impiety and expelled from his episcopate, as they were ejected from the clergy; nor did he withdraw from the Church for the sake of defending a heresy. Rather, he fell asleep piously within it, and his memory has been celebrated to the present day together with that of the Fathers, and has been entered with theirs in the roll. For if he had held the same views as they, or if he had not given an account of his writings, there is no doubt whatever that he would have been punished with the same penalty as they.”

Would Basnage also have disposed of this argument of Athanasius on behalf of Dionysius with the feeble answer that heretical bishops had sometimes slipped into martyrologies or sacred diptychs? Indeed, that would be a futile answer and wholly unworthy of a prudent man. Rather, an example should have been established in the case of a man in whom all these indications of orthodoxy were simultaneously combined, but who was nevertheless discovered to be a heretic after an interval of many centuries following his [Col. 0167C] death. Yet absolutely no example of such a thing exists in the ecclesiastical records.

The conjectures that Basnage derives from the authority of Tertullian and from the compiler of the Acts against Perpetua and her fellow martyrs, however, are of such a kind that they are not infrequently and most manifestly conjoined with falsehood. For, as we have shown, Tertullian very often praised Catholic martyrs and aspired to their communion; and the Novatians, finally, no less exiled from the Catholic Church than the Montanists, published the Acts of Catholic martyrs and commended them to remote posterity, and so forth.

The comparison of the internal arguments yields the same result. Our arguments are drawn from the holiness of the martyrs, from their virtues, [Col. 0167D] miracles, and prophecies, and from the fulfillment of those prophecies—all of which together are never found to have been conjoined with heresy. The arguments advanced by Basnage, however, arise from what appears to him to be the awkwardness of certain visions. Considered by themselves, such things could at most be interpreted either favorably or unfavorably. [Col. 0168A] But when they are found conjoined with holiness and simplicity of life, with miracles and the fulfillment of prophecies, reason demands that, so far as possible, we understand them in the most favorable sense.

From this arises an argument from which, as it seems to me, the adversary cannot escape by any reasoning. For he must either give credence to what is reported in the Acts concerning the virtues, prophecies, and miracles of our martyrs, or rather deny it. If he gives credence to it, the orthodoxy of the holy martyrs is certainly secure, since those virtues—in which nothing feigned, nothing adulterated, and nothing done through dissimulation or hypocrisy is found—together with those miracles and prophecies, do not suit heretical martyrs. Much less do the things contained in the Acts suit Montanists, [Col. 0168B] since, as we have shown, they are thoroughly alien to Montanist dogmas and discipline. In that case, as I have said, reason demands that we interpret the visions and revelations in the most favorable sense, just as we have done, and as before us Saint Augustine and other very learned men did.

But if Basnage denies credence to what is reported concerning the virtues of Perpetua, Felicity, and their fellow martyrs, and so forth, he will for the same reason also have to deny credence to what in the Acts pertains to the martyrs’ visions and revelations. For no principle permits him to believe the Acts when they speak against the martyrs while refusing them credence when they speak on their behalf. This is especially so because it would be far easier to suppose that the compiler of the Acts lied in what he narrates concerning visions than in what he reports concerning signs and miracles. [Col. 0168C] For God alone and the martyrs themselves were witnesses of the visions and revelations, and the martyrs had already been driven from the number of the living by the savagery of the executioners. But as regards the miracles, some of them are said by a contemporary writer to have taken place openly, in the middle of the amphitheater, where certainly there could have been no room in a contemporary author for fraud or suspicion.

Especially relevant here is Dodwell’s excellent observation concerning miracles of this kind:

[Note: [Col. 0168D] *Cyprianic Dissertation XII, On the Fortitude of the Martyrs*, § 45.]

If atheists are willing to approve such things according to the capacity and nature of historical evidence, there is absolutely nothing they could require here to prevent such accounts from appearing entirely true:

“For first of all,” he says, “they relate that the events were performed not in corners or before a few persons privy to them, whose interests might have been involved, but openly and in the sight [Col. 0168D] of everyone, enemies as well as friends—or rather especially enemies, who held the foremost places in the theaters. Thus it came about not only that those who wrote of the events could know them with certainty, but also that those who held a different opinion could easily and decisively refute them.

“Secondly, these events were of such a kind that it was in the highest degree to the interest of very many that the truth should not remain hidden, but should instead become as fully known as possible. It was in the persecutors’ interest that these things should not be believed true, since by them the persecutors would be burdened among those absent—both the living and posterity—with the gravest infamy of cruelty, and would also seem to have been condemned by divine [Col. 0169A] judgment. It was likewise, above all, in the interest of the Christians, if these things had been false, that they should not be believed true, since on their account the Christians would have had to endure such great torments in this life while gaining no recompense at all in the form of future rewards. There is therefore no reason to suspect that these narratives were disregarded. On the contrary, we should expect that both sides would have applied every diligence to investigating them.”

But if Basnage denies that the Acts of Perpetua were compiled by a contemporary author, the matter now comes down to this: we must deny credence not only to the miracles but also to the visions, and the whole controversy must be referred back to the external arguments, in which we have seen how greatly Perpetua’s case triumphs.

[Col. 0170A] Let us grant, nevertheless, that the arguments now advanced on both sides are equal. Since it is the part of a good man and an equitable judge to interpret doubtful matters in a favorable rather than an unfavorable sense, and since no one should be deprived of the possession of a good name and reputation that he enjoys unless he is shown by certain indications and clear arguments to be guilty, it was in no way permissible for Perpetua and her fellow martyrs—who for fifteen centuries had enjoyed a reputation for sound faith and holiness—to be deprived of it on account of a few slight conjectures. This indeed was the reason why I voluntarily assumed the role of defender in this controversy and published this dissertation of mine, such as it is. May the whole of it, in all its extent, redound to the honor of God and the glory of the martyrs!

## ANNOTATIONS ON THE FRAGMENT OF AN UNKNOWN AUTHOR CONCERNING THE CANON OF HOLY SCRIPTURE

(L. A. Muratori, *Antiquities of Medieval Italy*, vol. III, col. 854. Gallandi, *Library of the Ancient Fathers*, vol. II, p. 208. Routh, *Remains of the Sacred Writers*, vol. IV, p. 3. Everard Stosch, *Commentary on the Canon of the Books of the New Testament*, § 63, p. 199. Freindaller, *Commentary*, Linz, 1802. Kirchnoser, *Collection of Sources for the History of the New Testament Canon*, Zurich, 1842, pp. 1–2.)

[Col. 0169B] Muratori was the first to publish this most ancient fragment, which treats of the canon of the New Testament. In *Antiquities of Medieval Italy*, volume III, Milan, 1740, he prefaced the following remarks concerning the manuscript codex from which it was taken:

“But since we have begun to examine the wounds inflicted upon literature during the course of the uncultivated centuries, this fact too must not be passed over in silence: men of the greatest inexperience and ignorance were more frequently employed than before to copy manuscripts, which one consequently sees filled to the point of nausea with errors and filth. I have had no small number of these before me, and it is helpful to offer an example that, in more than one respect, unless I flatter myself too readily, appears to call for publication.

“The Ambrosian Library at Milan preserves a parchment codex received [Col. 0169C] from Bobbio, whose age seemed to me to approach a thousand years. For it was written in large, square letters. The title prefixed to it attributes everything to John Chrysostom, but wrongly.” Page 851.

Then, after mentioning the other treatises contained in the codex, the distinguished man continues:

“From the same codex, therefore, I extracted a most ancient fragment relating to the canon of the divine Scriptures. I spared no effort to discover its author and at the same time to learn whether it had previously been published. Unless my eyes deceived me, or unless I was hindered by the lack of many books—a lack that I have more than once lamented—I nowhere found that it had been published. [Col. 0170B] I therefore retain the hope that readers will receive it all the more gladly, particularly because it breathes an antiquity worthy of the highest veneration.”

Muratori then shows that he believes the fragment should be attributed to Caius, a presbyter of the Roman Church who flourished at the end of the second century. The distinguished Gallandi held the same opinion in the prolegomena to volume II of his *Library of the Fathers*, p. 23. Because the author of the fragment introduced mention of a certain heretic named Miltiades—or, as the name is written here, Miltiadis—Gallandi maintains that this excerpt belonged to the disputation that Caius composed against Proculus, or Proclus, a follower of the Cataphrygians.

For the anonymous writer in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book V, chapter 16—or above, in my volume II, p. 73—at the very beginning [Col. 0170C] of the work he composed against the Cataphrygians, names the heresy of Miltiades. Yet Gallandi’s argument, even if the reading *Miltiades* were sufficiently established—some wish to substitute *Alcibiades* in its place—would not be sufficiently strong to vindicate this fragment for Caius.

Furthermore, the Very Reverend Everard Stosch opposed Muratori’s conjecture concerning Caius. Stosch himself reproduced the fragment after Muratori in his *Dissertation on the Canon of the Books of the New Testament*, published at Frankfurt an der Oder in 1755. In § 63, p. 199, he says that the arguments Muratori advances in support of his conjecture lend it little probability:

[Col. 0171A] “For the fact that Caius rejected the Epistle to the Hebrews as noncanonical was shared by him with several others—Irenaeus and Hippolytus, for example—and indeed, according to Eusebius, book VI, chapter 20, and Jerome, *On Illustrious Men*, chapter 60, with several other doctors of the Roman Church. And the statement that Hermas composed the *Shepherd* in the author’s own time can be applied to any other person who lived in that age.”

Yet although the distinguished Stosch, in §§ 61 and 62, judges that this fragment should not be attributed to Caius because Caius, according to Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book III, chapter 28, regarded the Apocalypse of John as a work of Cerinthus, whereas the author of the fragment regards it as canonical, we must remember that the question whether Caius’ words pertain to the Apocalypse of Saint John is still under judgment. [Col. 0171B] We made some annotations on this matter among Caius’ fragments in volume II of the *Remains*, p. 16; and the distinguished Freindaller, the recent editor of this fragment, whom I shall mention presently, discussed it in §§ 9 and 10.

Stosch also proposed another argument against Caius: the author of the fragment either entirely failed to know, or at least failed to notice, that Paul’s epistles had been written long before the Apocalypse was seen and published by the apostle John. Such an error and lack of reflection could only with difficulty be conceived of Caius, whom Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* VI.2, calls “a most learned man” (ἄνδρα λογιώτατον), and whose disputation against Proclus Photius, codex 48, calls “an earnest discourse” (σπουδαίαν διάλεξιν).

Thus Stosch. Yet this would merely be a “chronological error” (*para ton chronon hamartēma*, παρὰ τὸν χρόνον ἁμάρτημα). Meanwhile, those learned men who, as the distinguished author himself also notes, refer John’s Apocalypse to the reign of the emperor Claudius would deny, together with Epiphanius, *Heresy* LI, chapters 12 [Col. 0171C] and 36, that the matter must be understood in that way. Compare also Thomas Newton, Bishop of Bristol, *Dissertations on the Prophecies*, written in English, volume III, § 24, chapter 1, and J. D. Michaelis, *Lectures on the Books of the New Testament*, final section.

For my part, I would be more troubled by the blemish found at the end of this fragment, where Basilides appears to be presented—if it please the gods—as the founder of the Cataphrygians. For if the passage is to be read and construed in that way, I fear that an argument for gross ignorance on the part of this author would have to be drawn from it.

Although the distinguished Stosch does not wish these words to be regarded as Caius’, an opinion shared both by the distinguished Keil in his additions to Fabricius’ *Greek Library*, volume VII, p. 285, and by [Col. 0171D] other learned men whom Keil mentions there, Stosch nevertheless regards it as highly probable that the fragment is altogether very ancient and was composed around the end of the second century or the beginning of the third. To confirm this opinion he appended many arguments, and very strong ones. So far as I know, all writers who have mentioned this short fragment agree in this judgment.

I shall subsequently present Stosch’s arguments in connection with the individual books of Holy Scripture named in the fragment, since they not only confirm its antiquity but also illuminate the fragment itself admirably.

Nor had the celebrated Mosheim, before Stosch, judged differently concerning the date of the work. In his *Commentary on the Affairs of the Christians before Constantine the Great*, [Col. 0172A] under the first century, § 54, p. 164, he wrote the following concerning it:

“The author is unknown. Muratori thinks that he was Caius, a Roman presbyter who flourished as the second century drew to its close. This is doubtful; but from the little work itself it is certain that he wrote in the second century, and specifically at the time when Hermas was alive. Thus, in this fragment of great value, the following is written concerning Hermas: ‘But very recently, in our own times, in the city of Rome, Hermas composed the *Shepherd*, while his brother Pius, the bishop of the Church of the city of Rome, occupied its chair.’”

Thus Mosheim. Accordingly, the author appears either to have lived near the middle of the second century, when the episcopate of Pius had indeed come to its end, or at least it is certain that he wrote before the century was over, even if a different judgment is made concerning the true date of the little work called the *Shepherd*.

How it happened that this fragment—which, unless I am mistaken, no one at all has rejected or disapproved—escaped the diligence of the distinguished Lardner, I confess I do not know, since the fragment had been communicated to the learned world long before Lardner himself again committed to print his altogether excellent work *On the Credibility of the Gospel History*. [Col. 0172B] Since its author must be placed among those writers who were the very first, with the exception of Papias, either to mention the evangelists themselves or expressly to cite their writings, it is impossible that this first catalogue of the books of the New Testament should not be judged worthy of our attention.

Nevertheless, to our great astonishment, he omitted an epistle that was always and universally received, namely the First Epistle of Saint Peter, [Col. 0172C] whose authority no one had doubted from the time of Polycarp and Papias onward. This omission was made by an author who acknowledged three of the seven “disputed” (ἀντιλεγόμεναι) writings, while admitting no “spurious” (νόθον) book into the canon of the New Testament, with perhaps one exception. I have already spoken of the other blemish in his work concerning the heretic Basilides.

Very recently, after I had stated on page XXI of the preface to my work that I had postponed presenting this fragment concerning the canon because its latest edition had not yet come into my hands, a small work was sent to me from Germany by a nobleman traveling abroad. It had previously appeared at Linz in 1802, with a commentary by Francis Freindaller, canon and public professor of theology.

[Col. 0172D] Freindaller nowhere mentions Stosch’s book on the canon. On the contrary, he maintains that the fragment should be vindicated for Caius the presbyter, although in § 8, p. 13, he says:

“It is highly probable that Caius’ conference with Proclus was conducted and written down in Greek. For Eusebius, Photius, and Theodoretus—who in book III of his *Heretical Fables* entitled it *Against Patroclus* (κατὰ Πάτροκλον)—read it in that form. I add that Valesius argues, on grounds by no means negligible, that this Proclus, one of the leaders of the Montanist sect, was a Greek from Asia. Caius therefore conferred in Greek with a Greek man. Yet our fragment has the stylistic flavor of a Latin rather than a Greek original. [Col. 0173A]

“This difficulty can be met only if one says that the disputation was immediately translated into Latin, or that Caius himself simultaneously composed it in Latin for the benefit of the Romans. Neither conjecture is, to be sure, discordant with the truth. For in this manner most of Saint Irenaeus’ works have come down to us in a very ancient Latin translation, as have very many other works of this kind.”

Thus Freindaller. Meanwhile, I myself seem to have detected from sufficiently clear traces that behind these Latin words lies a man who wrote in Greek, for the evidence is that their construction is so thoroughly Greek that they appear to have been translated from that language.

Freindaller continues in § 13 as follows, after having observed in § 11 that this Caius—Valesius and Ruinart agreeing—was the person of whom it is written at the end of the Acts of Saint Polycarp, “Caius copied these Acts from the manuscripts of Irenaeus, the disciple of Polycarp, with whom he was contemporary”; and that one should therefore not believe that a man who had long associated with Irenaeus despised the Apocalypse of the apostle John:

[Col. 0173B] “If arguments were available from which we could securely conclude that this fragment belonged to the same *Disputation with Proclus* from which Eusebius borrowed his material, and which is undisputedly the work of Caius, then indeed our vindication of it for Caius would rise to the level of fully established certainty.

“Muratori himself, in the passage cited, has already advanced a consideration favorable to this opinion, when he conjectures that Caius seized upon the occasion furnished by his adversaries’ shameless audacity in fabricating new writings to discuss the canon of the books of the New Testament, as is done in the fragment. [Col. 0173C]

“To this it may most appropriately be added that the fragment itself indicates a certain trace of continuity with a disputation—something that it is truly astonishing should have escaped the eyes of so acute a man. [Col. 0174A] For after mentioning Paul’s Epistle to the Romans and its subject, the author appended these words: ‘Concerning each of these matters, it is necessary for us to dispute.’

“Moreover, since nowhere else in the fragment’s entire argument does anything occur discordant with such a disputation, and since everything in it can instead be most readily reconciled with one, what can further delay us from once again restoring this most venerable monument of ecclesiastical antiquity to the ancient Caius, to whom so many arguments vindicate it, now that the opposing difficulties have, as I believe, been sufficiently resolved?”

Thus Freindaller.
